Chapter 31: The Ribs of a Dead Ship, Part 5 of 5: Six
Six
The dark came down at half past four, all at once, the way it does in deep winter on flat land.
One moment the marsh was gray, and the reeds were pale, and the water in the channels was a dull silver. The next, the gray had gone blue, and the reeds had gone black, and the water had gone the color of ink. The far smudge of the city on the edge of the sky turned from brown to a low smoky orange, as ten thousand lamps were lit, one after another. And over the sea, in the east, the first star came out. Small and hard and very bright. Corin had never seen a star come out before. In the city, the lamps were always lit first, and by the time you looked up, the stars were already there.
It got colder.
They could not have a fire. Not with a black boat on the river and a man in a gray cape who could smell last night's smoke in old wood. So they sat in the cave under the stern deck, all three of them, close together, under the horse blanket, with the rod lying across all their knees, glowing a faint dull red. Corin fed it now and then. Small things. The sound of a cart in Hook Lane. The color of a door he had passed once and never gone through. The taste of a cup of tea at the Intake Hall, too strong and too sweet. Little things he would not miss. He was getting good at choosing them.
He did not like that he was getting good at it.
Juno found the apple. The one she had put back in her pocket that morning. She took it out and looked at it, and then she leaned across Corin and put it on Wat's knee, without a word, and sat back.
Wat looked at the apple for a long time.
Then he picked it up in his good hand and ate it, slowly, all of it, core and seeds, the way Corin had. When he had finished, he wiped his beard with the back of his hand, and said, to nobody:
"Robin likes apples."
And nobody said anything after that for a long while.
At eight o'clock, by the far faint bells, Corin got up.
"Where are you going?" said Juno.
"To look at something."
He took the rod. He went along the ribs toward the bow, in the dark, with the red glow of it held out in front of him. The tide was out. The inside of the wreck was mud again, gray and shining, with the frost already forming on it in small white feathers. He went slowly along the beams, hand over hand, foot to foot, and his breath smoked in the red light.
He did not know why he was going. He only knew that he wanted to see the name board once more before they left. MARGERY. SALTGATE. Saints keep her. And the tally under it. The five cuts. He wanted to look at them again with his own eyes, so that when he wrote them down properly in the Ledger tonight, he would be sure.
He was ten feet from the bow when he heard it.
A small sound. Very small. So small that if the marsh had not been so silent, he would never have heard it at all. A scrape. A thin, dry, careful scrape, like a fingernail drawn across a slate. And then a pause. And then another.
Wood. Something cutting wood.
Corin stopped on the beam.
He held the rod up. The red light went out ahead of him, dim, a few yards, no more. He could see the great timbers of the bow coming together into their point. He could see the dark angle where the name board was. He could see the black shine of the mud below.
He could not see anyone.
The scraping had stopped.
He stood very still and listened. Nothing. The drip of water. His own heart. Far off, behind him, in the stern, the small sound of Juno saying something to Wat, and Wat not answering.
"Who's there?" said Corin.
His voice went out into the dark and came back to him off the curved black ribs. There. There.
Nobody answered.
He went on. Slowly. The last ten feet. To the bow. To the name board. He held the rod up to it, close, and the red light fell on the faded gold letters, and the knife-cut prayer, and the old initials, and the heart with no names in it.
And on the tally.
Five cuts. Four straight, one across. The way they had been this morning.
And a sixth.
It was beside the others. A little apart. One single straight cut, as long as his finger, starting a new group of five. The wood inside it was pale and clean. Paler than the others. And wet. Not with frost. With sap, or with the damp of the wood itself, the way a cut in green wood weeps. And caught at the bottom end of it, where the knife had come out, there was a tiny curl of wood. Fresh. So fresh that when Corin put out his finger and touched it, very lightly, it was still soft.
Minutes old.
He turned round fast. Too fast. The beam was slick and his foot went out from under him and he grabbed at the bow timber with his free hand and hung there, with the rod swinging in the other hand and the red light swinging wildly over the ribs, the mud, the dark.
Nobody.
Then he looked down.
On the mud below the bow, in the frost, there were footprints.
Small ones. Bare. He could see the toes. Five little round toe marks and the long soft print of the foot behind them, pressed into the gray mud, with the frost already beginning to creep in round their edges. A child's feet. Or a very small woman's. Bare, in the mud, on a night when the frost was forming in feathers.
They came from under the bow, from the dark angle where the timbers met the mud. And they went away. Out through a gap in the ribs. Out onto the open mud. Toward the reeds.
Corin held the rod out over the side as far as he could reach. The red light went out across the mud a few yards and showed him the footprints going away. One. Two. Three. Four. Small and even and unhurried. Not running. Walking. As if whoever had made them was not frightened at all. As if they did this every night.
Then the light gave out. And the footprints went on into the dark, toward the black wall of the reeds, where he could not see them.
"Wait," said Corin. Not loudly. "Please. I won't hurt you. I just want to—"
The reeds did not move.
The frost went on forming on the mud, slowly, in its small white feathers, filling in the little round toe marks one by one.
Boy, said Malakor, very quietly, from the rod in his hand. Come away.
"Did you see?"
I saw.
"Who was it?"
I don't know. A pause. But they cut it while you were standing in the stern feeding me the taste of bad tea. Ten yards away. And neither of us heard a thing until they wanted us to.
Corin stood on the beam at the bow of the dead ship for a long time, holding the rod out over the dark.
Then he took out the Ledger, and wrote, by the red light, with his hand shaking:
Six.
And under it:
Bare feet. Small. Into the reeds. Not afraid.
At nine o'clock the flood came in.
They felt it before they saw it. A change in the sound of the marsh. A soft, rising, whispering, as the water came sliding back up the channels in the dark. The boat lifted under the stern. The rope went slack, and then taut.
Wat untied it with his good hand. Juno took the oars. Corin sat in the bow with the rod in his lap, glowing faintly, and the Ledger inside his coat, over his heart, and the stones at his hip. Warm. They were warm again. He could feel them.
The boat slid out from under the ribs of the Margery into the open channel.
Corin looked back.
The dead ship stood up out of the black water behind them, its great curved timbers pale with frost against the stars, like the bones of something enormous that had died a long time ago and had not finished remembering it yet. And at the very point of the bow, high up, where the name board was, there was a shape.
Small. Dark. Very still.
It might have been a heron.
Corin watched it until the reeds closed between them, and it was gone.
Under the Lantern Bridge
The river at night was a different river.
By day it had been wide and gray and slow, a great flat road of water with the mist lying on it. By night it was black. Not dark. Black. So black that Corin could not see where the water stopped and the sky began, except where the stars were, and the stars were in the water too, upside down, trembling, so that the boat seemed to be rowing through the middle of the sky.
Juno rowed. Wat steered, with one oar over the stern, his good hand on it and his tied arm against his chest. Corin sat in the bow and watched.
The flood carried them. That was the strange thing. Corin could feel it. A long, slow, heavy push from behind, as if the whole sea had put its shoulder against the river and was leaning on it. Juno hardly had to row. She kept the oars going gently, clunk-splash, clunk-splash, more to keep warm than anything, and the boat went on up the channel faster than it had come down, with a soft whispering chuckle of water under the bow.
The Saltmarsh fell away behind them. The reeds gave way to mud banks, and the mud banks to stone. Low stone walls at first, old and broken, with weed hanging off them. Then wharves. Then warehouses. Long black buildings rising up out of the water on both sides, with great doors in their walls at the waterline, and iron cranes leaning out over the river like the necks of huge sleeping birds.
And then the city.
It came on them slowly, the way a sound grows. First a glow on the sky. Then lights. A few at first, scattered, yellow, high up in windows. Then more. And more. Until both banks were lit with them, a thousand windows, ten thousand, rising up the hills on either side in great slopes and terraces of gold, and every one of them was in the water too, upside down, shaking, so that the river ran between two cities, one standing up and one hanging down, and the boat went through the middle.
And the smell came back. Coal smoke. Wet stone. Tar. Fish. Horses. People. Corin breathed it in, and something in his chest that had been clenched for two days loosened a little, and then clenched again, harder.
Home. He thought the word without meaning to.
Hollow.
"Down," said Wat quietly.
Corin got down. He lay in the bottom of the boat, on the wet boards, under the horse blanket, with his face turned up. Juno pulled the huge coat over her head like a hood and bent over the oars. Wat sat as he was. A bargeman in a boat on the river at night. Nobody would look twice at a bargeman.
They passed the Customs House.
Corin knew it without seeing it. He knew it by the sound. The great stone wall going up beside them, close, so close that the slap of the water came back off it twice. And the outfalls. He could hear them. Under the sound of the river, a deeper sound, a hollow roaring, coming and going as they passed each round black mouth in the wall. Number one. Number two.
Number three.
He turned his head on the boards and looked.
There it was. A round black hole in the stone, just above the waterline, with the brown water pouring out of it in a long fan. The iron gate behind it, in the dark. The mudlarks' gap. The ledge. The wall of birds on strings, and the long row of marks under the word, and somebody, somewhere, still counting.
They went past. The roaring faded.
Six, thought Corin. On the Margery. Bare feet. Not afraid.
He did not know what it meant. He wrote it on the inside of his mind anyway, the way he wrote everything, so that he would not lose it before he could write it in the book.
"Boat," said Wat. "Ahead. Keep still."
Corin kept still.
He heard it before he saw it. Oars. Not the soft hush of the black boat. Clumsier. Four oars, out of time, and a man's voice swearing at another man for splashing him. And a lantern. He saw the light of it go across the sky above him, swinging, yellow.
"Evening," called a voice. Close. Very close. "Who's that?"
"Doon," said Wat. "Patient Grace."
"Where's your barge, then, Doon?"
"Up the locks. Loading hay."
"And what're you doing down here at this hour with a little rowboat?"
A pause.
"Fetching my sister's girl," said Wat. "From the Saltings. Her mother's poorly."
The lantern came closer. Corin saw the yellow light fall across Juno's bent back, across the hood of the huge coat. He did not breathe.
"Bit young to be rowing, isn't she?"
"She rows better than you," said Wat.
There was a silence. Then, from the other boat, a laugh. A real one. Rough and surprised.
"Likely true," said the voice. "Go on, then. Mind the ice at the bridge piers. It's bad tonight."
The lantern swung away. The clumsy oars went past, splashing, and the voices faded, still arguing.
Corin lay in the bottom of the boat and looked up at the sky and let out his breath very slowly.
"Your sister's girl," he whispered.
"Haven't got a sister," said Wat.
Juno made a small strangled sound from inside the hood that might have been a laugh. She kept rowing.
And then the bridge.
The Lantern Bridge came at them out of the dark like a wall. A great long wall of old stone, arched, with the black river pouring through the arches, and on top of it, all along it, from one bank to the other, the lamps. Forty of them. Corin had counted them once, years ago, walking across in the rain. Forty iron posts, each with its glass lamp on top, each burning with a small, steady, pale gold flame. And their light came down off the bridge onto the water in forty long trembling lines, like the strings of a harp.
The first lamp was at the near end. The south end. On the very first post.
It was brighter than the others.
Corin had always known that, in the way you know things you have never thought about. But he had never seen it from the water before. From down here, looking up, it was plain. The first lamp burned whiter. Steadier. As if whoever had lit it had used a different oil. Or a different fire. Its light came down onto the black river in one long straight line, brighter than all the rest, right to the bow of the boat.
It never goes out, Linus Hale had said. Nobody knows who lights it. People say it's for somebody who never came home.
And Wat had asked Hessa to marry him there. Under it. And she had said yes.
They went in under the arch.
The dark closed over them. The roar of the water through the arch was huge, and close, and cold, and full of echoes. The stones of the arch went up over their heads, black and dripping, hung with long icicles where the river spray had frozen. Juno pulled hard. Wat leaned on the steering oar. The boat shot through the arch on the flood like a cork through a pipe, with the ice crashing off the piers on either side.
And came out on the other side into the light.
Corin looked back. Up. At the bridge. At the first lamp, burning white and steady on its post, at the south end.
There was someone standing under it.
One person. Small and far off, against the pale glow. A figure in a long dark coat, standing very still at the parapet, looking down at the river. Not at the boat. Corin was almost sure, not at the boat. Just at the river. The way you look at a river when you have come out at night to look at it, for no reason, because you cannot sleep.
The figure's hands were on the stone of the parapet. They were white. Pale, small, white.
Gloves.
Then the boat went round the bend below the Fish Steps, and the bridge was hidden by the warehouses, and Corin could not see anything at all.
He lay in the bottom of the boat and stared at the place where it had been.
It's a long way, said Malakor quietly. And it's dark. And there are a thousand people in this city with white gloves.
"I know," said Corin.
He did not believe it. And he could tell, from the voice, that Malakor did not either.
The Stairs at Rope Street
Rope Street came down to the river at a place called the Ropewalk Stairs.
They were old stone steps, very steep and very narrow, going up from the water between two warehouses into the dark. The bottom ones were green and slimy with weed. The top ones were lost in shadow. A lamp hung on an iron bracket halfway up, and under it, someone had painted on the wall in white letters gone gray with age: NO MOORING. BY ORDER.
Wat brought the boat in under the lamp, very quietly, and held it against the bottom step with his good hand on an iron ring.
Juno shipped the oars.
She sat for a moment on the rowing bench, breathing hard, with her hands in her lap. Her hands were shaking. Corin could see them in the lamplight. Red and raw from the oars, with a blister broken on one palm.
"The Watch house is at the top," she said. "Third door on the left. There's a blue lamp." She did not look at him. "I'll tell them. Gran and Moll. About the cloth. I'll tell them everything."
"Tell them to leave," said Corin. "Tonight. Both of them. Anywhere. Not Rope Street. Not Tallow Street. Somewhere Kross has never heard of."
"Gran won't go."
"Make her."
Juno looked at him then. A long look. And something in it was almost a laugh, and almost not.
"Have you met Gran?" she said.
Corin did not answer.
She stood up. The boat rocked. She put one foot on the green bottom step, and then she stopped, and turned back, and reached into the deepest pocket of the huge coat. She took out the folded paper. His list. The doors.
She held it out to him.
"Gran's got her own," she said. "She doesn't need this one. And you might." A pause. "In case you forget anybody."
He took it.
She went up the steps. Fast and sure, the way she went up everything, without touching the slimy wall. Past the lamp. Her shadow went up the wall beside her, huge and thin and wavering. At the top she stopped, a small dark shape against the glow of the street beyond.
She looked down at them. At the boat. At Wat with his tied arm and Corin in the bow.
She did not wave this time.
Then she was gone.
Corin sat in the bow with the folded paper in his hand and looked at the empty top of the stairs for a long time.
"She'll come back," said Wat behind him.
Corin turned.
Wat was not looking at him. He was looking up the stairs too. At the place where she had gone.
"Hessa says that," he said. "Out loud. So it's true."
He pushed the boat off from the bottom step with his good hand, and took the steering oar, and turned the bow upstream. Into the dark. Toward the north. Toward the locks, and the hay, and the barge called Patient Grace, and a woman who told everybody everything twice, and a baby who liked apples.
Corin took the oars.
They were too big for him too. He did not care. He set them in the old iron rowlocks and dipped them, and they went in crooked, and splashed, and he said the word Juno had said that morning, the one Mrs. Marta would not have liked.
Wat said nothing. But under the gray beard, at the corner of his mouth, something moved.
Corin dipped the oars again. They went in clean.
And the boat went on up the black river on the flood, under the lights of the sleeping city, with the stones warm at his hip and the folded list in his pocket and six cuts on the bow of a dead ship behind him that he did not understand.
Clunk-splash.
Clunk-splash.
North.
