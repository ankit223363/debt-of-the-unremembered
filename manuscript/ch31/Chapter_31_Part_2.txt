Chapter 31: The Ribs of a Dead Ship, Part 2 of 5: The Flood Tide
The Flood Tide
The argument started before the water came back, and it was still going when the water was up to the boat's ribs.
"I'm going," said Juno.
"No," said Corin.
"I am. Somebody's got to. You can't go. Look at you." She jabbed a finger at him. "You can't walk across this deck without holding on to something. You'd fall in the first creek. And every Inquisitor in the city's got your face on a paper by now. They've probably drawn it. Badly. With a big nose."
"I don't have a big nose."
"They won't care. They'll draw one anyway. And Wat can't go, because if Wat goes, who stays with you? And if nobody stays with you, you'll do something stupid. You always do something stupid when nobody's with you. Vespera says so. Gran says so. I say so." She folded her arms inside the huge coat. "So it's me."
They were sitting at the open end of the cave, on the edge of the broken deck, with their legs hanging over. Below them the mud had changed. An hour ago it had been a long gray shining bank, with little birds running on it. Now it was going. The tide was coming in. Not in a wave, the way Corin had always thought tides came, but quietly, sideways, sliding up the little channels in the mud and spreading out from them in thin brown sheets, so that the bank seemed to sink, slowly, all at once, under a skin of moving water. The little birds had gone. The heron had gone. The water was round the boat now, lifting it, and the boat had begun to straighten up off its side with small creaking sounds, and to swing gently at the end of its rope.
Wat sat in it as if nothing were happening.
"Wat," said Corin. "Tell her."
Wat looked up at them. He looked at Juno for a long time. Then at Corin.
"She can row," he said.
Juno's whole face lit up.
"Wat—"
"She rowed us here," said Wat. "Half of it. While I bailed." He looked back at the water. "Flood'll carry her up. Easy. Ebb'll bring her back. Two hours after noon." He thought about it. "Three."
"You see?" said Juno.
Corin looked at her.
He thought about the city. About the gray cape on the Customs House steps. About Kross, standing at the grating in the silver light, saying Rowan, is that where he said he was going? in that soft pleasant voice. About all the eyes Kross had, everywhere, in every street, looking for a man with Corin's face. And for anybody who might be helping him.
And he thought about a girl of fourteen, rowing up the river alone in her grandfather's coat.
"If they see you," he said.
"They won't."
"If they do."
Juno looked at him. And for a moment her face changed. Not much. Just a little. The fierceness went out of it, and under it there was something older and more tired, and much more frightened, than he had ever seen in her.
"Then they see me," she said. "And I run. I'm good at running. I've been running all my life. Ever since Grandad came home a Slate and Gran had to go out cleaning steps and I had to do the messages." She shrugged. "It's what I'm for."
"That's not what you're for."
"It's what I'm good at," said Juno. "Same thing."
He did not know how to answer that. So he did not.
Instead he took out the Ledger, and opened it to the page from Tuesday night. The one with all the doors. The names, written so carefully, so that he would never lose them.
Toby, who calls me Inspector. Vespera. Hook Lane. The chair by the window. Mrs. Marta says rent is due Friday. Juno. 14. A key on a string. Terrence's girl. Oren. Captain. A scar from the temple to the mouth. Lyra Thorne. Lyra. Lyra. Moll at the Watch house. Knock fast. Briggs. Intake Hall. Fenn. Mother Grue. The arches under the Dye-Works. Hessa Doon. The Patient Grace. Wat. Robin. Bram. Quill Row. Under the loose board.
He tore a page out of the back of the book. He had never done that before. It felt like tearing a bandage off. He wrote the names on it, small and square, the way Wren wrote. Not the barge. Not Hessa. Not Lyra. He left those off on purpose. Then he folded the paper small and gave it to Juno.
"Everyone on there," he said. "Kross let me go so he could see who I ran to. Every door on that list is a door he's watching. Or will be. Tell your gran. Tell Moll. Don't go to any of them yourself. Find somebody Kross has never seen, and send them."
"Gran'll already be doing it," said Juno. "I bet you a copper. She'll have half the Lower City running notes by now." But she took the paper, and looked at it, and put it inside the coat, in the deepest pocket, against her chest.
"And don't go to Tallow Street."
"I know."
"And don't go north. Not up the river. Not past the locks. Not for anything."
Juno's eyes went to Wat in the boat. To his big quiet back.
"I know," she said, more quietly.
"And come back."
She did not answer that one. She just stood up, and pulled the huge coat tight round her, and climbed down the side of the broken hull to the boat, the way she climbed down everything, quick and sure, with her hands and feet finding holds Corin could not even see.
Wat had already moved. He was on the deck now, beside Corin, with the boat hook in his hand. Corin had not seen him climb up. For such a big man he moved very quietly.
Juno sat down on the rowing bench. She took the oars. They were much too big for her. The handles crossed over in front of her chest and she had to hold them with her arms spread wide, like a bird about to fly.
Wat untied the rope from the timber and dropped it into the bow.
"Keep to the reeds," he said. "Left bank. Watch the shallows at Gull Point. Come back on the ebb."
It was the most words Corin had ever heard him say at once.
Juno nodded. She dipped the oars. They went in crooked, and splashed, and one of them nearly jumped out of its rowlock. She said a word that Mrs. Marta would not have liked. Then she set her jaw, and dipped them again, and this time they went in clean, and the boat slid forward out from under the ribs of the dead ship, into the open water, onto the brown flood tide.
Clunk-splash. Clunk-splash.
Corin stood on the broken edge of the deck and watched her go.
She went slowly at first, and then faster, as the tide caught her. The boat grew smaller. The sound of the oars grew fainter. She did not look back. He had known she would not. She kept her eyes on the reeds of the left bank and her chin stuck out and her elbows going, and the boat went away up the long gray channel between the frosted reeds, smaller and smaller, until it was a dark speck on the water. And then the mist that still lay over the river took it, softly, the way a hand closes, and it was gone.
The sound of the oars lasted a little longer. Then that was gone too.
Corin stood there anyway.
Wat stood beside him.
"She'll come back," said Corin. He did not know who he was saying it to.
Wat did not answer for a long time.
"Hessa says that," he said at last. "Every time I go down the river. He'll come back. She says it to Robin. Out loud. So he hears it." He turned the boat hook slowly in his big hands. "I always do."
He looked down at Corin.
"So far," he said.
Five Cuts
The dead ship had been called the Margery.
Corin found that out by accident, an hour after Juno left, when he could not sit still any longer and went climbing through the ribs to keep warm.
It was easier than he had expected. The great curved timbers of the hull rose up out of the water on either side of him like the bars of a cage, black and thick and rough, and between them, here and there, there were still bits of the old deck, and old beams going across, and the stumps of planks that had not quite burned. You could go along them like a squirrel along branches. Hand over hand. Foot to foot. He went slowly, because his hands still hurt, and because the timbers were slick with frost on the side the sun had not reached. But he went.
The tide was nearly full now. The water had filled the inside of the wreck, so that he was climbing over a sort of long dark pool, still and brown, with the ribs standing up out of it and their reflections hanging down into it, so that each one seemed twice as long as it really was. Bits of frost floated on it. Leaves. A dead crab, legs up. The water was so still that when he dropped a flake of tar into it, the rings went out and out, slowly, and touched the ribs on both sides, and came back.
At the front of the wreck, the bow, the timbers came together into a great point, like hands pressed together to pray. And there, high up, out of the reach of the water and the weather, in the angle where two of the biggest timbers met, there was a flat place. A board, nailed across between them, sheltered by the curve of the hull. It had once had paint on it. Red, and gold. There was still a little of the gold, in the deep grooves of letters carved into the wood.
MARGERY. SALTGATE.
And under that, smaller, in a different hand, cut with a knife:
Saints keep her.
The first word was worn so smooth that he had to put his face almost against the wood to read it. Somebody, forty years ago, standing where he was standing, on a ship that was burning, or had burned, had taken out a knife and cut a prayer for her into her own bones.
He touched the letters. They were cold.
There were other marks round the name board. A great many of them. It seemed to be a place where people came to cut things. Initials. J.T. R.M. HAL. A heart with an arrow through it and no names inside. A crude little boat. A fish. A date, so old the numbers had gone soft. A row of notches, as if someone had been counting days. Some of the marks were old and gray and furred with green. Some were newer, the wood inside them still pale.
And near the bottom, on the curve of the timber, just above the reach of the highest tide, there were five cuts.
Four straight ones, side by side, as long as his finger. And a fifth, going across them. A tally. The kind of tally a child makes, or a sailor, or a man in prison counting days.
They were fresh.
That was what made him stop. He crouched on the beam and looked at them, very close, with his breath smoking on the wood. The edges of the cuts were sharp. The wood inside them was pale and clean, the color of new butter. Not gray. Not weathered. There was even a tiny curl of wood still caught at the end of the last cut, where the knife had come out.
Days old. Not more. Maybe less.
He looked at them for a long time.
He did not know why they made his skin go cold. It was only a tally. People cut tallies everywhere. On doorframes and prison walls and the backs of benches. It meant nothing. Somebody had been here, an eel-catcher maybe, sheltering from the rain, and counted something, and gone.
But the cuts were made the way the cuts on the sewer wall were made. He could see it. He did not know how he could see it, but he could. The same length. The same depth. The same small, careful, slanting stroke across the top, a little longer than it needed to be, as if the hand that made it had done it so many thousands of times that it no longer thought about it.
Under OAKHAVEN, in the dark, in the round room by Outfall Number Three, there had been a long row of marks like this. And some of them had been new.
He put his finger on the fifth cut.
The wood was cold and smooth. It told him nothing.
"Wat," he called.
Wat came along the ribs. He came quickly and quietly for such a big man, and stood on the beam beside Corin, and looked where Corin was pointing.
He looked for a long time.
"Eel-catchers come here," he said at last. "In the spring. When the elvers run. They sleep in the wreck sometimes."
"It's not spring."
"No."
"Who else comes here?"
Wat shrugged. His big shoulders went up and down slowly, like a hill moving. "Nobody," he said. "Mudlarks, maybe. Wildfowlers. Me." A pause. "Hessa and me. Once."
Corin looked at him.
Wat was not looking at the cuts anymore. He was looking at the name board above them. At the faded gold. MARGERY. SALTGATE.
"Brought her here," he said. "Before Robin. Summer. Tide was low. We walked out on the mud, all the way from the river, with our boots in our hands. She wanted to see the ship." He stopped. Corin waited. "She'd heard about it. When she was little. Her father told her. How it burned. How the crew got off in the boats, all but the cook, who went back for the cat."
"Did he find it?"
"The cat got off," said Wat. "The cook didn't."
The water in the wreck lay still and brown under them.
"Hessa cried," said Wat. "About the cook. Forty years dead, and she cried, standing in the mud. I didn't know what to do. So I just stood there. And then she stopped, and laughed, and said I was a great lump, and kissed me." He looked down at his hands on the boat hook. "That's when I asked her."
"Asked her what?"
Wat looked at him as if he were very stupid.
"To marry me," he said.
Corin did not say anything. He did not trust himself to.
"She said no," said Wat. "The first time. She said I had to ask her somewhere that didn't smell of mud." Something moved at the corner of his mouth, under the gray beard. It might have been a smile. "So I asked her again on the bridge. On the Lantern Bridge. By the first lamp. And she said yes."
"How did you meet her?" said Corin. He did not know why he asked. It was not the kind of thing he asked people.
Wat was quiet for so long that Corin thought he would not answer.
"She hit me," he said. "With an oar. At the Saltgate lock." A pause. "I was steering a coal barge. Came into the lock too fast. Put a hole in her father's boat. She came across the gap with an oar and hit me on the head with it, and knocked me in the lock." Something moved under the gray beard again. "Then she pulled me out. With the same oar."
Corin found that he was smiling.
"Never told her," said Wat. He was looking at the faded gold letters on the name board, not at Corin. "Never told her anything. She does all the talking. Says she likes a man who doesn't, because then she gets to." He turned the boat hook slowly in his hands. "Never told her I'd go back in that lock every day for the rest of my life. If it was her with the oar."
He was quiet for a moment.
"She keeps a book," he said. "A tide book. Every tide, every day, for twelve years. High water, low water, the wind, the weather. Who came on the river. What they were carrying. Who they were looking for." He turned the boat hook. "She says the river remembers everything. You just have to write it down."
"She sounds like someone I'd like," said Corin.
"You do like her," said Wat. "You met her." He paused. "Twice."
Corin stood very still on the beam.
He had met her. Of course he had. It was in his Ledger. Hessa Doon. The Patient Grace. Wat. Robin. He had read the line a hundred times. He had written it himself.
But he could not see her face.
He tried. He reached for it. A woman on a barge. Hessa. He had stood on her deck, and talked to her, and she had said something to him. He knew she had. He could feel the shape of where it had been. But when he reached in to look at it, there was only the shape. As if he had looked at her through a window streaming with rain.
He did not know if that was the word he had burned in the coal cellar, taking more with it than he had known. Or the sewer, and the cold, and the flood. Or just the ordinary rubbing away of things, that happened to him every day, and that he had stopped noticing years ago.
He did not know. And not knowing was worse.
"I'm sorry," he said.
Wat looked at him with his small calm gray eyes. He did not ask what for.
"She'll tell you again," he said. "Whatever it was. She doesn't mind. She tells everybody everything twice." He turned and went back along the ribs toward the stern, toward the cave, without looking back.
Corin stayed a moment longer.
He took out the Ledger, there on the cold beam, with the water lying still and brown below him. And he wrote, under the morning's list:
Hessa Doon. Wat asked her to marry him twice. In the mud by the Margery, and on the Lantern Bridge by the first lamp. She cried for the cook who went back for the cat. She keeps a tide book. She tells everybody everything twice.
Ask her to tell you again.
They met when she hit him with an oar at the Saltgate lock. He would go back in that lock every day of his life for her. He has never told her. Tell her, if he can't.
Then he wrote, under that, smaller:
Five fresh cuts on the bow timber of the Margery. Same hand as the sewer.
And he put the book away.
The Black Boat
Wat heard it first.
Corin was sitting at the mouth of the cave with the blanket round him, trying to make his hands warm under his arms, when Wat stood up. Just stood up, all at once, from where he had been sitting on the edge of the deck. He did not say anything. He turned his head toward the river and held it there, very still, the way a dog holds its head when it hears something far off that a man cannot.
Corin listened.
Nothing. The marsh. The drip of the deck. The bird, very far off, asking its question.
Then he heard it.
A knock. Wood on wood. Faint and regular. Tok. Tok. Tok. Like someone tapping on a door a long way off, slowly, patiently, over and over.
And under it, a softer sound. A long breathy hush, and a pause, and hush again. Many oars, all going into the water together.
"Boat," said Wat.
He went to the open end of the cave and lay down flat on his belly on the boards and put his head out, very slowly, past the edge of the broken timbers. Corin crawled after him and lay down beside him.
The mist had thinned since the morning. It still lay on the river in long pale strips, but between the strips the water was clear and gray, and he could see a long way down the main channel toward the city.
There was a boat on it.
Not a little boat like Wat's. A long one. Low and narrow and black, so black it looked like a hole cut in the water. It came up the channel out of the mist slowly, steadily, with a row of oars on each side going in and out together, eight of them, like the legs of a beetle. A man stood in the stern, very upright, with one hand on the long tiller. Another stood in the bow with a pole. And in the middle, between the rowers, there was a short mast with a flag hanging from it, wet and limp in the still air.
The flag was black. And on the black, when the boat turned a little and the flag stirred, Corin saw a shape in silver. An eye. Open.
The Office of the Inquisition.
His heart began to beat very hard and slow.
The knocking sound was the man in the bow. He had a long pole with an iron hook on the end of it, and every few strokes he leaned out over the water and knocked it against something. A post. A buoy. The side of a moored lighter. Tok. Tok. As if he were knocking on doors. As if he were asking, is anyone home?
The black boat came up the channel. And at the place where the channel bent, by a long low wreck half sunk in the reeds two hundred yards downstream, it turned.
It went in toward the wreck.
Corin watched. He could not take his eyes off it. The black boat slid in alongside the half-sunk hulk. Two of the rowers shipped their oars and stood up, and they had poles too, long ones with hooks, and they reached out with them and began to poke. Into the reeds round the wreck. Into the holes in its side. Under its broken deck. Slowly, carefully, one hole after another, the way a man probes a hedge with a stick for a lost ball. The man in the bow climbed up onto the wreck itself and walked along it, looking down. He kicked at something. He bent and picked something up, and looked at it, and threw it into the water.
Then he climbed back down, and the rowers sat, and the oars went in together, hush, and the black boat backed off, and turned, and came on up the channel.
Toward the next wreck.
Corin could see it. There were three more wrecks between that one and the Margery. A barge, upside down on the mud, with its flat bottom showing like the belly of a dead fish. A little sailing boat, its mast snapped, lying on its side in the reeds. And an old hulk, bigger than the rest, with a roof built over it like a house. They were searching every one.
"They're coming here," he whispered.
Wat did not answer. He was already moving.
He went back into the cave on his knees, fast and quiet, and Corin went after him. Wat looked round the cave once. One long look, taking everything in. The dead fire on its flat stone. The scatter of ash. The horse blanket. The crumbs on the boards where they had eaten. The place where Juno had slept, with the shape of her still pressed into the dust.
He took the flat stone with the ashes on it in his two hands, all of it at once, and carried it to the open end of the cave, and tipped it into the water. The ash floated for a moment, a gray scum, and then the slow water took it and spread it out and it was gone. He dropped the stone in after it. It went down without a sound.
He swept the boards with his big hands. The crumbs. The dust. Juno's shape. All of it, out into the water.
He picked up the horse blanket and rolled it tight and pushed it deep into a gap between two of the hull timbers, at the very back of the cave, where the tar had dripped and set in black curtains, and pulled some of the tar down over it. Corin heard it crack like ice.
Then he turned to Corin.
"Water," he said.
"What?"
Wat pointed. Down. Through the broken deck at the back of the cave, where there was a gap between the boards and the curve of the hull. Corin looked. Below the gap there was water. The dark still water of the flooded wreck, a foot below the boards. Black, and very cold, with frost floating on it.
"Under the deck," said Wat. "Hold the beam. Keep your face up. Don't move."
Corin looked at the water.
He had been in water last night. He had been in water so cold that it stopped his breath, and pulled at him, and filled his mouth, and he had thought he would die in it. He had been warm, or nearly warm, for less than a day.
Boy, said Malakor quietly. I know. But he's right.
Tok, went the pole, out on the river. Closer. Tok.
Corin took the Ledger out of his coat and looked at it. Then at Wat.
Wat held out his hand.
Corin gave it to him. It was the hardest thing he had done all day. Wat took it in his big hand as gently as if it were an egg. He wrapped it in the oilcloth. He reached up, and found a place high in the angle of the timbers above the gap, where two of them met and there was a little dry hollow, black with old tar, and pushed it in there, deep, out of sight. Then he took the rod from Corin's pocket, and put that beside it.
"Dry," he said. "Up there. Stays dry."
Corin nodded. He could not speak.
He sat down on the edge of the gap and put his legs through it. The water closed over his boots. Over his shins. Over his knees. It was colder than the sewer. It was colder than anything. It bit him like teeth. He let himself down into it, slowly, with his hands on the edge of the deck, until it was up to his chest, and the breath went out of him in a long shaking hiss, and he could not get it back.
His feet found the bottom. Mud. Soft and deep and sucking. He sank into it up to his ankles.
Wat came down after him. He did it without a sound. The water came up round his great chest and he did not even shiver. He reached up and pulled the loose deck boards back across the gap over their heads, one, two, three, until there was only a crack of gray light coming through.
They were in the dark. Under the deck. In the black water, with the curve of the hull behind them and the boards a foot above their heads. Corin found the beam Wat meant. A big square timber running along under the deck, just above the water. He got his arms over it and hung there, with his chin on the wood and his face turned up to the crack of light.
The water was up to his neck.
He could feel the pouch at his hip. Under the water. Warm. Very faintly warm. The only warm thing in the world.
Tok.
Very close now.
Hush. Hush. The oars.
And then a long soft grinding scrape, wood on wood, as something heavy came alongside the Margery, and stopped.
