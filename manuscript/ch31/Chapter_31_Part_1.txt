Chapter 31: The Ribs of a Dead Ship, Part 1 of 5: Gray Morning
Gray Morning
Corin woke because his face was cold.
Not the rest of him. The rest of him was buried under the horse blanket, curled on his side on the deck boards with his knees pulled up and his hands pushed between them, and it was not warm, exactly, but it was not cold either. It was the kind of not-cold you get when you have been lying very still for a long time and your body has stopped arguing. But his face was outside the blanket. And the air on it was like wet iron.
He opened his eyes.
Gray. That was all, at first. A gray so pale and soft and even that he could not tell how far away it was. It might have been a wall an inch from his nose. It might have been the sky. He lay and looked at it and waited for it to turn into something.
It turned into a plank.
An old plank, black and cracked, a hand's width from his eyes. The underside of the deck above him. He could see the grain of the wood, running in long wavy lines, and the places where the grain had split and opened and gone soft. He could see a nail head, rusted to a lump of orange. He could see a spider web strung across a crack, heavy with drops of water. Each drop held a tiny gray light inside it. As he watched, one drop slid down the thread and fell, and he felt it land on his cheek, cold as a coin.
He remembered where he was.
It came back slowly, the way the shapes of a room come back when your eyes get used to the dark. The ship. The dead ship. The ribs of it, standing up out of the mud like the bones of a whale. The little cave under the stern deck where Wat had tied the boat. The fire. The pie.
The sewer.
He lay still and let it come. The roaring water. The wall of names. The flood. The gate. The gray light at the end of the outfall, and the bells counting twelve, and the boat coming out of the mist with Juno screaming in the back of it.
And the word he did not have anymore.
He felt for it before he could stop himself. The way your tongue goes to the gap where a tooth has been pulled. He reached down into himself, to the very bottom, to the room where the old soft words had been.
Empty. Swept. Cold.
He took his hand away from it, inside himself, and lay looking at the spider web.
Then he sat up.
It hurt. Everything hurt. His back, where he had hit the iron gate. His shoulders, where the bars of the mudlarks' gap had scraped them raw. His knees. His hands. His hands were the worst. He held them up in front of his face in the gray light and looked at them. They were swollen and red, the knuckles split, the fingertips white and wrinkled like a washerwoman's, and there was a long scab across the back of the left one that he did not remember getting. He opened them and closed them. They opened and closed. Slowly. Like old gates.
He looked round.
The cave under the deck was about the size of a small room. It had a floor of old boards, gray and soft with age, sloping down toward the open end where the ship had broken in two. It had a roof of deck planks, low enough that he could not have stood up straight. And it had walls, if you could call them that, of the ship's own curved black timbers, the big ones, thick as tree trunks, rising up on either side and meeting over his head. Between the timbers, in the gaps, old tar had dripped and set in long black icicles. The whole place smelled of that tar. Tar and salt and wet ash and, very faintly, mutton pie.
The fire was out. There was only a gray heap of ash on the flat stone by the open end, and one thin line of smoke going up from it, straight as a thread, until the cold air took it.
Juno was still asleep.
She had not moved all night. She was lying where she had fallen, curled on the boards near the dead fire with her cheek on her arm, inside her grandfather's huge coat. Only her hair showed, and one hand. The hand was small and dirty, with bitten nails, and it was holding the edge of the coat closed under her chin in a tight fist, the way a much younger child holds a blanket.
Corin looked at that hand for a long time.
Then he looked out through the open end of the cave.
The Saltmarsh lay out there in the morning.
He had not seen it in daylight. Last night it had only been dark, and the sound of water, and the reeds a black line. Now he could see all of it, and it was so big and so flat and so empty that for a moment it frightened him.
It went on forever. That was the first thing. Flat gray-brown land, as flat as a table, going away in every direction until it melted into the gray sky. No hills. No trees. No houses. Just reeds. Miles of them. Tall pale reeds, the color of old straw, standing up in great still beds, with their feathery tops bent over and heavy with frost. Every single reed had frost on it. Every stem, every leaf, every feather. The whole marsh was white with it, a soft, furred, glittering white, as if someone had dusted it with sugar in the night.
And through the reeds, everywhere, water.
Not the river. The river was off to the left, wide and slow and the color of pewter, sliding away toward the sea. But here, in the marsh itself, there were a thousand smaller waters. Creeks and channels and pools, winding in and out of the reeds in every direction, splitting and joining and splitting again, like the veins on the back of an old man's hand. They were still. Perfectly still. Some of them had a thin skin of ice on them, gray and cloudy, with reeds frozen into it. Others were open, and black, and so smooth that the gray sky lay on them like a sheet of glass.
The tide was out.
He could see that from the mud. The ship lay on a long bank of it, gray and shining and smooth, with tiny channels running through it where the water had drained away. Little birds were walking on it. Small brown birds with long thin legs and long thin beaks, running about in quick jerky bursts and stopping and stabbing at the mud and running again. Their feet left tiny marks, like writing. Farther off, near the water's edge, a big gray heron stood on one leg with its neck pulled down into its shoulders, as still as a post.
There was no sound.
That was the strangest thing of all. In the city there was always sound. Carts. Bells. Voices. Doors. Dogs. Even at night, even in the stillest hour, there was always something. A drunk singing. A cat. A clock somewhere striking. Here there was nothing. Only, now and then, very far off, a bird calling. A long, thin, falling cry, like someone asking a question and not waiting for an answer.
And the drip of water from the deck above his head. And Juno's small whistling snore.
And behind him, very low, a voice.
Morning, boy.
"Morning," said Corin. His own voice came out cracked and strange, as if it had rusted in the night.
You slept.
"Did I?"
Five hours. I counted. You didn't dream. Or if you did, I couldn't hear it. A pause. That's new.
Corin did not answer. He did not know what to say to that.
He pulled the blanket round his shoulders and shuffled forward on his knees to the open end of the cave, and looked down.
The boat was gone.
For one moment his heart stopped. Then he saw it. It had not gone. It had dropped. When the tide went out, the boat had gone down with it, and now it sat on the mud six feet below him, tilted a little on its side, with its rope going up taut to the timber Wat had tied it to. The oars were shipped. The tin bailer was in the bottom, with ice in it.
And Wat was sitting in it.
He had not moved either. He was sitting in the middle of the boat exactly where he had been sitting when Corin fell asleep, on the rowing bench, with the long boat hook across his knees and his big hands resting on it. He had no hat. There was frost in his short gray beard. There was frost on his shoulders, and on the backs of his hands, and on the top of his bald head, a thin white crust, like salt on a ham. He looked like a man carved out of wood and left out in the weather.
But his eyes were open. Small and gray and calm. Looking out across the marsh toward the river.
"Wat," said Corin.
Wat turned his head. Slowly. The frost cracked on his collar.
He looked up at Corin for a moment. Then he nodded, once. And turned his head back to the river.
Corin realized that he had been sitting there all night. Awake. In the cold. Keeping watch.
And he had not said a single word about it, and he never would.
The Words That Are Left
Juno woke up angry.
She did it all at once, the way she did everything. One moment she was a small curled shape in a big coat, snoring like a kettle. The next she was sitting bolt upright with her hair over her face and her fists up, staring round the cave as if she expected to find an Inquisitor in it.
She found Corin.
She looked at him for a long moment through her hair. Then her fists came down, slowly, and she pushed the hair out of her eyes with the back of one hand, and said, in a voice thick with sleep:
"You're still here."
"Yes."
"I dreamed you went in the water again." She rubbed her face hard with both hands. "I dreamed you went in and I was holding your coat and it came off and you went down without it. I hate that dream. I've had it four times." She dropped her hands and glared at him. "Don't do that."
"Go in the water?"
"Any of it."
"I'll try," said Corin.
She went on glaring at him. Then she sniffed, and looked away, and began to dig in the pockets of her grandfather's coat. She had a great many pockets. She seemed to have more every time Corin saw her. Out of them, one after another, came: a heel of bread wrapped in a handkerchief, gone hard as wood. A lump of cheese in waxed paper. Two wrinkled winter apples. A tin box with a flint and steel in it. A ball of string. A clasp knife. A stub of pencil. And a small brown bottle with a cork in it.
"Gran's," she said, holding up the bottle. "For coughs. She says it's cherry. It's not cherry." She pulled the cork with her teeth and sniffed it and made a face. "It's mostly brandy. You can have some if you want. You look like you want some."
Corin did not want some. He took it anyway, because she was holding it out, and drank a mouthful. It went down like a hot coal. It was not cherry. He coughed, and his eyes watered, and the warmth of it spread out slowly from his middle into his chest and his arms, and for the first time since he woke he could feel his fingers.
"Thank you," he said.
"Told you." Juno took the bottle back and corked it and put it away. She broke the bread in half, with a crack like a stick snapping, and gave him the bigger half. Then she broke the cheese. Then she looked at the apples, and at him, and at the open end of the cave where Wat sat in the boat below, and put one apple back in her pocket.
"For Wat," she said. "He won't take it if I give it him. I have to leave it where he'll find it."
They ate.
The bread was so hard that Corin had to hold it in his mouth for a long time before it went soft enough to chew. The cheese was sharp and crumbly and tasted of the inside of Juno's pocket. The apple was soft and sweet and a little brown inside. He ate all of it, the core too, and the seeds, and licked his fingers.
And while he ate, he listened to himself.
He did not mean to. It was like listening for a noise in a house at night. You do not choose to do it. Your ears do it on their own, and you lie there, and every creak is the noise.
He listened to his own thoughts. The words going past in his head. Bread. Hard. Cheese. Juno. Cold. The boat. The tide. They were all there. They were all Veridian words, city words, the words he had learned again in the long white room with forty beds. They came when he called them. They meant what they meant.
But some of them were wrong.
Not gone. Wrong. He could not have said how. He only knew that when certain words went through his head, they felt different from the others. Thinner. Lighter. Like a coin that looks like a coin and rings like a coin but, when you bite it, is not gold all the way through.
Bread. That one. He thought it again, carefully. Bread. It meant the thing in his hand. It was the right word. But it was hollow. As if there had once been another word inside it, older and warmer, holding it up from the inside, and that word had been taken out and the shell left standing.
Night. Hollow.
Snow. Hollow.
Home. Hollow all the way down.
Mother. He did not try that one again. He knew.
"What's wrong?" said Juno.
He looked up. She was watching him over her bread with her eyes narrowed.
"Nothing," he said.
"You stopped chewing." She swallowed. "You stop chewing when you're thinking about something bad. You did it at Pruett's. You did it in the den. What is it?"
Corin looked at her for a long moment.
Then he told her. Not all of it. He did not have the words for all of it, which was, he thought, almost funny. But he told her about the coal cellar, and the sword, and the word he had burned. And about the words that were left. The hollow ones.
Juno listened without moving. When he had finished, she was quiet for a while.
"So which ones are hollow?" she said at last.
"I don't know. I keep finding them. The old ones, I think. The ones I learned first. Before I learned them again."
"Like what?"
"Bread. Night. Snow."
"Fire?"
He thought about it. "No. Fire's all right."
"Water?"
"Hollow."
"Dog?"
"All right."
"Cat?"
He almost smiled. "All right."
"Juno?"
"All right," said Corin. "Juno's all right."
She sniffed again, and looked away, and pulled at a thread on the sleeve of the huge coat.
"Gran says words are like coats," she said, not looking at him. "She says you wear them so long they get the shape of you, and then if someone else puts them on, they hang all wrong." She pulled the thread harder. "So maybe yours aren't hollow. Maybe they're just somebody else's coat now. And you've got to wear them a while till they get your shape again."
Corin did not say anything.
He thought it was probably not true. He thought that a word with the heart taken out of it would stay that way, the way a jar with nothing in it stays empty, however long you keep it on the shelf. But he did not say so. Because she had thought about it, and she had said it carefully, the way you put something down that might break.
"Maybe," he said.
"Try counting," said Juno suddenly.
"What?"
"Counting. Gran says the first thing you ever learn is counting. Before your letters. Before anything. You count your fingers and your toes and the steps up to bed. Try it."
He tried it. Out loud.
"One. Two. Three. Four." Easy. "Five. Six. Seven." He slowed down. Something was happening. "Eight. Nine." He stopped.
"Ten," said Juno.
"I know it's ten," said Corin. "I know that. I can say it." He held up his hands, and looked at them. "But I can't feel it. Up to nine I can feel it. The numbers sit where they should. After that, they're just words. I have to count on my fingers." He folded down his fingers, one by one, slowly. "Eight, nine, ten. Like a child. Like a child who's only just learning."
He put his hands down.
Your first counting was in the old tongue, said Malakor quietly, behind his left ear. Up to nine, the city words were laid over the old ones so long ago that they grew together. Like ivy on a wall. When you burned the wall, some of the ivy held. Past nine, it didn't. Your mother taught you to count to nine. Somebody else taught you ten.
Corin sat very still.
"How do you know that?" he whispered.
I don't, said Malakor. I'm guessing. I'm an old dead man in a silver stick, boy. Guessing is most of what I do.
But he said it too quickly. And Corin, sitting in the cold cave with half a heel of bread in his hand and the frost glittering on the reeds outside, thought: No. You don't guess. You never guess. You know something about my mother that I don't.
He did not say it aloud.
Juno was watching him. She had heard only his half of it. She did not ask. She just reached over, very quickly, and took his left hand, and held up his fingers in front of his face one at a time.
"Ten," she said, tapping the last one. "Eleven." She tapped the first one again. "Twelve. There. You've got me. I'll count the big ones for you. I'm good at big ones. I count all Gran's change."
She let go of his hand and went back to her bread as if nothing had happened.
Corin looked at his hand for a long time.
What He Carries
After they had eaten, Corin took everything out of his pockets and laid it on the deck boards in a row.
He did it every morning. He had done it every morning for six years, in his room at the top of the ninety-one steps, on the little table by the window, before he opened the Ledger. Everything out. Everything in a row. Everything looked at and named. It was one of the first rules on the second page. Know what you carry. If you do not know what you carry, you do not know what you have lost.
He did not remember writing that rule. He had followed it anyway, every single day, the way you follow a path someone else has worn through a field.
So he laid it all out now, on the old gray boards, in the thin light from the open end of the cave.
The Ledger first. Always the Ledger first. Mrs. Marta's oilcloth had kept most of the water out, but the leather cover was soft and dark at the corners, and when he opened it the first few pages were wavy, like the edge of a leaf. He turned them carefully. They held.
The rod. Thin and silver and cold, as long as his forearm, with the spiral cut round and round its shaft, turning to the right. He laid it down beside the Ledger very gently. The little blue light at its tip was out. It had gone out sometime in the night, while he slept.
The pouch with the stones. He did not open it. He laid it down a little way off from everything else, at the end of the row, by itself.
Wren's beach stone. Gray and smooth, with a white line round it like a ring. It was warm from his pocket. He turned it over in his fingers once before he put it down. Bring it back.
The oak-leaf key. Small and brass and old, with its bow cut in the shape of a leaf, every vein marked. Linus Hale had slipped it into his coat in the Lamplighter's Rest, and said nothing about it, and Corin still did not know what it opened.
Rowan's wrapper. Folded tiny and hard. He did not unfold it. He knew what it said.
And the card.
The plain white card from the Fourth Desk, the size of a playing card. He had kept it inside the back cover of the Ledger, flat, where it would not bend. Under audit. Not to be collected or harmed by order of the Fourth Desk. E.
He took it out and looked at it.
The green wax seal in the corner had gone gray.
Not faded. Not washed out by the water, the way the ink at the bottom of the first page had run. Gray. A deep, dull, even gray, like the color of a bruise a week old. He turned it to the light. He scratched at the wax with his thumbnail. It was gray all the way through.
He did not know what that meant.
He sat and looked at it for a long time. He thought of her hand, in the white glove, pushing the card across the glass desk toward him on Monday afternoon. Her voice, cool and level. If anyone tries to collect you, show them this. It will not stop an Inquisitor. But it will make him write a report about why he ignored it. And Inquisitors hate writing reports.
She had not said anything about the seal changing color.
He put the card back inside the cover of the Ledger, very carefully, flat.
Then he looked at the row.
It was not much. A book, a stick, a bag of stones, a beach pebble, a key, a sweet wrapper, a card. That was everything he owned in the world now. His room was gone. His shelf of jars was gone. His other coat, his good boots, his razor, his kettle, the blanket with the hole in it. Gone. Kross had them. Or nobody had them, and they were sitting in a searched room at the top of No. 14 Tallow Street with the door broken in, waiting for the next tenant to throw them out.
He found he did not mind as much as he thought he would.
He opened the Ledger to the first empty page, after last night's lines, and took out Juno's pencil stub, and wrote:
Thursday. Morning. The Saltmarsh. You carry:
And he wrote them all down. The book. The rod. The stones. The beach stone, Wren's. The key, Linus's. The wrapper, Rowan's. The card, Elara's.
He looked at the last word for a long moment. Elara's.
He wrote under it: The seal has gone gray. Find out why.
Then he put the pencil down and said, to nobody:
"Malakor."
Mm.
"You said on the river you'd tell me what you could. About the stones."
I did tell you.
"You told me what they are. You didn't tell me where they came from. Or why Pell had them. Or who sewed them up in black cloth and rubbed that corner thin."
I don't know those things.
"And you didn't tell me how you know what my mother taught me."
Silence.
Corin waited. Juno, who had gone out along the ribs to look for dry wood, was out of hearing. The marsh was still. Somewhere far off the bird cried again, that long thin falling cry.
Boy, said Malakor at last. Very quietly. There are some doors in you that I can see through, because I sit so close. Like a man in the next room who can hear through the wall. I hear things. Bits. Not much. I heard your mother's counting once, years ago, on a night you had a fever and didn't know you were saying it. One to nine. In the old tongue. And then you said ten in the city tongue, in a different voice. A man's voice. As if you were copying someone. A pause. That's all. That's all I know. I swear it.
"A man's voice."
Yes.
"Whose?"
I don't know, boy. Truly. I don't.
Corin looked at the rod lying on the boards.
"And the stones?"
A longer silence.
Some doors are shut for a good reason, said Malakor. I'm not going to open that one for you. Not today. Not in a dead ship, in the cold, with Kross's dogs on the river. Ask me again when you're warm and fed and somewhere with walls. Ask me again when you've slept in a bed. A pause. And maybe I'll still say no.
"That's not fair."
No, said Malakor. It isn't. Very little is.
Corin sat for a while longer. Then he picked up the things in the row, one by one, and put them back where they lived. The Ledger inside his coat, over his heart. The rod in the long narrow pocket on the right. The beach stone in the left pocket. The key and the wrapper in the small inside pocket. The pouch at his hip.
It was warm again.
Not hot. Not like the sewer. Just warm. Faintly. Like a hand that has been resting on a sleeping animal and has just been lifted away.
He took his fingers off it, and buttoned his coat.
But before he went to find Juno, he did the last thing. The thing he did every morning, after the row and the list. He opened the Ledger again, to the very first page, and read it.
Out loud. Quietly. The way the rule on the second page told him to. Read the first page aloud every morning, even when you think you know it. Especially then.
"Read this first," he said, to the gray boards and the tar and the spider web. "Your name is Corin. You were born in Oakhaven. You had a younger sister named Elara. You used to fear the dark."
His voice sounded strange in the low cave. Thin. Too careful. Like a man reading a letter written in a language he has only just learned.
He read it again.
Your name is Corin. That one was solid. He could feel it all the way down, like a stone you can stand on.
You were born in Oakhaven. That one was a word. Just a word now. A city word, with a city meaning. Oak. Haven. He knew there had been another name under it, the real one, the one the people who lived there had used. He could feel the place where it had been, like the dent a ring leaves on a finger after years of wearing. But the ring was gone.
You had a younger sister named Elara. He read that one three times. He did not know why. He thought of a desk made of glass, and a gold tooth, and white gloves, and a voice that said two thousand four hundred and five days. He thought of last night's last lines, written by the dying fire, pressing so hard the pencil nearly went through. The Auditor of the Fourth Desk is called Elara.
You used to fear the dark.
He stopped there.
He looked out through the open end of the cave at the marsh. At the gray morning. At the frosted reeds going away forever, and the still black water in the channels, and the heron on its post.
Last night he had lain down by a dying fire, in the ribs of a dead ship, in the middle of ten miles of empty marsh, with no walls round him and no lamp, and the dark had come down over everything like a lid. And he had not been afraid. Not even a little. He had lain in it and listened.
He did not know if that was because something had been taken from him, or because something had been given. He did not know if there was a difference.
He took Juno's pencil, and under the fourth line on the first page, very small, so small that only he would ever see it, he wrote:
Not anymore. Thursday.
Then he closed the book, and put it away over his heart, and went to find Juno.
