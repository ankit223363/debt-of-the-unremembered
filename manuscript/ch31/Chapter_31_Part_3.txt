Chapter 31: The Ribs of a Dead Ship, Part 3 of 5: The Gray Cape
The Gray Cape
Feet.
That was the first thing. Feet on the deck above his head. Heavy boots, landing hard, from a height. One pair, and then another. The boards over Corin's face jumped with each one, and a fine rain of grit and frost and old tar dust came sifting down through the crack of light and fell on his upturned face, into his eyes, onto his lips. He did not blink. He did not move. He hung on the beam with the black water up to his chin and let it fall.
"Nothing," said a voice. A man's voice, rough, bored, very close. Right above him. "Same as the rest. Rats and birds."
"Look anyway," said another.
This one was different. It was not loud. It was not rough. It was a quiet, flat, careful voice, a voice that did not need to be loud because it expected to be listened to. It came from farther off, from the open end of the cave, where the deck broke away over the water.
And a third pair of feet came down onto the boards. Lightly. So lightly Corin hardly felt them.
He turned his eyes, just his eyes, and looked up through the crack.
He could see a slice of the cave. A narrow gray strip of it, between two boards, no wider than his finger. The low roof. A dangle of tar. And a pair of legs, walking slowly past. Good boots, black, polished, wet to the ankle. Gray trousers. And the hem of a cape. Gray wool, heavy, beaded with water. It swung as the legs walked, slowly, and he could see that it was long, very long, down to the man's calves, and that it had been mended once near the bottom with a patch of slightly darker gray, stitched very neatly in tiny even stitches.
The legs stopped.
They stood still in the middle of the cave. Right above Corin's head. He could see the boots, side by side, and the water dripping off the hem of the gray cape, drop by drop, onto the boards. Each drop landed with a tiny tap. He counted them without meaning to. One. Two. Three. He thought: if one comes through the crack, if it lands on my face, will he hear it?
"Someone was here," said the quiet voice.
"Sir?"
"Smell."
A pause. A loud sniff from the rough-voiced man.
"Smoke," he said. "Old smoke. That's just the wreck, sir. She burned."
"Forty years ago," said the quiet voice. "Wood smoke doesn't keep forty years. That's last night's."
The boots turned. Slowly. All the way round. Corin watched them through the crack. They turned toward the back of the cave, where the blanket was pushed into the timbers under the tar. They stopped. They stood looking that way for a long time. Then they turned again, toward the open end.
And then, without any warning at all, something came down through the water beside Corin's face.
A pole. A long wooden pole, wet and black, coming down at a slant through a gap in the boards a yard away from him, and into the water. He saw it come. He saw the iron hook on the end of it, curved and rusty, slide down into the black water past his cheek, close enough to touch, and go on down, into the dark, and stop. And move. Sideways. Feeling. Searching. Sweeping along through the water under the deck, slowly, toward him.
He did not breathe.
The hook came on. He felt the water it pushed, cold against his neck. He thought: now. Now it'll catch my coat. Now it'll hook my collar and they'll pull and I'll come up through the boards like a fish.
And beside him in the dark, Wat moved.
Not much. Just a shift of his huge body in the water. A quarter turn. So that his shoulder was between Corin and the hook.
The hook found Wat.
Corin heard it. A small, soft, terrible sound. The tearing of cloth, and then something under the cloth. He felt Wat's body go rigid beside him, every muscle locked. But Wat did not make a sound. Not a gasp. Not a breath. He hung on the beam in the black water with the iron hook in his shoulder and did not move at all.
"Caught something," said the rough voice above. "Hang on."
The pole pulled.
Wat's body moved with it, an inch. Two inches. Corin saw his face in the faint gray light from the crack. It was perfectly still. His eyes were open. His mouth was shut. There was frost in his beard.
And then, slowly, deliberately, Wat reached up with his other hand under the water and took hold of the hook. And held it. And turned it, very gently, a little to the side. And let the hook slide off him, off his coat, and onto the old timber of the beam beside his shoulder, where it caught, and held.
The pole pulled again. Hard.
The hook stayed caught in the beam.
"It's the wreck," said the rough voice in disgust. "Caught on a timber." The pole jerked. Twisted. Something cracked, old wood splitting. And the hook came free of the beam and slid back up through the water and was gone, up through the gap in the boards, with water streaming off it.
Corin hung on the beam.
Beside him, very slowly, Wat let out a breath. It made no sound at all. Corin only knew it because he saw the frost on Wat's beard stir.
In the water between them, very faintly, in the gray light from the crack, there was a dark cloud. Spreading. Thinning.
Above, the boots had walked away to the open end of the cave.
"Mr. Vell," called the rough voice. "Sir. There's nothing. We've done the whole hull. Can we—"
"Wait."
The quiet voice had changed. Not louder. But sharper. The way a needle is sharper than a nail.
Corin turned his eyes up to the crack again.
He could see the end of the cave now, where the boots stood. On the very edge of the broken deck, where it hung out over the water. Beside one of the great outer ribs of the hull, black and curved, rising up out of the brown flood.
The man in the gray cape had reached out to the rib. To the outside of it. To something that was hanging there, on a stump of old nail, where Corin could not see it from inside.
He took it down.
Corin saw his hand, for the first time. A long, pale, clean hand, with no ring on it, and very short nails. It was holding a cloth. A square of cloth, blue and white checks, damp and limp. It had been tied round a parcel once. You could see the creases where the knot had been.
Mrs. Marta's cloth. From the pie.
Juno must have hung it there last night. To dry. When she went out along the ribs for wood. And in the dark and the cold and the tiredness and the sleeping, nobody had thought of it again.
The pale hand turned the cloth over.
In one corner, sewn in red thread, very small and neat, there was a letter. Mrs. Marta marked all her linen. Corin had seen her do it, at her kitchen table, on long evenings, with her glasses on the end of her nose. Every sheet, every towel, every cloth.
M.
The man called Vell lifted the cloth to his face, and smelled it.
"Mutton," he said quietly. "And onion. And a pastry made with lard, not butter. A home pie. Not a shop." He lowered the cloth. "Baked in a big old oven. A public oven, maybe. Or an inn. It's caught a little at the edge."
He stood for a moment, looking at the red letter in the corner.
Then he folded the cloth.
Corin watched him do it. Through the crack. With the black water at his throat. He watched those long pale clean fingers fold the blue and white cloth in half, and in half again, and again, and again. Neatly. Exactly. Each fold pressed flat with the thumbnail. Smaller and smaller. Until it was a hard, tight, blue and white square, no bigger than the top of a man's thumb.
The same way Kross folded his sweet papers.
The man called Vell put the little folded square into the inside pocket of his gray cape. And patted the pocket, once, flat.
"Back to the boat," he said. "We're done here."
"Sir? Don't you want to—"
"I've got what I came for."
The boots went away across the boards. Lightly. And the other two pairs after them, heavily, with a thump and a scrape and a curse as somebody slipped on the frost. The deck above Corin's head jumped, and jumped, and was still.
A grinding scrape. Wood on wood.
And then, slowly, fading, going away down the channel toward the city:
Hush. Hush. Hush.

They waited a long time before they came out.
Wat made them. Corin wanted to come out at once, the moment the sound of the oars was gone. His body wanted it so badly that it shook. But Wat put one big hand on his arm under the water and held it there, and did not let go. And they hung on the beam in the black cold, in silence, while the light through the crack changed from gray to a slightly different gray, and the frost on the floating leaves melted and set again, and Corin's legs went from hurting to aching to nothing at all.
At last Wat took his hand away.
He pushed the boards aside over their heads, slowly, one at a time. He put his head up through the gap and looked round. He stayed like that for a long time. Then he climbed out, pulling himself up onto the deck with one arm, his left arm. The right one he kept tight against his side.
Then he reached down with the left one, and took Corin by the collar, and pulled him out like a sack.
Corin lay on the boards and could not move.
He was not cold. That was what frightened him. He had been cold in the water, so cold that it was like being bitten, and then somewhere along the way the biting had stopped, and now he did not feel cold at all. He felt heavy. And slow. And far away. As if he were lying at the bottom of a deep, quiet well, looking up at a small round piece of gray sky, and it did not matter very much whether he ever climbed out.
Boy, said Malakor. Boy. Get up.
He could not find the rod. That was the next thing. The voice was faint, very faint, as if it came from a long way off. Of course it did. The rod was not in his pocket. Wat had put it up in the timbers, with the Ledger.
"Wat," said Corin. His mouth would not work properly. The word came out as Wah. "Rod."
Wat looked at him. Then he reached up, with his good arm, into the dark angle of the timbers above the gap. He felt about. He brought down the Ledger in its oilcloth, and laid it on the boards beside Corin's head. And then the rod.
He put it in Corin's hand, and folded Corin's fingers round it, one by one, because Corin's fingers would not fold themselves.
The voice came back at once. Loud and clear and close behind his left ear. Sharper than he had ever heard it.
You're freezing to death, boy. Do you hear me? This is how it starts. You stop shaking and you start feeling warm and sleepy, and that's the end. That's the cold telling you lies. Get up. Burn something.
"Smoke," said Corin. "Can't. Fire. They'll see."
Not a fire. Heat. Small. In the rod. The way you'd light a stair, but warm instead of bright. You've done it before. On the ice at Cinder Lane, for the boy. Do it now.
Corin lay with the rod in his frozen fist and tried to think.
It was very hard. Thinking was like walking through deep mud. He went down into himself, slowly, looking. Not for anything big. He could not do anything big. Something small. Something he would not miss.
He found the Intake Hall.
Not the whole of it. Just a corner of it. The smell of the floor. The long white tiled floor of the Intake Hall on Lantern Quay, that the porters mopped every evening with hot water and something sharp and yellow from a tin, so that when he came in the next morning to clear the glasses, the whole hall smelled of it. Clean and bitter and a little like lemons. He had smelled it every morning for a week. He had never liked it.
He took hold of it.
And burned it.
The rod grew warm in his hand.
Not hot. Not bright. Just warm. A soft, steady, spreading warmth, like a stone that has lain all day in the sun. It came up out of the silver into his fingers, and from his fingers into his wrist, and from his wrist up his arm, slow and thick, like warm water poured into a cold bath. The tip of the rod glowed. Not blue-white this time. A deep dull orange, like a coal at the bottom of a grate.
He felt it reach his chest.
And as it reached his chest, the cold came back.
All of it, all at once, the real cold, the biting cold, the cold that had been lying to him. It came roaring back into his legs and his feet and his hands, and with it the pain, and he curled up on the boards and shook so hard his teeth rattled and his heels drummed on the wood. He shook and shook. He could not stop. It went on for a long time.
Good, said Malakor. Good. That's right. Shake. Shaking is good. Shaking means you're alive. Keep holding it.
Corin kept holding it.
Slowly, the shaking eased. Slowly, the orange glow at the tip of the rod faded to a dim red, and the warmth in his body settled from a flood into a trickle. He found he could move his fingers. He found he could feel the boards under his cheek. He found he could think.
He sat up.
Wat was sitting against the hull timbers at the back of the cave, with the horse blanket pulled out of its hiding place and wrapped round his legs. His face was gray. Not pale. Gray, like old dishwater. And his right arm was still held tight against his side, and the sleeve of his coat was dark and wet from the shoulder to the elbow, and not with water.
"Wat," said Corin.
Wat looked at him.
"Let me see."
Wat did not move for a moment. Then he did. He let go of his arm. Slowly. And Corin crawled across the boards to him, and pulled back the torn wet coat from his shoulder, and the shirt under it.
It was bad.
The hook had gone in at the top of the shoulder, at the front, and come out again a hand's width lower, tearing as it went. A long ragged gash, deep, with the edges pulled open. It was not bleeding much anymore. The cold had done that. But it was the kind of wound that would bleed again, the moment it got warm. The kind of wound that needed a surgeon, and a needle, and a clean bandage, and a bed.
They had none of those things.
"Saints," said Corin softly.
"Tar," said Wat.
"What?"
Wat jerked his chin at the hull timbers. At the long black icicles of tar hanging down between them.
"Old sailors," he said. "On the barges. If you cut yourself on the river, you put tar on it. Keeps the water out." He breathed, slow. "Hurts."
Corin looked at the tar. At the wound. At Wat's gray face.
Then he did it. He broke off a piece of the old tar, as long as his finger. It was hard and cold and brittle. He held it in his palm, against the warm rod, until it went soft and sticky, and smelled of pine and smoke. And he pressed it into the wound, all along it, from top to bottom, as gently as he could. Which was not very gently.
Wat did not make a sound. His big hands closed on the blanket, and the knuckles went white, and his eyes shut. That was all.
When it was done, Corin tore a strip off the bottom of his own shirt, and then another, and tied them round Wat's shoulder as tight as he could, and tied the arm against Wat's chest with Juno's ball of string, round and round.
Then he sat back on his heels.
"You did that on purpose," he said. "Under the water. You moved. So it would hit you and not me."
Wat opened his eyes.
"You've got the book," he said.
"What?"
"The book. With everybody in it." Wat looked at the Ledger, lying on the boards in its oilcloth. "Hessa. Robin. Me. If they hook you, they get the book. If they hook me, they get a bargeman." He shrugged with his good shoulder. "Bargemen are cheap."
Corin did not know what to say.
So he did the only thing he could think of. He took the rod, which was still warm, glowing a faint dull red, and he laid it across Wat's knees, on top of the blanket.
Wat looked at it.
"Hold it," said Corin. "It's warm."
And after a moment, slowly, Wat put his big good hand on the silver rod, and held it.
Well, said Malakor, very faintly now, from farther away, in the voice of a man who has been put down on a stranger's lap and does not entirely approve. Well. This is new.

Wat slept.
Corin had not thought he would. He did not seem the kind of man who slept, any more than a tree sleeps. But a little while after he had taken the rod in his good hand, his head went back against the black hull timber behind him, and his eyes closed, and his mouth fell a little open in his gray beard, and his breathing went slow and deep and rough. His big hand stayed on the rod. It did not let go even in his sleep.
Corin left it there.
He sat at the open end of the cave, where Vell had stood, with his back against the great outer rib of the hull, and looked at the stump of old nail on the outside of it. The one the cloth had hung on.
It was just a nail. Rusted orange, bent a little to one side, sticking out of the black wood. You would not notice it. He had not noticed it. Nobody had noticed it except a man in a gray cape who had come up the river in a black boat looking for something, anything, and had found the one thing they had left behind.
M.
He closed his eyes and saw it again. The small red letter in the corner of the blue and white checks. The neat tight stitches. Mrs. Marta at her kitchen table on a winter evening, with her glasses on the end of her nose and the lamp turned up, sewing the same letter into the corner of everything she owned. Sheets. Towels. Pillowcases. Tea cloths. Year after year. So that if she ever lost one, at the wash house, at the public oven, at a neighbor's, it would find its way home to her.
So that it would find its way home.
He opened his eyes.
Don't, said Malakor. Faint, from across the cave, from under Wat's hand. I know what you're doing. Don't.
"I have to," said Corin quietly. "I have to think it through. All of it. The way he will."
He did not say Vell. He meant someone else.
He thought it through.
A cloth, found in a wreck on the Saltmarsh, the morning after a man with Corin's face had vanished into a sewer and not been found. A cloth that had been round a pie. A home pie, with lard pastry, mutton and onion, baked in a big oven that ran a little hot at the edge. A public oven, Vell had said. Or an inn.
Or a Watch house.
The cloth had a letter M on it. And the Inquisition had Mrs. Marta's name already. It was on the collection order. No. 14 Tallow Street. Household of: Mrs. M. (landlady). A girl, J., about fourteen. Kross had written it himself, in his small neat hand, on Tuesday. Kross had been in her kitchen, on Wednesday, while the Eraser broke her floor.
And Kross had smelled her kitchen. He would remember. He remembered everything.
Mutton and onion. That was what Mrs. Marta's kitchen always smelled of. It was in the Ledger. Corin had written it there himself, years ago, on the second page, under her name, so that he would always know which door was home. The passage that smells of onions.
So. A cloth with an M on it, that smelled of Mrs. Marta's pie, found on a wreck where someone had slept last night.
And where was Mrs. Marta now?
Not at No. 14. Kross knew that. The house was empty, the door broken, a constable outside. Kross would ask where she had gone. Someone would tell him. Someone always told. A neighbor, a coal man, a child paid a copper. The old woman from fourteen? Went off with the Watch sergeant. Moll. To Rope Street.
And Rope Street had a big old oven. Corin had seen it. In the back room of the Watch house, where the night men cooked their suppers. Iron, black, older than the building. It ran hot at the left side, Moll always said, and you had to turn things.
It would catch a pie a little at the edge.
Corin sat very still with his back against the dead ship's rib.
It would not be today. He did not think it would be today. Kross was not a man who hurried. Kross would take the cloth and look at it, and smell it, and turn it over in his long fingers, and smile, and put it away in a drawer. And wait. And watch. And see who came and went at the Watch house on Rope Street. And who they talked to. And where they went after.
I want all of them, Wren. Every one.
Corin had not been there when Kross said that. But he knew Kross had said it. He knew it the way he knew the cold.
He thought of Moll. Big and slow and limping, with his boiled-ham face and his cut hand wrapped in a bar towel. Moll had spilled a whole tray of gin on a lady in white fur so that Corin could get eleven people out of a cellar. Moll had laughed about it. Best night's work I've done in twenty-two years.
And Moll had taken in Mrs. Marta. Given her his best chair. Put a mattress on the floor by his stove.
And let her use his oven.
Boy, said Malakor.
"It was a pie," said Corin. His voice came out strange. "She made me a pie. That's all. That's all she did. She made me a pie, because I was cold and wet and she was frightened for me, and she couldn't think of anything else to do. And she wrapped it in a cloth with her letter on it. And now—"
He stopped.
It isn't your fault.
"Isn't it?"
No. A pause. Well. Not all of it. The cloth was the girl's doing. The pie was the old woman's. The man in the cape is Kross's. And Kross is— The voice stopped. Kross is Kross. You didn't make him.
"I made him follow me," said Corin. "Every door I knock on is a door he's watching. Linus said that. Before he said don't go home. And I knocked on all of them anyway. Because I didn't know what else to do. Because I needed them." He looked down at his hands. "I still need them."
Malakor did not answer.
Corin took out the Ledger.
He opened it to the page with the doors on it. All the names. All the careful, loving, stupid names. He looked at them for a long time.
Then, beside Mrs. Marta, in the margin, very small, he wrote:
Vell has her cloth. Kross will know it. Rope Street is not safe. Tell Moll.
And beside Moll at the Watch house, he wrote:
Tell him first.
He could not tell anyone. That was the worst of it. There was no boat. Juno had the boat. Juno was in the city, somewhere, running from door to door with his list in her pocket. Wat was asleep with a hole in his shoulder. The marsh went on for miles in every direction, flat and white and empty, threaded with water too deep to wade and too cold to swim. And the city was a gray smudge on the edge of the sky, ten miles off, with the smoke of ten thousand chimneys going up from it, and somewhere in it a big limping man was cooking his supper in an old black oven that ran hot on the left side.
Corin sat and waited for the tide to turn.
It was the longest afternoon of his life.
