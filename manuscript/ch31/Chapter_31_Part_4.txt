Chapter 31: The Ribs of a Dead Ship, Part 4 of 5: A Letter With No Address
A Letter With No Address
Somewhere in the middle of it, he stopped watching the river.
He had watched it for two hours, until his eyes ached. The brown flood going slowly down. The reeds coming up out of it, one bed after another, bent and dripping. The mud coming up after the reeds, gray and shining. And no boat. No girl in a huge coat with one oar over the stern. Nothing on the water at all but a single swan, very far off, white against the gray, going upstream on its own toward the city as if it had business there.
He could not watch any more. So he took out the Ledger again, and opened it, not to the doors this time, but to the last line he had written by the fire the night before.
The Auditor of the Fourth Desk is called Elara.
She has a gap in her teeth.
She does not remember anything from before six years ago either.
He read it three times.
He thought about a long white room on the third floor of the Oblivion Bank. Three tall windows over the river. A desk made of glass, and a woman behind it in a dark green coat, with white gloves on her hands and her short dark hair cut straight across. A woman who had put her bare hand on a sheet of glass and read him, and found a locked door in him, and the word OAKHAVEN chalked on all the others. A woman with a small gold cap over her front tooth, and behind it, when she spoke, a dark little space where a real tooth should have been.
And the glass. Going white under her hand. And inside the white, for one breath, a pool. Green weed at the edges. Sun on the water. A small girl standing in the shallows with her skirt bunched up in both fists, laughing at him through a gap in her teeth, and calling him something. Not Corin. Shorter.
Cor.
He had lied to her about that. He had sat across the glass desk and told her he saw nothing.
He waited, now, to feel something about it. The way he had waited that morning for the old words to come back. Nothing came. He could not remember the girl in the pool. He could only remember having seen her, once, in the glass. It was like being shown a painting of a house and told that you used to live there.
And yet.
He turned to a clean page, after the doors. He took Juno's pencil. And he began to write, slowly, without planning it, not knowing what the next line would be until it was on the paper.
To the Auditor of the Fourth Desk.
He stopped. He looked at it. He drew one hard line through the Auditor of the Fourth Desk, and wrote above it:
To Elara.
I do not know if you are my sister. I do not remember having a sister. I only have a book that says I did, and a picture in a sheet of glass, and a word over my heart that has nearly worn away.
I do not remember your face, or the pool, or the sun on it. I do not remember you calling me Cor. If you did, I am sorry I lost it. I did not mean to. Somebody took it, and I could not stop him.
I think he took you too.
I do not know where you were for eighteen years, or who raised you, or what they told you. I hope they were kind. I hope somebody was.
I lied to you on Monday. You asked what I saw in the glass and I said nothing. I saw you. I think I saw you. I was afraid to say it, because if I said it, it would be true, and then I would have something to lose.
I have it anyway now. So I might as well say it.
I saw you.
He stopped writing.
It did not look like a letter. It looked like any other page of the Ledger, in his own hard-sloping hand, pressed so deep that he could feel the words from the back of the paper. It had no address. It had no name at the bottom. He could never send it. If it ever reached the Oblivion Bank, if anybody there ever read it, it would put a rope round her neck as surely as round his.
He did not tear it out.
He closed the book gently, the way you close a door on somebody sleeping, and put it back inside his coat. And then he looked up, and went back to watching the river, because there was nothing else in the world he could do.
Juno on the Ebb
She came back at a quarter past three.
Corin knew it was a quarter past three because the bells told him. Very faint and far, from the city, carried across ten miles of flat marsh on the cold still air. Three strokes, and then the single small one that the Saint Bride's bell always rang a little after the others, as if it had been asleep. He had been counting bells all afternoon. It was something to do.
The tide had turned two hours before. He had watched it go. The brown water inside the wreck had begun, very slowly, to slide away, the way it had come, sideways and quiet. The ribs of the Margery had grown longer as it went, their wet black feet coming up out of the water one by one. The little birds had come back to the mud. The heron had come back to its post.
And then, out of the gray, the clunk-splash.
He was on his feet before he knew it. At the edge of the broken deck, holding on to the great rib, leaning out over the falling water.
The boat came down the channel between the frosted reeds, fast, on the ebb, with Juno in it. She was not rowing anymore. She was steering, with one oar over the stern, the way Wat did. Letting the river carry her. Her face was a small white shape inside the huge coat. As she came round the last bend, she looked up and saw him on the deck, and her hand came off the oar for a moment. Just for a moment. A small wave. And went back.
Corin let out a breath he had been holding since ten o'clock that morning.
She brought the boat in under the stern, neatly, and caught the rope he threw her, and tied it, and came up the side of the hull like a cat. And stood on the deck in front of him, out of breath, red in the face from the cold.
And then she saw Wat.
Wat had woken when the boat bumped the hull. He was sitting up against the timbers at the back of the cave, with the horse blanket round his legs and the rod across his knees and his right arm tied against his chest with her own ball of string.
Juno's face went white.
"What happened?"
Corin told her.
He told it plainly, all of it. The black boat with the open eye on its flag. The poles. The hook in the water. Wat moving. And then the man in the gray cape, and the nail on the outside of the rib, and the blue and white cloth, and the red letter in the corner.
And the folding.
Juno did not say anything while he talked. She stood very still on the deck with her hands hanging at her sides, and her face got whiter and whiter, until it was the color of the frost on the reeds. When he got to the cloth, she shut her eyes.
"I hung it up," she said. "Last night. It was wet. It was all over pie. I hung it on the nail to dry, when I went out for the wood, and I meant to bring it in, and then I—" She stopped. "I forgot. I just forgot it."
"I know."
"I forgot it." Her voice cracked right down the middle. "I never forget things. That's my whole—that's what I'm for. Remembering the doors. Remembering the messages. Remembering who's on what corner. And I hung Gran's cloth on a nail with her letter on it, right where anybody could—" She put both hands over her face.
Corin did not know what to do.
He had never known what to do when people cried. It was not in the Ledger. There was no rule for it on any page. He stood there on the cold deck with his hands at his sides and watched her shoulders shake inside her grandfather's coat, and felt something in his own chest go tight and hot and helpless, and could not think of one single thing to say.
So he did what Wat had done in the water. He just moved. A little. Closer. So that he was standing next to her, with his shoulder by hers.
After a while she took her hands down.
Her face was blotched and wet. She wiped it hard with her sleeve. She did not look at him.
"I have to go back," she said. "I have to tell Gran. And Moll. Now."
"The tide," said Wat, from the back of the cave.
They both looked at him.
"Ebb till nine," said Wat. "Can't row up against it. Not you. Not me." He moved his tied arm a little, and his face did not change, but Corin saw his knuckles go white on the rod. "Flood comes in at nine. We go up on the flood. All of us."
"That's six hours!"
"Yes."
"Anything could happen in six hours!"
Wat looked at her steadily.
"Yes," he said again.
Juno opened her mouth. Then she shut it. She sat down on the edge of the deck with a thump, with her legs hanging over, the way she had sat that morning, and stared out across the marsh at the far gray smudge of the city.
Corin sat down beside her.
"Tell me what you found," he said. "Tell me everything. Then at least we'll know what we're going back to."
She told him.
She told it fast, the way she always did, in a great rush, as if the words had been piling up behind her teeth all the way down the river. Gran was all right. Gran was more than all right. Gran had been up since four in the morning, sitting at Moll's kitchen table with a pen and a pot of tea and a list as long as her arm, and by the time Juno got there, half the Lower City was already running.
"Not runners," said Juno. "Not like me. Kross knows the runners. He's got the runners' names. She's using the step women."
"The step women?"
"The ones who scrub the front steps. On the Hill. In the High Quarter. Every morning, every big house. Nobody looks at them. Nobody ever looks at a woman on her knees with a bucket. Gran did it for six years, after Grandad came home." Juno's chin came up. "She knows every one of them. And they know every back door in the city."
Corin thought about that for a long moment.
"And they're taking messages?"
"They're taking your list," said Juno. "Gran had it before I got there. She'd made it herself. From memory. She said she's been carrying your parcels up those stairs for six years and she knows who your friends are better than you do." She almost smiled. "She said to tell you that. And that the rent is still due tomorrow."
"Of course it is."
The rest came faster.
Oren was not at Pruett's. He had gone north on Tuesday night, on foot, along the towpath, alone, to the barge. He had said a captain's place was with his lady, and that he did not trust a river he could not see the end of. Vespera's shop on Hook Lane was shut, with a man in a brown coat standing across the street from it all morning, eating chestnuts. But Vespera was not in it. She had gone to Mother Grue's, under the Dye-Works, with a bag of inks and needles, the night Corin went into the sewer. Gideon was there too. And eleven others. The arches were full.
Briggs had opened the Intake Hall that morning as usual, and stood at his grille as usual, and when a constable asked him about Corin Vane, he had said, Who? in a voice that made the constable go away.
And a lady had come to Tallow Street.
"Yesterday," said Juno. "In the afternoon. After you'd gone. A lady in a dark green coat, with white gloves on, and a giant with her holding an umbrella. Bigger than a door, the coal man said. They went into the house. Past the constable. They were in there nearly an hour. She went up the stairs. All the way up. To your room." Juno looked at him sideways. "The coal man said when she came out, she'd been crying. He said you could tell, even under the umbrella. And she had her hand on her chest, like this." Juno pressed her hand flat against the front of the huge coat. "All the way down the street."
Corin did not say anything.
He sat on the edge of the broken deck with his legs hanging over the falling water, and looked at the city, and saw nothing at all.
The Auditor of the Fourth Desk is called Elara.
She has a gap in her teeth.
He had written that last night. On this ship. By the dying fire. And at the same hour, or near it, ten miles away, she had been standing in his room.
On the wall, he thought. By the bed. Where he had cut the three words into the plaster on Tuesday night with a horseshoe nail, because he was afraid of losing the book.
Corin Vane. Oakhaven. Elara.
She had seen them.
She must have.
"Corin?" said Juno.
"Yes."
"Who is she?"
He was quiet for a long time.
"I don't know," he said at last. And then, because it was Juno, and because she had cried in front of him and he had stood there like a post, he said the rest. Very quietly. "I think she might be my sister."
Juno stared at him.
Then she did a thing he did not expect. She did not ask any questions. She did not say how or why or are you sure. She just reached over and took his hand, the left one, the one she had counted on that morning. And held it. Tight. With her small cold dirty bitten-nailed fingers.
And they sat like that on the edge of the dead ship, side by side, and watched the tide go out.
The Back Way
"There's a back way," said Wat.
It was later. The light was going. Juno had curled up under the horse blanket at the back of the cave, against the timbers, and fallen asleep almost at once, the way children do when they have been frightened for too long and their bodies simply stop. Corin sat by Wat with the warm rod between them. The marsh outside had gone the color of slate.
"North," said Wat. "To the barge. There's a back way. Not up the river. Round it."
He held out his good hand. Corin did not understand for a moment. Then he did, and took out the Ledger and Juno's pencil, and gave them to him.
Wat looked at the pencil in his big fingers as if it were a strange small animal. He did not write. He turned the Ledger to an empty page at the very back, and laid it on his knee, and with the blunt end of the pencil he began to draw.
He drew slowly. Heavily. Pressing so hard that the lead squeaked on the paper. A long wavy line down the middle of the page. The river. A cross on it, low down. Here. A square, higher up, on the left bank. The Customs House. A row of little arches across the river above it. The bridge.
"Up the river to the bridge," he said. "Has to be. No other way through the city. On the flood. Dark. Bargemen go up at night all the time." He touched the row of arches. "Then, past the bridge, we don't stay on the river."
He drew a smaller line, coming off the river on the right, just above the bridge. Thin. Wavy. Going away up the page in long bends.
"Mill Cut," he said. "Old channel. Dug for the tanneries, two hundred years ago. Runs behind the Dye-Works and the slaughterhouses. Stinks. Nobody watches it. Nobody wants to."
He drew the Mill Cut going up and up, past little squares that were buildings, until it came to a short straight line across it.
"Old weir," he said. "Broken. Have to carry the boat over. Twenty yards. Then the Tanners' Ditch." Another line. "Then the Long Pond. Then the back of the lock."
He drew a box on the river, far up the page, at the top. Two short lines and two long ones. A lock.
"North locks," he said. "Kross'll have men on the lock. On the gates. Watching every barge go through." He tapped the box. "But the Long Pond comes in behind the lock-keeper's house. Old Jory's house. There's a sluice. Under his garden. Only big enough for a little boat. Hessa found it when she was a girl. Jory lets her through." He paused. "Jory owes her."
"For what?"
Wat considered this for some time.
"She pulled his son out of the lock," he said. "When he was six. Hessa was nine."
Corin looked at the drawing. At the thin wavy line of the Mill Cut, and the broken weir, and the ditch, and the pond, and the little sluice under an old man's garden.
"How long?" he said.
"All night. If nothing goes wrong."
"And if something goes wrong?"
Wat did not answer that. He just held the Ledger out, with the drawing in it, and the pencil.
Corin took them. He looked at the drawing for a long moment. Then he wrote beside it, in his own small sloping hand, everything Wat had said. Mill Cut. Behind the Dye-Works. Stinks. Old weir, carry twenty yards. Tanners' Ditch. Long Pond. Old Jory's sluice under the garden. Jory owes Hessa. She pulled his son out of the lock when she was nine.
He looked at the last line.
"She was nine," he said.
"Jumped in," said Wat. "In her Sunday dress. Her mother beat her for the dress." Something moved under the beard again. "She says it was worth it."
Corin wrote that down too.
When he had finished, he sat with the Ledger open on his knee and looked at the page. Wat's heavy, squeaking, pressed-hard drawing, with his own neat words crowded all round it. It looked like a map in a child's storybook. A map to a place where treasure was buried.
He thought: I will need this. Some night, in some dark place, I will have forgotten all of it, and I will open the book and find this page, and Wat's lines, and I will not remember who drew them. But I will know the way.
He thought: That is what the book is for.
He closed it.
"Wat," he said.
"Mm."
"When we get there. To the barge. If Kross is watching. If anyone followed us. If the man in the gray cape—" He stopped. "I'll go. I'll go on my own, the same night, before it gets light. I won't stay. I won't put Hessa and Robin—"
"Yes, you will," said Wat.
Corin looked at him.
Wat was looking out at the darkening marsh. His face was gray in the dusk, gray with pain and cold and no sleep. But it was very steady.
"Hessa will make you," he said. "She'll feed you. She'll put you in Robin's corner by the stove. She'll tell you everything twice. And you'll stay." He paused. "Everybody does."
"Wat—"
"She knew," said Wat. "When she took the old lord on. Under the hay. She knew what it meant. She knew whose men would come looking." He turned his head, slowly, and looked at Corin with his small calm gray eyes. "She said: If we don't, who does?"
Corin did not say anything.
"So," said Wat. And that was all. He turned back to the marsh.
They sat in silence while the last of the light went out of the sky, and the reeds began to darken, and the cold came creeping in across the marsh.
And Corin thought about a girl of nine in her Sunday dress, jumping into the cold water of a lock to pull out a little boy. And about what it would cost her, one day, to keep doing it. And whether anyone had ever told her that she did not have to.
He thought probably not. He thought probably she would not have listened.
