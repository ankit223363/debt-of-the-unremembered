Chapter 56: Fire Under Glass, Part 3 of 5: Pallister
Pallister
The great stair was on the far side of the gallery. Of course it was.
Corin had seen it from below. A wide sweep of white marble, coming down from the gallery to the floor of the dome in one long curve, like a wave, with a gilt railing and a carpet of violet running down the middle of it. The lords had come down it at the ninth bell, two by two, with the Weigher calling their names. Vivienne had come down it at a quarter to midnight, with a servant carrying her train. It was the only way down from the gallery to the floor that did not go through the kitchens.
And it was on the far side. Half the way round the dome. A hundred paces of burning gallery away.
Mrs. Pratt was gone. He had lost her in the first white flash. He thought she had gone the other way, along the gallery to the right, toward the Lady's rooms. Toward Remy. He hoped so. He did not have time to hope it very hard.
He went left.
Without the stick it was very bad. He went with one hand on the carved railing where it had not caught, and the other on the wall where the curtains had already burned away, and his bad leg dragging behind him like a log. The boards under his feet were hot. He could feel it through Fenton's buckled shoes. The air was so full of smoke that he could see only a few paces in front of him, and the smoke was full of the sweet burnt-sugar smell of the Ash, and every breath he took had strangers in it. A wedding. A fever. A ship. He kept the door in him shut, as far as it would shut, and breathed through Fenton's sleeve, and went on.
The private rooms of the Fourth Tier were on his right as he went. Behind where the curtains had been.
He had not seen them before. Nobody below had. That was the point of them. Now, with the violet velvet burned away to a few black rags hanging from the brass rail, he could see into them as he passed. They were small. Each one no bigger than a box at a theater. A little square room with a gilt chair or two, and a small table, and a low railing at the front where the curtain had hung, so that you could sit and look down at the floor of the dome without being seen. And at the back of each one, a door. A narrow door, painted cream and gold, that let out into a passage behind.
Most of the rooms were empty. Their doors stood open. Whoever had been in them had gone out the back at the first boot on the bronze doors.
The seventh was not empty.
Corin heard it before he saw it. A sound, through the roar of the fire. A thin, high, hoarse sound, over and over. And a banging. Like somebody beating on a door with a stick.
He came level with it.
The door at the back of the little room was shut. There was a man in the room. Standing with his back to the railing, to the dome, to the fire, beating on the shut cream-and-gold door with the head of an ebony stick. Over and over. With all the strength in his thin arms.
"Open it," he was saying. "Open it. Open it. Open it."
He was thin. Thin and not tall, about sixty, with thin white hair that had been carefully combed and was now standing up in tufts. He had a white mask pushed up on top of his head, forgotten, like a man who has pushed his spectacles up and forgotten them. And under it, spectacles. Small round ones, gold-rimmed. His face was pink. Pink like a baby's, the skin very soft and smooth and clean. And on the lapel of his good dark coat, small and neat, a little enameled badge. Blue.
The ebony stick had a silver head. Shaped like a flower. A closed flower.
Corin stopped.
He had seen that stick before. In the counting room in the lower rooms, two weeks ago, through a knothole in the door of a broom cupboard. Laid across a table beside a bag marked A. P., while its owner took two peppermints from his pocket and put them on the table in front of Mistress Fane, and smiled.
We lost two mice.
Ambrose Pallister.
Under-Secretary of the Office of Public Health. Mr. Peppermint.
He turned round.
He had heard Corin. Or felt him. Or just turned, the way a hunted thing turns. And he saw a lame man in a cellarman's coat in the open front of his little burning room, and his soft pink face lit up like a child's.
"You," he said. "Oh, thank the saints. You. Man. Come here. The door." He pointed at it with the silver head of the stick. His hand was shaking. "They've locked it. Do you understand? One of his men. One of the gray men. He came up the passage at the twelfth bell and I heard him turn the key. From outside. I heard it. He locked it and took the key and went away." His voice went up. "He locked me in. He locked me in, and then the lamps—"
He stopped. He looked past Corin at the dome. At the fire.
"Help me," he said.
Corin did not move.
He stood in the front of the little room, holding the burning railing, and looked at Ambrose Pallister.
He was thinking of a lot of things. He did not want to think of them. They came anyway, the way the strangers' memories came through the crack in the door.
He was thinking of a bag on a table with twenty vials in it. Lull. Boiled grayroot. A weed that grew in graveyards and on old battlefields, that made sheep forget the way home. Care of the Dispensary, the Hill.
He was thinking of a cut-glass bowl of peppermints on a green leather desk, under a ship painting, in a white stone office with a hand-shaped knocker. For the clerks' children.
He was thinking of a girl called Bet, ten years old, in the fifth arch, and a gentleman from the Health on the Fish Stairs on a Friday morning with a paper of sweets.
He was thinking of Bet's brother, Ned, who had died before the sixth bell a week ago, in Silas's care, because he had forgotten how to swallow.
He was thinking of three hundred and twelve admitted. One hundred and eight died.
And he was thinking of Lyra, a little girl on the stairs of the house on Crown Walk at Midwinter, years ago, being given a peppermint from a round silver box by a kind pink gentleman who did coin tricks. Who Rowan had called Mr. Peppermint.
Pallister was looking at him.
And something in Corin's face must have shown. Even through Vespera's paint. Because the pink face changed.
"You know me," said Pallister.
Corin said nothing.
"You know me. You're not a cellarman. Who are you?" He came a step closer. The stick was trembling in his hand. "Is it money? Is that it? I've got money. I can get you money. A great deal. Name it. Name it and it's yours. Just open the door."
"I can't," said Corin.
"You can. You're a big man. Put your shoulder to it. It's only wood. Put your shoulder to it—"
"I can't," said Corin. "My leg."
Pallister looked down at his leg.
And Corin saw him understand. That he was looking at a man who could hardly stand. Who could no more break down a door than fly.
And then Corin did a thing he did not understand.
He let go of the railing.
And went into the little room. Past Pallister. To the cream-and-gold door at the back. And put his gloved hand on the handle, and turned it, and pulled.
It was locked. Of course it was locked. It did not move at all.
He put his shoulder to it anyway. His good shoulder. He leaned his weight on it, all of it, with his good leg braced on the hot boards and his bad leg stuck out stiff behind him. And pushed. And the door did not move. He drew back and hit it, with his shoulder, as hard as he could. And the pain went through him from his knee to the top of his head, white, so that for a moment he could not see. And the door did not move.
He hit it again.
It did not move.
He did not know why he was doing it. He knew what this man was. He knew what this man had done. He had written it in the Ledger, in square capitals, on a page of its own, under THOSE I OWE. He had lain in the arches a week ago, with his leg on fire, and heard Silas say that Ned was gone.
But he hit the door again.
I made you kind instead, Linus Hale had said. In the crypt. On Sunday. With his blue fingers on his knees, and his eyes very old. It was all I could do. When you were small. They had taken everything else. I could not give it back. So I made you kind instead.
The door did not move.
"Stop," said Pallister.
Corin stopped.
He was leaning against the locked door with his forehead on the cream-and-gold paint, breathing, and the fire was very loud behind him. He turned his head.
Pallister was standing in the middle of the little room. He was not looking at Corin any more. He was looking out, past the railing, at the dome. At the fire. At the black glass, breathing orange. And his soft pink face had gone quite calm.
"It's no good," he said. "Is it."
Corin did not answer.
"No." Pallister nodded, slowly, as if somebody had told him the price of something. "No. I thought not. He wouldn't have left the key, you see. He's very careful. He's the most careful man I know." He almost laughed. "I always liked that in him."
He put his hand in the pocket of his good dark coat.
And took out a box.
It was small and round and silver, with a lid that fitted very close. The kind of box a gentleman keeps snuff in. Or sweets. He held it out on his soft pink palm, toward Corin, with the lid shut.
"Here," he said. "Take them. For the children."
Corin looked at the box.
He did not take it.
Pallister looked at him over the gold-rimmed spectacles, kindly, puzzled, the way a man looks at a child who will not take a sweet.
"They were only sweets," he said. "That's all they ever were. Only sweets. For the children of the poor. Who never have anything sweet." His voice was soft. "And it made them quiet. And it made them sleep. And they were happier, quiet. You've never seen them, the children in the Lower Docks. Screaming. Fighting. Hungry. I made them quiet. I made them sleep. And what they lost, they never missed. That's the beauty of it. They never missed it." He looked down at the silver box in his hand. "And the Hill had its lamps. And everybody was happy."
"A hundred and eight of them died," said Corin.
Pallister looked up.
"A hundred and eight," said Corin. "In the House of Rest. On your books. And a boy called Ned, in Silas's care, a week ago. He forgot how to swallow."
The pink face did not change.
"Yes," said Pallister. "Yes. Some of them did. It's very sad. It's always sad. But there are so many, you see. So very many. More every year." He shook his head. "And I lost forty-seven of them on Sunday night. Forty-seven of my children. Gone from the crypt in a night. I never found out where." He looked at Corin, and something moved behind the spectacles, very far back. "It was you, wasn't it. You and the Thorne girl. I knew it was. I told him. I told him this morning."
He held the silver box out again.
"Take them," he said. "Please."
Behind him, the burning brass rail at the front of the little room, where the curtain had hung, came away from the wall.
It came away all at once, at one end, with a screech of iron, and swung down. A long bar of red-hot brass with the burning rags of velvet still hanging from it. It swung down across the front of the little room like a gate closing, and hit the railing, and the railing broke, and the burning velvet fell into the room. Onto the gilt chairs. Onto the small table. Onto the floor.
Onto Pallister.
Corin saw it go over him. A great slow fold of burning violet velvet, falling over his thin shoulders and his white tufted hair and his white mask, like a cloak being put round him by a servant. He did not cry out. He did not move. He stood there with the silver box held out on his palm and the fire going over him, and for a moment, just a moment, Corin saw his face through the flame. Calm. Puzzled. Kind. Looking at Corin over his gold-rimmed spectacles as if he still could not understand why Corin would not take a sweet.
Then the velvet folded down over his face, and he fell.
The silver box rolled out of his hand across the burning floor. It came to rest against Corin's foot. Its lid had come off. And there were peppermints in it. White and round and smooth. A dozen. Lying in a little silver nest, in the firelight, by the toe of Fenton's buckled shoe.
Corin looked at them.
He did not pick them up.
He turned away from the locked door. From the little burning room. From the thing on the floor under the velvet that had stopped moving. And went back out onto the gallery, and put his hand on the railing, and went on toward the stair.
Behind his left ear, Malakor said nothing.
And Corin was glad of it.
The Sister Vial
The great stair was burning at the top and not yet at the bottom.
Corin came to the head of it along the last of the gallery with his hands black from the railing and his lungs full of smoke and strangers, and stood for a moment and looked down it. The long white marble sweep of it, curving down to the floor of the dome like a wave. The gilt railing on each side had gone dark and was smoking. The violet carpet down the middle was alight for the first ten steps, a long red river of flame running down from the gallery. Below that, it was only smoldering. And below that, at the bottom, where the stair met the black and white floor, it was not burning at all.
He could not go down the middle. He went down the side. Down the bare white marble at the edge, by the burning rail, with his back to the wall and his bad leg first, one step at a time, the way you go down a ladder. The marble was hot. He felt it through the seat of Fenton's trousers when he had to sit, twice, and slide down a step on his hands, because the leg would not take him. The smoke came up the stair from the floor below and went into his eyes until he was crying with it and could hardly see.
Halfway down, he stopped.
Not because of the leg.
Because he could see the floor now. Not from forty feet up, the way he had seen it from the gallery. From close. From only a little way above it, through the smoke and the gilt bars of the stair rail. And he could see Kross.
Kross had got down off his chair.
Corin saw the chair first. The gilt chair with the plum velvet seat, standing alone just inside the bronze doors, empty, with the little heap of dropped masks round its legs. And the men in gray in front of the doors, still there, still looking at every face. And beyond them, Exchange Street, red with torchlight, and crowded with carriages and horses and running people, and the snow beginning to come down out of the black sky into the red.
And Kross was not on the chair.
He was walking across the floor of the dome.
Alone. Not hurrying. In his long plain gray coat and his hat, with his hands behind his back. Walking through the fire the way a man walks through a field of tall grass. Straight across the black and white squares, between the burning tables, over the broken glass, past the running lords, who did not see him, and the ones lying still, who did not see anything. The smoke went round him. The heat went round him. Once a burning rag of velvet came floating down from the gallery and fell across his path in front of him, and he stepped over it, the way you step over a puddle. He did not look up. He did not look at the hole, or at the girl in the gray gown standing at the edge of it. He looked straight ahead, at one place.
At the brass box.
No. Not the box. Beside it.
Corin had not seen it before. He saw it now. On the near side of the hole, at the very edge, where the black squares had sunk and the floor stopped. A small table. Not glass, like the others. Dark wood, and very old, with thin curved legs, and a top of green leather. It stood there at the edge of the drop with the smoke pouring up past it out of the red glow below, and it had not burned. Not yet. The fire had come round it on both sides and stopped, as if the old dark wood were too hard to catch.
The Lady's table.
He had not seen her at it. He had been under the floor by then. But Remy had told him, in the arches, how it went every Midwinter. At a quarter to midnight the Lady came down the great stair, and her own small table was carried down after her and set by the box, and she sat at it to open the bids, and the lords came to it one by one and bowed. And on the green leather in front of her, in a little rack of silver wire, like a rack for toast, she had kept her own vials. A dozen or so. Small ones. Each with a twist of colored ribbon round its neck. Not for sale. Her own. The ones she wore on the necklace sometimes, Remy had said, and sometimes kept beside her, to look at. To hold up to the light while she talked. To show.
The little silver rack was still there. On the green leather. With the vials in it, shining in the firelight.
Kross walked up to the table.
He stood in front of it for a moment, looking down. With his hands behind his back. The way a man looks down at a shop window.
Then he took his right hand from behind his back, and took off his glove.
Corin could see it clearly. Even from the stair, even through the smoke. Kross took off his right glove, finger by finger, and put it in his coat pocket. And then reached out with his bare hand. Past the first vial in the silver rack. Past the second. Past the third. Not looking at them. As if he knew exactly where to go. And stopped at one near the end.
It was very small. Smaller than the others. A little round vial of clear glass, no bigger than a cherry, with a twist of faded pink ribbon round its neck. And inside it, a light.
Corin could see the light from where he stood. It was not gray or blue or green. It was gold. Warm gold. Like the light of a candle seen through a window on a winter night. And it was moving, very fast and very lightly, round and round inside the glass. Bright, and quick, and full of something. Like laughing.
Kross picked it up.
He did it very carefully. With two fingers. The way you pick up a bird's egg. He lifted it out of the silver rack and held it up in front of his face, close, in the firelight, and looked at it.
And then he did a thing that Corin did not understand at all.
He held it to his ear.
Just for a moment. He bent his head a little, and held the small gold vial close against his ear, the way a child holds a seashell, and stood quite still in the middle of the burning dome with his eyes shut. Listening.
And his face.
Corin had seen Silas Kross's face many times. In the Ministry. On the stair. At the tower window. At the edge of the hole at a quarter to midnight, smiling. It was a quiet face. A calm, still, careful face, that never showed you anything it had not decided to show you. A face like a closed door.
The door was not closed now.
For one moment, standing in the fire with the little vial against his ear and his eyes shut, Silas Kross did not look like the Inquisitor of the Ministry of Records. He did not look like a hunter. He did not look like a man who would burn a house with a thousand people in it to hide a name in a ledger. He looked like a man who has been away a long time, and has come home, and is standing at the door of the house where he grew up, hearing somebody inside it laughing. Somebody he had thought was gone.
Then it was over.
He opened his eyes. He took the vial away from his ear. And he put it inside his coat. Not in a pocket. Inside. Against his chest, under the gray coat and the waistcoat, in the place where a man keeps a letter from home. And buttoned the coat over it. And took his glove out of his pocket and put it back on, finger by finger.
And turned. And walked back across the burning floor toward the bronze doors, with his hands behind his back, the way he had come. Not hurrying.
He did not look at the stair.
He did not look at the girl at the edge of the hole.
He did not take anything else from the table.
Corin stood on the white marble of the great stair, halfway down, with his hand on the hot rail and the smoke in his eyes, and did not know what he had seen. He knew it was something. He knew it the way you know, in a dream, that a door you have just walked past is important. But he did not know why. A vial. A small gold vial with a pink ribbon, from the Lady's own rack, held to an Inquisitor's ear in a fire. It meant nothing to him. He could not make it mean anything.
He thought he ought to write it down.
He did not have time.
Because above him, very high, in the black burning glass at the top of the dome, there came a sound he had heard once before tonight, from the gallery. A long, deep, slow groan of iron.
Only this time it did not stop.
It went on. And on. And got louder. A sound like a ship's timbers in a storm, like a great tree going over, like the whole sky bending. And with it, from everywhere at once, from all round the great shell of the dome, a high thin singing, like a finger on the rim of a wineglass. A thousand panes of glass in their iron frames, all singing at once, as the frames began to bend.
Corin looked up.
The ribs of the dome were moving.
He could see them through the smoke. The great curved iron ribs that held the glass. Twenty-four of them, going up from the gallery to the top like the ribs of an umbrella, meeting at a great iron ring at the very top, where the glass was black. He had counted them, at the eleventh bell, pouring the Duke's wine. They were moving. Slowly. Bending, very slowly, inward, toward the middle, the way the ribs of an umbrella bend in a high wind. And the great iron ring at the top, where they met, was not where it had been. It was lower. It was coming down.
The dome was falling.
Not all at once. Not yet. Slowly. The way a great tree falls, at first, so slowly you think it might stop. A hand's width. Another. With the iron groaning and the glass singing and the fire roaring up into it from below. Coming down. Over the black and white floor, and the burning tables, and the brass box, and the hole, and the people still lying on the floor among the broken glass who could not get up, and the porters still climbing out of the hole by the iron chains, and the girl who had sat with her mask in her lap.
And the girl in the gray gown at the edge of the hole, counting.
She looked up.
For the first time. Corin saw her do it. She lifted her face, with the white mask still in her hand, and looked up at the great black burning shell of the dome coming down over her, slowly, out of the smoke.
And she did not move.
At the bronze doors, the men in gray were going out. All of them. At once. The neat gray line was breaking, and they were going out through the doors into Exchange Street, quickly, not running but nearly, and as they went they were pulling the great bronze doors shut behind them.
Corin let go of the rail.
And fell down the rest of the stair.
