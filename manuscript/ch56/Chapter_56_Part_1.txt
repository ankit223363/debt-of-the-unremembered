Chapter 56: Fire Under Glass, Part 1 of 5: Cinder Lane
Cinder Lane
The air of Cinder Lane hit Corin in the face like a bucket of water.
It was cold. Real cold. The clean, moving, ordinary cold of a winter night out of doors, with a wind in it, and a smell of smoke from a thousand chimneys, and a sky over it. After the cold room it felt almost warm. He lay on his back on the frozen cobbles where Oren had dropped him, with the iron flap of the coal chute banging shut beside his head, and breathed it. In and out. In and out. Great white clouds of breath going up into the dark.
Above him, the sky was black and full of stars. And cutting across it, so close it seemed he could reach up and touch it, the edge of the dome.
He had never seen it from here. From the back. From below. On Exchange Street, at the front, the Memory Bourse was all marble steps and bronze doors and columns and lamps. Here, in Cinder Lane, it was a wall. A great sheer cliff of black stone going straight up, with no windows in it, only drain pipes and iron ladders and the soot of a hundred years. And on top of the wall, high up, very high, the dome. A great round shell of glass and iron, black against the stars, with light coming up through it from inside. Soft. Golden. The light of a thousand candles and lamps, shining up through the glass into the winter night, so that the whole dome glowed, like a lantern, like the moon coming up behind a hill.
It was beautiful.
Corin lay on the cobbles and looked at it, and could not think why it should make him want to weep.
"Get up," said Oren.
He was standing over Corin with the black case under one arm and his sword out in the other hand. Not raised. Just out. Held low, by his leg, the way a man holds a sword when he has had it out so often he no longer thinks of it as a sword. His breath smoked. His left arm was still in its sling of Mrs. Fitch's black shawl, and he held the case pressed into his side with the elbow of that arm, against his ribs, so that his sword hand was free.
Gideon was already up. He was crouched against the wall by the chute, very small, with both hands flat over the front of his coat. Looking up the lane. Toward the corner.
Corin got up.
It took a long time. His leg would not bend at all now. It had stiffened in the cold room the way a rope stiffens in a frost, and he had to roll onto his side and get the stick under him and push himself up the wall with his good leg, a hand's width at a time, like a drunk man getting up out of a gutter. When he was up he leaned on the wall and waited for the white flashes to stop.
"Where are they?" he said. "The two Inquisition men. Gideon said there were two at this end."
"Gone," said Oren. "Ran. When the whistle went. Round to the front." He jerked his head at the corner. "They all went to the front."
Corin listened.
And he heard it. On the other side of the great black wall, round the corner, in Exchange Street. The noise. It was very loud and very confused, like a noise heard under water. Boots, many boots, running. Horses. Wheels. Men shouting orders. A woman screaming, high, on and on. Glass breaking. Somewhere a door being broken in with something heavy, again and again, boom and boom and boom, like a drum. And over it all, high and thin and wild, a sound he did not know at first. A sound like a great many birds, all crying at once.
It was the lords. The lords and ladies of the Hill, in their masks and silks and feathers, in the dome of the Memory Bourse, finding that the bronze doors were shut, and that the men on the other side of them were in gray.
"He's taking them all," said Corin.
"Looks that way."
"Every lord on the Hill. In one night. In one room." Corin almost laughed. He did not. "He's mad."
"He's not mad." Oren's voice was flat. "He's got a paper. I'd lay money on it. Signed by somebody who doesn't like the lords. He's going to walk them out of those doors one at a time and look at every one of their faces. And then let them go. Most of them. And then every one of them will remember, all their lives, that he looked at them."
Corin looked at him.
He had not thought of that. He should have. He had worked at the Ministry of Records for ten years, and he knew how the Inquisition thought.
"And her," he said.
Oren did not answer.
He did not have to. They both knew. Lyra was in there. In the dome. In her mother's gray gown and a white mask. On the arm of the Duke of Marrow, who had paid the forty crowns and who would, very likely, be one of the ones Kross looked at and let go. But Lyra was not a lord. She was not on any list. She was a girl whose face was on a WANTED poster on the corner of Coopers' Lane. A girl whose father had been taken out of a cellar on Saltmarsh Row by a man in a cellarman's coat. A girl Kross had smiled at, across the hole in the floor, at a quarter to midnight. A girl Kross had lifted his hat to, at the tower window, on Sunday.
He would not let her go.
"The case goes to Saint Bride's," said Corin.
Oren's jaw moved.
"The case goes to the crypt," said Corin. "To Silas. Now. Tonight. While they're all at the front. You take it down Tanner's Steps and along the river and over Lantern Bridge and you don't stop. And you give it to Silas, and you tell him to put it somewhere cold, and you sit on it with your sword out till we come."
"And you?"
"I'm going back in."
Oren looked at him.
He had a way of looking. Captain Oren of the Thorne house guard, twenty-two years. A long, slow, flat look, that went over you from your boots to your hair, and missed nothing, and told you nothing. He had given that look to a great many young men in his time. Young men who had come to him with plans.
"On that leg," he said.
"On this leg."
"In that face. With the Inquisition at every door."
"In this face. It's a good face. Vespera made it. Nobody's looked at it twice all night." Corin straightened up against the wall. "I'm a cellarman. The house is on fire, or about to be, and the cellarman is going back in to save the wine. Nobody looks at a cellarman."
"The house isn't on fire."
Corin looked up at the dome.
He did not know why he had said it. The house is on fire. It had come out of his mouth the way words come out of a man in his sleep. But now he looked up at the dome, at the great glowing shell of glass and iron against the stars, and something in him went cold.
The light inside it had changed.
It was still golden. Still soft. But there was something else in it now. A flicker. At one edge, the east edge, nearest them, low down, near the rim. A place where the gold had gone a darker color. Orange. Moving. Like the light of a fire seen through a window from a long way off.
And as he watched, it went brighter.
"No," said Oren. Very quietly.
"Take the case."
"I'm not leaving you."
"You promised her." Corin turned and looked at him. "On the barge. Robin first. Her father first. That's what you said. That's what Hessa said. Robin first. And Hessa kept it. And it killed her." His voice was very steady. He did not know where the steadiness came from. "Her father's last evening is under your arm. It's all he's got left. It's the only thing in this city that knows what happened to him. If it burns, or if Kross gets it, it's gone. For good. There won't be another one."
Oren's face did not move.
"Her father first," said Corin. "Captain."
For a long moment the old soldier stood in the frozen lane with the case under his arm and his sword hanging by his leg and looked at Corin. And then he looked up at the dome. At the orange flicker at its east edge, growing.
And then he looked at Gideon.
"And him?"
"I'm going too," said Gideon.
He had not moved from the wall. He was still crouched there with his hands over his coat. But he was looking up at the dome too, now, and his thin face in the starlight was quite calm.
"No," said Corin.
"Yes." Gideon stood up. "They're still down there. In the stair. Stair's shut till the bell. They'll stand there till somebody tells them to move. And if that's on fire—" He nodded at the dome. "—then the stair'll be on fire, and the drum room, and the cold room, and all of it. And they won't move. Because nobody will tell them." He looked at Corin. "I said I'd come back for them. I didn't think it'd be tonight. But it's tonight."
He said it the way he said everything. Flat. Dry. A thing that needed doing. As if he were saying the boots wanted mending.
But his hands were shaking. Over the front of his coat. Over the two jars.
The cold, Corin thought. He can feel it now. A little. Just a little. The jar's against his skin and he can feel the cold.
Or it was not the cold.
"Gideon," he said.
"You can't stop me," said Gideon. "You can't even bend your leg."
That was true.
Oren let out a long breath through his nose.
Then he did a thing Corin had never seen him do. He put his sword away. He slid it back into its scabbard with one long smooth movement, and buckled the strap over the hilt. And he shifted the case from under his bad arm to under his good one, and held it there, tight, against his side.
And with his bad hand, the left one, in its sling, he reached out and took Corin by the back of the neck. Not hard. The way a father takes a boy by the back of the neck. And held him there for a moment, and looked at him.
"You bring her out," he said.
"I'll bring her out."
"You bring her out, or you don't come out."
"I know."
Oren let go.
He turned to Gideon. He looked down at him, the huge gray-bearded man and the small thin boy, for a moment. Then he put his hand on top of Gideon's head. Just for a moment. The way you put your hand on a dog.
"Mind your feet," he said.
And then he went.
Down Cinder Lane, away from the corner, away from the noise, into the dark. Not running. He never ran. Walking, fast and heavy and steady, with the black case held tight under his good arm and the sling of black shawl round the other, toward the top of Tanner's Steps and the river. His boots rang on the frozen cobbles. And then they did not. And then he was gone.
Corin watched the place where he had been.
Then he turned round. He looked at the coal chute, with its iron flap shut. He looked at the great black wall above it. And he looked up, past the wall, at the dome, where the orange light at the east edge had spread now, along the rim, and was climbing. Slowly. Up the glass. The way water climbs up a cloth.
"Not the chute," he said. "Not the stair. Not for me. I'll never get past them going down, and I'll never get up into the dome from the cold room. Not with the floor up." He looked along the wall, the other way. Toward the corner. Toward the noise. "The kitchen gate."
"The kitchen gate's at the front."
"The kitchen gate's at the side. In Mews Road. Where I came in." He settled his weight on the stick. "And there's a brass lamp on it that scorched my gloves, and a steward called Grice who'll want to know where I've been. And if I'm lucky, Grice will be too busy running to ask."
Gideon looked at him.
"And me?"
"You go down the chute," said Corin. "To your father."
He held out his hand.
Gideon looked at it. At the scorched white cotton glove, gray-black at the fingertips. Then he took it, with his own white curled hand that would not bend. And they held on to each other, there, for a moment, in Cinder Lane, under the burning dome.
Then Gideon let go and lifted the iron flap of the chute and went down into the dark, feet first, without a word.
Corin turned toward the corner.
And behind his left ear, very faint, an old dry voice said:
Boy. You don't have to do this.
"Yes I do," said Corin Vane, out loud, to nobody.
And went.
The Kitchen Gate
Mews Road was full of horses.
Corin came round the corner of Cinder Lane and stopped, with his back against the wall, and stared.
It was a narrow road, Mews Road. A service road, cobbled, with the high blank side wall of the Memory Bourse on one hand and the backs of the Crescent stables on the other. He had come down it at the eighth bell with the wine cart and Grice the steward, and it had been dark and quiet and empty except for a cat on a wall. Now it was packed from end to end. Carriages. Dozens of them. The fine glossy carriages of the Hill, black and dark blue and green and plum, with the arms of the great houses painted on their doors, that had brought the lords and ladies to the Night of Masks and had been sent round here to wait. And all of them trying to leave at once.
The horses were mad with it. Rearing and screaming and kicking at the traces, with their eyes rolling white in the lamplight. The drivers were standing up on their boxes, shouting, cracking their whips, trying to turn in a road that had not been made for turning. Two carriages had locked wheels at the far end and were stuck there, side by side, with their drivers beating at each other with their whips. A footman in sky-blue livery was lying in the gutter holding his head. And over it all, from the other side of the great wall, the noise of Exchange Street. The boots. The breaking glass. The high wild crying of the lords.
And the smell.
Corin smelled it now, on the wind coming down the road. Not wood smoke. Not coal. Something else. Something thin and sharp and sweet, like burnt sugar, like hot tin. He had smelled it before, but he could not think where.
And then he could.
In the wet house in Gutter Lane. In the attic over the dyer's shop. In every room he had ever gone into where a burner had died and there was Ash in the air. That smell. The smell of memory burning. Only here it was not one memory, in one room, faint. It was strong. It was coming down the road on the wind like the smell from a bakery door. As if somewhere, close, a great many memories were burning all at once.
The lamps, he thought.
The Ash lamps. The brass ones. The cold blue Crescent Gas. One tenth to the Treasurer. The rest to the lamps.
There had been a hundred of them in the dome. He had counted them, pouring wine for the Duke. All round the gallery. All round the great ring of glass tables where the lots were laid out on plum velvet. Hanging from the iron ribs of the dome on long brass chains. Each one burning its steady cold blue light, out of the jars from the back rows behind the pillar.
If they burst.
If somebody burst them.
He pushed himself off the wall and went down Mews Road.
Nobody stopped him. That was the strange thing. Nobody looked at him at all. He went down the road between the screaming horses and the shouting drivers with his stick and his stiff leg and his scorched white gloves and Fenton's heavy wine-stained coat, and they looked through him the way you look through a window. A lame old servant. Going the wrong way. Toward the noise instead of away from it. Some fool of a cellarman going back to save the wine. A driver in Langley sky blue nearly ran him down, and cursed him, and did not see his face. A horse swung its great head round and its hot breath went over him, and its rolling eye looked straight at him, and saw nothing it needed to be afraid of.
Nobody looks at a cellarman.
The kitchen gate was halfway down. An archway in the great blank wall, with a gate of iron bars, and beyond it the kitchen yard. The gate was open. Standing wide, swinging a little on its hinges. And on the post beside it, in its iron cage, the brass lamp.
It was the lamp that had scorched his gloves at the eighth bell. The Ash-finding lamp. A squat brass thing like a lantern, with a little round window of thick glass in its side, and behind the glass a small steady blue flame. It burned blue for ordinary men. It burned white, Remy had said, and hot, and hissed, for a man with Ash in him. He had come past it at the eighth bell with Grice at his elbow and held his breath, and felt the heat of it come out through the glass at him like a hand, and smelled the cotton of his gloves begin to scorch, and Gorse the head servant had looked round. And then Mrs. Pratt's ices had come out of the kitchen door on a great silver tray, and everybody had looked at the ices instead.
He came up to it now.
And it was white.
Not because of him. He was still ten paces off. It was white already. White and hissing and roaring, the little flame behind the glass gone huge and pale and wild, beating against the glass like a moth. The brass case round it had gone dull red with the heat. As he watched, the thick round glass in its side cracked across with a sound like a stick breaking.
It was not him.
It was the air. The air was full of Ash. Coming out of the building, out of the kitchen door, out of every window and crack and drain. So much of it that the lamp could not tell one man from another any more. It was finding Ash everywhere, all at once, and burning itself to death trying to show it.
Corin went past it.
He felt the heat on the side of his face as he went. Like walking past an open oven. He did not stop.
The kitchen yard was a square of cobbles with the kitchen on one side and the coal store and the wash house on the others. At the eighth bell it had been full of people. Cooks and maids and potboys and footmen in plum livery, and the porters with the wine, and Grice with his list. It was full of people now. But they were all going one way. Out. Toward the gate. Running, with their aprons over their heads, and their arms full of whatever they had snatched up. A maid with a stack of silver plates. A potboy with a ham. A footman with a candlestick in each hand. They streamed past Corin on both sides as if he were a rock in a river.
And from the kitchen door, the smoke.
It was not much. Not yet. A thin gray haze, hanging under the low roof of the passage, curling out of the open door into the night. But there was a glow behind it. Orange. Deep in the house. And the sweet burnt-sugar smell was so strong now that he could taste it on his tongue.
"You!"
Corin turned.
Mrs. Pratt was standing in the kitchen doorway.
She was the biggest woman he had ever seen. He had seen her at the eighth bell, with the ices, and again at the ninth, from across the servants' hall. A great tall wide mountain of a woman in a white apron, with arms like hams and a face like a red moon, and a voice that could stop a dog fight at fifty paces. She had a meat cleaver in one hand and a copper pan in the other, and her white apron was gray with smoke, and she was not running.
"You," she said again. "Fenton. Or whoever you are."
Corin stood quite still.
"Don't stand there gawping," said Mrs. Pratt. "I know you're not Fenton. Fenton's a little round man who drinks. You've been in my coal store twice this month, with the Lady's boy, and you left a hat on my peg and blood on my stair, and you think I'm a fool." She looked him up and down. "Where's Remy?"
"Upstairs," said Corin. "With her. He went back up."
Mrs. Pratt's red face went still.
"Then that's where I'm going," she said.
"No. Mrs. Pratt—"
"That's where I'm going," said Mrs. Pratt, "and that's where you're going, because you've come back, and nobody comes back into a burning house unless there's somebody in it they want. And I'll not have it said I let a lame man go up those stairs on his own." She pushed the copper pan into the hands of a passing scullery girl, who was so startled she stopped running and stood holding it. "Mags. Out. Take this. Go to my sister's on Tanner's Steps and stay there. Go on."
The girl went.
Mrs. Pratt turned back to Corin. She held the cleaver up between them, so that the light from the kitchen door ran along its edge.
"They came in at the front," she said. "The gray men. At the twelfth bell. All at once. Through the bronze doors with a ram. Like the doors were a barn. And they put a man on every door in the house. Every one. The garden door and the gallery doors and the back stair and all." She jerked her head at the kitchen passage behind her. "Except this one. They didn't put a man on this one."
"Why not?"
"Because this one's for the help." Her mouth twisted. "They want the help out. They want the help out quick. They had a man come down here and stand in my kitchen and shout it. All servants out. Out of the house. Now. By the kitchen gate. And then he went back up. And five minutes after, the first of the lamps went."
"Went."
"Burst. Up there. In the dome. I heard it through the floor. Like a gun." She looked at him. "And then another one. And another. Like a string of crackers at a fair. And then the smell."
Corin did not say anything.
He did not have to. He could see it in her face. She had worked in this house for thirty years, Remy had said. She knew it the way a sailor knows his ship. And she knew what it meant, when a man in gray came into your kitchen and shouted for the help to get out, and five minutes after, the lamps began to burst.
They want the help out.
They wanted the help out so there would be fewer bodies to explain.
"The back stair," said Corin. "From the kitchen yard. Up behind the gallery. To the Fourth Tier rooms. To her rooms." He had a key in the Ledger. A small iron key with a V and a 3 on it, that Lyra had given him from her mask. "Has it got a man on it?"
"It has." Mrs. Pratt's eyes narrowed. "At the top. But he's young. And he's frightened. I saw him when they came down. He's never been in a fire." She hefted the cleaver. "And I have."
She turned and went into the smoke.
Corin followed her.
The kitchen passage was long and low and stone-flagged, and it went past the kitchen itself, a great vaulted cave of a room with three ranges and a spit and a table as long as a barge, all empty now, the fires still burning in the ranges, a pan of something on the hob boiling dry and filling the air with a smell of burnt milk under the sweet burnt-sugar smell of the Ash. Past the servants' hall, where the long table was still laid for the help's supper at the first bell, bread and cheese and beer, with the benches pushed back as if everybody had got up at once. Past the stillroom, with its rows of jars. Past the door of the coal store, where Remy's hat had hung on a peg two weeks ago.
And to the foot of a stair.
Narrow. Steep. Wooden, painted green, worn pale in the middle of every tread. It went up into the dark in a tight square turn, round and round, the way a back stair goes in a great house, so that the servants could come and go between the kitchen and the fine rooms without ever being seen. Corin had come down it an hour ago, with Remy, to the iron door. Now he looked up it.
The smoke was coming down it.
Not a haze any more. A slow gray river of it, rolling down the green steps toward them, thick and sweet, glowing faintly orange from somewhere above. And with it, the heat. And with the heat, the noise. The noise of the dome. Very close now. Just above them. The crying of the lords. The boots. The bursting of the lamps, crack and crack, one after another, like guns. And under it all, a sound Corin had not heard before, and did not want to hear again. A long, low, deep, terrible groaning. Like the sound a great tree makes in a storm, a moment before it goes over.
Iron. Hot iron. Bending.
The ribs of the dome.
Mrs. Pratt looked up the stair with her cleaver in her hand and her red face gone gray with smoke.
"Mind the ninth step," she said.
And went up.
