Chapter 56: Fire Under Glass, Part 5 of 5: The Leads
The Leads
Hollis knew another way down.
He said so in four words, standing up from the edge of the big bed with the little gray dead hand still in his. "The garden stair. Stone." And then, after a moment, laying the hand down on the blanket very gently, the way you lay down something that might break: "It won't burn."
It was a stone stair inside the old wall, he said. From the Lady's room to the garden. Narrow and dark and very steep, a spiral, the kind of stair a fishwife's daughter would have climbed every night with a candle, a hundred years ago, before there were great white marble stairs and gilt rails and violet carpets. Nobody used it now but Hollis, and Remy, and sometimes, on her good nights, the Lady. It came out by the garden door. And the garden door would have a gray man on it. Or it would not. Either way, there was a wall at the bottom of the garden, and a gate in it, and beyond the gate an alley, and beyond the alley the Crescent.
"Can he do a spiral stair?" said Mrs. Pratt. Looking at Corin's leg.
"No," said the girl in the gray gown.
She said it before Corin could. She said it flatly, the way you say a thing you have known for some time. And Corin looked at her and wondered how she knew.
"Then he comes with me," said the small fierce face at the window. "Over the top. Come on. I've been sat in that gutter four hours and I can't feel my backside, and the lead's starting to go soft."
So they split.
Hollis went first down the garden stair, with a candle from the mantelpiece. Then Mrs. Pratt, sideways, because she was too wide for it any other way, with her cleaver held in front of her. At the top of the stair she stopped and turned and looked at Remy, standing by the big bed, looking down at the little old woman in it.
"Come on, lad," she said.
Remy did not move.
"Remy."
"I'm coming." He did not look round. "Go on. I'll catch you up."
Mrs. Pratt looked at him for a long moment. Then she went down.
Remy stood by the bed.
Corin watched him. He did not know why. He was sitting on the edge of the narrow bed by the door now, where they had moved him, with the Ledger shut in his lap and his gray black-tipped hands on top of it, waiting for the girl and the small fierce one at the window to work out how to get him over the sill. And he watched Remy, and he knew things about the old woman in the big bed that nobody else in the world knew any more. The fish market. The knife too big for her. Alistair behind the crates. The bow at Ashcombe. They were in him now. They were his. And he looked at the boy standing by the bed and knew, in a way he could not have known an hour ago, what that boy had been to her.
Not a son. She had not had it in her, by then, to have a son.
A thing she kept. In a room. Close. Because a hooded man with a voice like warm milk had told her to.
And she had kept him. Every night. On the little narrow bed by the door, where Corin was sitting now. And sometimes, in the night, when she woke and did not know where she was, the boy had held her hand.
Remy bent down.
He took the little gray dead hand off the blanket, and held it, for a moment, in both of his. And then he put it back. And pulled the blanket up, over the bare spotted head, over the dried-apple face. And stood back.
Then he went to the chest of drawers. To the stand with the great dark dome of hair on it, and the jet pins. And to the cloth beside it, where the necklace of vials lay. He did not touch the hair. He looked at the vials, for a moment, laid out on their cloth. A dozen or so. All of them on their black chain. Each one with its little twist of colored ribbon.
He counted them. Corin saw his lips move.
Then he looked across at the small dark table by the window, under the shutters. Where there was a little silver rack, like a rack for toast. Empty.
"She took them down," said Remy. Very quietly. To nobody. "The ones from the rack. At the eleventh bell. To show them. She always took them down to show them. And I didn't bring them back up." He was still looking at the empty rack. "There's one she'd want. The pink one. She'd want that one with her."
He did not say anything else.
He gathered up the necklace of vials in its cloth, quickly, and folded the cloth over them, and pushed it inside his burned livery coat. And went to the top of the garden stair. And stopped there, and looked back at Corin.
"You kept it," he said. "Your promise."
"Yes," said Corin.
"I won't forget." Remy's sharp face in the light of the low fire was quite white under the soot. "Whatever it cost you. I'll find out what it cost you. And I won't forget."
Then he went down the stair into the dark after Mrs. Pratt and Hollis, and was gone.
"Right," said the small fierce face at the window. "You. Leg. Over here."
Her name was Juno.
Corin knew that. He knew her. Juno, Mrs. Marta's girl, from Tallow Street, with the frostbitten feet and the stolen watchman's whistle and the grandfather's key on a string round her neck. Who had walked fourteen miles in the snow to find them. Who could do the letters from A to Z, and her own name, and Tallow Street, and Rope. She was in the Ledger. He did not have to read it. He knew her.
He did not know the girl in the gray gown.
He looked at her, as she and Juno got him by the arms and dragged him to the window. Close to. Her face. Her black hands. Her short ragged hair. Nothing. An empty room. He looked at her, and looked, the way you look at a word you have written a hundred times until it stops being a word.
She did not look at him at all.
They got him over the sill.
It was the worst thing yet. Worse than the stair. He had to sit on the sill, and swing his good leg over, and then get the bad leg over, and it would not bend, and Juno pulled at it from outside and the girl pushed at it from inside, and the pain was like a white wall. And then he was out. Sitting in the snow. On the leads.
The leads were the flat parts of the roof, between the slopes. Gray sheets of lead, laid like a floor, with low stone walls round the edges and gutters where the rain ran off. They ran all round the outside of the old stone wing, Juno said, the Lady's wing, like a path. And from the leads, you could get to the gutter round the bottom of the dome. And from the gutter round the dome, you could get anywhere.
Only there was no dome.
Corin looked.
He was sitting on the snowy lead with his back against the wall of the Lady's window, and in front of him, beyond the low stone parapet of the old wing, there should have been the dome. The great round shell of glass and iron, going up into the sky like a hill. He had seen it from Cinder Lane, glowing gold.
It was gone.
In its place, there was a hole in the sky.
That was what it looked like. A great round hole, edged with fire. The rim of the dome was still there, the stone ring at the bottom where the iron ribs had sat. And the gallery under it, burning, all the way round, a ring of flame. But inside the ring, nothing. A great round pit of smoke and red light, going down and down into the building, into the floor, into the cold room under the floor. And out of it, still, very faint now, the last of the white light. Coming up in thin pale threads, like steam off a pond on a frosty morning, and going up into the falling snow, and fading.
And the snow, where it fell through the threads, still faintly gold.
"I saw it go," said Juno.
She was crouched beside him on the lead with her whistle in her fist. She was looking at the hole in the sky. Her small fierce face was very still.
"I was in the gutter," she said. "Right up by it. On the far side, the Exchange Street side, where I could see the doors. Like we said. I saw them all come. The carriages. And the gray men, going round the back, at the half past eleven. That's when I did the long one. All's well. Because they were only going round. And then at midnight they all came running back to the front at once, with the ram, and I knew, and I did the three short." She swallowed. "And then I couldn't stay. The glass was getting hot. I could feel it through my coat. So I came round. Along the gutter. To here. To the old roof. And I lay flat and I watched it." She looked at Corin. "I saw it start to come down. And I saw it stop."
Corin said nothing.
"It just stopped. In the air. Like somebody had their hand under it." Juno's voice had gone very small. "And I knew it was you. I don't know how. I just knew. And I lay there in the snow, and I watched it hang there, for ages, and I thought, he's holding it, he's holding it, he's holding it. And then it came down." She looked at the hole. "And the light came up."
She was quiet for a moment.
"I remembered my dad," she said. "When the light came up. I didn't know I'd forgotten him. But I had. Bits of him. His hands. How he used to whistle. And when the light came up, they came back. Just for a minute. And then they went again." She wiped her nose on the back of her hand, hard. "That's all right. I had them for a minute."
The girl in the gray gown had climbed out of the window after them. She was crouched on Corin's other side now, in the snow. She was not looking at the hole. She was looking at Juno.
"Juno," she said. "Gideon. Did you see Gideon?"
Juno looked at her.
And then at Corin.
"Come and look," she said.
They went along the leads. All three of them. Juno first, low and quick, like a cat. Then the girl. And then Corin, on his hands and the knee of his good leg, dragging the other, in the snow, along the gray lead between the low stone walls. It was a long way. It was not very far. He did not know which. The cold came up through the lead into his hands, his bare gray burned hands, and he did not feel it much. The snow fell on his back. The fire roared on his left, beyond the parapet, in the hole where the dome had been. And he crawled.
They came to the corner of the old wing. To the back of the building. Where the leads ended at a low stone wall, and beyond the wall, a long way down, was Cinder Lane.
Juno lay flat behind the wall and looked over. The girl lay down beside her. Corin pulled himself up to the wall between them, and lay on the cold lead with his chin on the stone, and looked.
Cinder Lane was full of light.
Orange light, from the fire. And white light, very faint, coming up out of the coal chute halfway along the lane. The iron flap of the chute was gone. Blown off. Lying in the middle of the lane, bent, with the snow settling on it. And out of the black square hole where it had been, the last threads of the white light were coming up, pale, into the snow. As if something down there, very far down, were still breathing out.
And beside the chute, against the wall, in the snow, there was a man sitting.
A huge man. Barefoot. In canvas trousers rolled to the knee, and a leather apron. With his back against the black wall of the Memory Bourse, and his great bare gray legs stuck out in front of him in the snow, and his great gray hands in his lap.
And across his lap, across his knees, very small, a boy.
The boy was lying quite still. His head was on the man's arm. His thin face was turned up to the falling snow, and his eyes were shut, and his coat was buttoned up to the neck, one button at a time, over something at his chest.
"He's breathing," said Juno, before anybody could ask. "I watched him. He's breathing. They brought him up the chute. Both of them. Just after you went in by the kitchen. Long before the light. They came up out of the chute together carrying him between them like he was a crate. He'd gone down there and the smoke got him on the stair. And they carried him up and put him down there, in the snow, and the big one sat down and took him on his knees." She stopped.
"Both of them," said Corin.
Juno did not say anything.
Corin looked at the man in the snow. At the hook-shaped white scar on his cheek, from the eye to the mouth. At the crooked nose.
Big Gideon.
Only Big Gideon.
"Where's Saul?" said the girl.
Juno was quiet for a long moment.
"He went back," she said.
The snow fell.
"They put Gideon down," said Juno. "And the big one sat down. And the other one, the one without the scar, he stood there. In the snow. For a long time. Ages. Looking at the chute. And then he looked at Gideon. And he hummed. That tune. The one Gideon hums. Just a bit of it. And then he turned round and went back down the chute." Her voice had gone very flat. Like Gideon's. "I don't know why. I whistled. I whistled and whistled. Three short, three short, over and over. He didn't look up. He just went back down. Feet first. Like he was going to work."
Stair's shut till the bell.
Corin thought of the drum room. The small hot square room behind the wall of ice, with the brazier in the corner, and the great wooden drum on its axle, and the two worn iron handles. And the bench under the speaking pipe, with the two hollows in it, side by side, the shape of two big men who had sat there for a very long time.
Nobody had told Saul he could stop.
Nobody had rung the bell.
So he had gone back. To his place. To the handle. To wait for the bell, the way he had waited for it every night for nine years. And then the dome had come down through the floor of the Memory Bourse into the cold room, and the light had come up.
"How long before the light?" said Corin.
"Not long," said Juno. "A bit. A few minutes." She looked at him. "Long enough to get down there. Not long enough to get back."
Nobody said anything.
Below them, in the snow, in the orange and white light, Big Gideon Hollow sat with his back against the burning wall and his son across his knees. He was not looking at the chute. He was not looking at anything. His pale empty eyes were open, staring straight ahead of him, at the blank wall of the stables across the lane. His great gray hands lay in his lap, on either side of the boy, not holding him. Just there.
And then, as Corin watched, one of them moved.
The right one. Slowly. Like a great stone being lifted by a crane. It came up off the man's knee, and went across, and came down again, very slowly, on the boy's head. On his thin dark hair, white with snow. And stayed there.
Not stroking. Not holding. Just resting there. The way it had lifted, at the foot of the stair, a little way from his side, as if it were going to do something it had done a long time ago. Before. And had forgotten what.
It had remembered.
Or it had not. And it did not matter.
The snow fell on the man's gray hand, on the boy's dark hair, and did not melt.
And somewhere in the night, far off, over the roofs of Veridia, a church bell began to ring the second hour.
It Says So
Juno went down first.
There was an iron ladder fixed to the back wall of the old wing, she said, from the leads to the lane. For the sweeps. Forty rungs. She went over the parapet and down it like a squirrel down a tree, with the whistle swinging on its string, and they heard her bare frostbitten feet ring on the iron, and then the soft crunch of snow at the bottom. And then she was running along Cinder Lane toward the man in the snow by the chute, and kneeling beside him, and putting her hand on the boy's face.
Corin did not go down.
He could not. Not forty rungs. Not with the leg. He lay on the lead with his chin on the low stone wall and watched Juno kneel in the snow, and knew he would have to go down, some time, some way. But not yet. He could not make himself think about it yet.
The girl in the gray gown did not go down either.
She sat beside him on the lead, with her back against the wall, and her knees drawn up under the burned gray silk, and her black hands round her knees. Looking out. Not at the lane. At the city. At the roofs of Veridia going away from them in every direction under the falling snow, white and gray and black, with here and there a light in a window, and here and there a chimney smoking, and far off, very far off, the river, a dark line, with the lamps of the bridges strung across it like beads.
The bell had stopped ringing the second hour.
It was very quiet. Even with the fire. The fire was behind them now, on the other side of the old stone wing, roaring in the hole where the dome had been. But up here, on the leads, at the back, with the snow coming down, it sounded far away. Like the sea.
Corin sat up.
It took a long time. He did it against the wall, beside her, with his bad leg stuck out straight in front of him in the snow. When he was up, he sat for a while and breathed.
Then he took out the Ledger.
He opened it on his knee. To the first page. READ THIS FIRST. The snow fell on the page, and melted, and made small round wet marks on the paper, and he brushed them off with the side of his gray hand, carefully, so as not to smudge the ink.
He read it again.
All of it. Every line. Slowly. The way you read a letter that has come from very far away, from somebody you cannot quite picture.
And near the bottom of the page, the line.
LYRA THORNE. PARTNER. RULE ONE: NO LIES BETWEEN US. NOT EVEN THE KIND ONES.
In his own square capitals. And under it, in the other hand. The small quick one, leaning forward, as if it were in a hurry. The same words, written again. As if whoever had written them wanted to be quite sure.
He looked at the small quick hand for a long time.
Then he looked at the girl.
"Are you Lyra Thorne?" he said.
She did not turn her head. She went on looking out at the city.
"Yes," she said.
"It says you're my partner."
"Yes."
"It says no lies between us. Not even the kind ones."
"Yes."
Corin looked down at the page.
"Then I have to ask you something," he said. "And you have to tell me. Because it says so."
She did not answer. But he saw her hands tighten round her knees.
"On the floor," said Corin. "When I held it up. The dome. I burned something. Something big. The biggest thing I had. I can feel where it was." He put his gray hand flat on his chest, over Fenton's coat, without thinking. "It's empty. It's like a house with all the furniture gone. I don't know what was in it." He stopped. "Was it you?"
The snow fell.
A long way off, a dog was barking. On and on. Somewhere down by the river.
She turned her head and looked at him.
Her face was black with smoke. Her eyes were very dark, and very dry. And she looked at him for a long moment, the way he had looked at her, close, as if she were reading something written very small.
"Yes," said Lyra Thorne.
Corin nodded.
He did not know what he felt. He looked for it, inside himself, the way you look for your keys in a coat pocket, and there was nothing there. Not grief. Not sorrow. Not anything. Only the empty room, very large and very quiet. And in the last corner of it, faint, a voice. Saying his name. A little quick. A little cross.
"All of it?" he said.
"I don't know." Her voice was steady. "You'd know. Not me."
He looked.
He went through the empty room, inside himself, slowly, the way he had gone round the floor of the dome from the stair, a strip at a time, the way he had been taught. Every corner. Every wall. And it was empty. All of it. Clean and swept and bare, like a room after a flitting, when the cart has gone and the new people have not come yet.
Except the voice.
"There's one thing," he said. "Your voice. Saying my name. That's all. I kept that." He frowned. "I don't know why I kept that."
She made a sound.
It was very small. It was not a laugh and it was not a sob. It was the sound a person makes when they have been holding something heavy for a long time, and somebody takes a little of the weight, just a little, and they had not expected it.
She turned her face away again, quickly, toward the city.
"I said it," she said. "At the end. When you were holding it. I was saying it over and over. Corin, Corin, let go. You were going to burn that too. I saw your face. You were going to." Her voice shook, once, and steadied. "And then you didn't."
"Malakor said I didn't have to."
"Malakor." She almost laughed. "Malakor. Of course. The one time he says something kind."
Behind Corin's left ear, very faint, there was a dry rustle. Like an old man shifting in his chair. But no words.
Corin looked down at the Ledger again.
At the line in his own capitals. And the line in her small quick hand under it.
Then he felt in Fenton's coat, in the inside pocket, and found the stub of pencil that lived there. He always had one. He did not have to remember that. It was like his name. And he held it in his black-tipped gray fingers, which did not want to hold anything, and bent over the page on his knee, and wrote. Under her line. In square capitals. Slowly, because his fingers would not do it fast.
MIDWINTER. THE BOURSE. I BURNED HER TO HOLD THE DOME UP. ALL OF HER BUT HER VOICE. SHE GOT THEM OUT WHILE I HELD IT. SHE DID NOT CRY. SHE IS STILL HERE.
He looked at it.
Then he held the pencil out to her.
She looked at it. At his hand, holding it out. For a long moment.
Then she took it.
She took the Ledger off his knee, too, and put it on hers. And bent over it. And he watched her write, in the small quick hand that leaned forward as if it were in a hurry, under his square capitals. He could not see what she wrote. Her hair fell forward over the page. But she wrote for a long time. Longer than he had. And once she stopped, and sat with the pencil in her fingers, not moving, and he thought she was not going to go on. And then she did.
When she had finished, she gave it back to him. The Ledger, and the pencil. And looked away again at the city.
He read it.
Your name is Corin Vane. Mine is Lyra. You found me in the rain at the door of the Inkwell, in the autumn, and I said "You're the harvester," and you said "Yes," and you didn't say anything else, and I hated you. Then I didn't.
You carried my father out of a cellar. You walked into the Bourse twice on one leg. You held up the sky.
Rule one. No lies. So here is the truth. You don't know me now. That's all right. I know you. I'll know you for both of us until you do again, and if you never do, then I'll just keep knowing you. It's not hard. I've had practice. My father doesn't know me either.
It's still there. Under this. I promise.
L.
He read it twice.
Then he shut the Ledger and held it on his knee, with both his gray hands flat on the cover, HARBOUR TIDE TABLES, and looked out at the city too. At the roofs, and the snow, and the far dark line of the river with the lamps strung across it like beads.
He did not know her.
He sat beside her on the leads of the burning Memory Bourse at the second hour of the morning after Midwinter, with their shoulders not quite touching, and he did not know who she was. He did not know her face, or her name, or the sound of her step, or what she looked like when she laughed, or whether she ever did. He did not know what she had been to him. He only knew what the book said.
But the book said so.
And that would have to be enough. For now. That would have to do.
"Lyra," he said.
She went still.
It was the first time he had said it. He knew that, somehow. That it was the first time. That he had said it a thousand times before, in another life, an hour ago, and that this was the first time. It felt strange in his mouth. Like a word in a language he was only beginning to learn.
"Yes?"
"I can't get down that ladder."
She turned her head and looked at him.
And then she laughed.
It was short. Cracked. Hoarse with smoke. It went out of her into the cold air in a small white cloud, and hung there, and went away. It was not a happy laugh. But it was a laugh. Quick. A little cross. As if he were late for something.
And Corin knew it.
Not her. Not her face. Not her name. But the laugh. The shape of it. Quick and a little cross. It was the same shape as the voice in the last corner of the empty room. The one saying his name.
It was a small thing. It was almost nothing.
He held on to it.
"No," said Lyra Thorne. "No, I don't suppose you can." She wiped her face with the back of her black hand, and stood up in the snow, and looked over the parapet, down at the lane. "Juno! Juno. Can he walk? The big one. Can he carry?"
Far below them, in the snow, by the burning wall, Juno looked up.
And beside her, very slowly, like a great stone door swinging on old hinges, Big Gideon Hollow got to his feet. With his son in his arms. And stood there, in the snow, looking up at the roof with his pale empty eyes. Waiting to be told.
"Up," called Lyra. "The ladder. There's another one to carry."
There was a pause.
And then the stone voice, rolling up the black wall through the falling snow.
"Up."
In the end they carried him down between them. Juno above on the rungs and Big Gideon below, with Corin's arms round the great gray neck and his bad leg hanging. And at the bottom, Big Gideon put him over one shoulder like a crate, and his son over the other, and stood waiting in the snow for somebody to tell him where to go.
"Saint Bride's," said Lyra. "Down Tanner's Steps. Along the river. Over Lantern Bridge." She took hold of a fold of the leather apron, the way a child takes hold of its father's coat in a crowd. "I'll show you."
And so they went. Down Cinder Lane, away from the burning, through the snow. A huge barefoot gray man with two people over his shoulders, and a girl in a burned gray gown holding on to his apron, and a small fierce girl with a whistle running ahead to look round every corner. Past the corner where Oren had stood. Down the top of Tanner's Steps, where the Bell was dark and shut. Toward the river.
Corin hung over Big Gideon's shoulder with his face against the cold leather apron and watched the snow on the cobbles go past under him, and the girl's feet beside them. Small, in thin gray dancing slippers, black with soot, soaked through. Walking. Keeping up. Not stopping.
He watched them for a long way.
And somewhere about the bottom of Tanner's Steps, he realized that she was counting under her breath. Very softly. The way she always did. He knew that. He did not know how he knew it. But he knew it. One. Two. Three.
He did not know what she was counting.
He listened to her count, all the way to the river.
