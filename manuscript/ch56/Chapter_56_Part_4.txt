Chapter 56: Fire Under Glass, Part 4 of 5: Holding the Sky
Holding the Sky
He hit the bottom of the great stair on his side, on the black and white floor, and lay there.
The floor was hot. The air above it was hotter. And the noise was everything. The roar of the fire, all round the dome, in the gallery, on the tables, a sound like a great wind that never stopped. The bursting of the jars. The screaming. And over all of it, through all of it, the groan of the iron and the high thin singing of the glass. Coming down.
He got onto his hands and knees.
He could see the hole from here. The black ring of nothing round the brass box, with the smoke pouring up out of it, and the red glow below. Twenty paces away. The Lady's small dark table stood at the near edge of it, with its silver rack, one vial short.
And on the far side, across the smoke, the girl in the gray gown.
She was looking up.
He could not get to her. He knew that at once. Round the hole was thirty paces, perhaps forty, on a floor covered in broken glass and burning velvet and fallen people, and he could not walk. He could crawl. On his hands and knees, with his bad leg dragging. And the dome would be down long before he got there.
He looked up.
It was lower. He could see that now, from the floor. The great iron ring at the top, where the twenty-four ribs met, was not a dark circle high up in the black glass any more. It was a thing. A great black wheel of iron, as wide as a cart is long, hanging there in the smoke above the brass box, red-hot at its edges, coming down. The ribs bent toward it from all round, like the fingers of a hand closing. The glass between them was breaking now, pane after pane, and falling, turning over and over in the firelight, and bursting on the floor. A rain of glass. And the iron ring coming down through it, slow, slow, a hand's width at a time, groaning.
When it came down, it would come down on the brass box. And on the hole. And on everything round the hole, for twenty paces in every direction. And the whole great burning shell of the dome would come down after it, rib by rib, like a tent when you cut the pole. On the floor. On the people still on the floor. On the porters still climbing up out of the hole. On the girl who had sat with her mask in her lap, who had not moved. On the girl in the gray gown, who had not moved either.
And on him.
Boy.
Malakor's voice was very quiet.
Not a shout. Not the iron bar. Quiet. Quieter than Corin had ever heard it. The voice of an old man sitting beside a bed.
Boy. You know what this is.
Corin knew.
He had known since the glass passage. Since the sixth step, when the great cold eye of the glass had turned and found her in him and stopped, and looked, and wanted. It's looking at it too hard, Remy had said. It wants it. And Malakor had shouted NOT THAT so loud that the glass had flinched. Not yet. Not here. Not for this.
Not for this.
For something else, then. Something later.
He had known then. He had not let himself know. But he had known.
You can't hold that up with a song or a word, said Malakor. Or a winter, or a summer. Or the word for your mother. You burned that in a cellar, and your whole first tongue on the Saltmarsh, and it was hardly enough to light a lamp. You'd need a hundred winters. You'd need the whole of a life. You'd need—
He stopped.
You know what you'd need.
The high thin singing of the glass went on and on.
The higher the magic, Corin thought, the more you pay. That's the rule. That's the only rule. A little light costs a little thing. A breath of wind costs a day. And to hold up a falling sky—
To hold up a falling sky, you burned the thing you loved most in the world.
Not a thing you were fond of. Not a thing you would miss. The thing. The one. The one you read on the first page of your Ledger every morning to be sure it was still there. The one whose faces had come up through the green glass toward you, hundreds of them, so many that the glass had stopped you on the sixth step and wanted them.
He looked across the hole at her.
She was still looking up at the dome.
And then, as if she had felt him looking, she looked down. Across the smoke. Across the black ring of nothing. And saw him.
He saw her see him.
He saw her face change.
The white mask fell out of her hand.
"Corin," she said.
He could not hear it. Not over the fire. But he saw her mouth make it. Corin.
And he put his hands up.
He did not stand. He could not. He knelt on the burning black and white floor of the Memory Bourse with his bad leg out behind him, and lifted both his arms, in their scorched white gloves, straight up over his head, toward the great black iron ring coming down out of the smoke. The way a man lifts his arms to catch something thrown to him. Palms up. Fingers spread.
And he reached into himself.
It was not hard to find her. That was the worst of it. It was the easiest thing he had ever done. She was everywhere. In every room of him. On every page. He did not have to look. He only had to open his hand, inside himself, and she was in it.
It's me, he said to her. Inside. Where she could not hear. It's me. I'm sorry. I'm going to need you now.
And he burned the first one.
A door. The door of the Inkwell, in the rain. A girl in a gray cloak with the hood back, her dark hair long then, long and wet down her back, holding a letter. Her face set like a door. You're the harvester.
It went up in his hand like paper.
And the power came. Not much. A breath. A warmth, going up out of his chest and along his arms and out of his spread fingers, into the smoke above him. He felt it go. He felt it reach the iron ring, forty feet up, and touch it. The way your fingertips touch the underside of a shelf in the dark.
The ring did not stop.
He burned the next one.
The floor of Vespera's shop, by candlelight. A girl sitting with her back to the wall and her chin up. No lies between us. Not even the kind ones. Rule one.
It went up. And the power came, more of it. Hotter. He felt it go up his arms like a river going uphill. He felt it reach the iron ring, and spread, and get under it, the way your whole hand gets under a weight.
The ring slowed.
He burned the next.
The barge. The black river. Fog. A girl kneeling by her father in the dark, with his hand in hers, tapping. Four. Two. Nine. Three. And the old man tapping back, once, and holding on.
And the next.
The arches. Silas's knife. Long dark locks of hair falling round her onto the floor, and her face in the firelight, not crying. It'll grow.
And the next. And the next. Faster now. He did not choose them any more. He just took them, one after another, the way you throw wood on a fire. A girl on the wain under the hay, whispering, Not yet. When I can. A girl at the foot of his bed in the crypt, singing Hessa's song while Silas washed the coal out of his leg. A girl asleep against the wall under the grating, in her mother's gown, with the snow coming down through the bars onto her hair. A girl holding both his hands while Vespera's needles went into his face, her knuckles white, saying I'm here. I'm here. I'm here.
They went up.
And the power came up out of him in a great hot roaring river, out along his arms and out of his hands, so much of it that the white cotton of Fenton's gloves went up in flame on his fingers and fell away in black flakes, and his bare gray hands were burning blue at the tips. Up into the smoke. Up to the iron ring. Spreading out under it, all round it, like a great invisible hand. Under the ring and under the ribs and under the glass, all of it, the whole falling sky. And pushing.
The iron ring stopped.
Corin felt it stop.
It stopped in the air, twenty feet above the brass box, red-hot, hanging, with the ribs bent toward it all round like fingers. And the glass stopped falling. And the groaning of the iron stopped, all at once, and in the quiet after it there was only the roar of the fire and the high thin singing of a thousand panes of glass, holding.
He was holding it.
He was holding up the dome of the Memory Bourse with his bare burning hands, from his knees, on the floor, with the girl he loved.
And it was heavy.
It was the heaviest thing in the world. It was heavier than the drum in the drum room and the lift and the ice and all the jars in the cold room together. It pressed down on his hands, and his arms, and his shoulders, and his back, and it pressed down on the place inside him where the power came from, and the place was emptying. He could feel it emptying. The way a cup empties when you pour. Every breath, the dome pressed down, and he had to burn another one to hold it.
He burned another one.
And another.
He did not look at them now. He could not. He just took them, the way a man drowning takes air. And he looked across the hole at her.
She was not standing at the edge any more.
She was moving. Fast. He saw her go to the girl sitting on the floor with her mask in her lap, and take her by the arm, and pull her up, and push her toward the wall. Toward the gallery stair. Toward the kitchen passage. Toward the way out the gray men had not shut. He saw her go to the edge of the hole and kneel and reach down into the smoke and pull up a porter, a man in a plum apron with his face black, by the wrists, and push him toward the wall too. And another. And then she went to a lady in green satin who was lying face down among the broken glass, and turned her over, and the lady was not moving, and Lyra looked at her for a moment and then left her and went on.
She was getting them out.
She knew. She had seen him lift his hands. She had seen the ring stop. She knew what he was doing. She knew what it cost. And instead of coming to him, she was getting them out. Every one she could reach. Under the held sky. While it held.
Because that was what he had bought with it. That was the price. Not her. Them.
And she would not waste it.
He burned another one. He did not see what it was.
The floor was emptying. He could see it, through the smoke and the tears and the blue fire on his fingers. The people who could run had run. The people who could not, she was dragging. The porters were out of the hole. The girl with the mask was gone. The Duke, in his dove-gray silk, Corin had not seen at all. There were people at the kitchen passage, and at the foot of the gallery stair, pushing out. And on the floor round the hole, only the ones who were not going to get up again. And the broken glass. And the burning tables.
And her.
She was coming round the hole toward him.
Running now. In her mother's gray gown, with the hem burning, round the edge of the black ring of nothing, over the broken glass, toward him. Her face was black with smoke. Her short ragged hair was standing up round her head. She was saying something. Shouting it. He could not hear. He watched her mouth.
Let go. Corin. Let go. It's done. They're out. Let go.
He could not let go. If he let go, the sky would come down. On her. She was under it.
He burned another one.
And there were not many left.
He could feel it. The place inside him where she had been, in every room and on every page, was not full any more. It was nearly empty. Like a house after the furniture has been carried out, with only a few things left, here and there, in corners. A chair. A cup. A coat on a hook.
He burned the chair. He burned the cup.
She was ten paces away. Five. She was there. She was on her knees beside him, on the burning floor, with her black hands on his face, on his arms, pulling at him. Saying his name.
"Corin. Corin. Let go. Please. Please. Let go of it. Come on. Come with me. Please."
He heard her now. Close. Right by his ear.
And there was one thing left.
In the last empty room. The last thing. Not a face. Not a place. A voice. Her voice. Saying his name. Just that. Just Corin, the way she said it, the way nobody else in the world said it, a little quick, a little cross, as if he were late for something. The way she had said it a hundred times. A thousand. At the Inkwell, on the barge, in the crypt, on the stair.
The last thing he had of her.
She was saying it now. Right by his ear. Corin. And he heard it, and it was the last time he would hear it and know what it meant, and he knew that, and he held it for one moment, one breath, in his hand.
Boy, said Malakor. Very gently. She's out from under. Look. You can let go now. You don't have to spend that one.
He looked.
She was. They were both at the edge of it. Under the overhang of the gallery, where the stair came down. Where the wall was. Where, if the dome came down, it would come down in the middle, over the hole, and not here.
She had pulled him there. On his knees. While he held the sky. A pace at a time. He had not even felt it.
He let go.
Not of the voice. Of the sky.
He opened his hands, and the power went out of them, and the blue fire on his fingertips went out, and the great invisible hand under the dome was gone.
And the dome came down.
He watched it. From under the gallery, with his back against the wall, and her arms round him. The great black iron ring, red-hot, dropping out of the smoke like a stone dropped down a well. Straight down. Onto the brass box on its iron pillar. And through it. And through the place where the box had been, and down into the black ring of nothing, into the hole, into the red glow below. And after it, the ribs. All twenty-four, folding in toward the middle like the fingers of a closing hand. And after them the glass. All of it. The whole great burning shell of glass and iron, a thousand panes, pouring down into the hole in the floor of the Memory Bourse with a sound like the end of the world.
Into the cold room.
Onto the shelves.
Onto the jars.
There was a pause. One breath. As if the whole building had stopped to listen.
And then the light came up.
Out of the hole. Out of the cold room. Out of the thousands and thousands of jars, all bursting at once under the falling dome. Not fire. Light. A great white pillar of it, roaring up out of the hole into the open night where the dome had been, straight up, past the gallery, past the broken rim, up into the black sky over Veridia, where the snow was falling. A pillar of white light, as wide as the hole, as tall as a church. Full of faces. Full of hands. Full of rooms and trees and weddings and fevers and ships and summer afternoons and the smell of bakeries early and fathers' hands. Twenty years of the Bank. Forty years of the Lady's cold room. Everything anybody had ever sold, or been made to sell, going up at once into the falling snow. And the snow, where it fell through the pillar, turning to gold.
The people in Exchange Street saw it. Half the city saw it. Corin would hear that afterward. That they saw it from the Saltmarsh, and from the Hill, and from the boats on the river. A pillar of white light going up into the sky over the Memory Bourse, for the space of a hundred breaths, with the snow falling gold through it. And that for those hundred breaths, all across the city, people stopped where they stood, and remembered things. Small things. Things they had not known they had forgotten.
Corin did not see the end of it.
He was looking at the girl.
She was kneeling beside him under the gallery, with her arms round him, holding on. Her face was black with smoke. Her eyes were red. Her short dark hair was burned at the ends, and her gray gown was black at the hem, and there was blood on her hands from the broken glass. She was looking at him. Very close. As if she were waiting for something.
She was very thin. She had dark eyes, and a sharp chin, and a face that looked as if it had been set, once, like a door, and had not stayed set.
He did not know who she was.
He looked at her, and looked, and there was nothing. Not a name. Not a place. Not a feeling. An empty room, with the furniture carried out.
Only, in the very last corner of it, very faint, a voice. Saying his name. A little quick. A little cross. As if he were late for something.
He did not know whose it was.
"Corin," said the girl.
And he knew, then. That it was hers. The voice. He had kept the voice and burned the girl it belonged to.
He did not know what that meant.
"Are you hurt, miss?" said Corin Vane.
The girl looked at him.
She did not cry. She did not say anything. She just looked at him, for a long moment, very close, with her black hands on his face. And then she shut her eyes. Just for a moment. The way you shut your eyes when you have been hit, and are waiting to see how bad it is.
Then she opened them again.
"No," she said. "I'm not hurt." Her voice was quite steady. "Can you stand?"

He could not stand.
He tried. The girl got her shoulder under his arm and he pushed with his good leg against the wall, and got halfway, and the leg went out from under him like a rotten plank, and they both went down again onto the hot black and white floor. She did not let go of him. She held on to him the way you hold on to a man in deep water. He did not understand why. She did not know him. He did not know her. And she held on.
"Vane!"
A boy's voice. High and cracked and hoarse with smoke.
Corin knew that voice.
He turned his head. And there, coming down the last of the great white stair, through the smoke, by the bare marble edge where the carpet was not burning, was Remy Castellan. Sharp-faced. Thin. His good gray livery black with soot, and one sleeve of it burned away to the elbow, and his bare arm under it red and blistered. No lantern now. He came down the stair three steps at a time, and jumped the last four, and landed on the floor, and stood there, looking at Corin.
At Corin's hands.
Corin looked down at them too.
They were bare. The white cotton gloves were gone. Burned off. And his hands were gray. Not the pale ash-gray they had been this morning, from the fingertips to past the elbows. Darker. Gray like slate. Gray like the hands of the Hollow brothers. And at the very tips of the fingers, where the blue fire had burned, the gray had gone black.
"You held it," said Remy.
His voice was very strange.
"I saw you from the gallery. From the end, by her rooms. I saw you hold it. I didn't know anybody could do that. Nobody can do that." He came closer. "What did you burn?"
Corin did not answer.
He did not know how to answer. He had burned something. He knew that. He could feel the place where it had been, the way you feel the place where a tooth has been pulled, with your tongue, over and over. A great empty room. But he did not know what had been in it. He only knew it had been big. The biggest thing he had.
And that the girl beside him, holding on to him, had shut her eyes when he called her miss.
"It doesn't matter," said the girl.
Remy looked at her.
Corin saw his sharp face change. Saw him look from the girl to Corin and back again. And understand something that Corin did not.
"Oh," said Remy. Very quietly. "Oh, no."
"It doesn't matter," said the girl again. Her voice was quite flat. "What is it? Why have you come down? Is it her?"
Remy did not answer at once. He was still looking at Corin.
Then he said, "Yes."
And Corin remembered.
Not the girl. Not the empty room. But the cold room. The lift shaft. The frost. A boy with a carter's lantern, saying She sleeps with me in the room. Since I was seven. And his own voice, saying All right.
He had promised.
"Is she—" he said.
"Not yet." Remy's voice cracked. "Soon. Hollis says soon. She's asking for you."
"For me?"
"For the harvester. She knows you're here. She's always known everything that happens in this house." Remy looked at the stair, and the gallery above it, burning. "Her rooms are at the end. On the garden side. The walls are stone there, and the fire hasn't got through yet. But it will." He looked back at Corin. "Can you get up the stair?"
"No," said Corin.
"Yes," said the girl.
She got her shoulder under his arm again.
And Remy, without a word, got his under the other one.
And between them, a thin girl in a burned gray gown and a thin boy in burned gray livery, they carried Corin Vane up the great stair of the Memory Bourse. Up the bare marble edge, by the smoking rail, past the burning carpet. One step. And another. With his bad leg dragging behind him and his good one pushing when it could. He did not know how they did it. They were both so small. But they did it. And at the top they turned right, along the gallery, along the side that had burned first, where the curtains were black rags now and the fire had gone on past and left the boards charred and smoking. Past the little rooms of the Fourth Tier. Past the seventh one, where something lay on the floor under a fold of burned velvet, and a little silver box lay open by the door with white sweets in it. Corin looked at the box as they went past. He knew it. He did not know from where.
At the end of the gallery there was a door.
Not cream and gold. Dark wood. Old. Heavy. With iron bands across it, and a great iron ring for a handle. Set into a wall that was not plaster but stone, old gray stone, cold to the touch even now. The wall of the old house. The fish market. The first building, before the Bourse, before the dome.
Remy pushed the door open with his foot.
And they went through, into the Lady's room.
It was small. That was the first thing. After the dome, after the great white stair and the gallery and the gold, it was very small. A low room, with a low ceiling of dark beams, and walls of the same old gray stone, and a floor of wide boards with a worn carpet on it. A fire in a small stone fireplace, burning low. A window, with its shutters open, and beyond it the night and the falling snow, and an orange glow on the snow from the fire on the other side of the house. A chair by the fire. A chest of drawers. A small narrow bed by the door, very plain, with a gray blanket on it, folded square.
And a big bed.
It stood against the far wall. Dark wood, with four posts, and curtains of old faded violet, tied back. And in it, under a heap of blankets, propped on a heap of white pillows, there was an old woman.
She was very small.
Corin would not have known her. He had seen Lady Vivienne at the ninth bell, from across the floor of the dome, up in her box in the gallery. A great tall straight-backed woman in a violet gown, with a long pale face and a great dome of dark hair piled up on her head and stuck with jet pins, and a necklace of shining vials on a black chain at her throat, and a face like a painted queen's. That was not this.
This was a little old woman. As small as a child. With a face like a dried apple, brown and wrinkled and fallen in round the mouth, where the teeth had gone. Her head was bare. Quite bare. A few thin wisps of white hair on a scalp as pale and spotted as an egg. Her hands lay on the blanket in front of her, and they were tiny, and so thin you could see every bone, and they were gray. Gray to the wrists. Gray to the elbows, where the sleeves of her nightgown fell back. Gray all the way up, as far as Corin could see. As gray as his own.
On the chest of drawers, on a stand like a hat stand, was the hair. The great dark dome of it, with the jet pins still in it. And beside it, in a dish, the paint. Pots and brushes and powders. And the necklace of vials, laid out on a cloth.
A man was sitting on the edge of the big bed, holding one of the little gray hands.
Corin knew him at once. He would have known him anywhere. A thin old man, very straight, in a black coat, with ink on his fingers. Ink of every color. Blue and green and red and violet, in the creases of his knuckles and under his nails, so deep that no washing in the world would ever get it out.
Hollis.
Hollis, who had kept the ink loft in the autumn, before it burned. Hollis, who painted the Lady every evening for two hours. Hollis, who had told Remy what it would cost.
He looked up as they came in. He looked at Corin's hands. He did not say anything. He only moved a little along the edge of the bed, to make room.
And Mrs. Pratt was there. In the chair by the fire. With her cleaver across her knees, and her great red face wet. She looked at Corin. And then at the girl holding him up. And then she looked away, quickly, at the fire.
They put Corin down on the edge of the bed. On the side where Hollis had made room. Next to the little gray hand.
The old woman opened her eyes.
They were very pale. Pale blue, almost white, like the sky on a winter morning. And they were clear. Quite clear. For a moment she looked straight up at the dark beams of the ceiling, as if she did not know where she was. And then her eyes moved, slowly, and found Corin.
"The harvester," she said.
Her voice was a whisper. Dry. Thin. Like paper being folded.
"The one who said no to me. In the autumn." A little breath that might have been a laugh. "Nobody says no to me. You did. I have always remembered that." The pale eyes went over him. "You have come to collect."
"Yes," said Corin.

Remy made a sound. Very small.
The old woman's eyes opened again. They found him. Standing at the foot of the bed, black with smoke, with his burned arm held against his chest.

Remy looked at her.
Corin saw his throat move.

It was not true. Corin knew it was not true. He had been in the cold room at a quarter to midnight, and he had not seen. But the girl beside him had seen. And he felt her go very still.
The old woman smiled.
It was a terrible smile, in that fallen toothless face. And a beautiful one. The smile of a girl of eleven, at her first ball, being bowed to by a lord.
"Good," she said.
And went.
Corin felt it.
Not with his eyes. With the door in him. The door behind his left ear, under the place where Malakor lived, that he had shut on the gallery and had not quite been able to shut all the way. He felt it open. By itself. The way a door opens when the wind changes. And something came toward it.
It came up out of the little old woman on the big bed the way smoke comes up out of a candle when you blow it out. A thin gray thread, at first, rising from her lips, from her eyes, from the bare spotted skin of her head. And then more. Thicker. Gray and silver and violet, twisting, rising, spreading, filling the air of the small stone room. And it did not go up into the beams. It did not go out of the window into the snow. It turned. It hung in the air above the bed for a moment, looking, the way the cold eye of the glass passage had looked. And then it turned toward Remy. Toward the boy at the foot of the bed. The nearest. The one who had slept in this room since he was seven.
Corin put up his gray hands.
And took it.
It came into him like a river into an empty valley.
That was the thing he would remember. Not the pain. There was pain. There was a great deal of pain. But that was not what he would remember. He would remember how easily it came in. How much room there was. Eighty-one years of a woman's life, pouring into him all at once, a flood, and none of it pushing anything out. None of it having to fight for a place. Because there was a place. A great empty room in the middle of him, with the furniture carried out, that had been full an hour ago and was not full now.
He had made room.
He did not know what had been in it. But he had made room. And the Lady came in and filled it.
A girl in a fish market. Cold. Ice on the stones and the smell of fish, and her mother's red hands, gutting, and the girl beside her with a knife too big for her, gutting too.
A boy called Alistair, with a gap in his teeth, behind a stack of crates, kissing her. Sterling. Alistair Sterling. Who married a lord's daughter the next spring and never looked at her again.
A ball. At Ashcombe. Her first. Eleven years old, in a borrowed dress, at the back of the room with the servants. And a lord, an old lord, coming all the way across the room to bow to her, as a joke, and everybody laughing. And her not knowing it was a joke. Bowing back. Never forgetting.
The first jar. Her own. A summer afternoon, a river, a boat. Sold for four shillings to a doctor's wife. And the next day her mother's face, and how she could not remember why it mattered.
A glass garden. A great house of glass, full of orange trees, in the winter, warm. Somebody's. Hers, for a while.
The cellar. The ice. The shelves going in, one by one, and the jars on them. Year after year. Forty years.
And a basket.
A wicker basket, on a doorstep, at night. The step of this house. And in it, wrapped in gray wool, a baby. A boy. Very small. With his fists shut tight. And standing over the basket in the dark, in a long hooded cloak, a tall man she could not see the face of. Who had knocked, and waited, and when she opened the door had said only, in a voice like warm milk:
You asked for a child, Vivienne. Here is one. Keep him close. Keep him here. Let him learn the doors.
And had gone. Into the dark. And she had stood on the step and watched him go, and seen, as he turned, a hand come out of the cloak to draw the hood closer. A long, thin, very white hand.
And then it was gone too, into the flood, with all the rest. Ten thousand things. Twenty thousand. Faces and rooms and prices and lots and bows. Eighty-one years. Pouring in. And Corin on the edge of the bed with his gray hands up and his head back and his mouth open, taking it, all of it, every last thread of gray and silver and violet, out of the air of the small stone room. Until there was nothing left in the air but the smoke from the fire in the grate, and the cold coming in at the open window, and the snow.
He lowered his hands.
He was not sure, for a moment, who he was.
He was an old woman in a bed, dying. He was a girl in a fish market with a knife too big for her. He was a woman at the top of a great white stair with a thousand lords bowing. He looked down at his own gray hands and thought, How strange. When did my hands get so big?
Boy.
The dry voice. Behind his left ear.
Boy. Corin. Your name is Corin Vane. You are twenty-eight years old. You are a harvester. You are sitting on a bed. Open your book.
He opened his book.
He did not know how it got into his hands. It was there. Inside Fenton's coat. The Ledger, in its secondhand cover, HARBOUR TIDE TABLES. He took it out and opened it, there on the edge of the dead woman's bed, with his black-tipped gray fingers. To the first page.
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin Vane.
He read it. All of it. Every line. In his own square capitals. Your name is Corin Vane. You were born in a place called Oakhaven. You do not remember it. You are a harvester. And the names. And the rules. And he felt himself come back, line by line, the way a man comes back up a ladder out of a well.
And near the bottom of the page, there was a line he did not know.
It was in his own square capitals. And under it, in a different hand. Small and quick and leaning forward, as if it were in a hurry.
LYRA THORNE. PARTNER. RULE ONE: NO LIES BETWEEN US. NOT EVEN THE KIND ONES.
He read it twice.
He did not know who Lyra Thorne was.
He looked up.
The girl in the burned gray gown was standing by the narrow bed at the door. With her arms folded tight across her chest. Looking at him. Her face black with smoke. Her eyes very dark. Waiting.
And behind her, at the open window, out of the falling snow, a small fierce face with a stolen watchman's whistle hanging round its neck on a string looked in over the sill, and said, in a hoarse urgent whisper:
"Are you lot coming or not? Because the roof's going."
