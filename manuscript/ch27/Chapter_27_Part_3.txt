Chapter 27: Linus Hale, Part 3 of 5: The Kind Lie
The Kind Lie
"Tell me about Oakhaven," said Corin.
He had not meant to say it so soon. He had meant to wait. To drink his tea, and let the old man talk, and listen, and watch, the way he watched a witness in the Harvesting Section. But it came out of him before he could stop it, the way water comes out of a cracked jug.
Linus Hale did not answer at once.
He sat very still on the settle, with his blue hands folded on the open book. The fire crackled. The gray cat on the hearth stretched, and turned over, and showed its pale belly to the flames. Over by the door, one of the old men put down a domino with a small hard click, and the other one sighed.
"What do you know?" said the old man at last. Gently. "Already. What do you know about it?"
"The name." Corin put his hand flat on his coat, over the place where the Ledger lay. "It's written in my book. On the first page. You were born in Oakhaven. In my own hand. I've read it every morning for six years. And I don't know what it means."
"Nothing else?"
Corin thought of Elara's voice, very level, very careful. Every empty room in you has a word written on the door. He thought of the old green ledger. The empty debt column. Called in full. The fourth hour after midnight. He thought of Briggs's three gold glasses behind the grille. A bell. A field. An oak tree with a swing.
He did not say any of it.
"A schoolhouse," he said. "With a bell. And a big oak tree."
Something went across the old man's face. Quickly. Like the shadow of a bird across a window.
"Yes," he said softly. "Yes. That was Oakhaven."
He unfolded his hands. He took off his round gold spectacles, and polished them slowly on the end of his gray waistcoat, the way old men do when they need something to do with their hands. He did not put them back on. He held them in his lap, and looked at the fire, and when he spoke again his voice was quiet and slow and very even. The voice of a teacher reading aloud to a room full of children. A voice that had told a great many stories.
"It was a village," he said. "A small one. Up in the hills, to the north and east of here. Five days by cart. Past the last of the market towns, past the end of the good roads, up where the maps stop bothering to put names on things. There were perhaps forty houses. A mill on the stream. A forge. A church with a crooked spire." A small smile. "And a schoolhouse, with a bell in a little wooden tower on the roof, that I rang every morning at eight o'clock, and every afternoon at four. And in front of the schoolhouse there was an oak tree. The biggest oak tree I have ever seen, before or since. It was older than the village. Older than the church. The children used to say it was older than the hills. There was a swing on it. A plank on two ropes, hanging from the lowest branch, and you could swing out so high on it that you could see over the roof of the schoolhouse and all the way down the valley."
He was quiet for a moment.
"You used to do that," he said. "Every dinner time. You and the other boys. You used to see who could swing highest. You always won. You were not afraid of anything, in those days." The smile again, very faint. "Except the dark."
Corin did not move.
You used to fear the dark.
"I taught there for eleven years," said Linus Hale. "I came from the city when I was not much older than you are now. I was going to stay a year. Just one. I stayed eleven." He turned the spectacles over in his blue fingers. "I taught you your letters. I taught you to read. You were quick. Very quick. Too quick, sometimes. You'd finish your work before the others had started and then you'd be bored, and when you were bored you got into trouble."
"Frogs," said Corin.
The old man looked up at him sharply. For a long moment his faded gray eyes searched Corin's face. Then he looked away again, at the fire.
"Yes," he said. "Frogs." He did not ask how Corin knew. "Among other things."
"What happened to it?" said Corin.
The old man did not answer.
"Oakhaven," said Corin. "What happened? Why don't I remember it? Why is it on every door?"
He had not meant to say that last thing either. He saw the old man's hands go still on the spectacles.
"On every door," said Linus Hale very quietly.
"Inside me. Somebody read me. Yesterday. They said every empty room in me had Oakhaven written on the door. In chalk. As if somebody had tried to rub it off and couldn't."
The old man shut his eyes.
He sat like that for a long time. His thin face was very white in the firelight. His lips moved, a little, without any sound, as if he were saying something to himself. A word. Or a prayer. Or a name.
Then he opened his eyes, and put his spectacles back on, carefully, over one ear and then the other. And he looked at Corin through them. And his face was very calm, and very kind, and very sad.
"It was the fever," he said.
Corin waited.
"In the spring," said Linus Hale. "Early spring. Eighteen years ago, almost to the month. It came up the valley from the south, with a peddler. Nobody knew. He sold ribbons and needles and tin cups at the door of every house, and he coughed, and by the time he left, the fever was in the village." His voice was very even. "It was a bad one. The kind that comes once in a lifetime. It went through the houses like a fire through dry grass. In a week, half the village was ill. In two weeks, most of them were dead."
He looked down at his hands.
"Your father went first," he said. "Then your mother. You were ill too, very ill, for days, and we did not think you would live. But you did. You were always strong." A pause. "And when you were well enough to travel, I put you on a cart, with a letter, and sent you down to the city. To a school for orphans that I knew of. Where you would be safe. Where the fever could not follow."
"And you?"
"I stayed. Until the end. Until there was nobody left to teach." He was quiet. "And then the men came up from the city, from the Ministry, to see to things. And they said the village could not be kept. That the fever was in the houses, in the wood and the thatch and the wells. And they burned it. All of it. The houses and the mill and the forge and the church and the schoolhouse." His voice did not change. "And the oak."
The fire cracked. A log broke and fell in on itself, and a shower of sparks went up the black throat of the chimney.
"There's nothing there now," said Linus Hale. "Nothing at all. Grass, I suppose. The grass grows back after a fire. And the stream. Streams don't burn." He looked at Corin. "That's why you don't remember it, I think. Not because of anything anybody did to you. Because it hurt too much. You were nine. You'd lost everything. And children are very good at forgetting what hurts too much to remember. They put it in a room and shut the door and never go in again."
Corin looked at him.
He looked at the kind, faded, watery gray eyes behind the mended spectacles. At the thin white face, lined like a page that had been folded too many times. At the blue hands, folded on the book.
"And my sister?" he said.
The old man's hands went very still.
"I had a sister," said Corin. "It's written in my book. On the first page. Under Oakhaven. You had a younger sister named Elara."
For a long moment, Linus Hale did not say anything at all.
Then he said, very gently, "Yes. You did."
"What happened to her?"
The old man looked at him. And Corin saw his eyes fill with water, slowly, behind the round gold rims, until the gray of them was blurred and shining.
"She was three," said Linus Hale. "Three years old. With a gap in her front teeth where she'd fallen on the schoolhouse step and knocked one out, and it never grew back. She used to follow you everywhere. She couldn't say your name properly. She used to call you—"
He stopped.
"She used to call you Cor," he said.
Corin did not breathe.
"The fever took her," said Linus Hale. "In the first week. Before your father. Before your mother. She was so small, Corin. She didn't have the strength." The tears ran down behind his spectacles, slowly, into the lines of his face. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I held her hand at the end. I want you to know that. She wasn't alone. I held her hand, and I sang to her, and she wasn't frightened."
He took off his spectacles again, and wiped his eyes with the back of one blue hand.
"That's all," he said. "That's Oakhaven. That's all there is. A fever, and a fire, and grass. I'm sorry you had to come so far to hear it. I'm sorry it's taken me so long to tell you."
Corin sat on the stool by the fire, with his hands on his knees, and looked at the old man weeping on the settle.
He wanted to believe him.
That was the worst of it. He wanted to believe every word. It was such a kind story. Such a gentle, sad, ordinary story. A fever, a fire, and grass. A little girl who died holding her teacher's hand, not frightened. A boy sent away on a cart with a letter, to be safe. No ledgers. No empty debt columns. No man with a face that slid away and a ring with a dome on it, writing on every door.
He could have taken that story home with him and written it in his Ledger and read it every morning, and it would have been a comfort, in a way. A sad comfort. A grave you could visit.
But he had sat at a glass desk at the top of the Bank, yesterday afternoon, and looked into the dark careful eyes of a woman with a gold tooth capped over an old gap. A gap where a tooth had been knocked out, and never grew back.
And she had said: I see it every night. The pool, and the willow, and the girl with the gap in her teeth.
And she was alive.
Corin looked at the old man's blue hands, shaking a little on the open book.
And behind his left ear, very quietly, so quietly that it was hardly a voice at all, Malakor spoke.
Every Word a Kindness
He's lying, boy.
The voice was so quiet that Corin felt it more than heard it. A cold breath behind his ear. A thread of frost along the side of his neck.
He's lying. Every word. Kindly. Beautifully. The way he always did, when a child came to him crying and asked where the dog had gone. He'd sit them on his knee and tell them the dog had gone to live on a farm in the hills where there were rabbits to chase, and they'd stop crying, and go home happy. A pause. The dog was always under the cart.
Corin kept his face still. He did not look at the rod in his pocket. He looked at the old man on the settle, wiping his eyes with the back of his blue hand.
How do you know? he thought.
Because I know him. Thirty years. Longer. I knew him when he was a young man with ink on his fingers and his head full of books, and I know every face he makes. The voice was very low. Look at his left hand. On the book. Look at the thumb.
Corin looked.
Linus Hale's left hand lay on the open book, beside his spectacles. And the thumb was moving. Very slightly. Rubbing, slowly, back and forth, against the side of the first finger. Back and forth. Back and forth. The way a man rubs a coin in his pocket when he is nervous. The way a child rubs the corner of a blanket.
He always did that, said Malakor. When he told the dog story. When he told the school inspector the roof didn't leak. When he told the priest he'd been at his prayers. A pause. He's doing it now.
And the fever?
There was no fever. The voice was very sure. I'd remember a fever. I was there that spring, boy. In the valley. I was there. And then it stopped. Suddenly. Like a door slamming. And when it came again it was strange, thin, strained, like the voice of a man trying to lift something too heavy for him. I was there that night. I came up the valley road on the night of the—on the night—
Nothing.
Corin waited.
I can't, said Malakor at last. Very quietly. It's like the name on the bridge. It's there and then it slides. I've tried. For years, I've tried. I can see the road. I can see the oak at the top of it, black against the stars. And then nothing. A long silence. But I know there was no fever. A fever doesn't burn a village at four in the morning.
Corin looked at the old man's thumb, rubbing against his finger.
He thought of Elara, at her glass desk, saying in her careful level voice: Called in full. The fourth hour after midnight.
He made up his mind.
"Master Hale," he said.
The old man looked up. Something went over his face at the name. Master Hale. As if he had not heard it for a very long time.
"Yes, Corin?"
"What time did the fire start?"
The thumb stopped moving.
"The fire?"
"When they burned the village. After the fever. When the men came from the Ministry. What time was it?"
Linus Hale looked at him. For a long moment his kind, wet, faded eyes did not move from Corin's face. Then he said, slowly, "In the daytime, I think. I don't—it was a long time ago, Corin. I don't remember the hour."
"Was it the fourth hour after midnight?"
The old man went white.
Not pale. White. All at once, as if somebody had poured the blood out of his face like water out of a jug. His mouth opened a little, and stayed open. His blue hands, on the book, closed into fists.
"Who told you that?" he whispered.
Corin did not answer.
"Corin." The old man leaned forward across the little table. His voice had changed. It was not the teacher's voice anymore, the slow, even, story-reading voice. It was quick and low and urgent and frightened. "Corin, listen to me. Who told you that? Who said the fourth hour? Was it a man? A man with a gentle voice? Did he—" He stopped. His eyes went over Corin's face, quickly, desperately, the way Hollis Marsh's eyes had gone over it in the loft. Reading him. "Did you see him?"
"I didn't see his face," said Corin quietly. "Nobody can."
Linus Hale shut his eyes.
He sat back on the settle, very slowly, as if all the strength had gone out of him at once. He did not open his eyes. His thin chest went up and down under the shabby coat, quick and shallow. His lips moved again, without sound. And this time Corin was close enough, and watching closely enough, to see the shape of the words.
Not yet. Please. Not yet. He's not ready.
Then the old man opened his eyes. And looked at Corin. And his face was calm again. Very calm. Very gentle. The teacher's face. The face of a man who has decided something, and is not going to change his mind.
"It was the Bank," he said. "Wasn't it. The ledgers. Somebody at the Bank has been reading the old ledgers."
Corin said nothing.
"You needn't tell me. I can see it." Linus Hale picked up his spectacles from the table and put them on again, slowly, over one ear and then the other. "There was a ledger. Yes. I won't lie to you about that. When the fever came, the village owed a great deal of money. To the Bank. For seed corn, and a new roof on the mill, and a loan for the church bell. When everybody died, the Bank called the debt. That's what they do. They wrote it in their book. At four in the morning, I expect, because that's when clerks write things, when nobody's looking." A small sad smile. "It's only a ledger, Corin. A line in a book. It doesn't mean anything. It doesn't mean what you're afraid it means."
His left thumb was moving again. Back and forth. Back and forth. Against the side of his first finger.
There, said Malakor. Again. Seed corn and a mill roof. He's making it up as he goes, boy. He always was good at that. The best liar I ever knew. And the kindest.
Corin looked at the old man for a long time.
He thought about telling him. About the empty debt column. The clean yellow paper between the red lines, where there should have been a sum, and was nothing. About the Collected by scratched out with a knife. About Do not reopen. By order. About the red seal, the dome with the rays.
He thought about telling him that Elara was alive.
That she sat at a glass desk at the top of the Bank on Lantern Quay, not a mile from where they were sitting now, with a gold cap on her front tooth over the old gap, and a little black book of all the days she had lost. That she saw his pool every night, and did not know why. That she had nothing before the Bank. That she was going to reopen the account.
He looked at Linus Hale's thumb, rubbing and rubbing against his finger.
And he understood something.
The old man was not lying to hurt him. He was not lying for himself. He was lying the way you put your hand over a child's eyes when something terrible is happening in the road. Don't look. Don't look. It's nothing. Look at me.
He was lying to keep Corin from going somewhere.
From opening a door.
Not yet. Please. Not yet. He's not ready.
Corin did not tell him about Elara.
He did not know why. Or he did. Because if Linus Hale was lying to protect him, then Linus Hale knew what there was to be protected from. And if he knew that, then he knew who. And if a man like that, a man who had come down the plank path to the arches in daylight with his stick, a man who had sat in this inn since half past two with the tea going stewed, if a man like that was this frightened—
Then Corin was not going to give him one more thing to be frightened of.
Not yet.
"All right," he said quietly. "A fever. And a fire. And grass."
The old man looked at him. His eyes searched Corin's face. Whatever they found there, it made them fill up again, slowly, behind the round gold rims.
"Yes," he said. Very softly. "Yes. That's all."
They sat in silence for a while. The fire burned down a little. The bald landlord came over without being asked and put another log on, and went away again. The two old men by the door had finished their dominoes and were putting the tiles back into their wooden box, one by one, very slowly. The woman in the red shawl had gone.
Linus Hale reached for the teapot again, with a hand that was not quite steady, and poured more stewed tea into both their cups. He did not put sugar in his own. He put two lumps in Corin's, and milk, without asking. As if it were the most natural thing in the world.
And as he pushed the cup back across the table, his eyes fell on Corin's coat.
On the right side. On the long narrow pocket. Where the end of the silver rod showed, just a little, a thin bright line above the edge of the cloth.
The old man's hand stopped on the cup.
He stared at the rod. And Corin saw his face change again. Not white, this time. Something else. Something that was not fear, and was not grief, and was not love, but had all three in it, tangled up together, like threads in a knot.
"Where," said Linus Hale, in a voice Corin had not heard him use before, "did you get that?"
