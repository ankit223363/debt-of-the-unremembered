Chapter 27: Linus Hale, Part 1 of 5: Tuesday
Tuesday
He woke with his face against brick.
For a moment he did not know where he was. There was a smell of dye and river mud and coal smoke. There was a deep slow thudding somewhere very close, like a heart, and a hiss like a kettle the size of a house. There was something rough and heavy over his shoulders, scratchy wool, and under his cheek the brick was warm. Warm the way bread is warm when it has just come out of the oven and been left on the side to cool.
Then he remembered.
The arches. The Dye-Works. The warm back wall. Tam.
He sat up slowly. Every part of him ached. His knees ached from the hours on the stone. His back ached. His right hand ached, the fingers stiff and curled, as if they were still holding the silver rod. And his head ached in a strange new way, a full, heavy, crowded ache, like a room with too much furniture in it. Tam's life was in there. All twenty-two years of it. It had settled in the night, the way silt settles in a pond, and it was quieter now. But it was there. He could feel it, if he turned his mind toward it, the way you can feel a stone in your shoe.
He had slept against the back wall of the third arch, where Tam had lain. Somebody had put a blanket over him. Somebody had put his gray coat under his head, folded, for a pillow. The mattress was gone. The cup of cold broth was gone. There was only the bare stone, and the warm pale brick of the wall, and on the floor beside him, neatly, as if someone had set it there on purpose, his Ledger.
He picked it up. He opened it at the first page without thinking.
READ THIS FIRST. Your name is Corin.
He read it all, the way he did every morning. Then he read it again. And the name settled into the front of his mind where it belonged, and Tam's name stayed where it was, further in, kept and quiet. He let out a long breath.
The light was gray at the front of the arch. Morning. Late morning, perhaps. The red glow from the Dye-Works windows was still there, but thinner now against the daylight, the way a lamp looks thin when somebody opens the shutters. He could hear voices from the brazier. Low and ordinary. A kettle. The clink of a tin mug. Somebody laughing, quickly, and then stopping, as if they had remembered they were not supposed to.
He got up and went out.
The tide had gone out again in the night. The strip of mud in front of the arches was wide and gray and shining, with puddles in it that reflected the white sky, and a line of gulls standing at the edge of the water, all facing the same way into the wind. The river was flat and brown. There was nothing on it where Tam had gone. Only a barge, far out, going downstream, with its sail furled and smoke going up from its little chimney.
Around the brazier, the people of the arches were having breakfast.
It was not much of a breakfast. A big black pot of something gray, on the coals, that might have been porridge or might have been soup. A heel of bread, passed from hand to hand, each person tearing off a piece. Tea in tin mugs, black and very strong. John Rennick was ladling the gray stuff out of the pot into bowls with a tin cup. Pegg was cutting the bread with a knife that was nearly all handle. The white-haired old woman was sitting in the same place as last night, wrapped in her two blankets, holding her mug in both hands and smiling at the gulls.
Tilly was sitting next to her. Humming. Very quietly. Three notes up and three notes down.
Juno was not there. Corin looked for her, and did not see her, and felt something go tight in his chest before Mother Grue saw him looking.
"Gone up to Tallow Street," she said, before he could ask. "To her gran's. At first light. She said to tell you she'd see you there, and if you weren't there by noon she'd come and drag you." Mother Grue held out a bowl of the gray stuff. "Eat that."
He ate it. It was porridge, mostly, with something salty in it that might have been fish. It was hot. It was the first thing he had eaten since Mrs. Hobb's porridge at Pruett's, a whole day ago, and he had not known how empty he was until it went down.
Mother Grue watched him eat with her small black eyes, the way a farmer's wife watches a calf to see if it will thrive.
"You talked in your sleep," she said.
He stopped eating.
"Not in his voice," she said. "In yours. You said rain a lot. And Carrow Street. And once you laughed. Out loud. Like a boy." She sniffed. "I don't think I've ever heard you laugh, Harvester. Not once."
He did not know what to say to that. He looked down at his bowl.
"It was the bed," he said at last. "His bed. Under the roof. I think I was in it."
Mother Grue nodded slowly, as if that made perfect sense.
"Then you keep it," she said. "You hear me? Whatever else you burn. You keep that."
"I will."
Pegg came over when he had finished eating. She had a needle in her hand, a big one, threaded with dark wool, and she held out her other hand without saying anything.
He did not understand at first. Then he did. He took off his gray coat and gave it to her. She turned it over in her red, rough washerwoman's hands, and found the collar, and felt along it with her fingers until she came to the three cut stitches where he had opened the little pocket in the night. She looked at him. He nodded. And she sat down on a box beside the brazier and began to sew it shut again.
She sewed very small and very neat, with her head bent close over the work. Her stitches went in and out of the gray wool like a little dark mouse running along a hedge. Corin watched her. He thought of Madame Hesper, in her shop on the Hill, with her pins in her mouth and her quick small hands, sewing that pocket for the first time, weeks ago, while she talked about pattern books and the sea. He thought of the list inside it. THOSE I OWE. And the new name at the bottom.
Pegg bit off the thread with her teeth. She smoothed the collar flat with her thumb. She held the coat out to him.
"There," she said. It was the first word he had ever heard her say. Her voice was hoarse, from all the crying. "Nobody'll know."
"Thank you."
She shook her head. She did not say anything else. But as she handed him the coat, her rough red fingers touched the back of his hand, just for a moment, very lightly. And then she went back to the pot, and the bread, and the tin mugs.
Corin put the coat on. He felt the collar against the back of his neck, and the small stiff square inside it. He felt the Ledger go into the inside pocket, over his heart, with Lyra's letter and the green card from the Fourth Desk and the curl of orange peel all folded safely between its pages. He felt the silver rod go into the long narrow pocket on the right.
Morning, boy, said Malakor. Very quietly.
Corin waited. But Malakor did not say anything else. He had not said anything else since the one word, last night, by the river. Linus. Corin could feel him there, at the edge of things, behind his left ear. Awake. Silent. Like a man sitting by a window, waiting for someone he has not seen for a very long time to come up the road.
Corin looked out at the river.
Today was Tuesday. This afternoon, from three, an old man with round spectacles and blue fingers would be sitting in an inn at the end of the Lantern Bridge, waiting for him. An old man who had been his teacher. Who had cut the spiral on the silver rod. Whose initials were in a dead doctor's book, beside the words female, aged about two years, Bright. Whose name Lord Thorne had said aloud, at sundown, after weeks of saying nothing at all.
Tell him I'm sorry it's taken so long.
Corin did not know if he wanted to go.
He knew he would.
He said goodbye to Mother Grue. She did not say goodbye back. She just looked at him with her sharp black eyes and said, "Eat something at dinner, too," and turned back to the brazier. He said goodbye to John Rennick, who took off his cap again. He looked for Gideon, and found him at the back of the third arch, sitting cross-legged on the bare stone where the mattress had been, with his thin blue hands on his knees, looking at the warm wall.
"I'm going," said Corin.
Gideon looked up at him. His face was the same as always. Pale and flat and empty. But there was something in his eyes that had not been there before. Something very small. Like a single coal in a cold grate.
"Will you come back?" said Gideon.
"Yes."
Gideon thought about that.
"I don't care," he said. "But all right."
Then Corin went up the plank path through the mud, carefully, stepping where Juno had stepped, away from the arches and the Dye-Works and the river. At the top of the slope he stopped and looked back. The arches were small from up here. The brazier was a tiny red eye at the edge of the gray mud. And beside it, very small, a girl in a too-big coat was standing up and waving.
Tilly.
He lifted his hand.
Then he turned and went up into the streets of the Lower Docks, toward Tallow Street, and home.
The Purple Door
Tallow Street ran downhill, as nearly every street in the Lower Docks did, toward the river.
Corin came at it from the top, from the corner by the chandler's, the way he always did. He had walked down it so many times in six years that his feet knew every crack in it without his eyes. The cobbles, black and worn and greasy, with a gutter down the middle where the rain ran in winter and the slops ran all year round. The tall thin houses on both sides, five floors high, leaning toward each other at the top as if they were sharing a secret, so that the strip of sky between them was hardly wider than a ribbon. The washing lines strung across from window to window, sagging under shirts and sheets and long gray underthings that never quite dried. The shops on the ground floors. The cobbler, with his window full of boots. The cookshop, with its smell of fried onions and boiled pudding that came out of the door like a warm hand and took you by the nose. The pawnbroker with the three brass balls. The little dark shop that sold everything and nothing, candles and string and matches and pins, where the old man behind the counter was always asleep.
It was the middle of the morning, and Tallow Street was busy.
Women were out on their steps, scrubbing, or talking, or shaking mats. Children were running in the gutter, chasing a hoop, shouting. A coal cart was stopped halfway down, with its horse dozing in the shafts and the coalman carrying sacks on his back down into a cellar, black from head to foot. A man was selling hot potatoes from a tin oven on wheels, with a little chimney on top puffing smoke. A dog was asleep in a doorway. Somewhere a baby was crying, and somewhere else a woman was singing, and somewhere else two men were arguing about a horse.
All ordinary. All exactly as it always was.
Corin stopped at the corner and did not go down.
He did not know why, at first. Then he did. It was the man on the step.
There was a step on the left side of the street, about a third of the way down, opposite No. 14. The front step of a house that had been empty for a year, since the family who lived there had gone to the Bank one morning and not come back the same. The windows were boarded. The door was nailed shut. Nobody ever sat on that step, because it was cold and in the shade, and because it was bad luck.
There was a man sitting on it now.
He was an ordinary-looking man. That was the first thing. Not tall. Not short. Not young. Not old. A brown coat, a cloth cap, a scarf wound round his neck. He had a newspaper open on his knees, and he was reading it, or seeming to. He looked like a clerk on his morning off. He looked like a hundred men you might pass on any street in the city and never see.
But he was sitting on the bad-luck step. In the cold. In the shade. Reading a newspaper that, Corin saw, when he narrowed his eyes, was folded open at the shipping notices. Nobody in the Lower Docks read the shipping notices on a Tuesday. The ships came in on Thursdays.
And every few moments, without moving his head, the man looked up over the top of the newspaper. At No. 14. At the front door. At the windows, one by one, up and up, all the way to the top, to the little window under the roof where Corin's room was.
Then down at his newspaper again.
Corin stood at the corner, behind the end of the chandler's wall, and watched him do it three times.
Kross's, said Malakor quietly. It was the first thing he had said all morning, since Morning, boy. One of his. Or one of the Lady's. Doesn't matter which. They're the same now, near enough.
He's watching my room.
He's watching your door, boy. Your door, and who goes in it, and who comes out. You heard the man in the glass room. Every door you knock on.
Corin looked at No. 14.
It was a tall thin house like all the others, five floors of dirty yellow brick, with a crooked chimney stack and a broken gutter that dripped all winter onto the step below. The front door stood open, the way it always did in the daytime, onto the dark narrow hall and the foot of the staircase that had been built by someone who hated people. He could not see in. But he knew what was there. The hall, with its smell of cabbage and wet coats. The stairs going up and up, steep and narrow and creaking, five floors, with a broken banister on the third. His own room at the top, under the slope of the roof, with its narrow bed and its one window and its table with the candle stub on it.
And at the back of the ground floor, past the foot of the stairs, down a short dark passage that smelled very strongly of onions, a door.
A purple door.
It was the only painted door in the whole house. Mrs. Marta had painted it herself, years ago, she said, with a pot of paint a sailor had given her instead of rent. It was a deep, rich, shining purple, like a plum, like a bruise, like the robes of a king in a picture book, and it was the brightest thing in No. 14 Tallow Street. You could see it glowing at the end of the dark passage from the foot of the stairs. Juno said it was the ugliest color in the world. Mrs. Marta said that was why she had painted it. So nobody can ever say they couldn't find me.
Corin thought of Mrs. Marta behind that door. Small and stout and fierce, with her small black currant eyes. Her kitchen full of steam and onions. Her account book on the table, where she wrote down the rent, every Friday, in a hand like barbed wire.
He thought of the man on the step, writing down every person who went in at the front door.
He did not go down Tallow Street.
He went round.
There was a way. There was always a way, in the Lower Docks. Behind the houses on the south side of Tallow Street, there was a long narrow alley, hardly wider than a man's shoulders, where the privies were and the ash pits and the coal holes. The backs of the houses opened onto it, through little yards and broken gates. Nobody went down it if they could help it. It smelled of everything a back alley can smell of, all at once. But it went right along behind No. 14, and No. 14 had a back door, into the scullery, behind Mrs. Marta's kitchen.
Corin went down the alley.
He went carefully, stepping over the puddles and the rubbish and the broken bottles. A rat ran across his foot. A cat looked down at him from the top of a wall with its tail twitching. Somewhere above him a window opened and a woman's arm came out and emptied a bucket into the alley a pace behind him, and the window shut again. He did not look up. He counted the back gates. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.
Fourteen.
The gate was hanging off one hinge, the way it always was. He lifted it and went through into the little yard. The yard was paved with broken bricks, and there was a water butt in the corner, green with moss, and a line of washing, Mrs. Marta's washing, sheets and aprons and a pair of large pink bloomers flapping in the cold wind. And a back door. Low and narrow, with the paint all peeled off it, and a horseshoe nailed over the top.
He knocked. Three times. Not slowly. Quickly. Knock fast, Moll always said. So they know it's a friend.
The door opened before he had finished the third knock.
Juno grabbed him by the front of the coat and pulled him inside.
"You're late," she said. "I said noon."
"It's not noon."
"It's nearly." She shut the door behind him and bolted it, top and bottom. "Did you see him? On the step?"
"I saw him."
"He's been there since nine." Juno's face was fierce. "Gran saw him. She went out and asked him if he wanted a cup of tea, since he was going to sit in the cold all day, and he said no thank you very politely and went on reading his paper. And then she came in and said a word I'm not allowed to say."
"I heard that," said a voice from the kitchen.
Mrs. Marta came out of the steam.
She was just as she always was. Small and stout and square, like a little barrel, in a black dress with a white apron over it and her sleeves rolled up to the elbow. Her gray hair was screwed up in a tight knot on top of her head, with a pencil stuck through it. Her face was red from the stove. And her small black currant eyes went over Corin from the top of his head to the soles of his boots, and back up again, and missed nothing.
"Well," said Mrs. Marta. "You look like death warmed up and left on the side."
"Good morning, Mrs. Marta."
"Is it?" She sniffed. "Sit down. No, not there, that's the cat's. There. And take that coat off, it stinks of smoke. I'm not having smoke in my kitchen. I've got a pie in."
He took his coat off. He sat down where she pointed, on a hard wooden chair at the end of her kitchen table. The kitchen was small and hot and very full. Full of steam from the kettle, and the smell of onions, and the warm heavy smell of something baking. There were pots on every shelf and bunches of herbs hanging from every hook and a calendar from the coal merchant on the wall with the days crossed off in pencil. Her account book lay open on the table beside him. He saw his own name in it, in her barbed-wire hand. Mr. Vane. Top room. Paid. And a date. Last Friday.
He did not remember paying.
He looked at the line for a long time.
"You paid," said Mrs. Marta, seeing where he was looking. "Don't worry. You came down on Friday night with your face like a ghost and put it on the table and went up again without a word. I wrote it in." She put a mug of tea down in front of him. "You never forget the rent, Mr. Vane. You forget everything else. But never the rent."
He wrapped his hands round the mug. It was very hot.
"Mrs. Marta," he said. "The man on the step."
"What about him?"
"He's watching the house. For me."
"I know that," said Mrs. Marta. "I'm not stupid." She sat down across the table from him, heavily, and folded her red arms on top of the account book. "And you're going to tell me you'd better go away, I suppose. For my sake. And Juno's. So they don't come knocking."
Corin looked at Juno. Juno looked back at him, with her arms folded, exactly like her grandmother.
"I told her," said Juno. "About the glass room. About the doors."
"And I'll tell you what I told her," said Mrs. Marta. "I've lived in this house thirty-four years. I've had the Watch knock on that door, and the bailiffs, and the Bank, and once a man with a knife who thought my Terrence owed him money, which he didn't. And I've opened it to every one of them, and looked them in the eye, and told them what I thought of them, and shut it again." She leaned forward. Her currant eyes were very bright. "Nobody's ever made me hide behind it. And no gray man on a step is going to start now."
"They're not the Watch," said Corin quietly. "Or the bailiffs."
"No," said Mrs. Marta. "I know what they are. I've heard the knocking too. Three, slow. Up and down this street, these last few weeks. I've heard it." She was quiet for a moment. "Old Wick heard it."
Nobody said anything. The kettle hissed on the stove.
"So drink your tea," said Mrs. Marta. "And eat some pie when it's out. And then go and do whatever it is you've got to do. And come back here tonight and sleep in your own bed, like a sensible person. And if any gray man knocks on my purple door, I'll deal with him."
Corin looked at her for a long time.
Then he drank his tea.
