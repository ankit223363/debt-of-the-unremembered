Chapter 27: Linus Hale, Part 4 of 5: The Spiral
The Spiral
Corin took the rod out of his pocket.
He did not mean to, quite. His hand did it, the way his hand did a great many things these days without asking him first. It went to the long narrow pocket on the right side of his coat and drew the rod out, slowly, and laid it on the little table between them. Beside the brown teapot with the chipped spout. Beside the two cups. Beside the old man's open book.
It lay there in the firelight. Thin and silver and very bright. About the length of a man's forearm. Its tip toward Linus Hale. And in the tip, cut fine and clean into the metal, the spiral, going round and round toward its middle. To the right. The way a clock turns.
The old man did not touch it.
He looked at it. Only looked. For a long, long time. The firelight moved on the silver, and on the round gold rims of his spectacles, and on the wet in his faded eyes. His blue hands lay on the open book in front of him, quite still. Even the thumb had stopped.
"It came with my desk," said Corin quietly. "In the Harvesting Section. At the Ministry. Six years ago, when they gave me the job. It was in the drawer. Nobody else wanted it. They said it was old, and the new rods were better, and it would be melted down soon anyway." He looked at it. "Nobody ever came to melt it."
"No," said Linus Hale. Very softly. "No. I don't suppose they would."
He lifted one hand, slowly. It shook. He held it over the rod, a finger's width above the silver, the way Elara had held her hand over the Ledger. He did not lower it.
"I cut this," he said.
Corin waited.
"Thirty-one years ago," said the old man. "In the winter. By the stove, in the back room of the schoolhouse, in the evenings, when the children had gone home." His hand moved, very slightly, above the silver, as if he were feeling the shape of the spiral without touching it. "It took me all winter. You have to cut it very slowly, with a very fine file, a hair at a time. If you go too fast the silver tears and you have to start again. I started again eleven times."
"For a friend," said Corin.
The old man looked up at him sharply.
"How do you know that?"
Corin did not answer. He did not know how to answer. He sat on the stool and looked at the old man across the rod, and behind his left ear he felt something he had never felt before. Not a voice. A kind of pressure. A kind of leaning. As if something inside the silver were pressing itself up against the inside of the metal, trying to get as close as it could to the old blue hand hanging over it. The way a dog presses its nose against a window when it hears its master's step in the road.
Tell him, said Malakor.
The voice was not dry. It was not mocking. It was not frightened. It was something Corin had never heard in it in six years. It was pleading.
Tell him, boy. Please. I can't. He can't hear me. He never could, not after. Tell him it's me.
Corin looked at the old man.
"His name was Malakor," he said.
Linus Hale made a sound. A small sound. Like a man who has been struck very hard, very quickly, in the place under the ribs where the breath is.
His hand came down.
Not onto the table. Onto the rod. His thin blue fingers closed round the silver, very gently, the way you close your hand round a bird that has fallen out of its nest. And held it.
And Corin felt the cold go out of the rod.
The deep cold. The cellar cold. The cold that had been in it every single day for six years, so constant that Corin had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing the sound of the river when you live beside it. It went. All at once. And the silver under the old man's fingers was just silver. Cool. Ordinary. Warming slowly in the heat of a living hand.
Linus Hale sat with the rod in his hand and his eyes shut, and his face was wet, and he did not make a sound.
Linus, said Malakor.
Just that. Very quietly. Behind Corin's ear. And Corin knew the old man could not hear it. He could see that he could not hear it. But something in the old man's face changed, very slightly, at that moment. The way a sleeping man's face changes when somebody says his name in another room.
"I always wondered," said Linus Hale at last, with his eyes still shut, "where he went."
His voice was very thin.
"They told me he was dead," he said. "Afterwards. They told me they found him in the river, below the old ferry, and that he'd gone in drunk in the night and drowned. They told me there was nothing left to harvest." His fingers moved on the silver, very slightly. "I never believed it. He never drank. Not a drop. He said it made him remember things he'd rather not." A long breath. "But I never knew. Not for certain. Not until now."
He opened his eyes. He looked at the rod in his hand.
"He's in there, isn't he," he said. "Somehow. What's left of him. I can feel it. Like the warm in a chair when somebody's just got up."
"Yes," said Corin.
"Does he talk to you?"
"Yes."
The old man nodded slowly. He did not seem surprised. He seemed, if anything, a little comforted.
"What does he say?"
Corin thought about it.
"Mostly he tells me I'm a fool," he said.
And Linus Hale laughed.
It was a cracked, wet, broken sound, and it turned into a cough halfway through, and he had to put the rod down on the table and press his blue hand against his chest until the coughing stopped. But it was a laugh. A real one. The two old men by the door looked round at it.
"Yes," said the old man, when he could speak. "Yes. That sounds like him." He wiped his eyes. "He used to tell me the same. Every evening. By the stove. While I sat filing his rod. You're a fool, Linus. Nobody cuts a spiral by hand anymore. You'll be at it till spring." A pause. "I was."
He looked down at the silver lying on the table between them.
"He wanted it to turn to the right," he said. "I asked him why. Most of the old harvesters didn't care, one way or the other. He said, So that it's always going forward, Linus. So that whatever I take, I take toward something. Not away." The old man was quiet. "He was a good harvester. The best I ever knew. He never took a thing from the dead that they didn't want to give. He always asked."
Corin thought of Tam, on the mattress, under six blankets. Take it. All of it.
"There's another one," he said.
The old man looked at him.
"Another rod," said Corin. "Like this one. Silver. Old. With a spiral at the tip." He watched the old man's face very carefully. "But it turns the other way. To the left."
The change in Linus Hale's face was terrible to see.
It was not white, this time. It was gray. Gray like ash. Gray like the face of a man who has been very ill for a very long time. All the wet and the warmth and the laughter went out of it at once, and it was only old. Very, very old.
"You've seen it," he whispered.
"In a dead man's Ash. Two nights ago. In a glass room at the top of a house." Corin did not look away. "In the hand of a man whose face I couldn't see."
The old man did not say anything for a long time.
Then he reached out, slowly, and picked up Malakor's rod again. He held it in both hands. Not the way you hold a bird, this time. The way you hold a stick when you hear something moving in the dark.
"I didn't cut that one," he said. "I want you to know that. I would never have cut that one. Not for all the silver in Veridia." His voice was very low. "He cut it himself. With his own hands. He was always very good with his hands."
"Who?"
The old man shook his head.
"He said a rod should turn the other way," he said, as if Corin had not spoken. "The way water goes down a drain. So that everything goes in, he said, and nothing ever comes back out." He looked at Corin. "Malakor heard him say it. They argued about it. Once. In my schoolroom. After the children had gone. I've never seen Malakor so angry. Or so frightened."
"Master Hale. Who is he?"
Linus Hale looked at him for a long time over the silver rod. His faded eyes were very steady now. Very clear. And Corin understood, looking into them, that the old man was not going to tell him. That he had never been going to tell him. That he had come to this inn and ordered this tea and waited since half past two in order to tell Corin a great many things, kind things, sad things, a fever and a fire and grass, and that this one thing was the one he had come here, above all, not to say.
"Give me your hand," said the old man.
Corin hesitated. Then he held it out across the table.
Linus Hale took it. His blue fingers were thin and dry and very cold. He turned Corin's hand over, gently, palm up. And he laid Malakor's rod across it. And closed Corin's fingers over the silver, one by one, with his own.
The cold came back into it at once. The deep cold. The cellar cold. Corin felt it go up his arm like water.
Thank you, boy, said Malakor. Very quietly. Thank you.
"Keep it close," said Linus Hale. "Keep him close. Never let anybody take it from you. Not the Ministry. Not the Bank. Not anybody. Do you understand?" His hand tightened on Corin's. "It's the only thing in Veridia that turns the right way. And one day you're going to need it to."
Crown Walk
Corin put the rod back in his pocket.
He did it slowly, and the old man watched him do it, every inch of the way, until the silver had slid down out of sight into the long narrow pocket and only the thin bright line of its end showed above the cloth. Then Linus Hale let out a breath and sat back on the settle and folded his blue hands on his book again.
The fire had burned down. The light in the room had changed. Through the thick green glass of the little windows the afternoon had gone a dim gold, and then a dim gray, and the shadows had come out of the corners and spread across the floor. The two old men had gone. The landlord had lit a lamp on the counter, a small oil lamp with a brass base, and its light lay in a little yellow pool on the dark wood and did not reach much further.
"Master Hale," said Corin.
"Yes?"
"There's something else I want to ask you."
The old man looked at him over the rims of his mended spectacles. Tired. Very tired. But still kind.
"I thought there might be," he said. "Ask it."
Corin took out his Ledger.
He did not open it at the first page. He opened it near the back, where he had copied things out in the last two weeks. He turned the pages carefully, past Tam's life and Tam's map and the long account of the glass room, past Elara's ledger and the empty debt column, past Hollis and the jars, back and back, until he found the page he wanted. A page he had written on Sunday, with a small black book open on his knees. A book with a single letter on its cover. W.
He turned the Ledger round on the table so that the old man could read it.
Linus Hale did not reach for it. He bent forward, slowly, and looked at the page through his spectacles. Corin watched his face.
The words were in Corin's own hand. Copied out exactly, every word, the way he had been taught to copy evidence at the Ministry.
From the black book marked W. Entry in the writer's hand:
Brought to me at Crown Walk by H.L. himself, at night, by the garden door. Female. Aged about two years. He says she was registered. He says she is Bright.
The old man read it. Corin saw his eyes go along the line once, slowly. Then again.
Then he sat back.
His face did not change. Not this time. It stayed exactly as it had been. Tired and kind and gentle. But his left thumb, on the book, had begun to move again. Back and forth. Back and forth. Against the side of his first finger.
"I don't know any Crown Walk," he said.
Corin said nothing.
"I've lived in the Ink Quarter for seventeen years," said Linus Hale. "In two rooms over a printer's on Ferrule Street. I teach a few children their letters, for pennies, when their mothers can pay. I mend books for the shops on Quill Row. I don't go up the Hill. I've never been up the Hill. I wouldn't know Crown Walk if I fell over it." His thumb moved. "There must be a great many men with the letters H.L. in this city, Corin. Hundreds. Henry Lyle. Hugh Lambert. Harriet Langley, for that matter."
Harriet Langley. Corin had met a Harriet Langley. At a party on the Hill, in a borrowed footman's coat, with Tam Hollin beside him laughing at Gorse. He's from the Langleys' country place, isn't that right, Vane?
How did a man who had never been up the Hill know Harriet Langley's name?
Corin did not say it. He looked at the old man's thumb.
"Lord Thorne spoke yesterday," he said.
The thumb stopped.
"Lord Aurelian Thorne," said Corin. "Who was burned, and hasn't said a word in weeks. Who taps on walls. Four, two, nine, three. Who has a word written on his wrist, very small, where nobody would see it. A word that's a lock on a door." He kept his eyes on the old man's face. "At sundown yesterday he stopped tapping. And he looked at his daughter. And he said one word, quite clearly. Twice, so she could be sure."
Linus Hale did not move.
"He said Linus," said Corin.
The old man's face broke.
That was the only word for it. It did not go white, or gray, or anything else. It simply broke, the way a sheet of ice on a puddle breaks when you step on it, all at once, into a hundred pieces. His mouth opened. His eyes filled. His blue hands came up off the book and went to his face and covered it, and he sat on the settle with his face in his hands and his thin shoulders shaking under the shabby coat.
"Aurelian," he said, through his fingers. "Oh, Aurelian. Oh, my dear."
Corin sat very still.
He had not expected this. He did not know what he had expected. Another lie, perhaps. Another kind story. He had not expected an old man weeping into his hands in the corner of an inn by the Lantern Bridge, saying a lord's first name as if it were the name of his own son.
He waited.
It took a long time. The landlord, behind his counter, did not look round. The gray cat on the hearth got up, and stretched, and came and sat on the stone by the old man's feet, and looked up at him with its yellow eyes. Outside, through the thick green glass, it was nearly dark.
At last Linus Hale took his hands away from his face. He found a handkerchief in his coat pocket, a large one, very clean, and wiped his eyes and his spectacles with it, slowly, and put it away.
"He spoke," he said. His voice was cracked and hoarse. "After all this time. He spoke."
"You know him."
The old man did not deny it. He seemed to have gone past denying things.
"I taught him," he said. "Before Oakhaven. Long before. When I was young, and he was a boy. Eight years old, on the Hill, in a house with forty rooms and nobody in any of them who had time to talk to him." A faint, wet smile. "His father hired me for a year, to teach him the old letters and sums. I taught him to tie a fishing fly instead. And to cut a quill. And to fold a paper so small that nobody would ever think to look inside it." He shook his head slowly. "His father dismissed me at the end of the year. But Aurelian never forgot. He wrote to me. All his life. Even when he was a lord, with a seat on the Council. Even when—"
He stopped.
"Even when what?" said Corin.
"Even when it was dangerous to," said Linus Hale quietly.
He looked at the Ledger, still lying open on the table between them. At the line in Corin's hand. Brought to me at Crown Walk by H.L. himself, at night, by the garden door.
"Corin," he said. "Listen to me now. Very carefully. Because I am only going to say this once, and then I am going to stop, and you must not ask me any more."
Corin waited.
"There were children," said the old man, "in the old days, who were born Bright. You know what that means. You've seen it, I think. In the glass. Children who touch a reading glass and make it blaze. Who have something in them, in their blood, that the Ministry wants very badly." His voice was very low. "The Ministry kept a register. Of Bright children. In every village, every town, every street. And when a child was on that register, sooner or later, men came for it. To take it into the Ministry's keeping. For its own good, they said."
He looked at Corin over his spectacles.
"Some of us," he said, "did not believe it was for their own good."
The fire cracked.
"Some of us helped," said Linus Hale, "when we could. A very few children. Not many. Never enough. We took them away, at night, before the men came, and hid them. In houses that nobody would ever search. With people who would keep them safe and never tell." He was quiet. "One of those houses was on the Hill. Behind a garden door. And the man who lived in it was a boy I had once taught to fold a paper very small."
Corin did not breathe.
"That is all I will tell you," said the old man. "Do you understand? That is all. I will not tell you her name. I will not tell you where she is now. I will not tell you whether she lived. Not because I don't trust you, Corin. Because I do. Because I love you, I have always loved you, from the first day you stood on my schoolroom step with your knees all scraped and your sister holding onto the back of your shirt." His voice shook. "And because every name you know is a name that can be taken out of you. By a man with a rod that turns the wrong way."
He reached across the table and closed the Ledger. Gently. With one blue hand.
"The less you know," he said, "the fewer doors he can open."
Corin looked down at the closed Ledger under the old man's hand.
He thought of all the things in it. Lyra and Oren and Juno and Vespera and Mother Grue and Hessa and Tam. Every name. Every door. Written down so carefully, so that he would not lose them.
Every door he knocks on is a door we didn't know about.
"He already knows the names," he said quietly. "Kross has them. All written down. In a list."
"Then don't give him any more," said Linus Hale.
The Book on the Table
It was dark outside now. Properly dark. The green glass of the windows had gone black, and in the black, very faint, there were small gold points of light, wavering, one after another, in a long line.
The lamplighter was out on the bridge.
Corin could not see him. But he could see the lamps coming on, one by one, along the dark. Each one a tiny gold flame behind the thick green glass, like a coin dropped into deep water. The first. Then the next. Then the next. Slowly. Steadily. Going away across the river toward the far bank.
Linus Hale was watching them too.
"Every night," he said quietly. "For four hundred years. Somebody walks across with a pole and lights them. Even in the bad years. Even in the war." He was quiet for a moment. "Even when there was no oil, they say, in the winter of the siege, the lamplighters lit them with fat. Mutton fat. And the whole bridge smelled of cooking for a month." The faint wet smile. "I've always liked that story. I don't know if it's true."
"What war?" said Corin.
The old man did not answer. He went on looking at the lamps.
Then he looked down at the book on the table in front of him. The one he had been reading when Corin came in. It lay open, face down, with its spine cracked. He had kept his blue hands on it the whole afternoon, Corin realized. Folded on it. Resting on it. As if he were keeping it warm.
He picked it up. He closed it. And he held it out across the table.
"This is yours," he said.
Corin looked at it.
It was a small book. Thin. Bound in cloth that had been red once and had faded to the color of a dried rose petal. The corners were worn right through, so that the gray board showed. The spine had been mended with a strip of brown paper, and the brown paper had been mended with thread. On the front, stamped into the cloth in letters that had once been gold and were now only a faint dent, it said:
A FIRST READER. FOR THE YOUNGER CLASSES.
Corin took it.
It weighed almost nothing. He turned it over in his hands. He did not open it. He was afraid to.
"Open it," said Linus Hale.
He opened it.
The pages were soft and yellow and spotted brown with age, and they smelled of dust and damp and, very faintly, of something sweet. Woodsmoke. Apples. The first page was blank, except for the printer's name at the bottom in tiny letters. But on the inside of the front cover, in pencil, in big round wobbling letters that went uphill and then downhill again, the way a child's letters go when he has only just learned how to make them, somebody had written:
THIS BOOK IS CORIN VANE'S.
OAKHAVEN SCOOL.
KEEP OUT.
And under that, in a different pencil, there were two more words. They were huge and lopsided, each letter a different size, as if a very small hand had held the pencil and a bigger hand had held the small one and steered it, and they went right off the edge of the page because whoever had been steering had not left enough room:
AND ELARA
Corin sat very still.
He looked at the big round wobbling letters for a long time. THIS BOOK IS CORIN VANE'S. He did not remember writing them. He did not remember the book. He did not remember the school, or the pencil, or the hand that had held it.
But it was his hand.
He knew it the way you know your own face in a mirror. Under the wobble and the roundness, under the uphill and downhill, it was the same hand that wrote in his Ledger every night. The same hard slope to the right. The same way of pressing so deep that the pencil had dented the board.
And SCOOL. With no H.
He had spelled it wrong.
He did not know why that should be the thing that broke him. But it was. He sat on the stool by the fire in the Lamplighter's Rest with the little faded book open in his hands and he looked at that word, SCOOL, in his own nine-year-old pencil, and something came up in his throat that he could not swallow, and his eyes went hot and blind.
And then, under it. AND ELARA. Going off the edge of the page because she had not left room.
He heard her. Just for a moment. Not a memory. Not a picture. Just a sound, very small, from very far away, through a door that was shut and locked. A little girl's voice, outraged. It's MY book TOO, Cor.
And then it was gone.
He shut the book.
He did not know how long he sat there with it shut in his hands and his eyes shut too. When he opened them, Linus Hale was watching him across the little table, with his own eyes wet and his blue hands folded in his lap.
"You left it in your desk," said the old man softly. "The night before. I found it in the morning. I've had it ever since." He looked at the book. "I couldn't throw it away. And I couldn't give it back. Not until I knew you were—" He stopped. "Not until I knew you were ready to have it."
"The night before what?" said Corin.
The old man did not answer that either.
He reached for his walking stick, which was leaning against the end of the settle, and he got up. It took a long time. He had to push himself up with one hand on the table and one on the stick, and Corin saw his knees shake. But he stood. And he buttoned his shabby coat, carefully, all the way up, the button with the wrong-colored thread last of all.
"I have to go," he said. "I've been here too long. Longer than I meant."
Corin stood up too.
"Will I see you again?"
Linus Hale looked at him.
"I hope so," he said. "I hope so very much." A pause. "But not for a while, I think. Don't come looking for me, Corin. Not at the printer's on Ferrule Street. Not anywhere. I won't be there."
"Where will you be?"
"Somewhere else." The faint smile. "I've been somewhere else a great deal, these eighteen years. I'm good at it."
He came round the end of the little table, leaning on his stick. He stood in front of Corin. He was so much shorter that he had to tilt his head right back to look up at him, and the firelight caught his round gold spectacles and turned them for a moment into two small circles of flame.
"Corin," he said. "One more thing. And then I'll go."
"Yes?"
"Don't go home tonight."
Corin looked at him.
"Not to Tallow Street," said the old man. "Not to your room at the top of the house with the purple door. Not tonight, and not tomorrow night either. Go anywhere else. Go to the ink woman on Hook Lane. Go back to the old lady under the Dye-Works. Sleep on a barge. Sleep in a ditch. But don't go home."
"How do you know about Tallow Street?"
"I know a great many things about you," said Linus Hale. "I've made it my business to. For six years. Since they let you out of the long white room with forty beds, and I came to see you there, and you looked straight at me and didn't know who I was." His voice shook. "I've watched you, Corin. From a long way off. I've seen you go in at the side door of the Ministry every morning, with your shoulders up round your ears. I've seen you come out at night with your face like paper. I've sat on the step of the church opposite Tallow Street, in the rain, and watched your candle go out in that little window at the top."
He put out his free hand and laid it on Corin's arm. Very lightly.
"And this morning," he said, "early, I saw a man sit down on the step of the empty house opposite. With a newspaper. And I knew it was time."
Corin did not say anything. He could not.
The old man looked up at him for a moment longer. Then he did something Corin did not expect.
He let go of his stick. It fell against the settle with a small wooden clatter. And he put both his arms round Corin, and held him.
It was a thin, shaking, old man's embrace. He came up no higher than Corin's chest, and his arms were like dry sticks, and he smelled of old paper and ink and woodsmoke and the stewed tea. He held on very tight. Tighter than Corin would have thought he had the strength for. He pressed his face against the front of Corin's coat, over his heart, over the Ledger and the faded word beneath it, and his thin shoulders shook.
Corin did not know what to do with his hands. Then he did. He put his arms round the old man, carefully, the way you would hold something that might break. And he held on too.
He felt the old man's hands move on his back. On his coat. Slowly. Patting. The way you pat a child who has woken in the night from a bad dream. Up and down, between his shoulder blades. And then lower. At his side. At the pocket of his coat. The left side. The ordinary pocket, the one where he kept his stub of pencil and his knife and Mrs. Marta's slice of pie in its paper bag.
The hand rested there for a moment. Patting. Then it moved away.
"There," whispered Linus Hale, against his coat. "There. That's all. That's all."
He let go.
He stepped back. He bent, slowly, with one hand on the settle, and picked up his stick. He straightened. He took off his spectacles and wiped them, one more time, on his handkerchief, and put them back on, over one ear and then the other.
"Goodbye, Corin," he said.
And he turned, and walked away across the stone floor, through the sawdust, leaning on his stick, past the empty domino table and the lamp on the counter and the bald landlord, who did not look up. He opened the door. The cold night air came in, and the smell of the river, and the far faint sound of the lamplighter's boots on the bridge.
The old man did not look back.
The door closed behind him.
Corin stood by the fire with the little faded red book in his hands, and the gray cat looking up at him from the hearth with its yellow eyes, and behind his left ear, very quietly, so quietly that it was hardly a sound at all, Malakor was weeping.
