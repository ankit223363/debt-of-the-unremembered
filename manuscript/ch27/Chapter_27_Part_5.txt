Chapter 27: Linus Hale, Part 5 of 5: The Oak Leaf
The Oak Leaf
He did not follow the old man.
He wanted to. He stood by the dying fire with the little red book in his hands and every part of him wanted to run to the door and out into the dark and after the thin bent figure with the stick. To catch his arm. To make him turn round. To shout, What night? The night before what? Who is he? Where is she?
He did not.
He stood by the fire and counted to a hundred, slowly, the way Juno had taught him to do when he was being followed and wanted to give the follower time to get bored. He did not know why he did it. He was not the one being followed. Or he was, but not by the old man. He counted to a hundred so that whoever might be watching the door of the Lamplighter's Rest would see the old man come out alone, and walk away alone, and would not see Corin hurrying after him.
Not yet, the old man had said. He's not ready.
Corin counted to a hundred. Then he counted to a hundred again.
Then he put the little red book inside his coat, inside the Ledger, between the pages, next to Lyra's letter and the green card and the curl of orange peel. It fitted, just. He felt the Ledger close over it, thicker now, heavier, a little warm against his chest.
The bald landlord was looking at him from behind the counter.
"He paid," said the landlord. It was the first thing he had said. His voice was deep and slow, like Wat's. "For the tea. And for the room upstairs, for tonight, if you want it." He jerked his head toward a narrow stair in the corner. "He said you might. He said you'd need somewhere to sleep that wasn't home."
Corin looked at the stair.
"Thank you," he said. "I'll think about it."
The landlord shrugged, and went back to polishing his pewter.
Corin went out.
The cold hit him like a slap. After the warm low room with its fire, the night air off the river was sharp and wet and bitter, and it went straight through his coat and his borrowed shirt to the skin. He turned his collar up. He felt, against the back of his neck, the small stiff square of the pocket Pegg had sewn shut.
Ferrule Street was empty. The shops had shut. Their windows were dark, or shuttered, or showed only a single candle somewhere at the back. The cobbles shone wet in the light of the one gemstone lamp at the corner, cold and blue. There was nobody on the pavement. Nobody in the doorways. He looked both ways, slowly, the way Juno had taught him. Nobody.
The old man had gone.
Corin walked down to the end of Ferrule Street, to where it came out onto the embankment at the Ink Quarter end of the Lantern Bridge, and stopped by the first lamppost.
The bridge was lit.
All of it. From end to end. The lamplighter had finished his work. Every lamp on every post along both walls was burning, gold and warm, behind its glass, in its black iron cage. Two long lines of little flames going away from him across the river in the dark, getting smaller and closer together as they went, until at the far end they ran together into a single thread of gold. And under them, in the black water, the reflections. Two more lines of little flames, upside down, wavering, breaking into pieces and coming together again as the current moved under the arches.
It was the most beautiful thing Corin had ever seen. Or the most beautiful thing he could remember seeing. He did not know if those were the same.
He stood and looked at it for a long time.
The lamplighter was coming back. Corin could see him, a long way off, on the far side of the bridge, a small dark figure with a long pole over his shoulder, walking slowly back along the pavement toward the Ink Quarter end. He walked like a man who had walked the same way every night for many years and had no need to hurry. As he passed each lamp, he looked up at it. Just for a moment. As if he were checking that it was still there.
Corin waited for him.
It took a long time. The lamplighter was old, and the bridge was long. But at last he came up off the bridge onto the embankment, by the first post, and stopped, and looked at Corin. He was a small man, wrinkled and brown, in a long black oilskin coat that came down to his boots and a black cap with a shiny peak. His pole was taller than he was, with a little brass hook at the top and a little brass cup beside it with a wick in it, still burning, a tiny flame no bigger than a fingertip.
"Evening," said the lamplighter.
"Good evening."
"Cold one."
"Yes."
The lamplighter looked up at the first lamp, over their heads, in its black cage above the plaque with the smooth place in it. He looked at it for a moment, the way he had looked at all the others. Then he looked at Corin.
"You've been reading the stone," he said.
Corin did not ask how he knew. He thought perhaps everybody who stood by this post for very long ended up reading the stone.
"Built in the reign of," he said.
"Built in the reign of," agreed the lamplighter. He nodded slowly. "My father used to say there was a name there, when his father was a boy. He said his father could remember it. Used to say it, sometimes. In his sleep." He shrugged. "Then one year they came and chiseled it out. Ministry men. In the night. And the next morning my grandfather couldn't remember it anymore, and nor could anybody else." He shifted the pole on his shoulder. "Funny thing. He said it was like a word on the tip of your tongue. All the rest of his life. He'd stand here, by this post, and open his mouth, and nothing would come."
"Do you remember?"
The lamplighter looked at him.
"I light the lamps," he said. "That's all I do. I light them every night, and I put them out every morning, and in between I mind my own business." He looked up at the first lamp again. "But I'll tell you something, since you're standing here in the cold reading stones. This lamp. The first one. It's never once gone out. Not in my time. Not in my father's. The wind can blow, the rain can come down sideways off the river, every other lamp on the bridge can gutter and die. This one stays lit."
He touched the brim of his cap.
"Goodnight," he said. And he walked away up Ferrule Street with his pole on his shoulder and the little flame bobbing at the top of it, and turned the corner by the Lamplighter's Rest, and was gone.
Corin looked up at the first lamp.
It burned steadily in its black iron cage. Gold and warm. While the others along the bridge flickered and swayed a little in the wind off the river, this one did not. It burned quite still. Like a candle in a room with no draught.
He put his hands in his coat pockets, against the cold.
And his left hand found the pie.
Mrs. Marta's pie, in its paper bag. Mutton and onion. He had forgotten it. He took it out, and the paper was a little greasy, and the smell of it came up into the cold air, warm and brown, and his stomach turned over, because he had not eaten since Mrs. Marta's kitchen and that seemed a very long time ago.
And as he took the bag out of his pocket, something else came out with it.
It fell. He heard it. A small hard sound, metal on stone, at his feet, by the base of the lamppost. A sound like a coin dropped on a step.
He bent down.
It was lying on the wet flagstones of the pavement, in the gold light from the lamp. Small and dark. He picked it up.
It was a key.
An old key. Iron, gone dark with age, almost black, but smooth and shiny in places, the way iron gets when it has been carried in a pocket and rubbed by fingers for a great many years. It was about as long as his finger. The shank was round and thin, and at the end of it the bit was cut into three small square teeth, plain and blunt, the way old keys were cut before anybody thought to make locks clever. It was heavier than it looked. It was cold.
And the bow was a leaf.
Not a ring, the way most keys had. Not a loop to put a string through. A leaf. Flat, and broad, and cut out of the iron so carefully that it looked as if it had grown there. With a long stem where it joined the shank, and a rounded edge that went in and out, in and out, in deep soft curves, all the way round. And down the middle, a vein, cut fine into the metal, with smaller veins going off from it on each side, like the bones of a tiny wing.
An oak leaf.
Corin stood under the first lamp of the Lantern Bridge with the key in one hand and Mrs. Marta's pie in the other, and he did not move for a long time.
He had not put it there.
He knew every single thing in his left pocket. His stub of pencil. His knife. A bit of string. Two coppers. The pie. He had put the pie there himself, in Mrs. Marta's kitchen, at two o'clock, with her watching. There had been no key.
He thought of the old man's arms round him, by the fire. The thin shaking hands on his back. Patting. Up and down. Between his shoulder blades. And then lower. At his side. At his pocket. There. That's all. That's all.
He turned the key over in his fingers.
An oak leaf.
He thought of the height chart on the nursery wall, at Crown Walk. The third set of marks. The gouged-out name. And under the gouge, when he rubbed it with his pencil, a letter, and a tiny drawn leaf. W. And an oak leaf.
He thought of a dried leaf, pressed flat, in a book. She took this from the garden at Crown Walk on the morning she went.
He thought of three brass buttons in a tin, in a clock-mender's shop. With oak leaves on them.
He thought of the button woman under the bridge. A cart, twice a year, from the village with the big oak. Up past the end of the maps.
He thought of Briggs's three gold glasses behind the grille. A bell. A field. A great old spreading tree, with a rope swing on one branch, and leaves the shape of—
So many oak leaves, said Malakor, very quietly. His voice was rough. As if he had been weeping, and was not anymore, and did not want Corin to mention it. Have you noticed, boy? Once you start looking, they're everywhere. Like the smooth places. Only the other way round.
What does it open? Corin thought.
I don't know.
You knew the schoolhouse.
The schoolhouse didn't have a lock. A pause. Nobody in Oakhaven had locks. That was the joke. That was what Linus used to say, when he first came up from the city. A village with no locks. He said it was the first place he'd ever lived where he could sleep at night.
Corin looked at the key.
It had been made for a lock. Somebody had made it very carefully, by hand, with great love, the way Linus Hale had cut the spiral on Malakor's rod, a hair at a time, all winter, by the stove. Somebody had made a lock somewhere, for this key to open. And had made the key in the shape of an oak leaf, so that whoever held it would know.
Know what?
He did not know.
He closed his fingers round the key. He held it very tight, so tight that the edges of the leaf pressed into his palm and left a mark. Then he opened his Ledger, under the first lamp of the Lantern Bridge, and he wrote, in the gold light, as small and as fast as he could:
Tuesday. Lamplighter's Rest. Linus Hale. My teacher. Oakhaven: "a fever, a fire, and grass." LIES. Every word. Malakor says so (the thumb). Elara did NOT die. I know she didn't.
He cut Malakor's rod. 31 yrs ago. By the schoolhouse stove. He knew the LEFT one. "He cut it himself. So that everything goes in and nothing comes back out." Wouldn't say who.
H.L. = him. He hid Bright children. One at Crown Walk, behind a garden door. Aurelian was his pupil. "Oh my dear."
My first reader. THIS BOOK IS CORIN VANE'S. OAKHAVEN SCOOL. AND ELARA. He's kept it 18 yrs. "You left it in your desk the night before." Before WHAT?
"Don't go home tonight."
A KEY in my left pocket. He put it there. When he held me. Iron. Old. 3 teeth. The bow is an OAK LEAF.
He stopped. He looked at what he had written.
Then he put the key inside the Ledger too, between the pages, at the page where he had written about it, and closed the book over it, and put the book back over his heart.
And then, because he was very cold, and very tired, and had not eaten for seven hours, he stood under the lamp that never went out and ate Mrs. Marta's pie out of its greasy paper bag, all of it, every crumb, with the river running black and gold under the bridge and the wind coming off the water like a knife.
It was the best pie he had ever eaten.
When he had finished, he folded up the paper bag, very small, and put it in his pocket. And realized what he had done. And took it out again, and looked at it, a tiny folded square of greasy paper in his palm, in the gold light.
And threw it in the river.
The Gray Coat on Quill Row
He saw the man as he came off the bridge.
Not at once. At first he only felt it. A small cold prickle at the back of his neck, under the collar, under the little square pocket that Pegg had sewn. The feeling you get in an empty room when you are suddenly sure that the room is not empty. He did not turn round. Juno had taught him that, on the roofs. Never turn round. Turning round tells them you know. Look in something.
So he looked in something.
There was a shop on the corner of Ferrule Street, a print-seller's, shut for the night, with a big window of black glass. And in the black glass, as he walked past it, Corin saw the street behind him, dim and blue in the light of the gemstone lamp at the corner. The wet cobbles. The dark doorways. The end of the Lantern Bridge, with its first lamp burning steady and gold.
And a man.
Standing by the wall of the embankment, a little way along from the first lamppost, in the shadow between two pools of light. Not doing anything. Just standing. In a long coat. A gray one. With his hands in his pockets and his hat pulled down, looking out at the river, the way a man looks at a river when he has nothing better to do.
Corin walked on.
He did not hurry. He walked the way he had walked away from the Patient Grace on Monday morning, slowly, in plain sight, like a man going home. Up Ferrule Street. Past the Lamplighter's Rest, where a light was burning now in the window of the little room upstairs that Linus Hale had paid for, and nobody was in it. Round the corner, into the Ink Quarter.
At the next window, a stationer's, he looked again.
The man was there. A long way back. At the corner by the inn, under the creaking sign of the lamplighter with his flame. Not looking at Corin. Looking at the sign. As if he were admiring the painting.
He's good, said Malakor quietly. Better than the one on the step. That one was a clerk. This one's done it before.
Kross's?
Who else? He's walking ahead of you, the Auditor said. Looking back now and then to see you're still coming. A pause. This is him looking back.
Corin walked on into the Ink Quarter.
It was a part of the city he knew well. He had walked through it every day for six years, on his way to the Ministry and home again. Narrow streets, very old, with the upper floors of the houses jutting out over the cobbles so far that in some places you could have reached out of one window and shaken hands with somebody in the window opposite. And every house a shop, or a workshop, or both. Printers and bookbinders and paper-makers and pen-cutters and ink-boilers and sellers of old books and new books and maps and prints and sealing wax and string. By day the whole quarter smelled of ink, sharp and black and oily, and of hot glue and wet paper and the strange dusty sweetness of old books, and you could hear the presses thumping away in the cellars like the hearts of great slow animals.
At night it was quiet. The presses had stopped. The shops were shuttered. The streets were dark between the lamps, and the old houses leaned over them, black against the sky, with here and there a single lit window high up, where somebody was working late.
Corin knew every turning. He did not take the way home.
He went left, down a lane so narrow he had to turn sideways to pass a rain barrel. Right, through an archway into a little court where three printers shared a yard and the ground was sticky with spilled ink. Through a gap in a fence that he and Toby had found once, chasing a pickpocket, years ago. Along a passage behind a row of bookbinders' shops, where the smell of hot glue came out of every back door. Up a flight of iron stairs on the outside of a paper warehouse. Across a flat roof. Down a ladder on the other side.
At the bottom of the ladder he stood very still in the shadow of the warehouse wall and waited, and counted to two hundred, and listened.
Nothing. Water dripping from a gutter. A cat, somewhere, complaining. Far off, the bells of a church telling ten o'clock. Nothing else.
Gone? he thought.
For now, said Malakor. Or he's cleverer than both of us. Either way, you won't see him again tonight. Not if he doesn't want you to.
Corin came out of the shadow and walked on.
He was in Quill Row now. He knew it at once, though he had come into it from an end he never used. The pen shops. The little narrow fronts, each one with its sign hanging over the street on an iron bracket, and every sign shaped like a quill. Big ones and small ones, gold and black and silver and red. They creaked in the wind as he walked under them, a long row of dark feathers swinging over his head in the night.
Halfway along there was a shop he knew very well. A narrow front, with green shutters and a bow window of little square panes, and a sign over it shaped like a quill with a drop of ink falling from its tip. Bram's. Where the glue pot went blup in the back room, and Tam had sat with him drinking tea, on Thursday night, a hundred years ago. Where, under a loose board, six old Ledgers lay in their tide-table coats, waiting. The six books that a Colonel in an iron-colored coat wanted so badly.
The shutters were closed. There was no light.
Corin did not stop. He did not even look at it twice. He walked past it as if it were any other shop on Quill Row, and he felt, behind his ribs, how much he wanted to stop, and knock, and see if old Bram was all right.
Every door you knock on, said Malakor softly.
I know.
He walked on. To the end of Quill Row. Round the corner, into Mercer Street.
And stopped.
Mercer Street was short and crooked and very dark. There was only one lamp, at the far end, and its light did not reach this far. The houses were tall and narrow and old, like all the houses in the Ink Quarter, and most of them were shops on the ground floor, shuttered for the night. One of them was a bookbinder's. Corin could just make out the sign over its door, painted in faded gold on a black board. J. AMBROSE. BOOKBINDER. REPAIRS UNDERTAKEN. And above the shop, three floors up, under the slope of the roof, there was a small window.
There was a light in it.
A candle. Very small. Very low. Set on the sill, just inside the glass, so that from the street it was only a tiny yellow point in the dark, like a star seen through cloud.
Corin stood at the corner of Mercer Street and looked up at it.
He had been in that attic once. Only once. On a wet night, with the rain on the window, sitting on a hard chair beside a narrow bed piled with black books. Two thousand one hundred and six of them, she had told him. Two thousand one hundred and six names. All the people she had ever been sent to knock for, written down, so that somebody would remember them.
He had made her a promise that night.
Tell me first. When you know. Before anyone. Me first, and then Lyra.
He had written it in the Ledger. W. / Wren? With the question mark, because even then, even sitting in her attic with her black books all round him, he had not been sure whose side she was on. He was still not sure.
He stood at the corner and looked at the candle and did not move.
And somebody walked into him.
It was not hard. A shoulder, brushing his. A figure coming round the corner from Quill Row, quickly, with its head down, the way people walk at night in a cold wind. A gray cloak, with the hood up. Not a coat. A cloak. Long and plain and the color of rain. It bumped him, lightly, as it passed. And a voice said, low and quick, from inside the hood, Sorry, sir, sorry, the way anybody would.
And something was in his hand.
He had not felt it go there. One moment his hand was empty, hanging at his side. The next moment there was a scrap of paper in it, folded small and pressed into his palm.
The gray cloak went on. Down Mercer Street, quickly, into the dark. It did not stop at the bookbinder's door. It went past, and on, to the far end, under the one lamp, and round the corner, and was gone.
Corin stood very still.
Then he opened his hand.
The paper was thin and cheap, torn from the corner of something. Folded twice. He unfolded it. It was too dark to read. He moved to the edge of the faint light that came from the end of the street and held it up, close to his eyes.
The writing was small and neat and quick, and very upright, and he knew it. He had seen it once before, on the spine of a black book, in an attic, on a wet night.
Not home. Not tonight. They're coming for you there.
Come up. Back stair, through the yard. Knock twice.
You promised. Me first.
— W.
Corin read it three times.
Then he looked up at the little window under the roof of J. Ambrose, Bookbinder. At the tiny candle on the sill.
As he watched, the candle moved. Somebody inside had picked it up. He saw it go away from the window, a small gold light moving through the dark of the attic. And then, for a moment, against the glow of it, the shape of a head. Hair pulled back tight. A pale face, turned toward the window. Looking down into Mercer Street.
Looking at him.
Then the light went out.
Corin folded the scrap of paper and put it in his Ledger, over his heart, with all the other things. Lyra's letter. Elara's card. The orange peel. His first reader. The key with the oak-leaf bow.
Everybody's telling you not to go home tonight, said Malakor quietly. Have you noticed?
I've noticed.
Linus. And now her. A pause. Do you trust her, boy?
Corin looked at the dark window for a long time.
I don't know, he thought.
Then why are you going?
He did not answer. He did not need to. He was already walking. Not to the front of J. Ambrose, Bookbinder, with its gold sign and its shuttered door. To the side. To the narrow black gap between the bookbinder's and the house next door, where a gate stood half open onto a little yard, and a wooden stair went up the back wall of the house, steep and dark, toward the roof.
He put his hand on the rail.
It was wet and cold, and it shook a little under his weight, the way old stairs do.
He began to climb.
Behind him, a long way off, at the far end of the Ink Quarter, the bells of the church by the Lantern Bridge began to ring the quarter past ten. Slowly. One stroke, and then another, and then another, falling into the cold dark like stones into deep water.
And across the city, on Tallow Street, in a tall thin house with a crooked chimney and a broken gutter, at the end of a dark passage that smelled of onions, Mrs. Marta was sitting at her kitchen table with her account book open and her pencil in her hand and her purple door bolted top and bottom.
And on the bad-luck step across the street, in the cold and the shade, the man with the newspaper folded it up, very neatly, in half, and in half again. And stood up. And walked away up Tallow Street with his hands in his pockets, not hurrying, as if whatever he had been sent to wait for all day had finally happened.
As if he would not need to sit there anymore.
As if somebody, somewhere, already knew exactly which door to knock on tomorrow.
