Chapter 27: Linus Hale, Part 2 of 5: The Lantern Bridge
The Lantern Bridge
He left No. 14 the way he had come, by the back door and the alley, at a little after two.
Mrs. Marta had fed him pie. Mutton and onion, in a crust so thick and crumbly that it fell to pieces when you looked at it, with gravy that ran out across the plate and soaked into the bread. He had eaten two slices. She had stood over him while he ate, with her arms folded, the way Mother Grue had stood over him that morning, and when he had finished she had put a third slice in a paper bag and pushed it into his coat pocket and said, For later. Don't argue.
Juno had wanted to come with him.
"No," Corin had said.
"I'll stay outside. I'll stay on the roof. He won't even see me."
"He's my teacher, Juno. Or he says he is. I want to see him on my own." He had looked at her. "And I want you here. With your gran. With the door bolted. In case the man on the step gets tired of his newspaper."
Juno had looked at him for a long time with her fierce wet eyes. Then she had said, "Three hours. If you're not back in three hours, I'm coming," and turned away to the stove, and would not look at him again until he had gone.
He came out of the alley at the far end, two streets away from Tallow Street, and walked north.
It was a cold bright afternoon. The sky was very high and very pale, a thin washed blue, with long streaks of white cloud going across it like the marks of a brush. There was a hard little wind off the river, sharp as a knife, that found every gap in his coat. But the sun was out. Low and white and without much warmth in it, the way the sun is in winter. It lay along the tops of the houses like a strip of gold leaf and left the streets below in blue shadow.
He went by the back ways. Not because he thought anyone was following him. He had looked, three times, the way Juno had taught him, in shop windows and round corners and in the brass of a door knocker, and he had seen nobody. But because he did not know. Because Silas Kross had said let him run, and Corin no longer believed he could tell when he was running and when he was not.
The streets changed as he went north.
The Lower Docks fell away behind him. The tall leaning houses and the washing lines and the smell of fish and tar. The streets got a little wider, and a little cleaner, and the houses a little less tall and a little more straight. He passed a square with a stone fountain in the middle that had no water in it and a stone saint on top with no nose. He passed a row of shops with glass windows and gold letters, a hatter and a glover and a shop that sold nothing but umbrellas. He passed a church, gray and narrow, with its doors standing open and the sound of an organ coming out, very faint, practicing the same three bars over and over.
And then he came out onto the embankment, and there was the river again, and the bridge.
The Lantern Bridge was old. Older than most things in Veridia. It went across the river in seven great arches of gray stone, so wide that a barge with its mast up could pass under the middle one without lowering it. The stone had gone dark with age and soot and river damp, and in places it was green with moss and in others it was white with the droppings of the gulls that lived under its arches. It was wide enough for two carts to pass, with a narrow pavement on each side behind a low stone wall. And all along both walls, on both sides, from one bank to the other, there were lampposts.
That was why it was called the Lantern Bridge. Corin had read it once, in some old book in the Ministry, before he burned whatever had made him want to read it. There had been a lamp on every post since the bridge was built. Hundreds of years. And every night, at dusk, a lamplighter walked across it with his long pole, from the Ink Quarter end to the far end and back, and lit them all. They were the old kind. Not gemstone lamps, cold and blue, like the ones along the quay at the Bank. Oil lamps. Real flame, behind glass, gold and warm. From the river at night, people said, the bridge looked like a necklace of little fires laid across the dark water.
It was not dusk now. The lamps were unlit. They stood along the bridge in their black iron cages on their black iron posts, dark and cold, waiting.
Corin stood at the Ink Quarter end and looked at the first post.
It was different from the others. Older. The iron was thicker and the cage was a different shape, and the post stood on a block of stone instead of being bolted into the wall. And set into the stone, on the side that faced the street, there was a plaque.
He had seen it before. A hundred times. He had walked past it on his way to and from the Ministry nearly every day for six years. He had never really looked at it. Nobody did. It was just one of those things in a city that you stopped seeing, the way you stopped seeing the cracks in your own ceiling.
He looked at it now.
It was a square of pale stone, about the size of a book, set flat into the darker stone of the block. Old letters had been cut into it, tall and thin and very beautiful, with little curls at the ends, the way letters were cut a long time ago when someone took care over them.
LANTERN BRIDGE.
BUILT IN THE REIGN OF
And then nothing.
Not nothing. Something had been there. A name. Corin could see where it had been. There was a long patch of stone, the length of a word, maybe two, where the surface had been chiseled away. Smooth. Very smooth. Smoother than the rest of the plaque, as if somebody had taken great care, after cutting the name out, to rub the stone down until there was no trace of it left. Not a scratch. Not the ghost of a letter. Just a smooth pale hollow in the stone, like the hollow in a pillow where a head has been.
And under it, in newer letters, cut more roughly, in a plainer and uglier style:
RESTORED BY THE GRACE OF THE MINISTRY.
Corin put his hand on the smooth hollow.
The stone was very cold. He moved his fingers along it, slowly, the way you would read a word in the dark. There was nothing. Not a bump. Not a line. Somebody had been very, very thorough.
Built in the reign of.
Of whom?
He thought of the empty debt column in the old green ledger, the clean yellow paper between the red lines. He thought of the name scratched out of the Collected by column, so deep it cut the page. He thought of the man in the glass room, with the face that slid away. I wrote on every door. He thought of the gold dome in the slag he had drawn out of a little girl in a crypt, and the man in the plain coat in the field with his hands lifted, and the name that had been cut out of the memory itself, so that he could only hear the first sound of it.
C—.
So many smooth places, said Malakor quietly, behind his ear. Have you noticed, boy? So many places in this city where something's been rubbed out. Once you start looking, you see them everywhere.
Who was it? Corin thought. Whose reign?
Malakor did not answer for a long time.
I can't tell you, he said at last. And his voice was strange. Not dry. Not mocking. Almost frightened. I've tried. Over the years. I've tried to say it. It's like trying to pick up water with a fork. It's there, and then it's gone, and I can't hold it. A pause. Ask your teacher. If he'll tell you. He was always better at holding things than I was.
Corin took his hand off the stone.
The Lamplighter's Rest was on the corner of Ferrule Street, right at the Ink Quarter end of the bridge, where the street came down off the embankment and the bridge began. It was an old inn, narrow and crooked, three floors of black timber and white plaster leaning out over the street, with small square windows of thick green glass that you could not see through. A sign hung over the door on an iron bracket, creaking in the wind. It was painted with a picture of a man in a long coat, with a long pole over his shoulder and a little flame at the end of it. Somebody had painted it with great care, a long time ago. The man's face was kind and tired, and the flame at the end of his pole was very bright, much brighter than the rest of the picture, as if it had been painted again, more recently, by somebody who wanted to keep it lit.
Corin stood on the step and looked up at it.
Then a clock somewhere struck three. Slowly. A deep, round, old-fashioned sound, from a tower somewhere across the river.
He pushed open the door and went in.
The Old Teacher
It was dark inside the Lamplighter's Rest, after the bright cold afternoon. Dark and warm and low.
The ceiling was so low that Corin had to bend his head under the beams. They were great black beams of old oak, hard as iron, with hooks screwed into them and things hanging from the hooks. Copper jugs. Bunches of dried hops. An old lantern with a broken glass. A pair of ice skates with rusty blades. The floor was stone flags, worn hollow, sprinkled with sawdust. The walls were dark wood, polished by a hundred years of shoulders leaning against them, and on the walls there were pictures, small and brown and cracked, of the bridge and the river and men in long coats carrying long poles with little flames at the ends of them.
There was a fire. A big one, in a wide stone fireplace at the far end, with a black iron fireback and a pair of iron dogs holding up the logs. It was the only real light in the room. The small square windows of green glass let in nothing but a dim underwater glow. The fire threw its red-gold light out across the stone floor and up the dark walls and into the faces of the people sitting near it, and everywhere else was shadow.
There were not many people. It was the middle of a Tuesday afternoon. Two old men were playing dominoes at a table near the door, very slowly, without speaking, clicking the little black-and-white tiles down one by one. A woman in a red shawl sat alone by the window with a glass of something dark and a newspaper. A big gray cat lay stretched on the hearth in front of the fire, as close to the flames as it could get without catching alight. And behind the counter, polishing a pewter mug with a cloth, there was a big bald man in a leather apron, who looked up when Corin came in, and looked at him for a moment, and then nodded, once, toward the fireplace. As if he had been told to expect him.
Corin looked where he was nodding.
There was a settle by the fire. A high-backed wooden bench, set at a right angle to the hearth, so that whoever sat on it had their side to the flames and their back to the room. Its back was so high that from the door Corin could not see who was sitting on it. He could only see, on the little table in front of it, a brown teapot with a chipped spout, and two cups. Two. And a book, lying open, face down, with a pair of spectacles folded on top of it.
And a hand, resting beside the book.
An old man's hand. Thin, with the knuckles standing up like knots in a rope, and the skin spotted brown with age and loose over the bones. And the fingers were blue. Not blue with cold. Stained. A deep, old, permanent blue, soaked right into the skin round the nails and in the cracks of the knuckles, so deep that no amount of scrubbing would ever get it out. The blue of a man who had handled ink every day of his life.
Corin stood by the door.
He could not make his feet move. He stood there with his hand still on the latch and the cold air from the street coming in round him, and he looked at that blue hand, and he could not go forward.
Go on, boy, said Malakor. Very quietly. Very strangely. Go on. He won't bite. He never did. Not even when you put a frog in his desk.
I put a frog in his desk?
Twice.
And something happened in Corin's chest that he did not understand. Something that was nearly a laugh and nearly the other thing. He let go of the latch. He shut the door behind him. And he walked across the stone floor, through the sawdust, past the old men with their dominoes and the woman with her newspaper, to the settle by the fire.
He came round the end of it.
The old man was sitting very straight. He was thin, very thin, the way old men get thin when they forget to eat, and very small inside a long dark coat that had been a good coat once and was not anymore. It was shiny at the elbows and frayed at the cuffs, and one of its buttons had been sewn back on with the wrong color thread. Under it he wore a gray waistcoat and a white shirt with a soft collar, very clean, and a narrow black tie knotted carefully. His hair was white and thin and brushed straight back from a high forehead. His face was long and gentle and very lined, the way a page is lined when it has been folded and unfolded a great many times. A walking stick leaned against the settle beside him, with a worn brass handle.
He had heard Corin coming. He had picked up the spectacles from the top of the book. Now he was putting them on, slowly, carefully, hooking them over one ear and then the other. They were round, with thin gold rims, and one side had been mended with a little twist of black thread where the hinge had broken.
He looked up through them.
His eyes were gray. A soft, faded, watery gray, like the sky over the river on a winter morning before the sun comes up. They were the kindest eyes Corin had ever seen. They looked up at him over the top of the settle, through the round gold spectacles, and something moved in them. Something that came up from very deep down, slowly, the way a fish comes up from the bottom of a deep pond.
And the old man's mouth shook.
"Corin," he said.
Just that. Just the name. His voice was thin and dry and cracked, like old paper. But it said the name the way nobody had ever said it, in all the six years Corin could remember. Not the way the clerks at the Ministry said it, reading it off a list. Not the way Oren said it, short and flat. Not the way Lyra said it, or Juno, or Vespera. The way you say the name of somebody you thought you would never see again. The way you say a name you have been saying in your head, every night, for a very long time.
Corin did not say anything. He could not.
The old man put his blue hand flat on the table. He pushed himself up, very slowly, off the settle. It was hard for him. Corin saw his arm shake with the effort, and his knees, and he nearly reached out to help. But then the old man was standing. Not much taller than Mrs. Marta. Bent a little at the shoulders. Looking up at Corin through the mended spectacles.
"You've grown," said Linus Hale.
Corin heard the words go into him. And for one terrible moment they were not this old man's words at all. They were another man's. A gentle voice, in a glass room at the top of a house, with a face that slid away. He's grown, hasn't he.
He flinched. He could not help it.
The old man saw it. Corin saw him see it. The faded gray eyes went quickly over Corin's face, and something in them changed, very slightly. A shadow went through them. And then it was gone, and the old man was smiling. A small, tired, very gentle smile.
"I'm sorry," he said. "That was a foolish thing to say. Of course you've grown. You were nine when I last saw you. Nine, and all knees and elbows, and you couldn't sit still for more than five minutes together." The smile wavered. "I don't suppose you remember."
"No," said Corin. His voice came out very low. "I don't remember."
"No." The old man was quiet for a moment. "No. I didn't think you would. I hoped. But I didn't think." He looked down at his own blue hands. "Sit down, Corin. Please. You look very tired. And I am too old to stand for long, and I would very much like to sit across a table from you, just once, before—" He stopped. "Just once. Please."
Corin sat down.
He sat on the other side of the little table, on a stool, with his back to the room and his face to the fire. The old man lowered himself back onto the settle, slowly, with his hand on the table and then on the arm of the settle. He let out a breath when he was down, a small tired sigh. Then he reached for the brown teapot.
"Tea," he said. "I ordered it at half past two. In case you were early. You were always early, you know. For everything. First into the schoolroom every morning. First at the door when the bell went for dinner." His blue fingers shook a little on the handle of the pot. "It's stewed by now, I'm afraid. It always is, with me. I start talking and forget to pour."
He poured. The tea came out dark brown and very strong. He put two lumps of sugar in Corin's cup without asking, and a splash of milk, and pushed it across the table.
Corin looked at it.
Two lumps. And milk. That was how he took it. He had never told anyone. He did not know how he knew it himself. It was one of those things that had come through with him, out of the empty rooms, the way some things did. The way he knew how to tie his boots, and how to read, and which side of the road to walk on.
"How did you know?" he said.
"Know what?"
"Two sugars. And milk."
The old man looked at the cup. Then at Corin. And the smile came back, very small.
"You used to steal the sugar lumps," he said, "out of the bowl on my desk. When you thought I wasn't looking. Two at a time. Always two. You'd put them in your cheek, one on each side, and sit there looking innocent with your face all lumpy." He shook his head slowly. "I always knew. I always pretended I didn't. I used to put extra in the bowl on the days I knew your mother had no money for breakfast."
Corin did not say anything.
He picked up the cup. His hands were not quite steady. He drank. The tea was stewed and bitter and too hot, and it burned his mouth. But under the bitter there was the sweet, the two lumps, and the milk.
It tasted like something.
He did not know what. He could not reach it. It was in one of the rooms with Oakhaven written on the door, he was sure of it, behind the chalk, behind the dust. A schoolroom. A desk. A bowl of sugar lumps. A man with ink on his fingers, pretending not to see.
He put down the cup.
Across the table, Linus Hale was watching him. With his kind, faded, watery gray eyes. With his blue hands folded on the open book in front of him. And Corin, looking back at him, thought that he had never in his life seen a face so full of love, and so full of something else, underneath, that he could not name.
Something very like fear.
