Chapter 25: The Auditor's Question, Part 4 of 5: The Offer
The Offer
"I want you to tell me," said Elara, "when you find out."
Corin looked at her.
"Find out what?"
"Where Oakhaven is. What happened there. Who called the debt that was never owed." She laid her bare hand flat on the cover of the old green ledger. "You will find out. Sooner or later. I have read you, Mr. Vane. You are the kind of man who finds things out, whether he wants to or not. It is written all through you. Every room you have burned, you burned to find something. A dead man's last minutes. A thief's hiding place. A girl's lost name." A pause. "You will burn your way to Oakhaven in the end. If there is enough of you left."
"And when I do?"
"You will come here. Up the iron stair. And you will tell me. And I will reopen this account, and audit it properly, from the first line to the last, and find the error, and put it right." Her voice did not change. "And then whoever stamped it Settled will have to answer to the Fourth Desk."
"Even if it's the Archon."
"The Charter of the Oblivion Bank," said Elara, "was signed by the Archon's grandfather. Clause one. The Bank answers to its books, and to nothing else." The gold tooth showed, just for a moment. "I have always thought that was a very foolish clause for an Archon to sign. But he signed it."
Corin looked at her for a long time.
He thought: She is going to get herself killed.
He thought: She doesn't know what she's standing on top of. She thinks it's a column of figures. She doesn't know it's a grave.
He thought: She doesn't know it's her own.
"And in return?" he said. "What do I get?"
"Two things." Elara held up one bare finger. "First. This."
She took a card out of the little black book. A small square card, pale green, the Bank's color. She laid it on the glass and pushed it toward him with one finger. There was writing on it, in her small slanting hand, and her silver stamp. The number 4.
The bearer, C. Vane, is under audit by the Fourth Desk. No claim of the Bank shall be made against him, or any account in his name, or any person or property in his keeping, while the audit is open. By order. E.
"While I have an audit open on you," said Elara, "no clerk in this Bank can touch you. Or anyone you are protecting. No debt can be called on you. No collector can come to your door. Not from this Bank." She paused. "I cannot speak for the other one. The one with the white horses. That is not my Bank. But this one, I can close to you. And I have."
Corin picked up the card. He turned it over in his fingers. It was stiff and smooth and very light.
"How long will the audit stay open?"
"As long as I wish," said Elara. "I am very slow at audits, when I want to be."
He put the card in his coat, inside the Ledger, between the pages.
"And the second thing?"
Elara's face changed.
Not much. She did not frown. She did not look away. But something went out of it. The dry thing at the corner of her mouth. The bright hard stubbornness. It all went quiet, and her face was only pale, and tired, and very careful.
"A warning," she said.
She opened the little black book again. She turned back a few pages. She did not read from it. She only looked at it.
"Yesterday afternoon," she said, "at about four o'clock, a man came to the Bank. To the front. Up the white steps. He did not stand in the line. He went past it, the way you did this morning, and the porters did not stop him. He asked for the Treasurer. He was shown up. He was with the Treasurer for an hour." A pause. "When he came down again, he stopped at the Fourth Desk. He knocked on my door. He came in. He stood exactly where you are sitting. He did not sit down. He would not take off his gloves."
Corin felt the cold come into his chest.
"Gray gloves," he said.
Elara looked up at him.
"Yes," she said. "Gray gloves. A gray coat. Very pale hair. A pleasant face. He was eating a barley sugar, and when he had finished it, he folded the paper very small and put it in his pocket." She paused. "He said his name was Kross. Inquisitor Kross. He was very polite. He said the Inquisition had opened an inquiry into a Harvester of the Ministry, one Corin Vane. He said it was a routine matter. He said he would be grateful for the Bank's help. He asked to see every record the Bank holds in your name. Every deposit. Every debt. Every intake card. Every glass you have ever touched."
"What did you tell him?"
"I told him that the Bank's records are private, under the Charter, and that he would need a writ from the Council. He said he had one. He showed it to me. It was signed by Councillor Cressida Thorne."
Corin closed his eyes.
"And then," said Elara, "he asked me for one more thing. He was even more polite about it. He said he understood it was a very old record, and very dull, and he was sorry to trouble me. He asked if the Fourth Desk had any account on its books for a place called Oakhaven."
Corin opened his eyes.
Elara was looking at him steadily.
"I told him I had never heard of it," she said. "Which was true. Yesterday afternoon, at four o'clock, I had never heard that word in my life. He thanked me. He said it was probably nothing. He said good afternoon. And he went down the iron stair." A pause. "And this morning, at six o'clock, I read your intake card. And I saw the word on every door."
She closed the little black book.
"So I came up here," she said, "and I looked. And I found it. Exactly where he would have found it, if I had been the kind of Auditor who lets people look at her ledgers." She laid her bare hand on the old green cover. "He knows it is here, Mr. Vane. Or he thinks it is. And he asked for it the day before he asked for you."
The room was very quiet. The light from the window had gone a deeper yellow. The shadows of the iron spokes lay long across the floor.
"He's been following me," Corin said. "All night. He let me run. He wanted to see where I'd go." He swallowed. "And this morning he sent a carriage for whoever was standing in a gate. And now he's asking about Oakhaven."
"Then he is not following you," said Elara. "He is walking ahead of you. And looking back, now and then, to make sure you are still coming."
Corin stared at her.
She stood up.
She did it slowly, carefully, holding the edge of the glass desk with her bare hand, the way an old woman stands. For one moment she swayed. Mister Gage was beside her before Corin had seen him move, one huge hand just behind her elbow, not touching. Then she was steady, and he stepped back, and folded his hands, and was a wardrobe again.
"I am tired," Elara said. "I have read more today than on any day for a year, and I am not used to it. I think the interview is over, Mr. Vane." She picked up her white gloves from the arm of the chair. "Keep the card. Keep your Ledger close. And if you can, keep away from men in gray gloves."
Corin stood too.
They looked at each other across the glass desk. Across the old green ledger, and the little black book, and the shape of his hand that she did not know was there.
"Auditor," he said.
"Mr. Vane."
"Be careful."
She looked at him for a moment. Then the dry thing moved at the corner of her mouth, and the gold tooth gleamed, and it was almost a smile. Almost.
"I am always careful," said Elara. "It is the only thing I am sure I have always been."
Mister Gage
Corin went out of the long clean room and shut the green door behind him.
He stood on the little square landing under the roof for a moment, with his back against the door. The warmth of the whole building was gathered up here, close and dry, smelling of dust and old wood. The small blue lamp at the top of the iron stair threw its cold light across the landing and down into the dark well of the shaft. Far below, the murmur of the hall went on, fainter now, and thinning. It must be nearly closing time. He could hear, under the murmur, a bell ringing somewhere far down, slow and steady. Last in line. Last in line.
He put his hand on the rail of the stair.
And behind him, the green door opened again.
He turned. It was Mister Gage.
The big man came out onto the landing and shut the door behind him, very softly, so that the latch made no sound at all. Then he stood there. He was so tall that his bald head nearly touched the great dark beams overhead, and so broad that he filled the landing from side to side. The blue light fell on his unfinished face, on the smooth pale eyes and the small soft nose and the mouth that was only a line. It made him look like a statue somebody had begun to carve out of ice and then walked away from.
Corin did not move. He did not know what to do. In the Harvesting Section, Mister Gage had stood by the door for an hour without moving once. Toby had been afraid of him. Corin had been afraid of him too, a little, though he had not said so. Nobody knew what Mister Gage was. Nobody knew where he had come from. He carries things, Elara had said. He does not speak. You may ignore him. Everybody does.
Nobody could ignore him now. Not on a landing this small.
Mister Gage looked at him.
It was the second time. The first had been at the glass desk, when the smooth pale eyes had come round, for the first time ever, and fixed on Corin's face, and then gone down to the blue print of his hand on the glass. Now they were fixed on him again. Steady. Pale. Unblinking. And Corin had the strange feeling, the same one he had had at the desk, that there was somebody behind them. Somebody who was looking out, very carefully, through eyes that did not quite fit, the way a man looks out through the eyeholes of a mask.
Mister Gage lifted one huge hand.
Corin flinched. He could not help it. The hand was the size of a shovel blade, thick and pale, with fingers like the handles of hammers. It came up toward him slowly, and he pressed his back against the cold iron rail and waited for it to close round his throat.
It did not. It stopped in the air between them, palm up. And then, slowly, it opened.
There was something lying in it.
It was small. So small that in that great pale palm Corin did not see what it was at first. Something brown. Something curled. Something dry and hard and wrinkled, like a dead leaf, or a scrap of old leather, or a shaving of dark wood.
He bent closer.
It was a curl of peel. Orange peel. One long unbroken spiral of it, round and round, the way peel comes off when somebody takes great care, very slowly, with big careful fingers, to make it come off all in one piece. It had gone hard and brown with age, so long ago that it had no smell left at all. But it was still whole. Not one break in it, from end to end.
Corin looked up at Mister Gage's face.
Nothing moved on it. Nothing ever moved on it. But the huge hand stayed there, held out, steady, with the brown curl lying in the middle of the palm.
And I kept it in a drawer, Elara had said, until it went hard and brown.
And it is gone. I know the words. But there is nothing behind them now.
"You kept it," Corin said. His voice came out very quietly. "When she lost it. You kept it."
Mister Gage did not answer. He could not answer. But the hand moved, just a little, toward Corin. Offering.
Corin did not take it.
"I can't," he said. "It's hers. You should give it back to her. Put it in the drawer. Where she kept it. She'll find it one day, and—"
And what? he thought. And she'll pick it up and look at it, and it'll mean nothing to her. A bit of old peel. She'll throw it away.
He understood then.
Mister Gage could not give it back to her. It would mean nothing to her now. She had lost the orange, and the big fingers, and the long careful curl, and the drawer, and all of it, this very afternoon, at her glass desk, for him. She had paid it for him. For a reading of him. And there was nothing behind the words anymore.
But somebody should still have it. Somebody should know.
Corin held out his own hand.
Mister Gage tipped his great palm, very gently, and the brown curl slid off it and into Corin's hand. It weighed nothing. Nothing at all. Like holding a moth.
Then the big man did something else.
He touched his own chest. Over his heart. With two thick fingers. Once.
And then he lifted the same two fingers and pointed. Not at Corin. Up. At the green door behind him. At the long clean room on the other side of it, where a woman with a gold tooth was sitting at a glass desk in the last of the light, with a little black book in her lap.
And then he touched his chest again.
And then, slowly, he reached out, and touched Corin's chest. Over his heart. Over the place where the Ledger lay, and under the Ledger, the one faded word.
His fingers were very cold. Cold as the glass of the desk.
Corin did not breathe.
Mister Gage took his hand away. He looked at Corin for one more long moment with his pale eyes that were not quite his own. Then he turned, and opened the green door, very softly, and went back into the room, and shut it behind him. And Corin heard, faintly, through the wood, the small sound of the latch, and then nothing.
He stood on the landing for a long time.
He looked down at the curl of orange peel in his palm. Brown and hard and whole.
He thought about the blue print on the glass. The way the big man had come across the room without a sound and laid the heavy green ledger on top of it, exactly on top of it, so that Elara would not see.
He thought about the fingers on his own chest. And the two fingers pointing at the door. And the fingers on Gage's own chest, over his own heart.
Her, that had meant. He was almost sure. Her. And me. And you.
But what it meant after that, he did not know.
He knows, said Malakor, behind his left ear. Very quiet. Very thoughtful. That one knows something, boy. I'd stake my last splinter on it. He knew that print the moment he saw it.
Knew what? Corin thought.
But Malakor did not answer that.
Corin opened his Ledger. Very carefully, he laid the curl of peel inside it, between two pages near the back, where nothing was written yet. He closed the book over it. He put it back inside his coat, over his heart.
Then he put his hand on the cold iron rail, and began to go down.
The Slag Room
The iron stair was harder going down than going up.
Corin did not know why. His legs shook. Twice he had to stop on a turn and hold the cold rail with both hands and stand there, breathing, under the small blue lamp, until the shaking went away. The murmur of the great hall came through the brick around him, louder now, and the low hum of the glass under it, and he found that he was listening to it in a way he had never listened before. A hundred people forgetting at once. A hundred people walking up to a sheet of glass and putting their hand on it and giving away an afternoon, a song, a voice, a face. For coppers. For rent. Because they were tired.
And somewhere up above him, at the top of the stair, a woman with a gold tooth was sitting at a glass desk with her hand on an old green ledger, holding a whole village in her palm.
He thought of the empty debt column. The clean yellow paper. In full.
He thought of the scratched-out name, cut so deep that the red lines of the next page showed through.
He went on down.
At the bottom of the stair, in the narrow dark passage, Warden Briggs was waiting. He was standing at his tall wooden stand under the blue lamp, with his big red hands flat on the open book, exactly as Corin had left him. He did not seem to have moved at all. When he heard Corin's boots on the last steps, he looked up.
He looked at Corin's face for a long moment.
"Sit down," he said. "Before you fall down."
There was a stool beside the stand. A plain wooden one, worn smooth. Corin sat on it. He put his elbows on his knees and his head in his hands and sat there, and Briggs did not say anything, and did not ask anything, and for a while there was only the sound of the hall through the wall, and the drip of water somewhere, and the sour sweet smell coming up the stairs from the slag room below.
"Come and see something," said Briggs at last.
Corin looked up.
"Down there." Briggs nodded at the slag-room stairs. "Since you're here. Since you're not clearing." He took a lantern down from a hook on the wall and lit it from the blue lamp, slowly, with a long spill of paper. "I want to show you something. I've been wanting to show somebody for a long time. I don't know why it should be you. But it should be somebody."
He went down the stairs. After a moment, Corin got up off the stool and followed him.
He knew these stairs. He had come down them every morning for a week. Twenty-two stone steps, narrow and steep and worn hollow in the middle, going down between walls of wet brick into the dark. The smell got stronger with every step. Vinegar and old flowers. Something sweet that had gone bad. And under it, something else, something that had no name, that made the back of his throat ache and his eyes water. The smell of a great many memories, broken.
The slag room was long and low and very cold.
It had been the cellar of the old warehouse once, and it still had the great brick arches holding up the floor above, row after row of them, going back into the dark. Between the arches there were shelves. Iron shelves, rusty, bolted to the brick. And on the shelves there were glasses.
Not the great clear sheets of glass from the counter upstairs. Smaller ones. Each one the size of a book, set in a little iron frame, standing upright on the shelves in long rows like books in a library. Hundreds of them. Thousands, perhaps, going back and back under the arches until the lantern light could not reach them.
And every one was cloudy.
That was what slag did. Corin knew. He had cleared hundreds of them. When a person put their hand on the glass upstairs and sold a memory, the memory went into the glass. Most of it. The clean part. The part the Bank could use. And that part was drawn off, by the clerks, into the little jars and vials that went down to the vaults. But some of it did not come clean. Some of it was too broken, or too old, or too mixed up with other things. And that part stayed in the glass. Cloudy. Gray. Like breath on a window that would not fade. Slag. Bits of many people. Nobody's.
And every morning, somebody had to come down here and clear the glasses. Wipe the slag off them with a cloth soaked in salt and vinegar, and scrape it into the slag-jars, so that the glasses could go back up to the counter, clean, and be used again.
It had been Corin, for a week.
Briggs walked down between the shelves with the lantern held up. Corin followed. Their footsteps echoed under the arches. The glasses on either side of them caught the lantern light as they passed, and the slag inside them seemed to stir, slowly, like mist on water.
Briggs stopped at the far end. At the very last arch, where the brick wall at the back of the cellar went down into the river mud and the floor was always wet. There was a shelf there that Corin had never cleared. He had never been told to. It stood in the corner, behind a rusty iron grille, and the grille was locked with a padlock.
On the shelf, behind the grille, there were only three glasses.
They were older than the others. Corin could see that. Their iron frames were black with age and their glass was thick and greenish, like the glass of the desk upstairs. And they were not cloudy. Not gray. They were full of something else. Something that moved. Something gold.
Briggs took a key out of his waistcoat pocket. He unlocked the padlock. He swung the grille open. And he lifted the lantern, so that its light fell full on the three old glasses.
"Twenty years I've been Warden here," he said. "And these were here when I came. On this shelf. Behind this grille. The Warden before me showed me them, the day he left. And he said the Warden before him had shown him." His big creased face was very still in the lantern light. "He said, These came in a long time ago, Fenn. One night. Off a cart. Nobody's slag. Nobody ever cleared them. Nobody ever could. Leave them be."
Corin bent close.
The gold inside the three old glasses was not still. It moved. Slowly. Round and round. And as he watched, he saw that it was not just gold. There were shapes in it. Very faint. Very small. Like pictures seen from a long way off, through rain.
A bell, in a little wooden tower.
A wide green field, and a man standing in it, with his hands lifted.
A tree. A great old spreading tree, with a rope swing hanging from one branch, and leaves the shape of—
Oak leaves.
Corin straightened up so fast that he hit his head on the curve of the brick arch above him. He did not feel it.
"What are they?" he said. His voice came out strange. "Briggs. What are they?"
Briggs looked at him for a long time.
"I don't know," he said. "I've never known. I've stood here and looked at them, nights, when the hall's shut. More times than I can count. And every time, I get the same feeling." He put his big red hand up to his chest. Over his heart. The way he always did. "Like there's something I ought to know. Something I knew once. And it's just there. Just on the other side of a door. And I can't open it."
He lowered the lantern.
"A teacher," he said quietly. "A schoolhouse with a bell. A big oak tree." He shook his head slowly. "Never knew nobody."
He swung the grille shut. He put the padlock back on, and turned the key, and put the key in his waistcoat pocket.
"I just wanted somebody else to see them," he said. "Before I forget I ever did."
They went back up the stairs together, in silence, with the lantern swinging, and the sour sweet smell of the slag following them all the way to the top.
