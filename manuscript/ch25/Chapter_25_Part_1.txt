Chapter 25: The Auditor's Question, Part 1 of 5: Lantern Quay
Lantern Quay
The line outside the Bank was longer in the afternoon than in the morning. Corin had not known that. He had only ever come in the mornings, early, for his glasses, when the mist was still on the river and the line was a thin gray thread along the wall. Now, at a quarter to two on a cold bright Monday, it was a river of its own.
It began at the top of the Salt Stairs, where the Docks road came down to the water. It went down the stairs, two by two, three by three, people pressed against the iron railing. It went along the riverfront, past the pie sellers and the fish carts and the coils of rope and the men mending nets. It went past the end of Wharf Street and past the Customs House and past the long low sheds where the river boats tied up. And it ended, a long way off, at the foot of the white steps of the Oblivion Bank.
Corin walked beside it.
He did not look at the faces. He had learned, his first week clearing glasses, not to look at the faces. But he could not stop himself hearing. People in a line talk. They talk to pass the time, and to keep warm, and to stop themselves thinking about what they are standing in line to do.
"—just the one afternoon. Just one. My wedding day. I don't need it, do I? I know I got married. I've got the ring—"
"—they give you more for a bad one. Did you know that? A really bad day, the worst you've got. They give you twice as much for that as for a good one—"
"—my mother's voice. I've still got two of her. Saying my name. I'm only selling the one. I'll keep the other—"
"—rent's due Friday—"
"—rent's due Friday—"
"—rent's due—"
He walked faster.
He had washed again, at Pruett's pump, before he left. He had borrowed a shirt from young Pruett, a clean blue cotton shirt with the collar frayed, much too big, so that he had to roll the sleeves up twice and tuck it in hard at the waist. He had brushed his coat again. It still smelled of smoke. He had not been able to do anything about that. He had combed his hair with his fingers. He had looked at himself in the cracked mirror in Pruett's wash-house, and he had looked like what he was: a man who had not slept, and had been in a fire, and had climbed across half the roofs of the Lower Docks in the dark.
It would have to do.
The Ledger was in the inside pocket of his coat, over his heart, where it always was. The silver rod was in the long narrow pocket on the right side. He had thought about leaving the rod at Pruett's. He had held it in his hand for a long time in the stable, with Duchess breathing on him, and thought about it.
Don't, Malakor had said, behind his left ear. Don't you dare leave me in a horse box, boy.
So he had brought it.
The Bank stood on the very edge of the river, with its back to the water. It had been a warehouse once. You could still see that, from the side. Great blank walls of dark brick, stained with soot and river damp, with high narrow windows covered in iron bars. A roof of black slate. But somebody had built a new front onto it, a long time ago, and the new front was made of white stone. Four tall white columns, smooth and round and clean, held up a wide white stone roof over the doors, like the front of a temple. And carved into the stone above the columns, in letters as tall as a man, covered in gold leaf so that they shone in the cold afternoon sun:
THE OBLIVION BANK. LANTERN QUAY BRANCH. INTAKE.
And under that, smaller, also gold:
FORGET YOUR TROUBLES.
The great doors at the top of the steps stood open. Two Bank porters stood one on each side of them, in long dark green coats with gold piping on the cuffs and collars, and tall green hats. They were big men, both of them, with flat hard faces. They did not look at the people in the line. They looked over their heads, at the river.
Corin did not go up the steps. He never did.
He went round the side.
There was a narrow alley there, between the Bank's dark brick wall and the wall of a rope store next door. It was always in shadow. It smelled of river mud and wet stone and, faintly, of something sharp and sour, like vinegar. At the end of the alley, near the water, there was a door. A plain black door, set into the brick, with no handle on the outside. Only a small iron grille at the height of a man's face, and a bell-pull beside it on a rusty chain.
This was where the carts came. At midnight, the slag carts. And in the mornings, Corin.
He pulled the bell.
He heard it ring, a long way inside, a thin cracked jangle. Then footsteps. Slow and heavy. Then the little grille slid open, and an eye looked out at him. A pale blue eye, red-rimmed, in a big creased face.
"You're late," said Warden Briggs. "By about eight hours."
"I know."
"I said not to come in today. I sent the girl."
"She told me. I'm not here to clear the glasses."
The eye looked at him for a long moment.
"No," said Briggs. "I know you're not."
The grille slid shut. Corin heard a bolt being drawn. Then another. Then a key turning in a heavy lock. And the black door swung inward on its hinges with a long low groan, and there was Fenn Briggs, filling the doorway.
He was a big man. Not tall, so much, as wide. Wide in the shoulders and wide in the chest and wide in the belly, like a barrel stood on end. He wore the Bank's dark green, like the porters at the front, but his coat was old and faded almost to gray, and there was no gold piping on it. Only a small brass badge on the collar, with the word WARDEN on it. His hair was gray and cut very short. His face was big and creased and red, and it had the look of a face that had been kind once, a long time ago, and had forgotten how.
He looked Corin up and down.
"You smell like a chimney," he said.
"There was a fire."
"The rope-walk. I heard." Briggs stood aside. "Get in. Before somebody sees you."
Corin went in.
The passage inside was narrow and dark and cold. Its walls were bare brick, damp, with little white crusts of salt in the cracks. There was a smell, the smell that was always there, sharp and sour and faintly sweet, like vinegar and old flowers. The smell of slag. It came up from below, from the stairs at the end of the passage that went down into the dark. The slag room was down there. Corin had spent a week of mornings down there, clearing the glasses. He knew every step of those stairs.
But Briggs did not take him to the stairs.
He stopped at his stand.
It was a tall wooden desk, like a pulpit, standing against the wall at the top of the slag-room stairs, under a single blue gemstone lamp. A big book lay open on it, with columns of names and numbers. A pen. An inkwell. A wooden box of small square cards. Corin's own card was in that box. He had taken it out every morning for a week, and signed it, and put it back.
Briggs put his big red hand flat on the open book. He did not look at Corin.
"She came at six," he said. "I told the girl that. Did she tell you all of it?"
"She told me you said not to come."
"Did she tell you what the Auditor did?"
"She said she put her bare hand on my card. And shut her eyes. And when she opened them, she looked very tired."
Briggs was quiet for a moment.
"She stood there," he said, "right where you're standing. For a long time. With her hand on your card and her eyes shut. Longer than I've ever seen her read anything. I counted. I counted to three hundred." He looked down at the book. "And then she opened her eyes and she looked at me. And she said, Warden, how long has this man been coming here? And I said a week. And she said, No. Before that. How long? And I said I didn't know what she meant. And she looked at me for a long time. And then she said, Thank you, Warden. That's all I need."
He turned his big creased face toward Corin.
"And then she went up the iron stair," he said, "and she didn't come down again. All morning. And at eleven she sent the runner." He paused. "And at half past eleven she sent for me. And she asked me something else."
"What?"
Briggs was quiet for a long time.
"She asked me," he said slowly, "if I'd ever known a family called Vane."
Corin did not move. He did not breathe.
"And I said what I said to you," said Briggs. "The first day. I said I'd known a Vane once. A teacher. A schoolhouse with a bell. A big oak tree." He put his hand up to his chest, the way he had done that first day, without seeming to know he was doing it. "And then I said what I always say. That I never knew nobody. Because I don't. I don't know where it comes from. It just comes, when I hear the name. A teacher. A bell. An oak tree. And then it's gone."
"What did she say?"
"Nothing," said Briggs. "She didn't say anything at all. She just wrote it in her little black book. And then she sat there at her glass desk for a long time, looking at what she'd written. And then she told me to go."
He took his hand off the book.
"It's the iron stair," he said. "At the end of the passage. Past the stairs to the slag room. It goes up. All the way up. Right to the top of the old warehouse, under the roof. Her door's at the top. You can't miss it. It's the only one."
"Will you come up with me?"
"No," said Briggs. "She said alone." He looked at Corin. Something moved in his big red face. Something that might once have been kindness, a long time ago. "I'll be here. At my stand. When you come down."
If, thought Corin. When I come down. If.
He went along the passage, past the head of the slag-room stairs, where the sour sweet smell came up out of the dark, and on, to the end. And there it was. An iron stair. A spiral of black iron going up, round and round, into the shadows overhead, like the inside of a seashell. Its steps were thin plates of iron with holes punched through them. Its rail was cold and smooth and worn bright by a great many hands.
He put his hand on the rail, and began to climb.
The Fourth Desk
The iron stair went up a long way.
It went up through the whole height of the old warehouse, round and round, in a narrow brick shaft that had been built for it inside the wall. There were no windows. There was a gemstone lamp at every turn, small and blue and cold, set into the brick on an iron bracket, and between the lamps there was shadow. Corin's boots rang on the iron steps. The sound went up and down the shaft and came back to him from above and below, so that it seemed as if there were other people climbing with him, just out of sight.
He counted the turns. He did not know why. It was something to do. Twelve. Fifteen. Twenty.
Somewhere around the twentieth turn, he began to hear the Bank.
It came through the brick. A low murmur, like the sea heard from a long way off. Voices. Hundreds of voices, all speaking at once, too far away and too muffled to hear any words. The sound of the great hall below, with its long counter and its glass stalls and its clerks in green and its line of people selling their afternoons. And under the voices, steady and soft, there was another sound. A sort of hum. Very low. So low that he felt it more than heard it, in the iron rail under his hand and the soles of his feet.
Glass, said Malakor, behind his ear. All that glass. Taking it in. Can you feel it, boy? That's the sound of a hundred people forgetting at once.
Corin did not answer. He kept climbing.
The stair ended at a small square landing under the roof. The ceiling here was low, made of the great dark beams of the old warehouse, with the underside of the slates showing between them. It was warmer up here. The warmth of the whole building had risen up and gathered under the roof like water in the top of an upturned bowl. There was a smell of dust, and old wood, and something clean and sharp, like the smell of a new pen.
And there was a door.
It was a plain door, painted dark green, with a brass handle. On it, at the height of his eyes, there was a small brass plate. On the plate, in neat black letters, it said:
FOURTH DESK. AUDIT.
Nothing else. No name.
Corin stood on the landing for a moment. He looked down at his hands. They were clean, now. Scrubbed red. The cut on the back of the left one had closed into a thin pink line. He had left his gray leather gloves at the Ministry, in his desk drawer, a long time ago. A week ago. It felt like a year.
He thought of the first time she had come to his desk in the Harvesting Section. My letter asked that you be ungloved. And his answer. I read it. And her dry voice. And yet.
There were no gloves today.
He knocked.
"Come," said a voice.
He opened the door.
The room behind it was long and low, under the slope of the roof, with the great dark beams running across overhead. It ran right along the top of the old warehouse, from the back to the front. At the front end, there was a window. Only one. But it was a big one, a great round window like the window in a church, set into the gable of the building, high above the white columns and the gold letters below. It was made of many small panes of glass, set in a pattern of iron, like the spokes of a wheel. Through it, the cold afternoon light came in, pale and clear and very bright, and fell across the whole length of the room.
The room was very clean. Cleaner than any room Corin had ever been in. The floor was bare boards, scrubbed almost white. The walls were whitewashed. There was no dust anywhere. Not on the beams. Not on the floor. Not on the shelves.
And there were shelves. They ran down both long walls, under the slope of the roof, from the door to the window. Plain wooden shelves, very straight, very neat. On them there were books. Hundreds of books. All alike. All bound in the same dark green leather, all the same size, all standing in perfect rows with their spines facing out. On each spine, in small gold numbers, there was a date. A year and a month. The rows went back a very long way. Corin saw, near the door, on the bottom shelf, a book whose date was nearly a hundred years old.
The ledgers of the Fourth Desk. Every account it had ever audited.
By the door, standing with his back to the wall, there was Mister Gage.
Corin had forgotten how big he was. He had forgotten how strange. Mister Gage was taller than Oren, and broader than Briggs, and he stood quite still, with his huge hands folded in front of him, the way he had stood by Corin's door in the Harvesting Section. He wore a dark green coat with no buttons. His head was bald and very smooth. And his face—
Corin had never known how to think about Mister Gage's face. It was not ugly. It was not scarred. It was simply unfinished. As if someone had started to make a face, and had got as far as the shape of it, the forehead and the cheeks and the chin, and then had stopped. The eyes were there, but they were pale and smooth and did not seem to look at anything. The nose was there, but it was small and soft, like a child's. The mouth was there, but it was only a line. Nothing moved on it. Nothing ever moved on it.
Mister Gage did not look at Corin. He did not look at anything. He stood by the door like a wardrobe, and his unfinished face was turned toward the far end of the room.
Toward the desk.
It stood in front of the great round window, with the light coming through behind it. And it was made of glass.
Not a wooden desk with a sheet of glass on it, like the one she had carried into the Harvesting Section. The whole desk was glass. The top was one great thick slab of it, as long as a man lying down and as wide as a door, clear and greenish at the edges like the water in a deep pond. It stood on four legs of glass, thick as a man's arm, twisted like barley sugar. And inside it, inside all of it, in the top and down the legs, there ran the silver lines. Thousands upon thousands of them. Fine as hairs. Branching and branching and branching, like frost on a window, like the veins of a leaf, like the roots of a great tree seen through clear water. In the cold light from the window, the whole desk seemed to glitter. As if it were alive.
There was nothing on it. No papers. No pens. No inkwell. Only one small black book, lying closed in the very middle, square with the edges.
And behind the desk, in a straight-backed wooden chair, very straight, sitting on the front edge with her knees together, there was the Auditor of the Fourth Desk.
She was smaller than he remembered. Or perhaps the room made her look small. She wore dark green, a plain high-necked dress with long sleeves, and no jewelry. Her dark hair was cut short and straight, at the line of her jaw, so straight that it looked as if it had been cut with a ruler. Her face was pale and narrow and very still. Her eyes were dark, and very careful. They were looking at him.
She was not wearing her gloves. They lay beside her, folded, on the arm of the chair. Two white gloves, very clean. Her bare hands were folded on top of the glass desk, one over the other, and they were very white too. White and thin, with short clean nails.
And she was tired.
He could see it. It was in the skin under her eyes, which was faintly gray, like a bruise that has nearly healed. It was in the way she held herself, so very straight, as if she were afraid that if she let herself bend even a little she would not be able to stop. It was in the corners of her mouth.
"Mr. Vane," she said.
"Auditor."
"You came alone."
"You said alone."
"I did." Her eyes went down to his hands. Bare. Then back up to his face. And the dry thing moved at the corner of her mouth, very slightly, the thing that was almost a smile and was not, and he saw the gold tooth. One front tooth, capped in gold, over an old gap. It gleamed in the light from the window and was gone. "And no gloves."
"No gloves."
"Someone has stopped talking to you, then."
He did not answer that.
She did not seem to expect him to. She lifted one bare white hand from the glass and pointed, with two fingers, at the chair in front of the desk. A plain wooden chair, exactly like hers. It stood exactly one hand's width out from the edge of the glass.
"Sit," she said. "Please. You have had a long night. I have had a long morning. Neither of us, I think, wants to stand."
Corin crossed the long clean room. His boots sounded very loud on the bare boards. He passed between the shelves of green ledgers, a hundred years of them, and he felt Mister Gage's unfinished face not turn to follow him. He came to the chair, and he sat on it.
The glass desk was very close now. He could see the silver lines inside it, branching, branching. He could see his own face in the top of it, faint and pale, like a face under water. He could see her hands, folded, on the other side.
And under her hands, very faintly, the silver lines were moving. Curling, slowly, round and round. Like the inside of a seashell. As if they were listening.
"Thank you for coming," said Elara. "I know you did not want to."
"You said it wasn't a request."
"It wasn't." She looked at him. "But you could still have run. A great many people would have. You didn't." A small pause. "I wanted to know if you would."
Everyone wants to know where I'll run, Corin thought. Kross. Gant. Now her.
"And now you know," he said.
"And now I know," said Elara. She unfolded her hands. She laid them flat on the glass, on either side of the small black book. "Did you bring it?"
Corin put his hand inside his coat, over his heart. He took out the Ledger.
And he laid it down on the glass desk, between them.
