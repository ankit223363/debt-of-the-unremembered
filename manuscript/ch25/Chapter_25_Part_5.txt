Chapter 25: The Auditor's Question, Part 5 of 5: The Great Hall
The Great Hall
At the top of the slag-room stairs, Briggs did not turn toward the black door.
He hung the lantern back on its hook. He stood at his tall stand for a moment, with his big red hands on the open book, looking down at the columns of names. Then he closed the book. He did it slowly, the way a man closes a book he has been reading for a very long time.
"Closing bell's gone," he said. "The last of them'll be at the counter now. You've never seen it, have you. The hall. From the inside. At the end of the day."
"I came through it once," said Corin. "My first day. From the front."
"That's not seeing it." Briggs took a ring of keys off his belt. "Come on. One look. Then I'll let you out the side."
There was a door at the end of the passage that Corin had never been through. It was a narrow wooden door, painted green like everything else in the Bank, with a small round window of thick glass at the height of a man's face. Briggs unlocked it with the biggest of his keys. He opened it a crack. And light came through. Warm yellow light, and blue light, and noise.
"Stand here," said Briggs. "Behind me. Don't go out. Just look."
Corin looked.
The door opened onto the back of the great hall, behind the long counter, at the far end, near the river wall. From here he could see the whole of it, stretching away toward the great doors at the front. The white tiled floor, gleaming. The cream walls going up and up, to the beams of the old warehouse roof far overhead. The gemstone lamps hanging on their long chains, blazing cold blue. And the counter. The long dark polished counter, running down the middle of the hall from end to end, divided every few paces by thin brass rails into little stalls. And in every stall, set into the wood, a sheet of glass as big as a tabletop, with the silver lines branching inside it like frost.
Most of the stalls were empty now. The clerks had gone. Their chairs stood pushed back from the counter, and their little wooden boxes of cards were shut, and their brass stamps lay on their sides. A few porters in green were going along the hall with long brooms, sweeping. The great doors at the front had been closed and barred. The gold light of the sunset came in through the high narrow windows along the river wall in long slanting bars, full of dust.
But not all the stalls were empty.
At the far end of the counter, near the doors, three clerks were still working. And in front of each of them, on the near side of the glass, there was a person from the line. The last three. The ones who had been at the very end, and had waited all afternoon, and had got in just before the bell.
Corin could see them quite clearly from where he stood. The lamps were very bright.
The first was an old man. Very old. Thin and bent, with a long white beard and a coat that had been patched so many times it was more patches than coat. He was standing very straight in front of the glass, with his cap in his hands, turning it round and round. The clerk behind the glass, a young man with oiled hair and white gloves, was saying something to him. Corin could not hear what. The old man nodded. He put his cap down on the counter. And he laid his hand on the glass.
The glass went white. Not very white. A soft white, like milk. Like the light on a winter morning. It glowed for a moment under the old man's hand, and the silver lines in it curled toward his palm, and drank.
And Corin saw the old man's face change.
It was a very small change. It was gone in a breath. But Corin saw it. The old man's eyes had been looking at something, far away, the way old men's eyes do. And then they were not. They were looking at nothing. And for one moment the old face was quite empty and quite still and quite peaceful, like the face of somebody asleep.
Then the glass went clear. The clerk took his hand off his own side of it, and stamped a card, and pushed three coppers across the counter. The old man picked them up. He looked at them, in his palm, as if he were not sure what they were. Then he put on his cap, and turned, and walked slowly away down the hall toward the side door where the porters were letting people out.
He did not look back.
"What did he sell?" Corin said. His voice came out strange.
"Don't know," said Briggs. "Don't ask. Never ask. The clerks write it on the card, and the card goes in the box, and the box goes to the vault. I never read the cards." He was quiet for a moment. "He comes every Monday. Has done for a year. Three coppers, every time. Every time something small. That's what the clerk told me, once, when I didn't ask." Another pause. "He used to have a wife. He doesn't now. Not in his head. He sold her a bit at a time. Her voice. Her laugh. Her hands. The way she said his name. Three coppers a go. For his rent."
Corin did not say anything. He could not.
The second person at the counter was a woman. Young, younger than Lyra, with a shawl over her head and a baby on her hip. The baby was asleep, with its cheek on her shoulder and its mouth open. The woman was talking to the clerk, fast, in a low voice, leaning over the glass. The clerk was shaking her head. The woman talked faster. The clerk shook her head again, and pointed at something on a card, and the woman looked at it, and Corin saw her shoulders go down.
Then she put her hand on the glass.
It went white. Brighter than the old man's. Much brighter. A clear, clean, blazing white, like sun on snow. Corin had to narrow his eyes. And the woman's face—
He looked away.
When he looked back, the glass was clear again, and the clerk was counting out silver. Not coppers. Silver. Five coins, six, seven. The woman took them. She put them in a little bag at her waist. She shifted the sleeping baby on her hip. And she looked down at the baby's face, for a long time, the way you look at a stranger's baby in the street, kindly, with a little puzzled frown.
Then she walked away.
"Silver," said Corin. "That much silver. What—"
"The good ones get silver," said Briggs. "The bright ones. The ones that blaze." He did not say anything else. He did not need to.
The third person was a boy.
He was about Juno's age. Perhaps a little younger. Thin, with a dirty face and big boots and a cap pulled down over his ears. He was standing on his toes to reach the counter. And he was not putting his hand on the glass. He was holding it back, against his chest. The clerk behind the glass, an older woman with gray hair and spectacles, was talking to him. Kindly, it looked like. Patiently. And the boy was shaking his head.
"He's been here three days running," said Briggs. "Gets to the front. Gets to the glass. Can't do it. Goes home." A pause. "His mother's sick. The rent's due Friday. He's got a memory of his dad, who died. The last good day they had. Fishing, off the end of the coal wharf. The clerk says it'd fetch a silver. Maybe two."
The boy was still shaking his head. His face was screwed up tight. He was not crying. He was trying very hard not to cry.
Corin put his hand inside his coat.
He took out the Ledger. He opened it. He took out the pale green card that Elara had given him, with her silver stamp on it, the number 4.
Then he put it back.
It was no use. He knew it was no use. The card said no claim of the Bank would be made against him or anyone in his keeping. It did not say the Bank could not buy. Nobody had to sell. That was the Bank's great trick. Nobody ever had to.
He put his hand in his trouser pocket instead. He had six coppers there, and one silver, a thin worn silver with a hole in the middle, that he had been carrying for a week and had forgotten about.
"Briggs," he said. "Can you get that to him? Without him knowing where it came from?"
Briggs looked at the coins in his palm. Then at Corin's face. Then at the boy at the counter, on his toes, with his hand pressed against his chest.
He took the coins.
He did not say anything. He went out through the narrow green door into the hall, and walked along behind the counter, slowly, the way a Warden walks, looking at nothing in particular. He stopped behind the older clerk with the spectacles. He bent down and said something in her ear. He put something in her hand.
The clerk looked at the boy. She looked at whatever was in her hand. Then she smiled at the boy, and said something, and pushed the coins across the glass to him. Not as payment. Just pushed them. And shook her head, and pointed at the door.
The boy stared at the coins. He stared at the clerk. He did not touch the glass. He snatched up the coins and turned and ran, his big boots clattering on the white tiles, all the way down the hall to the side door, and out, and was gone.
Briggs came back. He shut the narrow green door behind him and locked it.
"She said she'd tell him it was a mistake in his favor," he said. "From last time. A rounding." He put the keys back on his belt. "She does that, sometimes. Mrs. Pickett. When she can. Don't tell anyone."
"I won't."
Briggs looked at him for a long moment.
"He'll be back next week," he said. "Or the week after. You know that. Rent's due every Friday. You can't buy him out of it forever."
"I know."
"Then why'd you do it?"
Corin thought about the curl of orange peel in his Ledger. About the old man walking away with three coppers, and no wife. About the young woman looking at her own sleeping baby like a stranger's.
"Because he could still say no," he said. "For one more week. I wanted him to have one more week of saying no."
Briggs said nothing. But after a moment he put his big red hand on Corin's shoulder, briefly, heavily, the way a father might. Then he took it away again.
"Come on," he said. "The side door. Before somebody sees you."
Dusk on the Quay
When Briggs let him out of the black door into the alley, the day was nearly over.
Corin had not known he had been inside so long. The light in the narrow alley had gone gray and thin, and the shadows had come up out of the cobbles and filled it like water filling a ditch. At the far end, where the alley came out onto Lantern Quay, the sky over the river was the color of a bruise, purple and yellow and gray, and the sun was a low red smear behind the warehouses on the far bank.
He walked out of the alley onto the quay, and stopped.
The line was gone. The Bank had closed its great doors for the day. The two porters in green had gone. The white columns stood empty and pale in the dusk, and above them, the gold letters caught the last of the red light and shone faintly, like the embers of a fire. FORGET YOUR TROUBLES. Along the quay, the lamplighters had begun their work. Corin watched one of them, an old man with a long pole and a little flame at the end of it, go from post to post along the river wall, reaching up, and one after another the gemstone lamps came on, cold and blue, in a long line along the water's edge.
The river was very still. It lay flat and dark and shining, with the lamps reflected in it, and the red of the sky, and the black shapes of the boats tied up along the quay. A barge was going past, slow and low in the water, with a lantern hung at its stern. Corin watched it until it was out of sight. Its sail was not red.
He sat down on a bollard at the edge of the quay.
He was more tired than he had ever been in his life. He could feel it in every part of him. In his legs, which would not stop shaking. In his arms. In his eyes, which kept closing on their own. In the back of his neck. And in his chest, over his heart, where the Ledger lay warm against his shirt, with a pale green card tucked between its pages. And under the Ledger, the one faded word.
He took the Ledger out. He opened it on his knee. He took out the stub of pencil he kept in its spine, and he wrote, as fast as he could, in the last of the light, before any of it could go.
Monday. Fourth Desk. Elara.
She would not read the Ledger. Glass went BLACK under it. "Something written in a hand not yours. Or yours but not now."
She read me. Empty rooms. OAKHAVEN on every old door. And a locked door at the end. She's never seen a locked door in 11,040 people.
She sees my pool. Every night. The girl with the gap. She has nothing before the Bank. Taken in at three or four.
I told her I don't know why. That was a lie. My first to her.
Her little black book. 2,405 days lost. Today she lost the orange Gage peeled for her.
OAKHAVEN in the Fourth Desk ledger. 18 yrs ago. Whole village one account. Debt EMPTY. Called in full, 4th hour after midnight, early spring. Collected by: SCRATCHED OUT WITH A KNIFE. Settled in full. "Do not reopen. By order." Red seal: a dome with rays. Archon's.
Kross asked her for Oakhaven yesterday. The day BEFORE he asked for me. Writ signed by Cressida.
He's not following me. He's walking ahead.
She gave me a card. Under audit. No Bank claim on me or mine while open.
My hand left a BLUE print on her desk. It didn't fade. She didn't see. GAGE saw. He covered it with a ledger. Why?
Briggs's slag room. Three old glasses behind a grille, never cleared. Gold. A bell. A field. A man with his hands up. An OAK TREE with a swing.
He stopped. His pencil had gone blunt. His hand was cramping.
He looked at what he had written. Then, under it all, slowly, he added one more line.
She has a gap under the gold. It never grew in.
He closed the Ledger.
And somebody sat down on the bollard next to his.
He had not heard her come. He never did. She was just there, all at once, the way a bird is there on a branch when you look up. Small and thin, in her too-big coat, with her short hair sticking up and the brass key on its string catching the blue light from the lamps.
"Juno," he said.
She did not say anything. She was looking at the river. Her face was very still. And it was wet. Not from rain. There had been no rain all day.
Corin felt his stomach go cold.
"What is it?" he said. "Lyra? Lord Thorne? The barge—"
"They're all right." Juno's voice was small and rough. "I found them. Up past the north locks, like Hessa said. Among all the others. I'm the only one who knows which. They're all right."
"Oren?"
"All right."
"Then what?"
Juno wiped her face with the back of her hand. Hard. The way a child does when she is angry with herself for crying.
"It's Tam," she said.
Corin did not say anything.
He remembered Tam. Tam Hollin, Lady Vivienne's second footman, with his pale narrow face and his quick brown eyes and his ears that stuck out because his hair was cut so short, and the small white scar through his left eyebrow like a tick on a page. Tam breaking his bread in half in the servants' hall on the Hill. Tam laughing at Gorse. Leave off, Gorse. He's from the Langleys' country place. And then Tam folded up small on the ice in the cold room under the Bell, with frost in his hair and his lips gone blue-gray, and lighter, when Corin lifted him, than a bundle of sticks. He remembered the hot sweet tea running down his chin, because his lips would not open. He remembered Wat's great hands lifting him up, gently, the way you lift a baby.
"Mother Grue sent me," said Juno. "From the arches. She said to find you, wherever you were. She said to tell you he's been asking for you. All day. He hasn't said anything else. Not his own name. Not where he hurts. Just you. The man with the rod. The man who carried me. Over and over." She swallowed. "And she said to tell you—"
She stopped. Her mouth shook.
"She said to tell you to come quick," Juno said. "She said she's seen a lot of people go cold, in thirty years under those arches. And she knows the look. And she said he's got it." She looked at Corin at last. Her eyes were red and wet and furious. "She said he won't see the morning."
The lamplighter had reached the end of the quay. The last lamp came on, cold and blue, and he shouldered his long pole and went away into the dusk, whistling.
Corin stood up.
His legs did not shake anymore. He did not know where the tiredness had gone. It was still there, he knew, waiting for him somewhere. But not now.
He put the Ledger back inside his coat, over his heart. He felt for the silver rod in the long narrow pocket on his right side. It was there. It was cold. The deep cold. The cellar cold.
Oh, boy, said Malakor, very quietly, behind his left ear. Oh, boy. You know what she's asking you to come for. Don't you.
Corin did not answer.
He knew. He had known the moment Juno said the name. A dying man, asking for the man with the rod. Not for comfort. Not only for comfort. Tam's mother had cleaned the Third Basement for nine years. Tam knew what a Harvester was. They knew what the rod was for. Tam had been in the cold room under the Bell, in the dark, with the Bank's frozen jars, for hours and hours before they found him. He had seen things down there. Heard things. Things that nobody else alive had seen or heard.
And in a few hours, all of it would turn to Ash.
"How far?" he said.
"The arches under the Dye-Works," said Juno. "Half an hour, running. Less over the roofs."
"Then over the roofs."
Juno stood. She looked up at him. Then she turned and ran, along the quay, toward the dark mouth of an alley between two warehouses, where a drainpipe went up the wall into the last of the light.
Corin ran after her.
Behind him, on the white front of the Bank, the gold letters went dark one by one as the sun went down. And high up, under the roof of the old warehouse, in a great round window like the window of a church, a single lamp was burning. Small and steady and yellow, behind the many panes, among the iron spokes.
He did not look back at it.
But he knew it was there. He felt it on the back of his neck, all the way across the roofs, the way you feel someone watching you from a window. Like a small warm hand on the place over his heart.
