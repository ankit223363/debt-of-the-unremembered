Chapter 25: The Auditor's Question, Part 2 of 5: The Book That Would Not Be Read
The Book That Would Not Be Read
The Ledger lay on the glass between them.
It looked very small there. Very old and very shabby. Its brown leather cover was worn soft and pale at the corners, and cracked along the spine, and stained in one place with a dark ring where a cup had been set down on it, years ago, in some room he could no longer remember. The edges of its pages were a little scorched from the night before, brown and curling, like the edges of a leaf in autumn. A thin strap of leather went round it, tied in a knot. Sticking out of the top, here and there, there were the ends of things he had tucked between the pages. A pressed flower. A scrap of a ribbon. The corner of a ferry ticket. A long black hair that was not his.
Under it, the silver lines in the glass were very still.
Elara looked at it for a long time. She did not touch it. Her bare white hands lay flat on the glass on either side of her small black book, and they did not move.
"May I?" she said.
Corin had not expected that. He had expected her to take it. He had expected her to reach out and pick it up and open it and turn the pages, the way the clerks in the Ministry turned the pages of a file, quickly, without asking.
"You said to bring it," he said. "I've brought it."
"That is not the same as yes."
He looked at her. She looked back at him, with her dark careful eyes, and waited.
He thought of everything in the book. Six years of it. Every name. Every place. Every price he had paid. Lyra. Lord Thorne and the word on his wrist. Vespera and her shop on Hook Lane. Oren and his words, chair and spoon. Juno and her key. Hessa Doon and her barge with the red sail, though not where it had gone, because he did not know. Mother Grue and her arches. Wren in her attic on Mercer Street. And the first page. READ THIS FIRST. And the third line on it.
He thought of her putting her bare hand flat on that page.
"Yes," he said. His voice came out rough. "You may."
She nodded, once. Then she lifted her right hand off the glass, slowly, the way a person lifts a hand to touch a sleeping animal they are not sure of. She held it in the air over the Ledger for a moment. Then she lowered it. And with the very tip of one finger, she touched the cover.
The glass went black.
Not white. Not the white Corin remembered from the Harvesting Section, the blazing white that had filled the sheet of glass when she read him, so bright he had had to look away. Black. It started under the Ledger, in a dark patch the exact shape of the book, as if the book had a shadow and the shadow had soaked into the glass. Then it spread. Out from under the Ledger, across the top of the desk, in every direction, the silver lines going dark one after another as it reached them, like lamps going out down a long street. Toward Corin. Toward Elara. Toward the edges.
Elara pulled her hand back.
She did it fast. Much faster than she had done anything else. Her finger came away from the leather cover as if it had been burned, and she held her hand against her chest, curled up, the way a child holds a hand that has been stung.
The black stopped spreading.
For a moment it hung there, in the glass, a great dark shape like spilled ink, with the Ledger in the middle of it. Then, slowly, it began to fade. The silver lines came back, one by one, flickering, like lamps being lit again. The dark drew in, smaller and smaller, back toward the book. Until there was only a thin black shadow left, just under the edges of the brown leather. And then not even that.
The desk was clear again. The silver lines glittered in the cold light. As if nothing had happened.
By the door, Mister Gage had not moved. But Corin saw that his huge folded hands had come apart. They hung at his sides now, open.
Elara was looking at her own fingertip.
"That," she said quietly, "has never happened before."
Her voice was quite steady. But Corin saw that her face had gone even paler than it had been, and there was a thin line between her dark brows that had not been there a moment ago.
"What did you see?" he said.
"Nothing."
"Nothing?"
"Nothing at all. That is what I mean." She lowered her hand slowly to the glass again. She laid it flat. Not near the Ledger. Well away from it, near her own little black book. "When I touch a thing, Mr. Vane, I see what it holds. A cup holds the lips that drank from it. A door handle holds the hands that turned it. A book holds every hand that wrote in it and every eye that read it and every thought that went into every word. I have read books before. They are very loud. They are like standing in a room full of people all talking at once." She looked at the Ledger. "That book was quiet."
"Quiet."
"Not empty. Quiet. As if there were a great deal in it, more than in any book I have ever touched, and all of it was holding its breath." She was silent for a moment. "And under the quiet there was something else. Something written in a hand that was not yours. In an ink I cannot read through."
Corin felt the cold go down his back.
He thought of a page near the back of the Ledger. A page he had found, a few days ago, in a hand that was his and yet was not his. The same big black letters. The same hard slope. The same way of pressing so hard the pen cut the paper. But shaking. Every letter of it shaking. As if it had been written by him in the dark, or in great pain, or a very long time ago. He had not been able to remember writing it. He still could not.
"Not my hand," he said slowly.
"No. Or—" She stopped. The line between her brows deepened. "Or yours, but not now. Yours from somewhere else. I cannot say it better than that. I am not used to not being able to say things." She looked up at him. "Do you know what is in your own book, Mr. Vane?"
"I thought I did."
"And now?"
"Now I don't know."
They looked at each other across the glass desk. Behind her, through the great round window, a gull went past, white against the pale sky, and its shadow went across the floor between the shelves and was gone.
"I will not read it," said Elara.
She said it simply. As if she were telling him the time.
"Why not?"
"Because it would cost too much." She folded her hands again, one over the other, on the glass. "A reading costs me a day, Mr. Vane. You know that. A man costs a day. A cup costs a day. A door handle costs a day. But a book that has been written in for six years, by a man who has burned as much as you have burned—" She shook her head. "I could feel it, just in that one touch. The weight of it. It would not cost me a day. It would cost me a month. Perhaps a year." She paused. "And I do not have a year to spare."
There was something in the way she said it. A flatness. A very careful flatness, like the flatness of ice on a pond that has not quite frozen through.
"Then why did you tell me to bring it?"
Elara looked at him.
"To see if you would," she said. "And to see what you would do when I touched it."
"And what did I do?"
"You held your breath," said Elara. "And you looked at my hand. And you did not look at the book at all. A man who is afraid of what is in a book looks at the book. A man who is afraid of the person reading it looks at the hand." The dry thing moved at the corner of her mouth. The gold tooth gleamed. "You are not afraid of your Ledger, Mr. Vane. You are afraid of me."
He said nothing. There was nothing to say. It was true.
She reached out, with her gloved hand this time. She had put on one white glove while he was thinking, he had not seen her do it. With the gloved hand she pushed the Ledger gently back across the glass toward him. It slid on the smooth surface, and the silver lines under it did not change at all.
"Put it away," she said. "Keep it close. Whatever is in it, it is yours, and it does not want to be read by anybody else. I respect that. The Bank does not, as a rule. But I do."
Corin picked up the Ledger. It was warm. Much warmer than it should have been, as if it had been lying in the sun. He put it back inside his coat, over his heart, and felt the warmth of it go through his shirt into his skin.
"Now," said Elara. She drew off the white glove again, finger by finger, and laid it on the arm of her chair with the other. And she put her bare hand flat on the glass, palm down, in the middle of the desk, between them. "I will read you instead. If you will let me. It will cost me a day. I am willing to pay it."
She looked at him.
"Put your hand on the glass, Mr. Vane," she said. "Here. Next to mine."
The Palm on the Glass
Corin looked at her hand.
It lay flat on the glass in the middle of the desk, palm down, fingers together. White and thin, with the short clean nails, and the bones showing faintly under the skin at the back. Under it, the silver lines had begun to move again. They were curling toward her, slowly, from every side, the way iron filings curl toward a magnet. Gathering under her palm. Waiting.
He had done this once before. In the Harvesting Section. With Toby watching from his neat little desk, white as paper, and Mister Gage by the door. He had put his bare hand on her sheet of glass because there had been no other way to close Pell's account, and keep the debt from going looking for Juno. And the glass had gone white. Blazing white. And in the white he had seen—
A pool. Green water, and sun on it, and willow branches hanging down. And a small girl standing in the shallows with her skirt tucked up, laughing, with a gap in her front teeth where a tooth had come out. And she had looked up at him, and said, Cor.
He had not known what it meant. He still did not know. He had written it in his Ledger that night, every word, before it could go. And he had read it every morning since.
"What will you see?" he said.
"I don't know," said Elara. "That is why I read."
"Last time—"
"Last time I was reading for an account. Pell Dorran's. I was looking for one thing, and I found it, and I stopped." She did not look away from him. "This time I am not looking for anything. I am only looking." A pause. "I will tell you what I see. All of it. If you wish."
"And if I don't wish?"
"Then I will tell you nothing, and write it in my book, and you may wonder for the rest of your life." The dry thing moved at the corner of her mouth. "Most people prefer to be told."
Corin looked at the glass. At the silver lines curling under her palm. At his own pale face, faint and wavering, under the surface.
Then he put his hand down.
He put it flat on the glass, palm down, next to hers. Not touching. A finger's width apart.
The glass was cold. Colder than he had expected. Cold as the river in winter. The cold went into his palm and up his wrist and into his arm, and he felt his fingers go numb at the tips.
And then the silver lines found him.
He felt them. Not on his skin. Under it. As if the lines in the glass had reached up through the surface and into his hand, thin as hairs, cold as frost, and were running up the inside of his arm, branching and branching, the way they branched in the glass. Up his wrist. Up his forearm. Past his elbow. Toward his shoulder. Toward his chest. Toward the place over his heart where the one faded word lay.
He did not pull away. He wanted to. Every part of him wanted to. But he did not.
Elara shut her eyes.
And the glass went white.
It went white from the middle out, from the place between their two hands, like the sun coming up. Bright. So bright he had to narrow his eyes. The whole top of the desk blazed, from end to end, and the light came up out of it and lit the underside of the roof beams and the spines of the green ledgers and the unfinished face of Mister Gage by the door. It lit Elara's face from below, so that she looked like a face carved out of white stone, with her eyes shut and her lips a little parted.
Corin did not see the pool this time. He did not see anything. He only felt.
He felt her looking. That was the only way he could think of it afterwards. As if somebody were walking through the rooms of a house in the dark, very quietly, with a candle, opening doors. Not touching anything. Just looking. Into this room, and that one, and the next. He felt the candle go past things. He did not know what things. He felt them as shapes, as weights, the way you feel furniture in a dark room with your shins. Some of them were heavy. Some of them were sharp. Some of them were very soft, and warm, and he did not want the candle to go near them, and it did not. It went past.
And some of the rooms were empty.
He felt those too. More than the others. He felt the candle go into a room and find nothing there. Bare boards. Bare walls. A cold, clean, swept emptiness, where something had been once and was not anymore. And then another. And another. Room after empty room. He had not known there were so many. He had known, the way you know a thing from reading it in a book. Burned. Burned. Burned. But he had never felt them like this, one after the other, like a long cold corridor of doors standing open on nothing.
The candle went down the corridor.
It went down a long way. Further than he had known his own house went. Past the last of the rooms he knew. Past the last of the rooms he had burned. Into the dark. Where it was very cold, and very old, and very quiet.
And at the end of the corridor, it stopped.
It stopped in front of a door.
He felt it stop. He felt Elara stop, on the other side of the glass, the way a person stops in the dark when their hand touches something they did not expect. He felt her breath catch. He heard it, in the room, small and sharp.
The door was shut. It was not empty, like the others. It was not burned. It was shut. And it was locked. And on the other side of it, Corin felt, there was something very large. Something that was not asleep. Something that was holding its breath.
The candle stood in front of the door for a long time.
Then, very slowly, it went back.
Back up the corridor. Past the empty rooms. Past the full ones. Back into the light.
Elara opened her eyes.
The white went out of the glass all at once, like a lamp turned down. The silver lines came back. The room was just a room again, long and clean and cold, with the pale light coming through the great round window. Corin's hand was numb to the wrist. He could not feel the glass under it anymore. He could not feel his fingers.
He lifted it.
It came up off the glass slowly, stiffly, the way a hand comes up off ice. He held it against his chest. He could feel the blood beginning to come back into it, pins and needles, prickling.
Across the desk, Elara had not moved. She sat very straight, with her bare hand still flat on the glass, and her eyes open, and she was looking at nothing. At the air between them. Her face was very white. Whiter than before. And the gray under her eyes was deeper, like a shadow.
She had paid. A day. He did not know which day. Perhaps she did not know either.
Corin looked down at the glass.
And saw the print.
It was where his hand had been. Next to hers. A finger's width away. The shape of his palm and his five fingers, pressed into the silver lines in the glass. Very faint. So faint that he would not have seen it at all if the light from the window had not been falling across the desk at just that angle.
It was blue.
Not the blue of the gemstone lamps on the stair, cold and hard. A soft blue. A clear deep blue, like the sky late on a summer evening, just after the sun has gone. It lay in the glass like a breath on a window, and the silver lines that ran through it had turned that color too, every one of them, inside the shape of his hand.
And it was not fading.
He waited. He watched it. A breath on a window fades. He had seen it fade a thousand times. This did not. It stayed exactly as it was. The shape of his palm, and his fingers, and the line of his thumb, in that soft deep summer-evening blue.
He looked up at Elara.
She had not seen it. She was not looking at the glass. She had turned her face a little away, toward the great round window, and she had put her bare hand up to her mouth, and she was pressing the back of it against her lips, hard, as if she were trying very hard not to be sick. Or not to say something. Her eyes were shut again.
Corin looked at the door.
Mister Gage was looking at him.
For the first time, ever, the pale smooth eyes in that unfinished face were looking at something. At Corin. And then, slowly, they went down. To the glass desk. To the soft blue print of Corin's hand, lying next to the place where Elara's hand had been.
And Mister Gage moved.
He came across the room. He came quite quickly, for so big a man, and without any sound at all, his great feet soft on the white boards. He came to the side of the desk. He did not look at Elara. He did not look at Corin. He reached up to the nearest shelf, and took down one of the dark green ledgers, a big heavy one, and laid it on the glass desk.
On top of the blue print.
Exactly on top of it. Covering it completely. As if he were setting it down there by chance, the way a clerk sets a book down on the corner of a desk while he looks for something else.
Then he went back to the door. And folded his huge hands. And stood. And his unfinished face turned toward the window, and did not look at anything at all.
Corin stared at him.
Elara took her hand down from her mouth. She opened her eyes. She turned back from the window and looked at the desk, at the green ledger lying on it, and frowned very slightly, the small polite puzzled frown of a woman who finds a book on her desk that she does not remember putting there.
She did not move it.
"Mr. Vane," she said. Her voice was very quiet. Not quite steady. "I am going to ask you a question. And I would like you to answer it truthfully. If you can."
She folded her bare hands on the glass.
"Where is Oakhaven?"
The Question
"I don't know," said Corin.
It was the truth. It came out of him quite easily, because it was the truth, and because he had said it to himself so many times, in so many dark rooms, that the words had worn smooth.
"I don't know where it is," he said. "I've never known. I don't know if it's a town or a village or a farm or a street. I don't know if it's in Veridia or across the sea. I don't know if it's still there." He looked down at the glass. At the green ledger lying on it, over the place where his hand had been. "I only know the word. It's written in my book. On the first page. In my own hand. You were born in Oakhaven. I wrote it before I forgot. I must have. But I don't remember writing it. And I don't remember Oakhaven."
Elara was watching him. Her dark eyes did not move from his face.
"You are telling the truth," she said.
"Yes."
"I know. I can see it. Not in the glass. In you." She was quiet for a moment. "You have a very honest face, Mr. Vane. It must be very inconvenient for you."
He almost laughed. He did not. He was too tired, and too frightened, and the cold was still in his arm.
"Why do you ask?" he said.
She did not answer at once. She looked down at her own hands, folded on the glass. At the bare white skin. Then she looked up at him again.
"When I read a person," she said slowly, "I walk through them. You felt it. I know you did. I felt you feel it. It is like walking through a house. Each memory is a room. Most people's houses are small, and crowded, and very untidy. Full of furniture. Full of noise. You open a door and there is a birthday, and the next door there is an argument with a brother, and the next door there is a dog that died. Everything is jumbled together. Nothing is where it should be. It is very tiring."
She paused.
"Your house is not like that," she said.
"No."
"Your house is very large. Much larger than most. And most of it is empty." Her voice was quite level. "I have read harvesters before. Burners. Men and women who have paid for a great deal of magic with a great deal of memory. Their houses are always half empty. But not like yours. Yours is nearly all empty. Room after room after room. Swept clean. Bare to the boards." She looked at him. "How do you live like that?"
"I write things down."
"Yes. I saw." A small pause. "But that is not why I asked about Oakhaven."
She unfolded her hands. She laid one of them flat on the glass again, near the edge, away from the green ledger.
"Every empty room in you," she said, "has a word written on the door."
Corin felt something go very still inside him.
"Not all of them," Elara said. "Not the new ones. The rooms you have burned this year, last year, the year before, those doors are blank. But the old ones. The ones at the back. The ones that were emptied long before the others, so long ago that the dust has had time to settle on the floors." She was looking at the glass now, not at him. As if she could still see them, there, in the silver lines. "On every one of those doors, the same word. Very faint. As if somebody had written it there in chalk, a long time ago, and then tried to rub it off, and could not quite manage."
"Oakhaven," said Corin.
"Oakhaven," said Elara.
The gull went past the window again. Or another one. Its shadow slid across the white boards and up the green spines of the ledgers and was gone.
"I have never seen that before," she said. "In anyone. Rooms emptied all at once, and marked. Like a house where somebody has gone through with a list, and taken everything on it, and written on each door what was taken, so that nobody would put anything back."
"Someone emptied me," Corin said. "Six years ago. All at once. I woke up in a long white room with forty beds and I didn't know my own name."
"Yes," said Elara. "I saw that too." She did not say anything else about it. She did not say what she had seen. Corin did not ask. He did not think he wanted to know. Not now. Not here.
"And at the end," she said. "At the very end of the house. Past all the empty rooms. There is a door that is not empty."
Corin said nothing.
"It is locked," said Elara. "I could not open it. I did not try very hard. It did not want to be opened. I have read eleven thousand and forty people, Mr. Vane. I count them. And I have never, ever found a locked door in any of them." Her bare hand moved, just a little, on the glass. "Do you know what is behind it?"
"No."
"Do you know who locked it?"
"No."
She looked at him for a long moment.
"I believe you," she said. "That frightens me more than if you were lying."
She was quiet. Her fingers lay on the glass, very still. And then, slowly, she drew her hand back, and laid it in her lap, out of sight, under the edge of the desk.
"There is one more thing," she said. "And then I will ask you nothing else about it today. I promise you that."
She was not looking at him now. She was looking at the round window. At the pale sky. At the gulls.
"The last time I read you," she said, "in your office in the Ministry. When I was closing the account of Pell Dorran. I saw something. I did not tell you what. It was not part of the account, and it was not my business, and so I did not write it in my book." A pause. "But I have not been able to stop seeing it."
Corin did not breathe.
"A pool," said Elara. "Green water. Sun on it. Willow branches, hanging down to the surface. And a little girl in the shallows, with her skirt tucked up into her belt, and a gap in her front teeth." She was very still. "She was laughing. And she looked up. At you. And she said a word. A name. Cor."
She turned her head and looked at him.
"I see it every night," she said. "When I shut my eyes, before I sleep. Every night since I read you. The pool, and the willow, and the girl with the gap in her teeth. I see it so clearly that I can smell the water. I can feel the sun on the back of my neck." Her voice was very quiet now. "And I have nothing before the Bank, Mr. Vane. Nothing at all. No pool. No willows. No sun. The Bank took me in when I was very small, and there is nothing before that, not one single thing, and I have never minded, because you cannot miss what you never had."
She looked at him with her dark careful eyes.
"So tell me," she said. "Why is your pool in my head?"
Corin opened his mouth.
And shut it again.
He thought of the first page of his Ledger. Of the third line. You had a younger sister named Elara. He thought of the gold tooth, capped over an old gap. It never grew in. He thought of a small girl in the shallows, laughing, with a gap in her front teeth.
He thought of Silas Kross, standing on the scorch mark in Linden Walk, looking up at him with his pale pleasant face. He ran to all the right places. I have them all written down.
He thought: If I tell her, she becomes one of the places.
He thought: If I tell her, and I'm wrong, I'll have put my own lost life into a stranger's head, and she'll carry it forever, and it won't even be hers.
He thought: If I tell her, and I'm right—
He could not finish that one.
"I don't know," he said.
It was the first lie he had told her. He felt it go out of him like a stone dropped into deep water. He felt it sink. He watched her face, and he waited for her to see it, the way she had seen everything else.
She looked at him for a long, long time.
Then she said, very softly, "No. I don't suppose you do."
And she looked away, out of the window, and he could not tell, then or ever afterwards, whether she had believed him.
