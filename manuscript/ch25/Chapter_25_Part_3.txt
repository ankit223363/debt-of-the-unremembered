Chapter 25: The Auditor's Question, Part 3 of 5: The Book of Lost Days
The Book of Lost Days
For a while neither of them spoke.
The light from the round window had moved. It was later than Corin had thought. The sun had gone round to the west, and the light was coming in at a slant now, long and yellow, laying the shadows of the iron spokes of the window across the floor like the spokes of a great wheel. The dust in the air turned slowly in it. Somewhere far below, through the floor and the walls, the murmur of the great hall went on and on. The hum of the glass.
Elara picked up her small black book.
She did it slowly. She held it in both bare hands for a moment, in her lap, looking down at it. It was a plain little book, not much bigger than her palm, bound in black cloth that had gone shiny and soft at the corners from being held. There was a pencil tucked into its spine, a short one, worn down almost to nothing.
She opened it. She took out the pencil. And she wrote something, small and quick, on a page near the back.
Corin could not see what she wrote. But he saw her hand. It was not quite steady.
"May I ask you something?" he said.
She did not look up. "You may ask."
"What's in the book?"
She finished writing. She put the pencil back in the spine. Then she sat for a moment, looking at the page. And then, to his surprise, she turned the book round and laid it on the glass and pushed it across the desk toward him. Open.
"Look," she said.
He looked.
The page was covered in writing. Small and neat and close together, in pencil, slanting a little to the right. It was not words, mostly. It was numbers. Rows and rows of them. And beside each number, a few words.
2,391. Pell Dorran, deceased. Jar, empty. Day lost: unknown.
2,392. C. Vane, Harvester. Glass. Day lost: the morning of my first day at the Fourth Desk. (It used to be there. It is not.)
2,393. Mrs. A. Fell, Gutter Street. Teacup. Day lost: unknown.
And on, and on, down the page. And at the bottom, in fresh pencil, darker than the rest:
2,404. C. Vane. Intake card, at Warden's stand. Day lost: unknown.
2,405. C. Vane. Glass desk. Day lost: —
The last line was not finished.
"Two thousand four hundred and five," said Corin slowly.
"Readings," said Elara. "Since I first sat at a glass. Every one costs a day. So that is two thousand four hundred and five days."
He did the sum in his head. He was not good at sums. He did it slowly.
"That's—"
"Six years and a little over seven months," said Elara. "Yes."
He stared at her.
"But you're—" He stopped. He did not know how old she was. He had never asked. She looked young. Younger than him. Perhaps twenty. Perhaps not even that.
"I am about twenty-one," said Elara. "The Bank has a date for me. It may be right. It does not matter." She took the little book back, gently, and held it in her lap. "The Bank took me in when I was very small. Three, they think. Perhaps four. So I have had perhaps seventeen or eighteen years, with the Bank. That is six thousand days, or a little more. Of which I have spent two thousand four hundred and five." She closed the book. "And I do not get to choose which ones."
"You don't know which day it takes?"
"Never. Not until I go looking for it and find it gone." She looked down at the black cover. "That is why the book says unknown. Mostly. Sometimes I find out. Sometimes I go to remember something, the way you reach for a cup on a shelf without looking, and my hand closes on nothing. And then I know. And I write it in, afterwards, in brackets." She turned the book over in her hands. "I do not remember learning to read. That went a long time ago. I can read, of course. Very well. But I do not remember learning. I do not remember who taught me, or what the first word was, or whether I was proud." A pause. "I do not remember my first winter at the Bank. Not one day of it. I know it happened. I have a ledger with my name in it. But the winter is gone."
She was quiet for a moment.
"And today," she said, "I went to remember the day Mister Gage first brought me an orange. I don't know why. I just thought of it, while you were sitting there with your hand on the glass. I used to think of it often. I was very small. It was the first orange I ever had. He peeled it for me with his big fingers, very slowly, so that the peel came off in one long curl, and he gave me the curl to keep, and I kept it in a drawer until it went hard and brown." She looked down at her book. "And it is gone. I know the words. I have just said them. But there is nothing behind them now. No orange. No curl. No big fingers."
By the door, Mister Gage did not move. His unfinished face was turned toward the window. But Corin, watching him, thought that his huge folded hands had tightened, just a little.
Elara opened the little book again. She took out the stub of pencil. And on the last line, the one that was not finished, she wrote, slowly:
(The orange.)
Then she closed the book and laid it down on the glass, square with the edges, the way it had been when he came in.
Corin sat very still.
He thought of his own Ledger, warm against his chest. Of the long lists in it. Burned: the taste of my mother's soup. Burned: my first friend's name. He thought of all the times he had read those lists by the light of a candle stub and felt nothing, because the words were only words. He thought of Hollis Marsh in his loft full of jars, with his hand over the hole in his chest. There was something here. There was something good.
And he thought of her, sitting at this glass desk at the top of the Bank, every day, for six years, paying a day of her life for every hand she touched. Two thousand four hundred and five times. Not knowing, ever, which day it would be.
"Why do you do it?" he said. "If it costs that much. Why do you keep on?"
She looked at him as if the question did not make sense.
"Because I am good at it," she said. "Because I am the best reader the Bank has had in a hundred years. Because the accounts must be checked, and someone must check them, and if it is not me it will be someone who reads badly, and makes mistakes, and calls in debts that were already paid, and takes things from people that the Bank has no right to take." A small pause. "Do you know how many errors I find, Mr. Vane? In a year? In the Bank's own books?"
"No."
"Hundreds. Every year. Debts called twice. Deposits that were never written down. Memories taken that were never sold." Her voice was quite level. "I put them right. Every one I find. I cannot give back what was taken. Nobody can. But I can stop it being taken again. I can close the account. I can write Settled at the bottom, in my own hand, and stamp it, and then no clerk in this Bank can ever touch that person again."
She folded her hands on top of the little black book.
"That is why," she said. "Because the Bank is very large, and very old, and very hungry. And somebody has to sit at the top of it and count."
Corin looked at her for a long time.
He thought he understood something about her then. Something he had not understood in the Harvesting Section, when she came in with her sheet of glass and her silent giant and her dry voice, and he had thought she was the enemy. She was not the enemy. Or not only. She was a person who had decided, a long time ago, that the only way to bear a terrible thing was to be very, very good at it.
He knew a little about that.
"Thank you," he said. "For showing me."
She looked surprised. Just for a moment. It was the first time he had ever seen her look surprised, and it made her face, briefly, much younger.
"Nobody has ever thanked me for that," she said.
"Nobody's ever been shown it, I expect."
She was quiet.
"No," she said at last. "I don't suppose they have."
She looked down at the green ledger that Mister Gage had laid on the glass. She frowned at it again, slightly, the small puzzled frown. Then she put her gloved hand on it, the one glove she had put back on, and drew it toward her, and opened it. And Corin, before the cover came up and blocked his view, saw the shape on the glass underneath it. Still there. Soft and deep and blue.
But Elara was looking at the ledger, not the glass. And when she had the ledger open in front of her, the shape was hidden again under its pages.
"There is one more thing I wanted to show you," she said, turning the pages with her gloved fingers. "It is the reason I sent for you today. The real reason. Not your Ledger. Not my pool." She found the page she wanted, and stopped. "This."
Settled in Full
She turned the ledger round on the glass, so that it faced him, and pushed it across.
It was old. Much older than it had looked on the shelf. The green leather of its cover had faded to the color of dry moss, and the gold numbers on its spine were worn so thin that Corin could hardly read them. But when he looked closely, he could make out a year. Eighteen years ago. Nearly nineteen.
The pages inside were thick and yellow and soft, like cloth. They were ruled in red ink into columns. Narrow columns, wide columns, columns with headings written at the top in a tall, thin, old-fashioned hand. Account. Debtor. Deposits. Debt. Called. Collected by. Settled. And down the columns, row after row after row, there were names. People's names, mostly. Joseph Harrow, carter. Mary Fenn, widow. Thomas Abel, his son Thomas. And beside each name, figures. Dates. Small neat sums. And at the end of each row, in the last column, under Settled, a little round stamp in silver ink, faded now to gray. The number 4. The Fourth Desk.
Elara put one gloved finger on the page.
"Here," she said.
Corin looked where she was pointing.
It was a row like all the others. It was written in the same tall, thin, old-fashioned hand. But it was different. He saw that at once. It was longer than the other rows. It took up two lines instead of one. And in the first column, under Account, there was not a person's name.
OAKHAVEN. (Village, with its lands, its school, and all its people.)
Corin stopped breathing.
He made himself read the rest. Slowly. Column by column, the way he would have read a file at the Ministry.
Debtor: The whole.
Deposits: None.
Debt: The column was empty. Not a zero. Not a dash. Empty. Clean yellow paper, with the red lines running down it, and nothing written between them at all.
Called: In full. And a date. A day in early spring, eighteen years ago. And beside the date, a time. The fourth hour after midnight.
Collected by: Something had been written here, and then scratched out. Not crossed through. Scratched. With the point of a knife, it looked like, so deep that it had cut right through the soft yellow paper in places. Corin could see the red lines of the next page showing through the cuts. There was nothing left of what had been written there. Not one letter.
Settled: The silver stamp. The number 4. And beside it, in the same tall, thin hand, two words.
In full.
And down in the margin, beside the whole row, in a different ink, a dark brown ink that might once have been black, someone had written a note. The hand was smaller than the other, and pressed harder, and not as steady.
Do not reopen. By order.
And under that, a second stamp. Not silver. Red. A small red circle, with something inside it. Corin bent close to see. It was a shape. A simple shape. A dome, with lines coming out of it, like rays. Like a sun rising.
He had seen that shape before. Last night. Small and gold, painted on the door of a cream-and-gold carriage with four white horses, that had rolled forward without a sound and closed off the top of the lane behind the Memory Bourse.
He sat back slowly in his chair.
Elara was watching him.
"I found that this morning," she said quietly. "At about seven o'clock. After I had read your intake card at the Warden's stand." She folded her gloved hand over her bare one, on the glass. "When I read the card, I saw the doors. The empty rooms. The word on every door. Oakhaven. I had never heard it before. Not once. So I came up here, and I did what I always do when I find a word I do not know. I went to the ledgers."
She looked along the shelves. At the hundreds of green spines, standing in their perfect rows.
"There are a hundred years of accounts on those shelves," she said. "Every one the Fourth Desk ever audited. I have read most of them. Not with my hand. With my eyes. In the evenings, when the hall is closed. It is how I learned to do my work." A pause. "And in a hundred years of accounts, Mr. Vane, that is the only row where a whole village is written down as one debtor. And it is the only row in which the debt column is empty."
Corin looked at the empty column. The clean yellow paper between the red lines.
He found that he could not stop looking at it. He had read a great many files in the Ministry. He had read death reports and harvest reports and intake lists. He had read the names of people who had burned themselves hollow and the names of people who had been found in the river and the names of children who had been taken into the Ministry's keeping because there was nobody else to keep them. He had learned, over six years, to read a page the way a carter reads a road, quickly, looking for the holes. And he had learned that the worst things were nearly always in the empty places. In what was not written down.
This was the emptiest place he had ever seen.
He thought of all the things that might have been written in that column. A sum. A date. The name of a loan. Seed corn, three years' worth. A new roof for the schoolhouse. A bridge. Things a village might owe. Ordinary things. And there was nothing. Just the red lines going down the page, side by side, like the two rails of a road with nothing on it.
A schoolhouse with a bell, Briggs had said. A big oak tree.
He thought of the first page of his Ledger. You were born in Oakhaven. He had read that line every morning for six years. Two thousand mornings, more or less. He had never known what it meant. He had imagined it, sometimes, in the dark, before he slept. A town by the sea. A farm on a hill. A street of narrow houses, like Tallow Street. He had never imagined this. A row in a ledger. A whole village written down as one name, like a man. And a debt that was not there.
Steady, boy, said Malakor, very quietly, behind his left ear. Steady. Keep your face still. She's watching it.
He kept his face still. He did not know how well he did it.
You knew, he thought, at the voice. Didn't you. You knew there was something.
The voice was silent for a long time. When it came again, it was lower than he had ever heard it. Not dry. Not mocking. Only old.
I knew a schoolhouse, said Malakor. With a bell. A long time ago. I knew a man who taught in it, and cut spirals on silver rods in the evenings, by the stove, for the love of it. I knew a girl who put her hand on a glass in that schoolhouse and made it blaze so bright that the master said a word I've never forgotten. A pause. I didn't know there was a ledger. I swear that to you, boy, on whatever I've got left to swear on. I didn't know they wrote it down.
Who's they?
But Malakor did not answer that. He never answered that.
Corin made himself look up from the empty column. He made himself look at Elara's face instead. Pale and tired and very still, across the glass desk, with the light from the round window behind her making a soft edge round her short dark hair.
She had been waiting. She had seen something go across his face. He knew she had. She did not ask what it was.
"What does that mean?" he said. "An empty debt."
"It means," said Elara, "that Oakhaven did not owe the Bank anything."
She let that sit for a moment.
"It means," she said, "that somebody called in a debt that did not exist. From a whole village. At four o'clock in the morning, on one night in early spring. And collected it. All of it. In full. And then scratched out the name of whoever did the collecting, so deep it cut the page. And then wrote in the margin that nobody must ever look at it again." Her voice was very level. Very careful. The voice of a woman finding an error in a column of figures. "And then stamped it with a seal that does not belong to the Bank at all."
"Whose seal is it?"
She was quiet for a long moment.
"I do not know," she said. "Not for certain. I have seen it once before. On a letter. A very old letter, in the Bank's own archive, in the vault under this building. It was a letter from the Ministry to the Treasurer of the Bank. Many years ago." She looked at him. "It was signed by the Archon."
Corin felt the room go cold around him.
"The Archon," he said.
"Or someone who was allowed to use his seal. Yes."
They sat in silence. Far below, through the floor, the hum of the glass went on. A hundred people forgetting at once. Somewhere a clock struck the half hour, a long way off, soft and deep, like a bell under water.
"I read the page," Elara said. "After I found it. I put my bare hand on it. I wanted to know who had scratched out the name."
"What did you see?"
"Nothing." She looked down at the ledger. "Somebody has read it before me. Long ago. And when they had finished reading, they burned what they had read out of the page. On purpose. The way you would burn a letter after you had read it. There is nothing left in it now. It is clean. Cleaner than any page I have ever touched." She paused. "That cost me a day too. For nothing."
She drew the ledger back across the glass toward her. She closed it. And she laid both her hands on the cover, one gloved and one bare.
"I am the Auditor of the Fourth Desk," she said. "That account has my desk's stamp on it. Settled. In full. My desk said that. Eighteen years ago, before I ever sat here, but still my desk." She looked at him. Her dark eyes were very steady. "And it is wrong. I know it is wrong. A debt that was never owed cannot be settled. It can only be stolen. And I do not let errors stand in my books, Mr. Vane. Not one. Not ever."
"It says Do not reopen."
"It does," said Elara. "I can read."
The gold tooth gleamed.
And for the first time since he had met her, Corin saw something in her face that he recognized. Not the dry thing at the corner of her mouth. Not the careful stillness. Something under it. Something stubborn and hard and bright, like the tip of a nail. He had seen it before. He was sure he had seen it before. In a small face, laughing, in the shallows of a green pool, with a gap in its front teeth.
He looked away.
"What do you want from me?" he said.
