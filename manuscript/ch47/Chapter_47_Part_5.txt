Chapter 47: Oren's Barrier, Again, Part 5 of 5: Marks
Marks
The mews was empty.
The men had gone. The carriage had gone. There were tracks in the snow, a great many of them, trampled and crossing, going away toward the Crescent end, and in the middle of them a long dark smear where something heavy had been dragged, and drops of dark beside it, going away too. Already the new snow was filling them in. In an hour there would be nothing. A smooth white mews, with the backs of the tall houses rising up on one side and the shut coach-house doors on the other, and the glass on the top of the far wall wearing its little white caps.
Oren walked down the middle of it, toward the wall, with the wrapped sword in his good hand and his left arm hanging.
There was a board on the coach-house door opposite Madame Hesper's. He had seen it on the way in. A painted board, with letters on it. He had read it. He did not remember now what it had said. Something about horses. Something about a name. He looked at it as he passed.
Marks.
Black, on a white board, with snow on the top edge. A row of them. Some tall, some short, some round. He could see every one. He could have drawn them, if he had had a piece of chalk. He could not have told you what a single one of them meant.
He walked on.
At the end of the mews, where it turned toward the alley, there was a post, with a little iron plate nailed to it at the height of a man's eyes. Blue, with white letters. The name of the mews. Every street in the High Quarter had one. He had read hundreds of them in his life, walking behind the Thornes. He had never once thought about them.
He stopped in front of it.
He stood there in the snow and looked at it for a long time. A blue plate. White marks. Neat. Painted by somebody careful. Round ones and straight ones, in a line, and he knew that the line was a name, and that the name was the name of the place where he was standing, and that if anybody asked him where he was, he would not be able to say.
It was a strange feeling. He tried to find the edge of it, the way you feel with your tongue for the edge of a broken tooth.
It was not like the words. The six words. When he reached for spoon, there was a gray smooth place, and he could feel it, and go round it. The thing you eat soup with. It was a hole in a wall. You could see through it to the other side.
This was not a hole. There was no other side. He looked at the blue plate and there was nothing behind it at all. It was not that he had forgotten what the marks meant. It was that they had never meant anything. They had always been marks. Shapes. Like the patterns that frost makes on a window. He could not remember, now, what it had been like when they were anything else.
He thought of the old woman with the stick in the dame school in the north. Rapping his knuckles. Again, Oren Vance. Again. A is for apple. What is A for?
He did not know what A was for.
He did not know what A was.
He stood in front of the blue plate in the snow, a big man in a coat too short in the arms, with a bundle of bloody sacking in his hand. And for the first time since the arch in the autumn, since the drums on the Saltmarsh, since he had started losing pieces of himself one by one to keep other people alive, Oren Vance was afraid.
Not of the men. Not of the Watch, or the woman in the gray gloves, or the club that had come down on his shoulder. He had been afraid of those things all his life, in the ordinary way, the way a soldier is afraid, and it had never stopped him.
He was afraid of the next time.
Because there would be a next time. There always was. Another door, another window, another wall that needed something big. And he had burned the letters. And the next biggest thing that was not a person was—
He did not know. He stood there and looked at himself, the way he had looked in the shop with his eyes shut. And there was not much left that was not a person. His hands knew the sword. His feet knew the march. His eyes knew how to count men in a street. Those were what he used to keep the house. He could not burn those and still be a guard.
And behind them, there was only the house itself. The faces. His mother at the window with the spoons. His brother at the table. The Lady Isolde on the stair. A girl at the pond, asking why the ducks did not get cold feet.
The next time, he would have to choose one of those.
He turned away from the blue plate.
He went round the end of the mews, past the high wall with the glass on top, and down the crooked alley Bridie Kane had told him, in the dark, with the snow coming down. Left at the bottom. He did not need to read anything for that. He had been told. Left, and down, and a gate in a wall that was never shut.
He would have to be told everything now.
He found that he could bear that. He had been told things all his life. Where to stand. Which door. Captain, take Lyra to the park. A guard is a man who is told. He did not mind being told.
What he minded was that the girl would have to do the telling.
He went on down the alley toward the gate.
Bald Heads
The gate in the back wall of Fothergill's yard was not shut. Bridie Kane had been right. It was a narrow gate of gray boards in a high brick wall at the bottom of a crooked alley, with no latch on it at all, only a loop of rope, and it swung open when Oren pushed it with his good shoulder, and he went through.
The yard was full of snow, and barrels, and dusk.
It was nearly dark now. The snow was still coming down, slower, the flakes smaller and drier, so that they hissed a little when they landed. In the last of the gray light he could see the shapes of the barrels standing about the yard under their white caps, a dozen of them, like fat men waiting for something. A pump in the corner, with a long icicle hanging from its spout. A low wall, the one he had climbed over yesterday behind the girl. And the back of the wigmaker's shop, with one window lit, a small square of yellow, low down, by a back door.
And on the back step, under the overhang of the roof, a shape. Small. Sitting. With its knees drawn up and its arms round them, in a coat that was too big, and something gray and silken coming out of the bottom of the coat and lying on the step in the snow, with pins in it, catching the light from the window like frost.
She was looking at the gate.
She had been looking at the gate, he understood, for a long time. Since she came down off the roof. Since before it got dark. Sitting on the cold step in her mother's gown under her coat, in her boots now, with her arms round her knees, looking at the gate in the wall and waiting for it to open.
It opened, and he came through it.
She did not get up. She did not run to him. She sat on the step and looked at him coming across the yard toward her through the snow, between the barrels, slowly, with his left arm hanging and a long bundle of sacking in his right hand. And he saw her look at his face. And he saw her see. He did not know what she saw. He did not know what his face was doing. But she saw it, and her arms round her knees went tight.
He stopped at the bottom of the step.
"Hesper," she said.
He did not say anything.
He did not need to. He stood at the bottom of the step in the snow and looked at her, and she looked back at him, and she knew.
She did not cry. Not then. She put her face down on her knees, on the rough brown cloth of her coat over the gray silk, and stayed like that, very still, with her shoulders not moving. For a long time. And Oren stood at the bottom of the step with the snow falling on his hat and his shoulders, and did not move either. A guard waits.
At last she lifted her head.
"How?"
"At her door," said Oren. "Holding it. With her shears." He paused. "She cut him. The big one. Before."
The girl closed her eyes.
"And her pins," said Oren. He did not know why he said it. He had not meant to. "She had them in her mouth. All four. Still." He swallowed. "I took them out. I put them in the cushion. On her wrist. Where they go."
The girl made a sound.
It was not a sob. It was smaller than that and worse. And then she put her face down on her knees again, and this time her shoulders moved.
Oren stood in the snow.
After a while he came up the step, slowly, and sat down beside her on it, under the overhang, with his back against the door, and laid the wrapped sword across his knees. He did not put his arm round her. He had never done that. He was the captain of her father's guard. He sat beside her on the step, one pace to her left, so that his body was between her and the gate, and he let her cry.
"Agnes," he said, after a long time.
The girl lifted her head.
"Her name," said Oren. "Before she was Hesper. The step woman told me. Her husband called her Aggie. Nobody else ever did."
The girl looked at him. Her face was wet and red and very young.
"Agnes," she said.
"Yes."
She said it again, under her breath, the way you say a word to learn it. Agnes. And then she wiped her face on the sleeve of her coat, hard, and was quiet.
The back door behind them opened.
A little man stood in it. Very little. Not much taller than the girl, sitting. Bald, completely bald, with a head as round and pink and shining as one of his own wooden ones, and a fringe of gray whisker round his jaw, and a pair of half-moon spectacles on the end of his nose. He had a lamp in one hand. In the other, a parcel, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string.
He looked at Oren. At the sacking bundle across his knees. At the blood on Terrence's coat, which Oren had not known was there until he saw the little man look at it.
Then he looked at the girl.
"You'll come in," he said. "Both of you. Before you freeze to my step. I've a fire." He stood back from the door. "Mind the heads."
They went in.
The back room of Fothergill's shop was the room Oren had come through yesterday, behind the girl, very fast, on the way to the wall. He had not looked at it then. He looked at it now. It was small and warm and low, with a fire in a little grate, and a workbench under the window covered in combs and pins and curling tongs and pots of paste. And all round the walls, on shelves, from the floor to the ceiling, the heads.
Wooden heads. Dozens of them. Hundreds. All the same size, all the same shape, smooth and round and pale, with no hair on them at all. Painted faces. Pink cheeks, and blue eyes, and small red mouths, all the same, all a little smiling, all looking out into the room. In the firelight they seemed to move. To turn a little toward you, when you were not looking, and turn back when you were.
The girl sat down on a stool by the fire, among the bald heads, in her coat and her mother's gown with the pins in it. Oren stood by the door. The little man put the lamp on the workbench.
Then he held out the parcel to the girl.
"For the lady," he said. "I was going to send it round to the Needle and Thimble in the morning. With my compliments." He did not look at Oren. "I don't suppose there's any point sending it there now."
The girl looked at the parcel in his hand.
She took it. She untied the string. She folded back the brown paper, on her knee, slowly, the way you open a thing that you are afraid of.
Inside it, coiled round and round on itself, long and dark and shining, with red in it where the firelight caught it, was her hair.
She did not move.
"I had it in my window," said the little man. "Since Monday. Four crowns, I put on it. It's worth six." He looked at the bald heads on the shelves. "Madame Hesper came by yesterday afternoon, after you'd gone through my yard with the big gentleman. She came in at the front, and stood at my counter in her black dress, and looked at me over her spectacles, and said, Fothergill, that is a Thorne's hair in your window, and I want it out of there by tomorrow. And she put four crowns on my counter. In silver. Counted out." He paused. "I told her it was worth six. She said she knew. She said she'd been buying from me for thirty years and I'd been cheating her for twenty-nine of them, and it was time I gave her something back."
The fire ticked.
"There's a card," said the little man. "In with it. She wrote it at my counter. She said to put it in."
The girl found it. A small white card, tucked into the coil of hair. She held it to the firelight and read it. Oren watched her read it. He watched her lips move, the way they moved when she read something that mattered. And then he watched her face.
She held it out to him.
She did not say anything. She held it out, across the little firelit room, between the bald smiling heads, with her hand shaking. As if it were too heavy to hold alone. As if somebody else had to read it too.
Oren took it.
He looked at it.
A small white card. Stiff. Clean. With writing on it in black ink. Four lines. A sharp, slanting hand, fierce and quick, every letter leaning forward as if it were in a hurry to get somewhere. He knew that hand. He had seen it on the slate in the passage, an hour ago. He had seen it on the tags pinned to the ends of the bolts of cloth in the shop.
Marks.
Thin, black, slanting. Like the tracks of a bird in the snow. Four rows of them. He looked at them. He looked at them very hard, the way you look at a face across a crowded room. He turned the card a little toward the fire, as if the light were the trouble. It was not the light.
Nothing came.
No word. No thing in his head. Just the marks, beautiful and black and meaning nothing, on a small white card, in the hand of a woman who was sitting dead against a whitewashed wall in a passage on Velvet Street with four pins in a row on the cushion on her wrist.
He held it for a long time.
Then he held it out again. Back to the girl.
"Lady," he said.
She looked up at him.
"I can't read it," said Oren Vance.
The girl looked at him across the firelit room. She did not understand. He saw her not understand. He saw her look at the card in his hand, and at his face, and back at the card.
"The wall," he said. "In the shop. It needed something big." He looked at the card. "I gave it the letters. All of them."
The bald heads on their shelves looked down at them, pink-cheeked and blue-eyed and a little smiling, in the moving light of the fire.
The girl took the card out of his hand. Very gently. The way he had taken the pins out of Madame Hesper's mouth.
And then, slowly, in a voice that was not steady at all, holding the card up to the firelight so that he could see her looking at it, she read it to him.
"For Lady Lyra Thorne," she read. "Her own. Not to be sold again. Grow it long, child, and wear it up, the way she did. A.H."
Oren stood by the door and listened.
It was the first thing anybody had read to him since the letters went. It would not be the last. From now on, everything would be read to him, or nothing would.
He found that he did not mind it. He found that he would have liked her to read it again. And again. Slowly. So that he could carry it in his head, the way he carried the list of the words that were gone, and go through it every morning before he opened his eyes.
Grow it long, child, and wear it up.
Outside, in the dark, the snow went on falling on the yard and the barrels and the gate in the wall that was never shut. And far off, over the roofs, from the river, the bells of the city began to strike the sixth bell. One after another. Saint Clement's first, deep and slow, a little before the others. Then the Ministry, high on the Hill. Then all the rest, tumbling.
Oren counted them.
He could still count. That was something. He stood among the bald wooden heads in the wigmaker's back room with his left arm hanging and the big man's blood on his coat, and counted the bells, one to six, and found that every one of them was still a number.
