Chapter 47: Oren's Barrier, Again, Part 4 of 5: The Stair
The Stair
The back room was wrecked.
He saw it in one look, going through, the way you see a room by lightning. The long table knocked crooked across the floor, its white sheet dragged half off, the bolts of cloth tumbled. One of the dummies lying on its back in the middle of the boards with its wooden arms out and its half-made gown spread round it like a woman who has fainted. The cheval glass on its side, cracked. The little wooden box that the girl had stood on, kicked into the corner. Scraps of gray silk on the floor. Pins everywhere, glittering. And on the hot black top of the stove, the kettle, still singing, as if nothing had happened at all.
And to his left, the dark passage. Going away to the green door.
He did not stop. He did not let himself stop. But his eyes went down the passage as he passed the end of it, because they were a guard's eyes and they always went where the danger had come from.
The green door was open. Hanging. One hinge torn out of the frame, so that it leaned inward at a slant, and the snow-light from the mews came in round it, gray, and lay along the floor of the passage. And at the bottom of the door, on the floor, against it, a shape. Sitting. Tall and thin and black, with its back against the broken door and its legs out straight on the stones, the way a tired woman sits down at the end of a long day.
He did not stop.
He went up the stair.
It was a narrow stair, and steep, and dark. Bare wooden treads, worn in the middle, with a rail on one side and the wall on the other, going up in short straight flights with a turn and a small landing at each floor. The kind of stair that is built at the back of a house for servants to carry coal up. There was no light on it but what came down from above. And something was coming down from above. Cold. A long breath of cold air, with snow in it, falling slowly down the stairwell from the top of the house, where the skylight was open.
And the footsteps were above him.
Heavy. Slow. Not hurrying. The footsteps of a man who has never had to hurry in his life, because nothing has ever been able to get away from him. He was on the second flight. Oren could hear the treads groan under him, one at a time. He could hear him breathing. Thick, and wet, through the nose.
Oren went up the first flight in four strides, with the sword in his right hand and his left hand on the rail.
He was slow. He knew it at once. He had always been fast on a stair. Twenty-two years of stairs at Crown Walk, up and down, night and day. But there was a hollow in him now where the letters had been, a great raw cold hollow going down through everything, and it had taken something with it. Not strength. Something under strength. The thing that makes your legs go where you send them without being told twice. His legs went where he sent them. But slowly. As if he were wading.
He came round the turn onto the first landing.
And the man was there. On the second landing above him, at the top of the next short flight. He had heard Oren coming, and stopped, and turned round. And was looking down.
He was very big.
Not tall, the way Oren was tall. Big. Wide. A great slab of a man, as wide as the stair, with shoulders that touched the wall on one side and the rail on the other, and a neck as thick as his head. A red face. Very red, the color of raw meat, with small pale eyes in it, and a thin wet mouth. He had no hat. His hair was pale and cropped close to a skull like a bucket. He was holding something in his right hand, down by his leg. A club. Short. Thick. Wood bound with iron. There was something on the end of it, dark, and it was dripping, very slowly, onto the stair.
Dace. Oren knew him. He had never seen him. But Corin had written him in the Ledger. The big man. Huge. He held the lamp in the lane, the night Pell died. Very strong. Very quiet. And Wat had hit him in a cellar, once, in the autumn, like a falling wall.
Dace looked down at him. The small pale eyes went to the sword in Oren's hand. Then back to Oren's face.
He did not say anything. He was very quiet.
Then he turned his back, and went on up the stair.
Oren went after him.
He took the next flight two treads at a time, and at the top of it, on the second landing, he lunged. Not to kill. There was no room on a stair like this to swing a sword, and a thrust into that great back would go in and stick and not stop him. He went low. For the back of the leg. The big cord behind the knee, the one the rod had cut in Corin's. The one that makes a man go down.
Dace was not there.
He had turned. Quick. Quicker than anything that size had a right to be. He turned on the stair with his back to the wall, and the sword went past his leg into the tread, and stuck there in the old wood with a jar that went up Oren's arm into his shoulder. And the club came down.
Oren saw it come. He could not get out of the way. There was no way. He twisted, and it caught him on the left shoulder instead of the head, and the shoulder went white. Not pain. White. Like the barrier going white. A great white flash that went down his arm into his fingers and up his neck into his teeth, and his left hand let go of the rail, and he went down on one knee on the stair.
The club went up again.
Oren pulled the sword out of the tread. He did not try to stand. He pushed up from his knee, under the club, close, too close for it to come down properly, into the man. Into the great slab of his chest. Shoulder first. His bad shoulder. It went white again and he did not care. He drove into Dace the way he had driven into a door once, at Crown Walk, a long time ago, with nine men behind it and a broken arm.
Dace went back against the wall. Not far. A step. The wall stopped him. And Oren had the sword short, in his right hand, low, between them, and he put it in.
Into the leg. The top of the thigh. As hard as he could.
Dace made a sound.
It was the first sound he had made. Not a scream. A grunt. Deep, and surprised, the sound a bull makes when it has walked into a gate it did not know was shut. The club fell out of his hand and went bouncing down the stair, end over end, bang, bang, bang, and hit the bottom.
And then he fell.
Not back. Forward. Onto Oren. The whole great weight of him, all at once, the way a wall falls. And they went down the stair together.
Oren did not remember the stair. He remembered the rail going past. He remembered the corner of a tread hitting him in the back, and then another, and the man's weight on top of him, and the smell of him, beer and onions and wet wool. He remembered the landing hitting him all along his body like a slammed door. He remembered lying there with the breath gone out of him, and the great weight rolling off, and a roaring that he thought was in his own head.
It was not in his head. It was Dace. Lying on the landing beside him, on his back, with both his big red hands round his own thigh, and the blood coming through his fingers, roaring. Like a bull. Like a man who has never been hurt before in his life and does not know what to do about it.
Oren got up.
He did not know how. His shoulder was white. His back was white. There was something wrong with his breathing. He got up anyway, on the landing, with his back to the wall, and found that he still had the sword in his hand. He looked at it. The blade was red to the hilt.
He looked up the stair.
The cold came down it. And the snow. Slowly, in the gray light, falling down the stairwell from the open skylight four floors above, the big soft flakes coming down past the turns of the rail, past the landings, past him. He watched one fall all the way down past his face and land on the back of his hand on the hilt of the sword, and not melt.
No footsteps above. No sound at all. The skylight was open, and the snow was coming in, and nobody was up there.
She was gone. Over the roofs. To the left.
He looked down.
At the foot of the stair, in the back room, among the pins and the scraps of gray silk and the fallen dummy, there were men. Three of them. Coming in from the shop. One with the iron bar. One with a club. The young one with the thin mustache, white-faced, with a knife. They had come through the window when the wall went, through the gold dust, and through the shop, and now they were at the bottom of the stair, looking up. At Dace, roaring on the landing with his hands round his leg. At Oren above him, with his back to the wall and the red sword.
And behind them, in the door from the shop, tall and pale and empty, with gold dust on her gray shawl like pollen, the woman in the gray gloves.
Oren moved.
He stepped over Dace, slowly, carefully, the way you step over a dog that might bite. And stood at the top of the lower flight, on the landing, with the wall at his back and the rail at his side. The stair below him was narrow. One man at a time. One man, coming up, into a red sword, with nowhere to swing a club.
A guard keeps the house.
This was the house now. This one flight of stairs. He could keep it.
"Come up," he said.
It came out of him low and rough and nearly steady.
The three men at the bottom looked at each other.
The woman in the doorway did not look at anyone. She began to walk across the back room toward the stair, without any hurry at all, peeling off one of her gray gloves.
The Rattle
She took the glove off finger by finger, the way a lady takes off her glove at a ball.
Oren watched her do it from the landing. He could not look away. The thick gray leather came down off each long pale finger, and the hand underneath was white. Not pale. White. The white of a fish's belly, of a thing that lives in the dark. And as the air touched it, a mist came off it. A faint gray breath, like the breath that comes off a block of ice in a fishmonger's window in summer. It curled up round her wrist and hung there.
She put the glove in the pocket of her gray apron.
Then she came to the foot of the stair.
The three men at the bottom got out of her way. Quickly. Without being told. The young one with the thin mustache pressed himself back against the wall so hard that his head knocked a picture crooked, and he did not notice. They did not look at her. They looked at the floor, the way the clerks on the Ministry Steps looked at the floor when an Inquisitor went by.
She put her foot on the first tread.
Dace was still roaring. He had stopped, for a moment, and started again, lower now, a long groaning roar through his teeth, with his hands round his leg and his heels drumming on the boards of the landing. The woman did not look at him. She came up the stair toward Oren, one tread at a time, with her bare white hand held a little out from her side, and the mist curling off it, and her empty face turned up toward him.
Oren held the sword out in front of him. Low. Pointing down the stair at her.
She did not stop.
She was going to take hold of the blade. He knew it. She was going to put her bare white hand round the red blade of his sword, the way you take hold of a stick that a child is holding out to you, and the cold was going to go up it. Up the steel. Into the hilt. Into his hand, and his arm, and his chest, into the raw hollow where the letters had been, and he would not be able to let go. He had felt what her glove could do through a wall of light. He did not want to know what her hand could do through iron.
She was on the fourth tread. The fifth.
He could feel her already. The cold coming off her. Coming up the stair ahead of her, the way the cold comes up out of a cellar when you open the door. It touched his face. His hands. The blood on his sword went dark, and dull, and then, at the very tip, where the blade was nearest her, white. A fine white fur. Frost.
The sixth tread.
She lifted her hand.
And outside, in Velvet Street, somebody began to swing a rattle.
Oren knew the sound. Everybody in the city knew it. A watchman's rattle. A thing of wood, on a handle, with a flat clapper that went round and round when you swung it, and made a noise like a giant tearing calico. It cut through the snow and the cold and the quiet like a knife through a sheet. Rrrrrrrrrrrrrrr. Very loud. Very close. In the street outside the shop.
And then another. Further off. At the Crescent end. Answering.
And then a whistle. And another. And a man's voice, big and hoarse, shouting something about the Hill and broken glass and in the name of the Archon. And the sound of boots. A great many boots, running in the snow, which you could not hear in the snow, except that there were so many of them.
The woman on the stair stopped.
She did not turn her head. She stood on the sixth tread with her white hand lifted, a foot from the frosted tip of his sword, and the mist curling off it, and she looked at Oren's face.
And from the street, through the shop, through the broken window, clear and high above the rattles and the boots, came another sound. A whistle. Not a watchman's whistle. A small, sweet, two-note whistle, like a man calling a dog he is fond of. Up, and then down.
The woman lowered her hand.
She stood there a moment longer. Then she took the gray glove out of her apron pocket and began, without any hurry at all, to put it on again. Finger by finger. And when it was on, she turned round on the narrow stair, and went down, one tread at a time, the way she had come up.
At the bottom, she said something to the three men. Oren did not hear what. It was two words, perhaps three, in a voice so flat it hardly moved the air. And the three men came up the stair, past her, toward him.
He lifted the sword.
They did not come for him. They came for Dace. Two of them took him under the arms, and the third took his legs, and they lifted him, roaring, a great red slab of a man with the blood running down his leg and dripping on the treads. And they carried him down the stair like a sack, bumping, and through the back room, past the fallen dummy and the singing kettle, and into the dark passage. Toward the green door. Toward the mews.
Oren heard them go. He heard them stop at the green door, and swear, and lift something, heavy, out of the way. He heard it fall against the wall.
Then he heard the boots in the mews. And then nothing.
The woman in the gray apron was the last to go. She stood at the foot of the stair and looked up at him one more time. At his face. At the sword. At the place in his chest, under Terrence's coat, where the letters had been. She looked at it as if she could see it. The hollow. The way you look at a well, to see how deep it goes.
Then she turned and went out through the back room after the others, and did not look back.
And Oren Vance stood on the landing of Madame Hesper's back stair, alone, with the snow coming down past him from the open skylight four floors up, and the frost on the tip of his sword slowly turning to water, and the rattles going round and round in Velvet Street. And his knees went, at last, and he slid down the wall and sat on the landing with the sword across his knees, in Dace's blood, and breathed.
He did not know how long.
Long enough for the rattles to stop, and start again, further away, and stop. Long enough for a man's voice to come in through the broken window of the shop, shouting Hullo? Hullo in there? Watch! and go away again when nobody answered. Long enough for the kettle in the back room, at last, to boil dry, and begin to tick, and smell.
Then he got up.
He went down the stair. Slowly. Holding the rail with his good hand. Through the back room. He took the kettle off the stove as he passed, without thinking, and put it on the floor. Then he went into the dark passage, toward the green door, where the snow-light came in gray round the edges of the broken frame.
And she was there.
Madame Hesper
They had moved her to get the door open.
She was sitting against the wall of the passage now, beside the broken green door, where they had dragged her, with her back to the old whitewashed brick and her long legs out straight on the stone floor in front of her, in their black stockings and their black buttoned boots. Her hands were in her lap. Her head was a little on one side, against the wall, the way a woman's head goes when she has sat down for a moment at the end of a long day and closed her eyes.
Her eyes were not closed.
Oren knelt down beside her.
It hurt to kneel. His shoulder, and his back, and something in his side. He did not notice. He knelt on the cold stones of the passage with the snow-light coming gray round the broken door onto her face, and he looked at her.
The club had hit her once. On the side of the head, above the ear, where the iron-gray hair was pulled back tight. Once was enough, from a man like that. There was not much blood. A little, in her hair. A little, on the whitewash behind her, where her head had gone back against it. Her spectacles had come off their silver chain and lay on the floor beside her hand, folded, the way she always folded them, as if she had taken them off and put them down herself.
And in her right hand, in her lap, the shears.
The big black dressmaker's shears, as long as a forearm, that she cut silk with. She was still holding them. Her long fingers were still through the handles. And one of the blades was dark, all along its length, and there were drops of dark on the stones of the passage by the door, a line of them, going out into the mews, where the men had gone.
She had cut him.
Before he hit her. She had stood with her back to her own green door, with the bolts gone and the key gone and the hinge tearing out of the frame, and when it came open she had been standing there, and she had cut the big man who came through it. Oren saw it. He saw it as clearly as if he had been there. A tall thin old woman in black, sixty-four years old, with knees like two bags of gravel, standing in a doorway with a pair of shears. Not in my shop.
And in the corner of her mouth, the pins.
Four of them. Still there. Sticking out from between her lips at the left side, one beside the other, neat, the way they had always been, like the whiskers of a cat. She had put them back in before she went down the passage. He had watched her do it. She had taken them out to say Dilly. Poor child, and put them back, carefully, one by one. And she had gone to her door with them in her mouth, because she had been dressing the Hill for forty-one years and she did not go anywhere without her pins.
Oren looked at them for a long time.
Then he put down his sword on the stones beside him, and reached out, very slowly, with his good hand, and took them out.
One. Two. Three. Four.
He did it the way she had done it. Carefully. One at a time. The way you take the pins out of a hem when the gown is finished. Her lips were cold. He held the four pins in the palm of his hand, small and bright and sharp. Then he found the little pincushion that she wore on her left wrist, on its band of black ribbon, a little round red velvet cushion stuck all over with pins like a hedgehog, and he pushed the four pins into it, one beside the other, in a row.
There. Where they belonged.
Then he closed her eyes.
He had closed a great many eyes. In the war, and after. He was good at it. You put two fingers on the lids, lightly, and you drew them down, and you held them for a moment, so that they stayed. He did it now. Her eyelids were thin and cool, like the tissue paper in the box that had come for the girl.
They stayed.
He knelt there.
The snow came in round the broken door. A little drift of it was building up on the stones, white, against the edge of her black skirt. He did not brush it away.
After a while he looked round the passage. He did not know what for. At the scullery door. At the coal store door. At the whitewashed wall, and the hooks on it, and the things hanging from the hooks. An old apron. A broom. A slate on a nail, with chalk marks on it. He looked at the slate. There was writing on it, he could see that. Lines of it. A list. Madame Hesper's sharp slanting hand.
Marks.
He looked at them for a long time. Thin, black, slanting. Like the tracks of a bird in the snow. He did not know what they said. He would never know. He did not know if it was a list of orders, or of debts, or of the things she needed from the market tomorrow. Bread, perhaps. Thread. Pins. He did not know if her name was on it. Her own name. Her other name, the one before Hesper, the one her husband had called her on summer nights on the leads with his pipe. He had never known it. He would never be able to find it out now, not by himself, not from any slate or any sign or any book in the world.
He thought of the sign over the green door. The one he had read twice, on the step, an hour ago, in the snow, because he liked the shape of the H.
He did not look at it. He knew what he would see.
"Captain."
The voice came from the mews. Low. Hoarse. A woman's.
He turned his head.
There was a shape in the broken doorway, against the snow. Broad as the door. In a brown shawl and a man's boots, with a bucket in one hand and a brush in the other. A wide flat nose. Small bright clever eyes, like a pig's, red-rimmed, wet.
Bridie Kane.
She looked down at Madame Hesper sitting against the wall. She looked for a long time. Her wide red face did not move. Then she put the bucket down on the step, very carefully, so that it would not clank.
"I said I'd be back at five to do the step," she said. "I was early. I came round the end of the mews at the half bell, and there was a carriage at the Crescent end with the blinds down, and three men by the coach house doors that I didn't know, and one of them was the big one. The red one. I know him. Everybody on the steps knows him." She swallowed. "So I didn't come in. I went for the Watch. Round by the Crescent. I ran." She looked at her own boots. "I haven't run in twenty years. I ran all the way to the Watch house at the top of Velvet Street and banged on the door with my bucket and told them there was murder on the Hill. And they came." Her voice cracked. "Not quick enough."
Oren did not say anything.
"Is the girl—"
"Gone," said Oren. "Over the roofs."
Bridie Kane shut her eyes. Then she opened them again.
"Then you'll go too," she said. "Now. This minute." She came in over the step, into the passage, and bent, with a grunt, and picked up his sword off the stones beside Madame Hesper. The red one. She did not look at the blade. She pulled a piece of sacking out of her bucket, the rag she wiped the steps with, and wrapped the sword in it, fast, blade and hilt and all, and held it out to him. "The Watch will be back round this way in five minutes, when they've done looking up the street. They'll come in at the front through the window and they'll come down this passage and they'll find a big man in a coat that isn't his, with blood on him, kneeling by a dead woman. And they'll hang you for it by Saturday." She pushed the bundle into his hands. "And they'll be right to think it, from how it looks. So go."
Oren took the sword.
He got up. It took a long time. Bridie Kane did not help him. She stood in the passage and waited, with her red wet eyes on Madame Hesper.
"Where?" said Oren.
"Where she sent the girl. Fothergill's yard. Off Comb Street." Bridie Kane jerked her head at the mews. "Out the end, by the glass wall. Left at the bottom. Down the alley. There's a gate in the back wall of his yard. It's never shut."
Oren stood in the passage with the wrapped sword in his good hand.
He looked down at Madame Hesper one more time. At her closed eyes. At her long hands in her lap, still through the handles of the shears. At the little red cushion on her wrist, with four pins in a row at the edge of it, where he had put them.
"Her name," he said.
Bridie Kane looked at him.
"Her name. Her own name. Before Hesper." He did not know why he was asking. "Do you know it?"
Bridie Kane was quiet for a moment.
"Agnes," she said. "Her husband called her Aggie. Nobody else ever did."
Oren nodded.
He went out past Bridie Kane into the mews, into the snow, and did not look back. And behind him, as he went, he heard the step woman kneel down on the stones of the passage with a creak of her knees, and wet her brush in her bucket, and begin, very gently, to wash the blood off the whitewash behind Madame Hesper's head.
