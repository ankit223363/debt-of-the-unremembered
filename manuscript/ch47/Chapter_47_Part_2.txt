Chapter 47: Oren's Barrier, Again, Part 2 of 5: Pins
Pins
He did not look round. He had been told not to. But the door to the back room stood a little open, the width of a hand, and the voices came through it, and he listened. A guard listens. It is most of the work.
"You've lost more than half a stone," said Madame Hesper. "You've lost the better part of a whole one. Look at this. Look at this waist. I could put my two hands round it and my fingers would meet. What have they been feeding you?"
"Bread," said the girl. "Soup. Porridge, on the barge."
"Porridge." Madame Hesper said it as if it were a word in a foreign language that she did not much care for. "Arms up."
The rustle of silk. A long soft sound, like wind in dry grass.
"Hold still. Dilly, the screen. Higher. I can't see her shoulders with you waving that thing about."
"Yes, Madame."
"Turn."
Oren looked at the street through the backward gold letters. Empty. White. The gas lamps in their soft yellow balls of light. The snow coming down past them, straight, slow, heavy, so thick now that the houses on the far side of Velvet Street were only gray shapes, like houses in a dream.
A man went by on the far pavement. Hunched. Hurrying. A clerk, by his black coat, going home early because of the snow. He did not look at the shop. Oren watched him all the way to the corner. Then he was gone.
"There," said Madame Hesper, behind him, through the door. "There. Now look."
A silence.
"Oh," said the girl. Very softly.
"Yes. Oh." A pause. "You have her shoulders. I always thought you would. You have his chin, which is a pity, but nobody will be looking at your chin." Another pause. The small sound of a pin going into silk. "There. And there. Turn a little to the light. No. The other way. Toward the stove."
Oren did not look round.
But he saw it anyway. He could not help it. In the glass of the window, in front of him, faintly, over the white street and the backward gold letters, the back room was there. Reflected. Very small and very dim, like a picture painted on smoke. The stove, a red glow. The lamp. The long table. And in the middle of it all, standing on a little wooden box so that the hem would hang right, with her arms held a little away from her sides, a slim gray shape.
Gray silk. Moving like smoke.
He looked away. At the street. At the snow. He looked at the street for a long time.
"She came here last," said Madame Hesper, through the door, "the week before she went. I told you that yesterday. I didn't tell you why."
The girl did not answer. Oren heard the silk rustle as she turned her head.
"Hold still. I said hold still." A pin. Another. "She came for a gown. Not a gown like this. Not for a party. She came for a traveling dress. Wool. Dark blue, heavy, the heaviest I had. With a cape to go over it, lined with fur, and a hood. And deep pockets, inside, at the waist." A pause. "Like the pocket I put in your gray one. For a list."
The kettle sang on the stove.
"I asked her where she was going," said Madame Hesper, "in wool, in winter, with a hood. She said to the coast. To take the sea air, for her chest. Everyone on the Hill said the same, after. The coast. The sea air." Another pin. "I made her the dress. And the cape. And she stood here, where you're standing, on that box, and I pinned the hem, and she looked at herself in that glass. And she said, Make it warm, Hesper. Make it as warm as you can. They say the wind on the Narrow Sea in winter can cut you to the bone."
There was a long silence in the back room.
"The Narrow Sea," said the girl.
"Yes."
"That isn't the coast. The coast is west. The Narrow Sea is—"
"East," said Madame Hesper. "Yes. I know where the Narrow Sea is, child. I have been sewing for the wives of ship captains for forty-one years." A pin. "I didn't say anything to her. It wasn't my place. I made her the warmest dress I have ever made, and she paid me, and held my hands in her gray gloves by the stove, and said the thing about you that I told you yesterday. And went. And the next week the Hill said she had gone to the coast, for her chest, and the week after that, the Hill said she had died there. And a box came back. A closed one. And they buried it in the Thorne vault at Saint Clement's with the lid nailed down." Madame Hesper's voice did not change at all. "And I have thought about that wool dress for eleven years. And the wind on the Narrow Sea."
Oren sat very still in his chair by the window.
He remembered that winter. Not much of it. A guard remembers doors and faces and which horses were lame. He remembered the carriage going away down Crown Walk in the gray early morning, with the luggage strapped on the back. He remembered that there had been a great deal of luggage, for the sea air. More than you would take for a month. He remembered that he had been sent to ride beside the carriage to the edge of the city, and no further, and that at the city gate the Lady Isolde had let down the window, and looked out at him, and said, Thank you, Captain. You may go home now. And he had gone.
He had not thought about which way the road went, after the gate. He had not been asked to.
It went east.
He sat by the window with his hand on his knee near the hilt of his sword, and looked at the snow, and felt something cold move in his chest. Not fear. Older than fear. The feeling you have when you look back along a road you walked a long time ago, in the dark, and see for the first time, by daylight, where it really went.
"Why are you telling me?" said the girl, very quietly, in the back room.
Madame Hesper did not answer at once. Oren heard her take the pins out of her mouth. All of them, one by one. Then she spoke without them, and her voice was different. Lower. Not sharp at all.
"Because yesterday," she said, "somebody sent you a mask with a thread in its lining that comes from the far side of the Narrow Sea. And I have been sitting up all night with that, Lady Lyra, and the wool dress, and a christening gown that came to me to be mended twenty years ago. And I am an old woman, and I don't sleep well, and I find I can't carry it on my own." A pause. "There. Now you have it too. I'm sorry. Turn round. I need to do the back."
The silk rustled.
Oren looked at the street.
And the street was not empty.
There was a carriage at the corner. He had not heard it come. You could not hear anything come, in this snow. A plain closed carriage, dark, with no crest on the door and the blinds down, standing at the corner of Velvet Street where it met the Crescent, under a gas lamp, with two black horses steaming in the cold. Nobody got out of it. It only stood there, with the snow settling on its roof and on the horses' backs. As if it were waiting for somebody.
Oren looked at it.
Then he looked at the gas lamp above it.
There was a mark on the post. Low down. Just above the snow. Chalk, he thought. A little white chalk mark on the black iron, the kind boys make. He could not see what it was from here. A cross, perhaps. Or a circle.
Or a V.
"Dilly," said Madame Hesper, behind him, in her pin voice again. "Thread. The gray silk. I'm out. There's a reel on the shelf in the shop. Second drawer from the left."
"Yes, Madame."
The door to the back room opened wider. And the girl with the pointed freckled face came through it, into the cold shop, very quickly, without looking at him, with her eyes on the floor.
Thread
She went behind the counter, to the wall of little square drawers.
Oren watched her. He did not turn his head. He watched her in the edge of his eye, the way he had watched the clerk on the far pavement, and the carriage at the corner. A small thin girl in a white cap, with her back to him, standing in front of the drawers with her hand out.
She did not open the second drawer from the left.
She stood there with her hand out, and did not open anything. Her hand was shaking so hard that he could see it from across the shop. Then she put it on the brass handle of a drawer, any drawer, and pulled, and the drawer came out too fast and too far and fell on the floor with a crash, and a hundred jet buttons went across the boards like black hail.
"Oh," said the girl. "Oh, no. Oh, no."
She did not bend to pick them up.
She stood there in the middle of the scattered buttons with her hands pressed flat against her apron. And Oren saw her shoulders begin to shake. Not with sobbing. She made no sound at all. Only her shoulders, in her thin gray dress, shaking.
He got up.
He did not go to her. He stood by his chair in the corner by the window, with his hand on the hilt under his coat now, not near it.
"Dilly," he said.
The girl did not turn round.
"Dilly. Look at me."
She turned round.
Her face was wet. Her big frightened eyes went to his face, and then away, quickly, past him, to the window. To the backward gold letters, and the white street, and the corner where the gas lamp was, with the little chalk mark on its post.
And the carriage.
"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry."
"What did you do?"
"My mother," said the girl. Her voice was a thread. "My mother's got the cough. The bad one. The blood one. The doctor wants two crowns a week and we haven't got two crowns a month." The tears were running down into her collar. "And a man came. On Saturday. To the back step. After the lady came in her gray gown. A nice man. Round. With a nice face. And he said if I was to put a mark on the lamp, when the lady came back, just a little chalk mark, he'd pay the doctor. All winter. For as long as it took."
"And you did."
"At four o'clock. When I took the ash out to the yard. I went round by the front." The girl's hands pressed harder against her apron. "It's only a mark. He said nobody would be hurt. He said the Lady only wanted to talk to the lady. About her father. Nicely."
Oren looked at her.
He did not say anything. There was nothing to say. He had seen a great many frightened people in his life, in a great many bad places, and he knew that there was nothing you could say to this one that would help her, or help him, or put the drawer back in the wall.
"And now you're to go out," he said. "Aren't you. When you see the carriage. To show them which door."
The girl's face crumpled.
Then she ran.
Not to the back. Not to Madame Hesper. To the front. Past him, so close her shoulder brushed his arm, through the jet buttons that rolled and skittered under her boots, to the street door. Her hand found the latch. The bell over the door jangled, loud, bright, terrible in the quiet shop, and the cold came in, and the snow, and she was gone. Out. Into the white. Running, in her thin gray dress and her white cap, down the middle of Velvet Street, with her arms wrapped round herself, toward the corner. Toward the carriage.
The carriage door opened.
Oren saw it open. Slowly. A black square in the black side of the carriage. And a hand came out of it. A small neat hand, in a brown glove. And held out something to the girl as she reached it. Something small that caught the light of the gas lamp. A coin. Or a purse.
The girl took it.
She stood there in the snow by the carriage, with the thing in her hand, and looked at it. And then she looked back up the street. At the shop. At the window, with its gold letters. At him, in it.
Then she turned and ran again, the other way, round the corner into the Crescent, and was gone.
The carriage door did not close.
And out of the snow, from both ends of Velvet Street at once, from the corner by the carriage and the corner at the other end where the street went down toward Silk Street, out of the gray falling white where nothing had been a moment before, people came.
Not running. Walking. Quietly. The way people walk who have done this before, and know that there is no need to run.
Oren counted them. He did it without thinking, the way he had counted men all his life. From the carriage end, four. From the Silk Street end, three. Seven. All in dark coats, with their collars up and their hats pulled down against the snow. Two carried something long between them, low down, close to their legs. A pole, perhaps. Or a bar. One had a club. One had his hands in his pockets.
And one was not a man.
She came from the carriage end, a little behind the others. Tall. Very tall. In a long gray apron that came to her ankles, over a gray dress, with a gray shawl round her shoulders and no hat, so that the snow lay on her hair. Pale hair, very pale, scraped back. A long pale face with nothing in it. Nothing at all. No fear and no anger and no hurry. The face of somebody who has been emptied of everything but one thing, and the one thing is walking.
And on her hands, gray gloves. Thick ones. Leather. The kind a smith wears.
Oren knew her.
He had seen her once before. In the autumn. At the mouth of an arch in the Lower Docks, on the morning of the hay wagon, through a wall of clear light with gold round its edges. She had put her gloved hand flat on his barrier, and the cold had gone out of her palm into it like ink into water, and the barrier had gone white, and hard, and brittle, like the frozen skin on a pond. And he had felt it go into him. Into his chest. Into his hands. Cold, like nothing he had ever felt. Colder than the river. Colder than dying.
Gorse. That was her name. Corin had written it in the Ledger. Gorse always does the holding.
She walked down the middle of Velvet Street in the snow toward the Needle and Thimble with her empty face and her gray gloves, behind the others, and did not hurry at all.
Oren turned his head.
"Madame Hesper," he said.
He did not shout it. He said it the way you say a thing that is already true.
In the back room, the rustling stopped.
Front and Back
Madame Hesper was in the doorway before he had finished turning.
She did not say anything. She stood in the door between the back room and the shop, tall and black and thin, with the pins in her mouth and her spectacles on their chain and a reel of gray silk thread in her hand. She looked at the scattered jet buttons on the floor. At the street door, standing open, with the snow blowing in over the step and the bell still trembling on its spring. At the window.
At the street beyond the gold letters, and the seven dark shapes walking down it in the snow, and the tall pale one behind them.
Her long face did not change at all.
She took the pins out of her mouth.
"Dilly," she said.
"Yes, Madame."
"Poor child." Madame Hesper put the pins back. All four. Carefully. In the corner of her mouth, one beside the other. "Poor, silly, frightened child. Her mother will be dead by spring anyway. There's no doctor in the city can mend that cough." She turned her head, and called back into the back room, round the pins, sharp and clear as a bell. "Lady Lyra. Your coat. Over the gown. Now. Don't take it off. There isn't time."
From the back room, a rustle. A gasp. The sound of a wooden box going over.
Oren crossed the shop to the street door in four strides, through the buttons. He shut it. He shot the bolt at the top, and the bolt at the bottom, and turned the key that was in the lock. The door was good oak. The bolts were iron, as thick as his thumb. It would hold a man's shoulder. It would not hold a bar, or a pole. And it would not hold the window.
The window was glass. Small square panes of old glass, in a frame of black wood, the whole width of the shop. A child with a stone could have come through it.
He turned round.
The girl was in the door of the back room, behind Madame Hesper. She had her coat on, the too-big one, over the gray silk, and the gray silk came down out of the bottom of it to the floor, pinned up in a dozen places with Madame Hesper's pins, the pins catching the light like frost. She had her boots in one hand. Her feet were bare on the cold boards. Her face was white.
"The back," said Oren to Madame Hesper. "The mews."
"I know." Madame Hesper did not look round. "Three. I saw them through the scullery window when I went for the thread. Before you called. By the coach house door. One very big, with a red face. Two others." She turned the reel of gray thread over in her long fingers. "I bolted it. Top and bottom. And the key."
"Will it hold?"
"It's held for forty-one years." Madame Hesper put the reel of thread in the pocket of her apron. "It's never been asked to hold against a man like that."
Outside, the seven dark shapes had reached the shop.
They stopped in front of it, on the white pavement, in a loose half-circle, under the gas lamp at the door. Close enough now that Oren could see their faces through the backward gold letters. Ordinary faces. Red with cold. One with a broken nose. One very young, with a thin mustache he was trying to grow. One with a scar through his lip. Men you would pass in the Lower Docks without looking twice. The two with the long thing between them set it down on the snow. It was a bar. Iron. Long, and heavy, with a flat end, the kind that is used to lever up paving stones.
The tall pale woman in the gray apron did not stop with them. She walked on, past them, slowly, to the window, and stood in front of it. Close. So close that her breath, if she had any, would have misted the glass. And she looked in.
Not at Oren. At the back of the shop. At the girl in the doorway, in her coat, with her boots in her hand.
Her face did not change.
She lifted one gray-gloved hand, and laid it flat on the window. On the glass. On the gold letter H.
And where her hand lay, a white flower began to grow on the glass. Frost. Fine and feathery and fast, spreading out from under her gloved fingers in long branching lines, across the pane, into the next pane, white, crackling, the way frost grows on a window on the coldest night of the year. Oren heard it. A tiny dry sound, like somebody crumpling paper very slowly.
The pane under her hand cracked.
A single line, straight across, from one corner to the other. Not loud. A small sharp tick. And then another.
"Madame," said Oren. "The roof."
Madame Hesper looked at him.
"The skylight," said Oren. "Between the chimneys. I saw it from the mews. Can she get up to it?"
Something moved in the long face. Something that, in another woman, on another day, might have been a smile.
"From the attic," said Madame Hesper. "There's a ladder. My husband put it in, forty years ago, when the chimney sweep wanted too much. He used to go up there himself and sit on the leads with his pipe on summer nights." She turned. She took the girl by the shoulders, with both her long cold hands, the way you take hold of a child who is about to run the wrong way. "Up the stair. Four floors. The door at the top, the little one. The ladder's behind the trunk. Push the glass up and out. It's heavy. Push hard. Then along the leads, to the left, over two roofs, to the gutter where the wall drops down. You'll see a yard below with barrels in it. That's Comb Street. That's Fothergill's." She shook the girl, once. "The little man with no hair. He'll let you through. He let you through yesterday."
The girl stood in her hands and did not move.
"Come with me," she said.
"No."
"Hesper, come with me. Please. Both of you—"
"No." Madame Hesper's voice did not rise. "I am sixty-four years old, child, and I have knees like two bags of gravel, and I have not been up that ladder since my husband died. I would not get past the first rung. And if I tried, I would be on the ladder when they came through my back door, and then they would have both of us." She let go of the girl's shoulders. "Go up. I'll see to my door. The Captain will see to his. Go."
The girl looked past her. At Oren.
He did not say anything. He could not. He looked back at her, across the cold shop, through the scattered jet buttons, and he thought: Since she was nine. One pace behind, and one pace to the left. And he could not go with her. Not this time. There were two doors, and one window, and one of him. And somebody had to stand in front of the window.
He nodded. Once.
The girl's face twisted.
Then she turned, with her boots in her hand and the gray silk dragging behind her bare feet out of the bottom of her coat, and the pins flashing, and ran. Through the back room. Past the stove and the long table and the three silent dummies. Into the passage. He heard her bare feet on the stair, going up. Fast. Very fast. Round the first turn.
And from the far end of the passage, from the green door, the first blow.
It came through the whole house. A great dull thud, like a sack of wet sand dropped from a height onto a wooden floor. The green door shook in its frame. Oren heard the bolts rattle. Heard the key jump in the lock.
Madame Hesper turned toward it.
She went down the passage without hurrying, a tall thin black figure, with the pins in her mouth and her spectacles swinging on their chain, and her long cold hands at her sides. At the door of the back room she stopped, and took something from the long table as she passed. He saw it flash. Her shears. The big black dressmaker's shears, as long as a forearm, that she cut silk with.
She did not look back at him.
She went down the dark passage toward the green door, and the second blow came, and the house shook, and she was gone into the dark.
Oren turned to the window.
The frost had covered three panes now. White. Thick. Cracked across, all three, in long straight lines, like the lines on the palm of a hand. Behind it, blurred, he could see the pale shape of the woman in the gray apron, with her hand still flat on the glass. And behind her the dark shapes of the men, lifting the iron bar off the snow between them, and stepping back, and back, for a run.
He took his sword out from under Terrence's coat.
He did not draw it. He held it in its scabbard, the whole thing, sword and leather and brass, across his body, with his left hand on the scabbard and his right hand on the hilt. The way he had held it at the mouth of the arch in the autumn. Not to fight with. To lean on. To hold up.
And he stood in front of the window, in the middle of the shop, with his feet apart on the scattered jet buttons, and closed his eyes, and reached.
