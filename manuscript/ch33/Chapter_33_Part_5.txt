Chapter 33: The Long Way North, Part 5 of 5: I Will
I Will
She came up on deck a little after the fourth bell of the morning.
Corin heard the bell. It came from very far away, across the fields, from some village church he could not see. Four slow notes, muffled by the fog, each one a long way after the last. He counted them without meaning to. One. Two. Three. Four. And then the quiet came back.
He was sitting in the bow, on the coil of rope, with his back against the raised edge of the deck and his knees drawn up and Hessa's spare blanket around his shoulders. Mrs. Hobb had given it to him. It was made of old knitted squares sewn together, every square a different color, and it smelled of woodsmoke and baby. He had not been able to sleep. Every time he shut his eyes, he saw a gloved hand on an iron wheel. So he had come up here, where the cold kept him awake, to wait for the light.
He heard the cabin door. He heard her step on the deck. He knew it was her before she came round the end of the hay. He did not know how. He just knew.
She had her own blanket. A gray one, wrapped round her over the too-big coat. She sat down on the deck beside him, with her back against the same raised edge, and pulled her knees up, and looked out at the fog.
For a long time, neither of them said anything.
The fog was beginning to change. Not lift. Not yet. But the white of it was not the same white as before. It had a faint gray light inside it now, very faint, the way a sheet hung on a line has light in it when the sun is behind it. Dawn was somewhere out there. A long way off. Coming.
"Wat's asleep," said Lyra. "Hessa gave him something of Mrs. Hobb's in his tea. He's snoring." A pause. "Hessa says he always snores. She says it twice."
"And your father?"
"Awake." She pulled the blanket closer. "He's always awake at this time. Mrs. Hobb says it's the old soldier in him. He wakes before the bugle." Another pause. "He's tapping."
Corin listened. Under the sound of the water lapping against the hull, and the creak of the mooring ropes, he heard it. Faint. Muffled. Coming up through the deck boards from somewhere under the great mound of hay.
Tap tap tap tap.
A pause.
Tap tap.
A pause.
The next one was long. He counted it very carefully, the way a man walks along the edge of a roof. Seven. Eight. Nine. It stopped there. Right at the edge. At the last number he still had.
Tap tap tap.
And then a long pause, and it began again.
"Four, two, nine, three," said Lyra. "All day. All night. Every day since we left the city. On the wall. On the blanket. On my hand, when I hold his." She was quiet. "Mrs. Hobb says she dreams it. She says she wakes up in the night and her own fingers are doing it on the pillow."
Corin did not say anything.
He was thinking about what Oren had said. Tonight. Before she hears it from anybody else. And he was thinking that there was nobody else for her to hear it from, out here, on a hay barge in the fog, and that he could wait. One more day. One more night. Until things were less terrible. Until he had slept.
Things will never be less terrible, said Malakor. You know that.
He did.
"Lyra," he said.
She turned her head and looked at him.
"I saw your brother," said Corin.
She went very still.
It was not the stillness of somebody who is surprised. It was the stillness of somebody who has heard a sound in the night that they have been waiting for and dreading for a very long time, and does not want to move in case it comes again.
"Rowan," she said.
"Yes."
"Where?"
"At Tallow Street. On Wednesday. When they came for me." Corin looked at his hands. "He was with Kross."
She did not say anything. He made himself go on.
He told her all of it. The way he had told Hessa. Slowly. In order. The ninety-one steps, and the gray cadet's coat, and the new boots that creaked on the stairs. The light voice of the Inquisitor, all the way up and all the way down, talking to her brother the way a kind uncle talks to a boy he is teaching to fish. Mind the third step, Rowan. It's short. The trap in the scullery floor. The cellars. The coal. The lantern, held up high, and its yellow light, and the sword.
"He found me," said Corin. "In the coal cellar. By the grating. He had his sword out. He was trying to say the words. In the name of the Ministry of Records and the Office of the Inquisition. He couldn't remember the end of it."
Lyra had her hand over her mouth.
"He said you'd ruined them," said Corin. "Your father. Your name. Your house. He said you'd ruined me too." He took a breath. "And I told him you were alive. That you were on a barge on the river with your father. That you got him out."
She took her hand away from her mouth.
"You told him," she said. "A cadet. With Kross in the next room. You told him I was on a barge."
"Yes."
"On the river."
"Yes."
She looked at him. And he saw her see it. All of it. The way Hessa had seen Wat's wound, in one look. How many barges there were on the river. How many places a barge could be. How long it would take a man like Kross to search them all, if he knew to look. How long it would take, if a boy told him.
Corin did not look away.
"He didn't tell him," he said. "Kross came down. Kross was calling for him. Rowan, have you found him? And he shouted back, Here, sir, he's here, by the grating." He saw her flinch. "And then I did something. A word. The lantern burst. Everything went dark. And when Kross got there, and asked him where I'd gone, and what I'd said, he said he didn't hear. He said his ears were ringing. He said he was on the coal, and he couldn't hear anything after."
The fog moved.
"He lied to him," said Corin. "To Kross. To his face. I heard him do it. His voice was flat. Like a stone." He paused. "He's sixteen, Lyra. And he stood there in the dark with the Inquisitor's hand on his shoulder and lied to him. For me. For you. I don't know which."
Lyra did not say anything.
She was looking out at the fog. Her face was turned away from him, and he could only see the side of it. The line of her cheek. The new smudge of ink on the cheekbone, which she still had not noticed. Her chin, which was shaking very slightly.
"And then he let me go," said Corin. "Down the drain. He could have stopped me. He was standing over the hole. He didn't."
He reached into his coat. Into the small inside pocket, where the key lived, and the other thing.
He took it out.
It was very small. A barley-sugar wrapper, folded into a hard tight square, the size of a fingernail. It had been through the drain and the sewer and the river. It had been soaked and dried and soaked again. Its edges were soft and gray, and one corner had torn. But it still held its folds.
"He dropped this," said Corin. "Down the hole. After the grating closed. He looked down at me, and then he went away, and then this came down."
He held it out to her on his open palm, beside the cut.
She looked at it. For a long time, she did not take it. She looked at it the way Corin had looked at the folded note in the split stick. As if it might bite.
"That's Kross's fold," she said. Her voice was very small.
"Yes."
"He taught it to him." She swallowed. "Juno told me. She used to carry my letters to the Academy gate. She said the cadets all fold their papers like that, because the Inquisitor does, and they all want to be like him." She looked up at Corin. "Is that what this is?"
"Open it."
She took it.
She did it very carefully. With the tips of her fingers. As if it were a moth. She turned it over and over. Then she began to unfold it, one fold at a time, slowly, picking at the soft gray edges with her thumbnail, the way Oren had picked at the gray cape's note. It came open. It tore a little more at the corner. She held it flat on her knee, on the gray blanket.
The waxy white side was up. And on it, smeared and black, written with something soft, a lump of coal, held in a black-dusted hand, there were two words.
I will.
Lyra stared at them.
"I will," she said. "I will what?"
"I don't know."
"You didn't ask him?"
"There wasn't time. I was in the water."
She went on staring at it. I will. Two words. Crooked and smudged. A boy's writing. Not her small fierce slanting hand. Bigger. Rounder. Pressed down too hard, so the coal had crumbled.
And then Corin saw her understand something.
He did not know what it was. He saw it come into her face, the way the light was coming into the fog. Slowly. From the inside.
"My letter," she whispered. "The last one. The one I sent before the Hollow." Her fingers had gone tight on the edges of the wrapper. "I said—I asked him—when they tell you who your enemies are, ask them how they know." Her voice broke. "Just ask."
She looked up at Corin. Her eyes were very bright.
"He read it," she said. "Corin. He read it. He never reads them. He's never read any of them, not in two years, I know he hasn't, he never once wrote back. But he read this one." She looked down at the wrapper. "I will. He's telling me. He's going to ask."
She put her hand flat over the two smeared words, as if to keep them warm.
And then she began to cry.
Not the way she had on deck, when he came. Not with her fists in his coat. She just sat there on the cold boards with her hand over the wrapper on her knee, and her face turned toward the fog, and the tears ran down her face without a sound and fell onto the gray blanket and onto the back of her own hand, and she let them.
Corin sat beside her.
He did not touch her. He did not say anything. He thought of what he had learned in the arches, from Briggs on his barrel, and Hessa by her pillar, and Moll with his cold pipe. That there was a kind of quiet that was not empty. That you could sit in it with someone, and wait.
So he waited.
The light grew in the fog. Gray, then paler gray, then something that was almost white. The tapping went on under the hay. Four. Two. The long one, right at the edge. Three. The water lapped. The ropes creaked.
After a long time, Lyra wiped her face with the corner of the blanket. She looked at the wrapper on her knee. Then, very slowly and very carefully, she began to fold it up again. Along the old creases. The way it had been. Smaller. Smaller. Until it was a hard tight square again, soft-edged and torn at one corner, the size of a fingernail.
"Fold it small," she said quietly. "Mother used to say that. When we were little. Whenever we were frightened, or sad, or cross. Fold it small, and keep it close. I put it at the bottom of the letter. So he'd know it was really me." She turned the tiny square over in her fingers. "And it's the same fold. His fold. Kross's."
She was quiet for a moment.
"Isn't that strange," she said. Not really to Corin. "That they should both say the same thing."
Corin did not answer. Something cold had touched the back of his neck, and he did not know what it was, and it was gone before he could turn and look at it. Like a draft from a door that is shut.
Lyra held out the wrapper to him.
"You keep it," she said.
"It's yours. It's from your brother."
"It came to you." She took his hand, the cut one, and turned it over, and put the little square in his palm, and closed his fingers round it with her own. "And you keep things. That's what you do. You write them down and you keep them." She did not let go of his hand. "Keep this one for me. Until I see him. Then you can give it back."
He looked at her hand on his.
He wanted to tell her. Then. About the other thing. The thing he had not told anybody but Juno, on the river, in the dark. About a white-gloved figure under the first lamp of the Lantern Bridge. About an audit card with a seal gone gray. About a gold tooth, and a green coat, and a girl of six on a wagon at Oakhaven who had not died in any fever or any fire, whatever his teacher said. About a sister.
She had just found her brother again. In two smeared words of coal. She would understand. She, of all people.
He opened his mouth.
And when you have told her, said Malakor softly, behind his left ear, what will she ask you next?
Corin knew. Who is she? Where is she? What does she want?
And you will say, I do not know.
Yes.
And she will lie awake on this barge every night after that, listening for a knock. For white gloves on the hull. A pause. She has enough to listen for.
Corin shut his mouth.
Lyra was looking at him. "What?" she said.
"Nothing," said Corin. "Nothing. Just tired."
She looked at him a moment longer. He did not think she believed him. But she did not ask again. She let go of his hand, and leaned back against the raised edge of the deck, and pulled her blanket round her, and shut her eyes.
He put the wrapper back in the small inside pocket, with the key.
And sat there beside her in the growing light, with one more thing in his pocket that he had not said, and the tapping going on and on under the hay.
Under the Hay
"Do you want to see him?" said Lyra.
She had opened her eyes. The fog was paler now. Corin could see the far end of the deck, and the cabin roof, and the tin chimney with its thin gray thread of smoke. He could see the next barge in the line, a dark shape with its own mound of hay, and beyond it the ghost of a third.
"Yes," he said.
She got up, and he got up after her, stiffly, with Mrs. Hobb's blanket round his shoulders. She went along the deck to the side of the great mound of hay, about halfway along, where the brown canvas sheets overlapped. She looked up and down the landing stage. There was nobody. She knelt down and lifted the edge of the lower sheet.
Underneath it, there was a hole in the hay.
It was a tunnel. Low and round, about as wide as a man's shoulders, going straight in. Somebody had built it with care. The hay round the mouth of it was packed hard and tied in bundles, so that it would not fall in, and there were short planks laid along the bottom, like a floor.
"Hessa made it," said Lyra. "Before we left the coal wharf. She said her father used to carry brandy this way, from the Saltgate boats, before there were Excise men on the river." She almost smiled. "She said it twice."
She went in on her hands and knees. Corin went after her.
It was dark inside, and warm, and close. The smell of the hay was very strong. Sweet and dusty and dry, like a summer afternoon shut up in a box. Stalks pricked at his hands and his neck. The planks creaked under his knees. In front of him, he could see nothing but the dim shape of Lyra's boots, and beyond her, faint, a glow of yellow light.
The tunnel went in about six feet. Then it opened out.
There was a room in the middle of the hay. A small one, low and square, no higher than a man kneeling. Its walls were planks. Old rough boards, set on end and held up with posts at the corners, so that the weight of the hay above and around them could not fall in. Its floor was the deck of the barge. There was a lamp hanging from a hook in one of the posts, turned down very low, and it made the planks glow gold, and the bits of hay that had come through the cracks between them shine like threads of wire.
And in the middle of the room, on a straw mattress with a patchwork cover over it, lay Lord Aurelian Thorne.
Corin had seen him before. In the white room at Pruett's. Thin and white and still, against white pillows, with his hands folded on the sheet. He had seemed very old then, and very far away. Like a statue on a tomb.
He seemed older now. And thinner. His face had sunk in on itself, so that the bones of it stood out under the skin, the cheekbones and the brow and the long fine nose. His hair had been white at Pruett's. It was white still, but it had grown, and it lay on the folded blanket under his head in long soft strands, like the fluff of a dandelion clock. Somebody had shaved him. Mrs. Hobb, Corin thought. There was a tiny nick on his chin, healing.
His eyes were open.
They were gray. Pale gray, and very clear. They looked up at the low plank ceiling of the hay room with no expression in them at all.
And his right hand lay on the patchwork cover beside him, and the long fingers of it were moving.
Tap tap tap tap.
On the cover. On a square of red cloth with a pattern of small white stars.
Tap tap.
Corin knelt down beside the mattress.
The hand went on. The long one. He watched the fingers rise and fall, rise and fall, and held on to the count the way you hold on to a rope in the dark. Seven. Eight. Nine. And the fingers stopped, exactly there, at the very end of what he had left. As if they knew.
Tap tap tap.
Then the long pause.
Lyra had knelt on the other side. She reached out and took her father's left hand, the one that was still, and held it in both of hers.
"Father," she said. "It's Corin. Corin Vane. The harvester. The one who brought you out of Pruett's." She said it slowly and clearly, the way you say a thing to somebody very deaf. "He came back. He's safe."
Nothing happened.
The gray eyes looked at the ceiling. The right hand went on tapping.
"He doesn't know," said Lyra. Not sadly. Just telling Corin. "Most of the time he doesn't know anything. Mrs. Hobb says it's like talking to a house with the shutters closed. You know somebody's in there. You can hear them moving about. But they can't come to the window."
"Most of the time?"
Lyra did not answer at once.
"Sometimes," she said. "Once or twice. He looks at you. Really looks. Just for a moment. As if he's come all the way up from somewhere very deep to see who's there." She looked down at the still hand in hers. "And then he goes back down."
Corin watched the tapping hand.
Tap tap tap tap.
Four.
He thought of the white room at Pruett's. Lord Thorne's lips moving, once, without sound, and making the shape of a letter. W. He thought of Oren, in the yard, saying it sounded like the drums. The army drums. The calls they beat for the men to march by, and halt by, and stand by, and come home by. He thought of the old soldier waking before the bugle.
Tap tap.
Two.
Malakor, he thought. Do you know what it means?
No answer.
He waited. The long one went by. Nine. The edge.
Malakor.
Nothing. Not a whisper. Not a breath behind his left ear. Corin realized, with a small cold shock, that he had not heard the voice since they came into the hay. Not once. Not since the tunnel. As if, somewhere at the mouth of it, Malakor had stopped, and stayed outside.
Tap tap tap.
Three.
And the long pause.
Corin did not know what made him do it. He reached out, slowly, and put his own hand on the patchwork cover. Palm up. Beside Lord Thorne's tapping one. Close enough that their fingers almost touched. The cut across his palm was dark and dry now. A line.
The long fingers began again. And this time, they did not come down on the red cloth.
They came down on Corin's hand.
Tap tap tap tap.
On his palm. On the cut. Light and dry and cool, like a bird's feet. Corin did not move.
Tap tap.
Lyra had stopped breathing. He could hear that she had.
The long one. On his palm. He felt every touch of it. He counted every touch. Nine. Right at the edge of what he had left. And stop.
Tap tap tap.
And then the old man's head turned on the folded blanket.
Slowly. So slowly. The long white strands of hair sliding on the wool. Until the pale gray eyes were not looking at the ceiling any more.
They were looking at Corin.
And they saw him. Corin knew it the way you know when someone has walked into a room behind you. The eyes were exactly the same as before, pale and clear and gray. But there was somebody behind them now. Somebody at the window. Somebody who had come up a very long stair in the dark to see who was there, and had found a stranger, kneeling, with a cut hand, and was looking at him with an expression that Corin had seen once before, on the face of a man in a jar. A dying soldier, in a muddy field, looking up at the face of the boy who had come to sit with him.
Not fear.
Something much worse than fear.
Pity.
The dry lips moved. No sound came out. They made a shape. One shape. Then another. Corin could not read them. He had never been able to read lips.
Then the eyes went away again.
Not shut. They stayed open. But the person behind them went down the long stair, back into the dark, and the window was empty, and Lord Thorne's head turned slowly back on the blanket until his gray eyes were looking at the plank ceiling once more.
The fingers lifted from Corin's palm.
And came down again on the red cloth with the white stars.
Tap tap tap tap.
Neither of them said anything for a long time.
Then Lyra said, very low, "He's never done that before. Not to anybody. Not to me."
Corin looked at her. Her face, in the low gold light of the lamp, was very white.
"What did he say?" she said. "His lips. Did you see? What did he say?"
"I don't know," said Corin.
And it was true. He did not know. He had seen the shapes, and he had not been able to read them. But he had felt something, when the eyes were on him. A cold touch on the back of the neck, the same as on the deck when Lyra said isn't that strange. Like a draft from a shut door.
And behind his left ear, as they crawled back out of the warm dark tunnel into the gray light of the morning, and the cold air of the canal touched his face, a voice came back. Very quiet. Very tired. As if it had been holding its breath.
Do not do that again, said Malakor.
Why?
But the voice did not answer.
The Tide Book
The fog lifted at eight.
It did not blow away. There was no wind to blow it. It just thinned, slowly, the way a breath fades off a window, until all at once Corin could see the far bank of the canal, and the line of black poplars on it, and the fields beyond them, white with frost, going away flat and empty to a gray line of hills. The sky above them was low and pale and the color of dishwater. There was no sun. There was only light, thin and cold, coming from everywhere at once.
The hay wharf at Brindle woke up.
It woke up slowly, like an old man getting out of bed. A cabin door banged on the barge at the end of the line. Somebody coughed for a long time. Somebody else threw a bucket of something over the side. A boy came down the cart ramp from the farm with two buckets of milk on a yoke across his shoulders, slipping on the frozen boards, and went along the landing stage handing them up to the barges one by one, and the bargewomen came out in their shawls to take them, and called things to each other across the decks. Ordinary things. Cold enough for you. Ice on the pump again. Tell your Sam he owes my Sam a shilling. Up at the barns, a horse whinnied, and a man began to shout at it.
Corin sat on an upturned bucket by the cabin door and watched it all and did not move.
He had not slept. He did not think he would ever sleep again. His eyes felt as if somebody had rubbed sand into them. But it was good to sit there in the cold with a tin mug of Mrs. Hobb's tea in his hands, and watch people do ordinary things. It was like looking at a picture of a place he had once lived.
Hessa came up the cabin ladder with a book under her arm.
It was a fat little book with a cover of black oilcloth, cracked and peeling at the corners, and held shut with a loop of string. She sat down on the cabin roof beside him with her feet on the deck, and untied the string, and opened it on her knee.
The pages were full of numbers. Columns and columns of them, printed in small gray type. Times. Heights. Days of the month. And all over the printed columns, in the margins, between the lines, there was writing. Pencil. Ink. Some of it faded almost to nothing. Some of it fresh. A dozen different hands.
"Tide book," said Hessa. "My father's. And his father's before him. The printed part comes out new every year, from a man in Saltgate. We sew the new pages in and keep the old ones." She turned a page. "The writing's ours."
Corin looked over her shoulder.
Ice at Brindle. Three weeks. Bad. In a big, square, old-fashioned hand. Abel short on the hides. Again. Coldwater gates sticking. Tell Jory. Robin born. Midnight. Wat cried. That last one in pencil, in a small round hand Corin knew was Hessa's own.
He looked at her. She did not look up.
"Don't you say anything," she said. "Don't you say a word."
He did not.
She ran her finger down a column of printed numbers. Her lips moved. Then she turned back a page and ran her finger down another. Corin watched her. He did not understand any of it. The canal had no tide. But the river did, down at the bottom of the locks, where the canal ran out into it. And the river ran down to Saltgate, and the sea. And everything that went up and down the water moved by those numbers, whether it knew it or not.
"Oren wants to go at first light," said Corin.
"I know what Oren wants."
"He says you'll know where."
"I know where." Hessa turned a page. "North. Up past Withy Reach. Four days. There's a backwater up there, at the old peat cuttings, that nobody's used in twenty years. You could hide a fleet in it." She ran her finger down another column. "We could be there by Monday, if the ice stays off."
"Then we'll go?"
"No."
Corin looked at her.
Hessa closed the tide book over her finger and looked up the landing stage. At the line of barges. At the bargewomen in their shawls, and the boy with his empty buckets, and the hay. Great shaggy mounds of it, on every deck, roped down under brown canvas, all exactly the same.
"Seven barges came in to Brindle on Monday night," she said. "To load hay for the city stables. Seven. Not one of them has finished loading. Not one of them is going anywhere until the farmer's men bring down the last of the stacks, and that's Saturday, if they're quick." She looked at him. "If the Patient Grace casts off this morning, half-loaded, and goes north instead of south, with every other barge on the wharf watching her do it, what do you think they'll say?"
Corin did not answer.
"They'll say Hessa Doon's got something on board she doesn't want seen," said Hessa. "And they'll say it in every tavern from here to Saltgate by Sunday. Twice." She opened the book again. "A barge that runs is a barge with a secret. A barge that stays and loads hay is a barge."
"And if the gray cape comes?"
"Then he'll have to climb on seven barges and dig through seven loads of hay. With seven bargemen watching him." She did not look up. "Bargemen don't like gray coats. Never have. They put sticks down in the coal at the locks. They break things. They don't say sorry." She turned a page. "He'll get a cold welcome on this wharf. Colder than the weather."
Corin looked at her bent head. At the small square red hands on the cracked black book.
"Oren won't like it," he said.
"Oren is not the master of this barge," said Hessa Doon.
She said it once. Corin had begun to understand what that meant.
And it was then, while she was still bent over the tide book, that the coal boat came up the canal from the south.
They heard it before they saw it. The slow clop of a horse on the towpath, and the creak of a towrope, and a man's voice calling gee up, gee up in a tired singsong. Then it came round the bend below the wharf. A short, fat, dirty black boat, low in the water with a heaped load of coal, pulled by an old brown horse with frost on its whiskers. A boy walked beside the horse with a stick. A man stood at the tiller in the stern, in a coat so black with coal dust that it shone.
As it came level with the wharf, the bargewoman on the first barge called out to the man at the tiller. Corin could not hear the words. He saw the man answer. Saw him jerk his head back the way he had come, toward the south. Toward the locks.
The bargewoman put her hand over her mouth.
The man at the tiller said something else. Then the coal boat went on past, slowly, up the canal, and the bargewoman on the first barge turned round and called to the woman on the second. And the woman on the second called to Hessa.
She was a thin gray woman in a red shawl. She leaned out over the rail of her barge, and cupped her hands, and called across the gap between them, not loudly.
"Hessa. Hessa Doon. You'll want to hear this."
Hessa stood up.
"What is it, Nan?"
"Coal boat's come up from Coldwater," said the thin woman. "They had the gray coats all over the lock last night. Stopping everything. Putting sticks in the coal." She paused. "And this morning they took Jory."
Hessa did not move.
"Took him where?" she said.
"Up to the city. In a closed cart. First light." The woman pulled her shawl tighter. "Davy tried to stop them. Davy Hobday. Big as a door, you know Davy. He got in front of the cart. They knocked him down with a stick and he got up and they knocked him down again." She shook her head. "He's all right. They say he's all right. He's got a broken head, but he's all right."
Nobody said anything.
"And the dog," said the woman. "Jory's old brindle bitch. She went for one of them, when they took him out of the house. Went for him and got him by the arm." She paused. "They shot her."
The canal was very quiet.
Up at the barns, the horse had stopped whinnying. The boy with the buckets had stopped on the cart ramp and was standing still, looking down at the barges, with his mouth open.
"What for?" said Hessa. Her voice was perfectly flat. "What did they take him for? An old man. What did they say he'd done?"
The thin woman shrugged.
"Nobody knows," she said. "Nobody ever knows, with them." She looked at Hessa a moment longer. "Coal boat says the gray coats were asking about his shed."
Then she went back into her cabin, and shut the door.
Hessa stood on the roof of the Patient Grace with the tide book in her hand.
She stood there for a long time. She did not look at Corin. She did not look at anything. She looked at the canal, going away south between its banks toward the locks, and the city, and the Hill.
Then she sat down again. Slowly. Like a much older woman. And she opened the tide book on her knee, and took a stub of pencil out of her apron pocket, and turned to the page for that day. Friday. And in the margin beside the printed numbers, in her small round hand, she wrote:
Jory taken. Bramble shot. Davy's girl, spring. I am to come.
And then, under it, after a long moment:
I am to come.
She closed the book. She tied the string round it. She stood up and went down the ladder into the cabin without a word, and Corin heard her say something to Mrs. Hobb, and then he heard nothing at all.
He sat on the bucket.
After a while, he put down his tea, which had gone cold. He took the Ledger out of his coat, and the stub of pencil from the spine of it. He opened it on his knee to a clean page. His hands were shaking. It was the cold, he told himself. Only the cold.
He wrote.
Friday. Morning. Brindle hay wharf. On the Patient Grace.
Burned, in the sluice under Jory's garden, to get the boat free: my first night at No. 14. Mrs. Marta's bread. The first plate. (I am told by myself that it was good. I do not remember. There is a hole there, before everything else about her.)
The gray cape is called V. He folds like Kross. He saw us in the reeds and wrote NOTHING. He then went to Jory's house and put his hand on the warm wheel. I do not know why he wrote NOTHING. I do not know which of those two things is the lie.
Lord Thorne tapped on my hand. Four, two, nine, three. He looked at me. He knew I was there. He looked sorry for me.
Malakor would not come into the hay.
Lyra has Rowan back. Two words. I have the wrapper, to keep for her. I did not tell her about E.
Old Jory was taken at first light. His dog was shot. Davy has a broken head. They asked about the shed.
He stopped.
He looked at what he had written for a long time.
Then he put the pencil down, and put his hand up to his collar. To the little square pocket that Pegg had sewn shut. He could feel the paper inside it, through the cloth. The list. THOSE I OWE. He could not open it. Pegg had sewn it shut so that he could not open it and lose it.
So he wrote it in the Ledger instead. At the bottom of the page. In small careful letters, under all the rest.
Add to THOSE I OWE:
Old Jory Hobday. Who let the gate down.
Bramble.
He looked at the last name. A dog's name. He did not know if it was foolish. He did not care if it was.
Under the hay, faint and steady, the tapping went on.
Four.
Two.
Nine.
Three.
And far away down the canal to the south, where the coal boat had come from, where the locks were, and the dam, and the old willow leaning over the ice, a church bell began to ring the hour. Corin counted it. Nine strokes. He could feel every one.
He closed the Ledger, and put it back inside his coat, against his heart, and sat in the cold gray light on the deck of the Patient Grace with the hay barges waking up around him, and waited for whatever came up the water next.
