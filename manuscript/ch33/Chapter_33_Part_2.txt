Chapter 33: The Long Way North, Part 2 of 5: In the Reeds
In the Reeds
The reeds closed round them like a crowd.
They were taller than Corin had thought. From the open water they had looked like a low brown wall. But once the bow pushed in among them, they rose up on every side, higher than his head, higher than Wat's head, dry and pale and rustling, with heavy feathery tops that nodded and scraped against each other far above. The stems were as thick as his thumb. They bent as the boat pushed through, and sprang back behind it, and slapped against the gunwale with a sound like hands clapping softly.
Too loud. Everything was too loud.
"Stop," breathed Wat.
Corin lifted the oars. The boat slid on a little way, pushing the reeds aside, and slowed, and stopped. They were perhaps twenty yards in. When Corin looked back the way they had come, he could still see the open lane of water, a gray gap between the stems, and the dark path they had broken through the reeds to get here. Bent stems. Snapped stems. A clear track, for anybody who looked.
"He'll see that," he whispered.
"Maybe." Wat reached out with his good hand and took hold of a bunch of reed stems beside the boat, and pulled, and the boat moved a little way sideways, into a thicker place. "Pull us round. Behind this clump. Gently."
Corin did. He shipped the oars and leaned out and took hold of the stems, the way Wat had, and pulled. They were cold and dry and sharp-edged. One of them cut his palm, a long thin cut, and he did not feel it until he saw the dark line of blood. Hand over hand, they dragged the boat round behind a thick high clump of reeds, so that it stood between them and the open lane like a screen.
Then they sat still.
The cold came into the boat.
Corin had been cold before. He had been cold in the sewers, wet through to the skin, with the black water up to his chest. He had been cold on the roof of the Archive, in the rain, waiting for a light in a window. He had been cold on the Saltmarsh at dawn, with the frost on the dead ship's ribs. But he had been moving, all those times. Rowing. Running. Climbing.
Now he was sitting still. And the cold came up out of the water through the bottom of the boat, and in through the soles of his boots, and up through his legs, slow and steady, like water rising in a lock. It came in through his gloves. It came in through the cut on his palm. It came down through his collar and lay along his spine like a bar of iron.
And around them, the ice was coming.
He could hear it. He had not known you could hear ice grow. But you could, if everything else was quiet enough. A tiny, thin sound, all around them in the reeds. Not a crack. Smaller than a crack. A whisper, like someone breathing on glass. Like a thousand tiny needles being laid down, one by one, on a sheet of black paper.
He looked down over the side of the boat. In the dim light, he could see it. The water between the reed stems had gone pale and still. When he touched it with one finger, it did not move. It was a skin. Thin as paper. It broke under his finger with a tiny sound, tik, and the black water welled up through the hole.
He took his finger away. Quickly.
Tik.
So loud.
Wat was not looking at the ice. He was looking through a gap in the reeds, at the lane of open water. Corin followed his eyes.
The red light was coming.
It came up the lane slowly. It was very close now. Close enough that Corin could see the boat behind it. A narrow black skiff, low in the water, much better than theirs. A man at the oars. Big, broad, hunched forward, with a constable's flat cap pulled down over his ears and a scarf wound round his face so that only his eyes showed. He rowed beautifully. Long, slow, even strokes, and the oars went into the water without a sound and came out without a drip, and the skiff slid forward like a fish.
And in the stern, the gray cape.
Corin could see him properly now. A man of perhaps fifty. Not big. Not small. He sat very upright on the stern bench with his hands folded in his lap and the shuttered lantern on the seat beside him, and he wore a long plain coat of Inquisition gray with the short cape over the shoulders, and a plain round hat. His face, in the red glow, was the plainest face Corin had ever seen. Not handsome. Not ugly. Not kind or cruel or clever or stupid. A face you would see in any crowd and forget before you had turned your head. A face like a blank page.
The skiff came level with the place where they had gone into the reeds.
"Easy, Hagan," said the gray cape.
His voice was as plain as his face. Quiet and flat and a little tired. A clerk's voice. The voice of a man reading out a list of figures that he has read out many times before.
The big constable lifted his oars. The skiff slowed, and slid, and stopped. Right beside the broken track in the reeds. Right there. Not ten yards from Corin's hand.
Corin did not breathe.
The gray cape reached out and picked up the lantern. He opened the shutter a little more. Just a little. The red light became orange, and spread, and fell across the water and onto the reeds at the edge of the lane. Onto the bent stems. Onto the snapped stems. Onto the dark gap where the boat had pushed through.
He looked at it.
He looked at it for a long time.
Corin felt Wat beside him go very still. Stiller than still. Like a stone. Like a man who has already decided what he will do if the next thing happens, and is only waiting to see if it does.
Corin thought of the rod. The silver rod, in his right coat pocket. He could feel its weight against his hip. He could take it out. He could burn something. Anything. A summer. A song. He did not know what he would do with it if he did. Knock the man out of the boat. Light the reeds. Stop the man's heart. He did not know. He had never been good at burning in a hurry. He did not know what was left in him that would be enough.
Do not, said Malakor, very quietly. Not yet. Look at him.
Corin looked.
The gray cape had not moved. He was still looking at the broken reeds, with the lantern held up in one gloved hand. His face had not changed at all.
"Ducks," said the constable, Hagan, through his scarf. His voice was thick and muffled. "Or a fox. They come down for the ducks, the foxes. When the ice comes."
The gray cape said nothing.
"Or a boat," said Hagan, after a moment. More quietly.
The gray cape looked at the reeds for a little while longer.
Then he looked down at the water beside the skiff. At the white skin of ice, creeping out from the reeds, closing in. He looked up the lane of open water toward the lock, where the five lanterns swung on the top of the dam. He looked back down the lane toward the Ditch.
"The ice is coming in, Hagan," he said.
"Sir."
"Anybody who went into those reeds tonight is going to be in them until the morning thaw." He said it the way another man might say it looks like rain. "Or until they freeze."
"Sir."
"So there is no hurry."
The constable shifted on his bench. The skiff rocked a little.
"We could go home, sir," he said, very low, through the scarf. "The Inquisitor sent you home. I heard him say it. Go home and get some sleep."
"He did," said the gray cape. "And I went."
He was quiet for a moment.
"It was very quiet there," he said.
He set the lantern down on the bench beside him, and closed the shutter until the light was red again. Then he took off one glove, finger by finger, and laid it on his knee. He put his bare hand inside his coat and brought out a small black notebook and a short pencil. He opened the notebook on his knee. And he wrote something in it.
Corin watched the pencil move. It was too far, and too dark, to see what it wrote. Just a few words. A line. Maybe two.
The gray cape tore the page out of the notebook.
He did it very neatly. Slowly, along the fold, so that the edge was clean. Then he began to fold it.
Corin had seen a great many people fold paper. Clerks at the Archive, folding letters. Mrs. Marta, folding her rent receipts into her tin. Juno, folding her maps. He had never seen anybody fold paper like this.
The gray cape folded the page in half. And in half again. And again. Each fold slow and exact, pressed down hard with the side of his thumbnail so that it was sharp as a knife's edge. And again. And again. Until the page was no bigger than a fingernail. A tiny, hard, white square, folded so tight that it looked less like paper than like a little chip of bone.
Corin's skin went cold. Colder than the night.
He had seen that fold once before. Not with his own eyes. In another man's memory, harvested from a jar. A small, tidy hand folding a barley sugar wrapper, under a lamp, in an office on the Hill. Fold it small. Fold it small and keep it close.
The gray cape leaned out over the side of the skiff.
There was a stick in the water, at the edge of the reeds. An old dead branch, half sunk, with one end sticking up out of the mud. Corin had not noticed it. The gray cape had. He reached out and pushed the little white square into a split in the top of the stick, very carefully, until it was wedged tight. Then he sat back and put his glove on again, finger by finger.
"On, Hagan," he said. "To the lock. I want to speak to the sergeant before the ice closes the lane."
The big constable put his oars in.
The skiff slid away up the lane of open water toward the lanterns. Slowly. Silently. The red light got smaller. The sound of the oars, which had been no sound at all, faded into the whisper of the reeds.
And Corin breathed.
It came out of him in a long, shaking cloud of white. His hands were trembling. He put them between his knees and pressed until they stopped.
"He saw," he whispered. "He saw the reeds."
"He saw something," said Wat.
"He knew."
Wat did not answer that. He was watching the red light going up toward the lock.
"He left something," said Corin. "In the stick."
"I saw."
"For who? For Kross?"
"For somebody coming after." Wat breathed. "That's how they do it on the river. The Watch boats. Leave a note in a split stick at every turn, so the next boat knows where you've been." He looked at the stick. "You touch it, he'll know somebody touched it."
Corin looked at the stick. At the tiny white square in the top of it, pale in the dark, ten yards away across the creeping ice.
You want it, said Malakor.
He did. He wanted it more than he had wanted anything in days. It was the first thing about the gray cape that was not plain. The first thing that was a thread.
Then think, said Malakor. Is it worth what it will cost you to get it?
The ice between the boat and the stick was a thin, unbroken skin now. To reach the stick, they would have to break it. Ten yards of it. Tik. Tik. Tik. All the way across. With five lanterns on the dam a few hundred yards away, and the gray cape between them and the lanterns, and the night so still that a dropped pin would carry.
And behind them, all around them, the reeds were filling up with ice. Every minute they sat here, it got thicker. Soon it would be too thick to break with an oar. Soon they would be stuck here until morning, as the gray cape had said. Or until they froze.
Corin looked at Wat.
Wat was shivering now. Hard. His whole body was shaking, in long, slow waves, and his teeth had begun to chatter. He had clenched his jaw to stop it, and the muscles in his cheek stood out like cords.
The hook wound. The cold. The long row. The weir.
"We have to go," said Corin.
"Yes," said Wat, through his teeth.
"Now. While he's at the lock."
"Yes."
Corin took up an oar. Not to row. He turned it round and held it like a pole, with the blade up and the handle down, and leaned out over the bow, and brought the end of the handle down on the ice in front of the boat.
Crack.
It went across the water like a pistol shot.
Up on the dam, one of the lanterns stopped moving.
Corin froze, with the oar in his hands.
The lantern swung toward the reeds. It hung there, very still, for a long moment. Then a dog began to bark. From the garden of the lock-keeper's house. Deep and fierce and furious, on and on, the way a dog barks at something it can smell and cannot see.
The lantern swung toward the house.
A voice called something, from the dam. Angry. Shut that dog up. And a door opened in the house, and a slice of yellow light fell across the garden, and an old man's voice, cracked and high, shouted at the dog. And the dog stopped.
And the lantern on the dam began to move again. Slowly. Along the top of the bank.
"Now," said Wat. "While they're cursing the dog. Go."
Corin brought the oar down again.
Crack.
And again. And again. Breaking a path through the white skin ahead of the bow. Not toward the open lane. Not toward the lock. Toward the stick.
Wat looked at him. He did not say anything. But his good hand reached out and took hold of the reeds, and pulled, and helped the boat along the broken path.
It took a long time. Ten yards. It felt like ten miles. Every crack of the ice was a shout. Every time, Corin stopped and waited and looked at the dam, and every time the lanterns went on walking their slow beats and did not turn. The dog barked again, once, and was shouted at, and was quiet.
And then the bow nudged against the stick, and Corin reached out with fingers he could no longer feel, and took hold of the tiny, hard, folded square of paper, and pulled it out of the split.
He did not open it. There was no light to read by, and his hands were shaking too badly to unfold it without tearing it. He put it inside his glove, against his palm, against the cut. He felt it there. Sharp-edged. Like a tooth.
Then he put the oar down, and took up both oars properly, and turned the boat in the narrow broken lane of black water, and began to row toward the west. Along the edge of the reeds. Through the ice. Crack. Splash. Crack. Away from the lock. Away from the lanterns.
Toward the dark garden, and the leaning tree, and the small warm window of Old Jory's house.
Old Jory's Garden
The cut in the reeds was where Wat had said it would be.
Corin would never have found it alone. It was nothing. A narrow gap in the great reed bed on the west side of the pond, no wider than the boat, with the dead stems leaning over it from both sides so that their feathery tops met overhead like a roof. From the open water it looked like more reeds. Only when the bow was right against it did Corin see the black water going on between the stems, straight and narrow, like a corridor.
The ice in here was thinner. The reeds kept the cold wind off, or else somebody had broken it earlier and it had not yet closed again. Corin did not know which. He did not like to think which.
They went in.
The corridor went on for about fifty yards. Then the reeds ended, all at once, and the bow bumped against something hard. Stone. A low wall of rough stone blocks, green with moss, rising up out of the water. And above it, a bank of earth, very steep, covered in dead brown grass and brambles. And above that, the garden.
And the tree.
It was a willow. Very old, and very big, with a trunk so thick that two men could not have put their arms round it. It grew right at the top of the bank, at the edge of the garden, and it leaned out over the water at a steep angle, as if it had been trying to reach the pond for a hundred years and had nearly made it. Its long bare branches hung down in a curtain, all the way to the water. They trailed in the ice. When the bow pushed in under them, they slid over Corin's head and shoulders, thin and cold and whippy, like the fingers of someone feeling his face in the dark.
Under the willow, it was black. Black and close and very quiet. The branches made a room around them, a tent of thin bare whips, and the lanterns on the dam were gone behind it. Only the faintest glow came through.
Wat reached up with his good hand and caught hold of a root that stuck out of the bank. He pulled the boat in against the stone wall, and held it.
"Here," he said.
His voice was very thin.
Corin shipped the oars. He looked at the bank. At the brambles. At the steep wet grass going up into the dark.
"Where's the sluice?"
"Under us." Wat put his foot against something in the bottom of the boat and pushed. "In the wall. There's a grating, down under the water. And an arch behind it. You can't see it. Jory has to wind it up from the shed." He breathed. "Go up. Knock on the shed. Three, then one. He'll come."
"And if he doesn't?"
Wat did not answer that.
Corin looked at him. In the dark under the willow, he could see almost nothing of Wat's face. Only the pale blur of it, and the glitter of frozen sweat in his beard, and his eyes, very dark, very tired.
"Go on," said Wat.
Corin went.
The bank was very steep and very slippery. He went up it on his hands and knees, pulling himself up by the brambles and the dead grass, and the brambles tore at his gloves and the grass came away in his hands in wet cold clumps. Halfway up, his foot slid out from under him and he went down flat on his face in the mud, and lay there for a moment, listening.
Nothing. The whisper of the reeds. The faint tick of the ice. Far off, on the dam, a man's voice saying something, and another man laughing.
He got up again and went on.
At the top of the bank there was a hedge. A low, thick hedge of something with small hard leaves that stayed green in winter. He found a gap in it, and pushed through, and stood up.
He was in the garden.
It was a lock-keeper's garden. A working garden, not a pretty one. Long and narrow, running from the willow at the bottom up to the back of the house at the top, with a path of old bricks down the middle, sunk and cracked and edged with moss. On both sides of the path were beds. Corin could see them in the faint glow from the dam. Rows of winter cabbages, round and dark, each one with a frill of frost round its edges like a lace collar. Rows of leeks, tall and stiff. A bed that had been dug over for the winter, the earth turned up in rough clods that were frozen hard. Bean poles, standing in a bare row, tied at the tops in threes, like little tents with nobody in them.
Along the left-hand wall, under a lean-to roof of old boards, there were beehives. Five of them. Not wooden boxes, like the ones on the farms Corin had seen as a boy. Old-fashioned ones. Round domes of woven straw, like big upturned baskets, each one sitting on a flat stone on a little wooden stand. Somebody had wrapped them for the winter in old sacking and tied them round with string, so that they looked like five small fat people huddled against the cold.
There was a smell of woodsmoke from the house, and of frost, and of something sweet and dark that might have been the bees' wax, or the earth, or the rotting apples under a tree he could not see.
And at the top of the garden, near the house, was the shed.
It was small and square and made of tarred boards, with a door on leather hinges and a tiny window with no glass in it. Beside it was a rain barrel. And beside the rain barrel, lying on a sack with its great head on its paws, was the dog.
Corin stopped.
It was the biggest dog he had ever seen. Bigger than any dog on Rope Street. Bigger than the Inquisition's tracking dogs, the lean gray ones with the long noses. This one was not lean. It was broad and deep and heavy, with a head like a block of wood and a short brindled coat, brown and black in stripes, like a tiger's in a picture book. There was a thick leather collar round its neck, and a chain on the collar, and the chain ran to an iron ring in the wall of the shed.
It was looking at him.
It had not barked. It had not moved. It just lay there on its sack with its head on its paws, and looked at him, with small dark eyes that caught the faint glow from the dam and shone.
Corin did not move either.
The dog lifted its head. Slowly. It sniffed the air. Its big black nose worked, in and out, in and out. And then, very low, so low that Corin felt it more than heard it, it began to growl.
It smells the river on you, said Malakor. And the blood.
Wat's blood.
And yours. You cut your hand on the reeds.
The growl went on. Low and steady, like a cart wheel rolling a long way off.
Corin took a step. Very slowly. Not toward the dog. Sideways, toward the shed door, keeping as far from the end of the chain as he could. The dog's head turned to follow him. The growl did not stop. It did not get louder. It just went on.
Another step.
Another.
He reached the shed door. He lifted his hand, and made it into a fist, and knocked. Softly. On the tarred boards.
Three. Knock, knock, knock.
Then one. Knock.
The dog stood up.
It stood up all at once, with the chain clinking, and it was even bigger standing than it had been lying down. It came to the end of its chain and stopped there, with the chain pulled tight, three feet from Corin's legs. It did not bark. It did not lunge. It just stood there and growled, and looked at him, and waited.
Nothing happened in the shed.
Corin waited. His heart was going very hard. He could feel it in the cut on his palm, where the little folded paper was pressed against it inside his glove. Thump. Thump. Thump.
Nothing.
Then, behind him, at the top of the garden, a door opened.
Yellow light fell out across the brick path. The back door of the house. Corin turned, slowly, and saw a shape standing in it, black against the kitchen glow. A small shape. Bent. With a stick in one hand and a lantern in the other.
"Bramble," said the shape. "Down."
The dog lay down.
It did it at once, the way a dog does who has been told many times by somebody it loves. It folded itself down onto the frozen ground at the end of its chain and put its great head on its paws again. It stopped growling. But it did not stop looking at Corin.
The small bent shape came down the path.
It came slowly, with the stick tapping on the old bricks, and the lantern swinging, and as it came out of the light of the doorway Corin saw that it was a man. A very old man. Small and thin and bent nearly double, with a long white beard that came down to the middle of his chest, and a woolen cap pulled down to his eyebrows, and a coat that had once been blue and was now no color at all. His face was brown and wrinkled like an old walnut. His eyes, under the cap, were very bright.
He stopped a few steps from Corin and held the lantern up.
He looked at Corin's face for a long time.
"You're not Hessa," he said.
His voice was high and cracked, like an old door, but not weak.
"No," said Corin.
"You knocked Hessa's knock."
"Wat told me to."
The old man's eyes moved. Past Corin. Down the garden, toward the hedge, and the dark bank, and the willow.
"Wat Doon's here?"
"In the boat. Under the tree." Corin swallowed. "He's hurt. An Inquisition hook. In the shoulder. This morning. He's lost a lot of blood. He's very cold."
The old man did not say anything for a moment.
Then he turned his head and looked up the garden. Past the house. Toward the dam, and the lock, and the five lanterns moving on the top of it.
"They're in my kitchen," he said quietly. "One of them. Sitting by my fire, with his boots on my fender, drinking my tea." His mouth moved under the beard. "A boy. Not twenty. He's writing a letter to his mother. He asked me how to spell Coldwater."
Corin said nothing.
"And my Davy's up on the dam," said the old man. "Holding their lights for them. They told him to. He's the lock hand. He does what he's told." He looked back at Corin. "Davy's the one Hessa pulled out. Did Wat tell you that?"
"Yes."
"Twenty-five years old now. Big as a house. Got a girl in Saltgate, he's going to marry her in the spring." The old man's voice did not change. "He'd not be alive for any of it. Not one day. Not without Hessa Doon going in that water after him, when she was no bigger than a sack of meal." He lowered the lantern. "They don't know about the sluice. The gray coats. Not yet. The boy in the kitchen asked me what was in the shed. I told him tools."
He turned, and went past Corin, and past the dog, and pushed open the shed door with his stick.
Inside it was very dark, and smelled of oil and rust and old sacks. The lantern light swung over the walls. Spades. Forks. Coils of rope. A row of clay pots on a shelf. And in the middle of the floor, an iron wheel. A big one, as wide as Corin's arms could reach, set on its side on a heavy iron post that went down into the ground. There were handles sticking up out of the rim of the wheel, four of them, worn bright.
The old man hung the lantern on a nail.
"Is it greased?" said Corin.
"Every Sunday," said Old Jory. "For sixteen years." He put his two thin hands on one of the handles. "Help me. It's heavy, and I'm old, and I've a boy in my kitchen who'll come out to see what the noise is, if there's any noise."
Corin put his hands on the next handle.
"Slowly," said Jory. "Slow as you can. Slow is quiet."
They pushed.
The wheel did not move. Then it did. Very slightly. A tiny shift, under Corin's hands, like a great sleeping animal turning over. Somewhere under their feet, deep down in the earth, something groaned. A long, low, wooden groan. And then a gurgle. And then the sound of water moving.
They pushed again.
And it was at that moment, with the wheel just beginning to turn and the water just beginning to move under the earth, that Corin heard the knock on the front door of the house.
Not a loud knock. Not an angry one. Three soft, polite taps. The knock of a man who does not need to knock loudly, because he knows that whoever is inside will open the door to him anyway.
Old Jory's hands went still on the wheel.
And through the thin wall of the house, through the open back door, through the frozen garden, Corin heard a voice. Quiet. Plain. A little tired. A clerk's voice, reading out a list.
"Good evening. I'm so sorry to call so late. Is the lock-keeper at home?"
