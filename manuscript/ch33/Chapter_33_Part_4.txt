Chapter 33: The Long Way North, Part 4 of 5: The Patient Grace
The Patient Grace
The hay wharf at Brindle was not much of a wharf. It was a long wooden landing stage built out from the left bank of the canal on posts, old and gray and crooked, with a row of iron bollards along the edge for tying up, and a sloping ramp at one end where carts could come down from the farm lanes above. Behind it, up the bank, Corin could just see the shapes of great dark buildings in the fog. Barns. Haystacks, tall and round and thatched on top like little houses. A cart with no horse, its shafts resting on the ground.
And all along the landing stage, tied up nose to tail in the fog, there were barges.
Six of them. Seven. He could not see the end of the line. Long low black shapes, all alike, each one with a great shaggy mound of hay on its deck, roped down under old brown sheets of canvas, and a short mast, and a little cabin at the stern with a tin chimney. Most of them were dark. On one, far down the line, a dog was barking in a bored, sleepy way, and somebody inside the cabin was telling it to shut up. On another, a little lamp burned in a cabin window, and Corin could see the shadow of a man moving behind the curtain, and hear, very faintly, someone coughing.
Nobody looks at a barge in the ice, Hessa had said, a long time ago, in the arches. Nobody ever looks at a barge.
She had hidden them in a crowd. A crowd of barges, all the same, all loading hay at the same wharf on the same cold night. A gray coat walking along this landing stage with a lantern would have to climb onto every one of them, and pull back every sheet of canvas, and dig through every mound of hay, before he found anything. And the bargemen on all the others would be watching him do it.
The Patient Grace was the third in the line.
Corin brought the little boat in against her side. The black hull rose above him like a wall. It was tarred, and the tar was cold and hard and smelled of pine, and there were old rope marks on it, and scrapes, and a long scar near the waterline where she had once hit something hard. Halfway along, a short rope ladder hung down over the side.
Hessa was already at the top of it.
She had put the baby down somewhere. Her sleeves were still rolled up. She leaned down over the side with her strong red arms and said, "Rope," and Corin threw her the bow line, and she caught it without looking and made it fast round a cleat in one movement, the way another woman might tie a ribbon in her hair.
Then she looked at Wat.
Wat was trying to stand up. He had his good hand on the gunwale and he was trying to get his legs under him, and they would not go. They would not hold him. He got halfway up and his knees went and he sat down again, hard, on the stern bench, and the little boat rocked so badly that Corin had to grab the ladder to keep it from going over.
"Don't," said Hessa. "Don't you dare. Don't you dare, Wat Doon." She turned her head and shouted, not loudly but very clearly, back over her shoulder at the barge: "Oren! Oren, get up here! Now!"
The cabin door at the stern of the barge banged open.
And Oren came out of it like a bull coming out of a gate.
Corin had forgotten how big he was too. Not as big as Wat. Nobody was as big as Wat. But broad and hard and heavy, with a thick dark beard going gray at the edges, and a face that had been knocked about a great deal in its life and had never once looked sorry about it. There was an old white scar running down from the corner of his left eye into the beard, like a crack in a wall. He was not wearing his green livery. That was long gone. He was wearing a bargeman's canvas jacket, too small for him, that pulled across his shoulders, and he had his sword in his hand. Bare. Out of its scabbard.
He came along the deck in three long strides, and stopped at the top of the ladder beside Hessa, and looked down.
He looked at Wat first.
Then at Corin.
And his face, which had been hard and ready and fierce, the face of a man who has come out of a door expecting a fight, went very still.
"Vane," he said.
"Oren."
They looked at each other.
Corin did not know what he had expected. Something. A word. A hand. Oren had walked north alone on Tuesday night, from Pruett's yard to the barge, through a city full of gray coats. Corin had not known if he had made it. He had written his name in the Ledger every night since, under THOSE I OWE, with a question mark after it, and every night he had looked at the question mark and not known whether to cross it out.
Oren was here. He had made it.
And he was looking at Corin the way a man looks at a letter he does not want to open.
Then Hessa said, "Oren. Wat."
And Oren put his sword down on the deck, and came down the ladder.
It took both of them. Oren on the ladder, and Corin in the little boat, and Hessa on the deck above, hauling. Corin got his shoulder under Wat's good arm and lifted. Oren reached down from the ladder and got his hands under Wat's armpits and lifted. And between them, slowly, with Wat making that long slow breathing sound through his teeth the whole way, they got him up. Onto the bottom rung. The next. The next. Until Hessa could reach down and take hold of the back of his coat with both fists and pull, and Oren pushed, and Wat came up over the side of the Patient Grace and fell onto the deck on his knees, in the loose hay, and stayed there.
Hessa knelt down beside him.
She did not say anything this time. She put her hand on his face. Just that. Her small red hand, flat against his gray cheek, over the beard. And she held it there.
Wat leaned into it. Like a horse leaning into a hand. He shut his eyes.
Corin looked away.
He climbed up the ladder. His legs were shaking so badly that he nearly missed the top rung. Oren put out a hand without looking and caught his arm, and hauled him over the side, and let go of him at once, as if he had picked up something hot.
Corin stood on the deck of the Patient Grace.
It was just as he remembered it. That was the strange thing. So much had happened, and so much of him had gone, and the barge was just the same. The long black deck, scrubbed pale where people walked and dark where they didn't. The great mound of hay, piled up higher than a man's head along the whole middle of the deck, roped down under its brown canvas sheets, smelling of summer. The short mast, with the red sail furled on its yard. The little cabin at the stern, with its tin chimney smoking, and its one small window glowing yellow, and its narrow door standing open on a ladder that went down into warm light.
And in the doorway of the cabin, holding on to the frame, there was a big soft round woman in a brown dress and an apron, with gray hair pinned up under a cap, and baby Robin on her hip.
Mrs. Hobb.
She looked at Corin. At his face. He did not know what his face looked like. Torn by brambles, he supposed. Muddy. White with cold.
"Well," said Mrs. Hobb. "You'd best come in by the stove before you fall down. And the other one. Hessa, love, bring him in. I'll put the kettle on."
And she went back down the ladder into the warm light with the baby, humming.
Hessa and Oren got Wat up off his knees between them. Oren took most of his weight. They went along the deck toward the cabin, slowly, the three of them, like a strange beast with six legs. Corin followed them.
He was halfway along the deck, beside the great mound of hay, when the canvas moved.
Not much. One corner of one brown sheet, lifted from underneath. And a face looked out at him from the dark gap between the canvas and the hay.
A young woman's face. Pale. With hay in her hair. With dark eyes, very wide, and a smudge of ink on one cheekbone that she had not noticed. With her lips pressed together hard, the way somebody presses their lips together when they have been holding their breath for a very long time and have just been told they can stop.
She looked at him.
He looked at her.
And for one terrible moment, standing on the cold deck in the fog, Corin Vane did not know her name.
It was only a moment. Less than a moment. The space between one heartbeat and the next. He looked at her face, and it was a face, and it meant something, it meant a great deal, he could feel how much it meant, like heat coming off a stove. And he reached for the name, the way he had reached for ten in the boat, and his hand closed on nothing.
Then it was there.
Lyra.
Lyra Thorne.
It came back so fast and so hard that he took a step backward, and nearly fell over a coil of rope on the deck.
The Inkwell, said Malakor, very softly. The rain. The girl with ink on her hands who walked into your life and asked you to find her father. A pause. It is all still there. You did not burn it. Not yet. You are only tired.
Not yet.
Not yet.
Lyra came out from under the canvas.
She came out on her hands and knees, in a long gray coat that was much too big for her, and stood up, and hay fell off her in a shower. She had been under there a long time. Corin could tell from the way she stood. Stiff, and stooped a little, like somebody who has been folded up small in a tight place and has not yet remembered how to be any other shape.
She did not say anything.
She came across the deck. Three steps. And she stopped in front of him, very close, and looked up into his face. At the bramble cuts. At the mud. At his eyes.
He did not know what she saw there. He hoped it was not the moment. The empty moment, before the name came back.
"You're late," said Lyra Thorne.
Her voice cracked in the middle.
"I know," said Corin.
"Two days. You said one night. You said you'd be one night behind us." Her voice was steadier now. Fierce. Small and fierce, like her handwriting. "Two days, Corin. With nothing. No word. Juno didn't come. Nobody came. And Oren walked in out of the dark on Tuesday night with his boots worn through and said you were going to sit in Mrs. Marta's house and wait for the Inquisition to come up the stairs for you. On purpose. And that was all he knew. That was all anybody knew."
"I know."
"I thought you were dead."
He did not say I know this time.
She was still looking up at him. And then her face did something. It crumpled. Just for a moment. Like paper in a fist. And she put her hands up and took hold of the front of his coat, both fists, the way Hessa had taken hold of Wat's, and she did not pull him. She just held on.
And she put her forehead against his chest.
Corin stood very still.
He could feel her shaking. Through the coat. Through the Ledger in his inside pocket, pressed between them. Through the little sewn-shut pocket in his collar, the one with the list in it, where her name was the second one down, after a name that had been struck out.
He did not know what to do with his hands. He had not known for a long time. He lifted them, and they hung in the air for a moment, uselessly, on either side of her.
Then he put them on her back. Lightly. Through the much-too-big gray coat. And he held on too.
He did not know how long they stood there. Not long. Long enough. The fog moved around them. The barge creaked gently against the landing stage. Somewhere down the line, the bored dog barked once more and was quiet.
Then Lyra let go of his coat, and stepped back, and wiped her face hard with the heel of her hand, and left a new smudge of ink on the other cheekbone.
"You smell terrible," she said.
"Tanneries," said Corin.
She almost laughed. It came out of her like a cough.
Then her eyes went past him, to the cabin door, where Oren and Hessa were lowering Wat down the ladder into the light, with Mrs. Hobb's voice coming up from below, saying mind his head, mind his head, oh, the poor great lump. And her face changed again. Went hard and quick and practical.
"Is it bad?" she said.
"A hook," said Corin. "This morning. He stood in front of me."
Lyra looked at him.
And behind her, at the cabin door, Oren straightened up from the ladder. He had heard. Corin saw him hear it. He stood in the doorway with the yellow light behind him and his scarred face in shadow, and he looked at Corin across the length of the deck, over the top of Lyra's head.
He did not say anything.
He did not need to.
A Sail Needle
The cabin of the Patient Grace was about the size of Mrs. Marta's scullery, and there were six people in it.
Seven, counting Robin. Eight, counting Lord Thorne, who was not in the cabin itself but in the little space beyond it, behind a curtain of old sacking, where Corin could hear him breathing.
It was a low room, so low that Oren and Wat could not stand up straight in it, and Corin had to duck his head under the beams. The walls were planked and painted a dark, faded green, and covered all over with things hanging from hooks and nails. Pots and pans. A string of onions. A bunch of dried herbs. A pair of boots. A coil of rope. A bird cage with no bird in it. A small mirror with the silver going brown at the edges. On one wall there was a shelf with a row of tin mugs on it, and a jar of sugar, and a jar of tea, and a little china dog with one ear broken off. On another, a cupboard bed, set into the wall like a box with a sliding wooden door, where Hessa and Wat must sleep. The door was open. Inside, Corin could see a patchwork quilt, very bright, red and yellow and blue.
At the end of the cabin, against the wall that backed onto the hay, was the stove.
It was a little black iron stove, squat and round like a pot on legs, with a tin chimney going up through the roof. It was red hot. The air around it shook. Mrs. Hobb had a big black kettle on it already, and the kettle was beginning to tick and grumble, and the whole cabin was so warm after the night outside that Corin's face began to burn the moment he came down the ladder, and his fingers, which he had not felt for an hour, began to come back to life one by one, and hurt so badly that he had to sit down on the bottom step and hold them between his knees.
They put Wat on the floor in front of the stove.
There was nowhere else. They laid him on a pile of sacks with his head on a folded blanket, and he lay there with his eyes shut and his great bald head shining with sweat in the firelight, and his chest going up and down too fast.
Hessa knelt beside him with a knife.
She cut the sacking off his arm. Then she cut his shirt off. She did it quickly and neatly, without a word, the way she had tied up Corin's boat, and pulled the cloth away from the wound where it had stuck. Wat made a sound. Low, in his chest. Hessa did not stop.
Corin looked at the wound, and then looked away, and then made himself look back.
It was in the thick muscle at the top of Wat's arm, near the shoulder, at the front. A ragged hole, about as wide as a thumb, where the iron hook had gone in. And a second hole, not quite so big, a little way round toward the back, where the point had come out. The skin around both holes was swollen and dark and shiny. The blood had dried black around the edges, but in the middle of them it was still red and wet, and when Hessa pressed the skin beside the front hole with her thumb, a little more came out.
Mrs. Hobb put a basin down beside Hessa. Steaming water. A cloth. A brown bottle with no label.
"Is it clean?" said Hessa. She did not look up.
"Boiled," said Mrs. Hobb.
"The cloth?"
"Boiled."
Hessa picked up the brown bottle and pulled the cork out with her teeth and spat it into her hand. The smell came across the cabin like a slap. Sharp and hot and raw. Corin's eyes watered.
"This'll hurt," said Hessa to Wat.
She said it once.
Corin had never heard her say anything once before.
Wat opened his eyes and looked at her. Then he nodded. And Oren, without being asked, knelt down at Wat's head and put one big hand flat on Wat's good shoulder, and the other on his chest, and leaned his weight on them.
Hessa poured.
Wat did not scream. Corin had thought he would. Any man would. But he did not. His whole body went rigid on the sacks, from his heels to his head, like a bow being drawn. His back came up off the floor. Oren leaned harder, and pushed him down. The cords in Wat's neck stood out like ropes. His teeth were clenched so hard that Corin heard them grind. And a sound came out of him, out of somewhere very deep, through his clenched teeth, a long, low, terrible humming, like a bell that has been struck and goes on and on.
Then it was over.
Wat lay back on the sacks. He was breathing in great long gasps. Tears were running out of the corners of his shut eyes, down the sides of his face, into his beard. He did not seem to know they were there.
Hessa put the bottle down.
Then she took a needle out of the front of her dress, where it had been stuck through the cloth. It was not a sewing needle. It was a sail needle, long and thick and three-sided, with a sharp point and a big eye, the kind used for stitching canvas. She threaded it with a length of waxed brown thread from a reel in her apron pocket. She held the point of it in the stove door, in the fire, until it glowed. Then she let it cool.
And she began to sew up her husband's arm.
Corin watched her hands. He could not watch anything else. Small, red, rough, square hands, with short nails and a burn scar across the back of one, and they did not shake. Not once. In. Through. Pull. Knot. Cut. In. Through. Pull. Knot. Cut. Each stitch small and tight and even, the way she would have stitched a torn sail. She did not hurry. She did not slow down. She did not say anything at all.
Wat lay with his eyes shut and Oren's hand on his chest, and did not make a sound.
Mrs. Hobb stood by the stove with Robin on her hip, and rocked him, and hummed her low wandering tune. Robin had his fist in his mouth. He was watching his mother's hands too, with great round solemn eyes.
Lyra sat on the edge of the cupboard bed with her knees drawn up. She was watching Corin.
Seven stitches in the front. Five in the back. Corin counted them, and for once he could feel them both. Seven. Five. Small numbers. Numbers he still had.
Hessa tied the last knot. She cut the thread with her teeth. She put the needle back through the front of her dress. Then she took a clean cloth from Mrs. Hobb and folded it into a pad and laid it over the stitches and bound it in place with a long strip of linen, round and round the arm and over the shoulder and under the other arm, firm and neat.
Then she sat back on her heels.
And she put her face in her hands.
Nobody said anything.
She stayed like that for a long time. Not crying. Corin could see she was not crying. Her shoulders were not moving. She was just sitting there, on her heels, on the floor of her own cabin, with her face in her small red hands, very still. As if she had been holding something up for a very long time, all day, all week, and had just been allowed to put it down, and did not yet know how to stand without it.
Wat's good hand moved on the sacks.
It went out, slowly, across the floor. Feeling. It found her knee. It stopped there.
Hessa took her hands away from her face.
She looked down at the big hand on her knee. Then she put her own hand over it. And she looked across at Corin.
"Tell me," she said. "Everything. Twice, if you have to. I'm too tired to hear things once."
So Corin told her.
He told her about the sewers, and the light voice at the grating, and how that voice had let him go. He told her about the wreck, and the boat with the eye on its flag, and the hook. He told her about Juno, and the cloth. He told her about the Ropewalk Stairs, and the Mill Cut, and the weir, and the Ditch. He told her about the tanner on his bucket.
"He says the hides were short last spring," said Corin. "He says you owe him a goat."
Something happened at the corner of Hessa's mouth. "Big Abel," she said. "He always says that. I've never owed him a goat in my life. Never in my life."
He told her about the Long Pond, and the ice, and the lanterns. He did not tell her about the gray cape. Not yet. He saw Oren's eyes on him when he left it out, and he knew Oren had noticed, and he went on.
He told her about Old Jory.
About the garden, and the dog, and the beehives in their sacking, and the boy in the kitchen asking how to spell Coldwater. About the wheel. About the old man taking his hands off the wheel, and going up the path to answer the door, slowly, because old men are slow.
"He said to tell you," said Corin. "Davy's getting married. In the spring. To a girl in Saltgate." He swallowed. "He said you're to come. He said to tell you he said so. Twice."
Hessa looked at him for a long moment.
Then she looked down at the floor.
"Davy," she said quietly. "Little Davy Hobday. He had red hair. When he came up out of that water his hair was all dark with it and I thought, he's not got red hair after all, I've got the wrong boy." She was quiet. "He bit me. When I had him by the hair. He bit my arm right through my sleeve." She pushed up the sleeve of the big coat, and there it was, on the inside of her forearm. A small white mark. A ring of tiny white dots, like a seal pressed into wax. "I said, I'll not pull you out if you bite me. And he let go. And I pulled him out."
She pulled the sleeve down again.
"Married," she said. "Well. He was always going to be big."
Then she looked up at Corin. And her eyes were very steady.
"And the gate," she said. "Who let the gate down?"
Corin did not answer.
"Jory let it down," said Hessa. "Didn't he. Behind you. With somebody standing there watching him do it."
"I don't know," said Corin. "I didn't see. I heard it close. That's all."
Hessa nodded slowly.
She did not say anything else. She did not need to. She just sat there on her heels on the floor, with Wat's hand under hers, and looked at the stove. And Corin knew that she was thinking what he had been thinking all the way up the canal in the fog. About an old man with a stick, alone in a shed, with a gray-caped stranger beside him and a big iron wheel that had been rusted shut for thirty years, and was warm.
The kettle began to sing.
Mrs. Hobb took it off the stove and poured, and the smell of strong black tea filled the cabin, and she put a tin mug into Corin's hand and closed his fingers round it, because he did not seem to be able to do it himself. It was so hot it burned him. He held on.
And Oren stood up from beside Wat, ducking his head under the beams, and said, quite quietly:
"Vane. A word. On deck."
Not a question.
Corin looked at Lyra. Lyra was looking at Oren. Her face had gone very still.
Corin put down the tea, and stood up, and followed Oren up the ladder into the cold.
On deck, the fog was as thick as ever. The light from the cabin window made a small gold square on the deck boards, and beyond it there was nothing but white. Oren walked to the bow, past the great mound of hay, to where the deck ended in a short raised platform with a coil of rope on it and a bucket. He stopped there and turned round. He was a dark shape against the fog. Big. Very still.
"You were followed," he said.
It was not a question either.
"Yes," said Corin.
"Who?"
"A man in a gray cape. Inquisition. A watcher. Kross's man. His constable called him sir. He was rowed by a constable called Hagan." Corin took a breath. "I don't know his name. Juno saw him once, on the steps at Tallow Street. Plain face. Writes everything down."
"And you lost him."
"I think so."
"You think so."
"We hid in the reeds. He went past. He went to the lock. Then he came to Jory's house. And then he went into the shed." Corin made himself say the next part. "And he put his hand on the wheel. After I had turned it. While it was still warm."
Oren said nothing.
The fog moved between them. Corin could not see his face. Only the shape of him, and the dull gleam of the sword hilt at his side, where he had picked it up again off the deck and belted it on.
"Kross let you run," said Oren at last. Quietly. "Out of the sewers. You told it to Hessa just now, as if it were good news. A man like Kross does not let a hare go. Not unless he wants to see which hole it runs to. It is what I would do, in his place." A pause. "And you ran here."
"Where else was I going to run?"
"Anywhere." Oren's voice did not rise. That was worse than if it had. It went lower. Harder. "Anywhere in the world, Vane. South. East. Into the sea. You could have walked into the Saltmarsh and lain down in the reeds and let the tide have you, and it would have been better for every person on this boat than this."
Corin did not answer.
"There's a baby on this barge," said Oren. "There's a woman who cannot be more than twenty-five years old, who never asked for any of this, who took in my lord out of the kindness of her own heart because a girl came to her in the arches and asked her to. There's a nurse who has never hurt anybody. There's an old man under the hay who cannot tell his own daughter from a coat on a peg." He stopped. "And there's Lyra."
"I know."
"Do you?"
"Yes."
They stood in the fog.
Then Oren put his hand into the front of his canvas jacket and took something out. Corin could not see what it was. Oren held it in his fist for a moment. Then he opened his hand.
It was a scrap of paper. Folded twice. Creamy and thick, with a faint smell coming off it, even in the cold. A smell of flowers.
Lilies.
"Cressida's," said Oren. "My lord's sister. The Councillor. She sent this to Pruett's yard, by a boy, the day before I left. Asking after my lord's health. Asking where he had been moved to." He closed his fist on it again. "I did not answer. I did not know who else had read it before it came to me. I did not know who else knew where I was."
He put the paper away.
"That is how you keep people alive, Vane," he said. "Not by running to them. By not knowing where they are. By not letting anybody follow you there. By staying away."
"I know."
"Then why are you here?"
Corin opened his mouth.
And found that he did not have an answer. Not one that he could say out loud. Not one that would mean anything to a man like Oren, standing in the fog with his hand on his sword.
Because I had nowhere else, he thought. Because Mrs. Marta's house has my empty room in it and a bird cut on the wall. Because I burned the first night I ever spent there, an hour ago, in a tunnel, to get here. Because there is a list in my collar of the people I owe, and three of them are on this boat. Because I thought she was going to be under the hay, and she was.
He did not say any of that.
Instead he took off his left glove.
The little folded square of paper was still there, stuck to the cut on his palm with dried blood. He peeled it off. It came away with a small sting. He held it out to Oren on his open hand, in the faint gold light from the cabin window.
"He left this," he said. "Vell. In a split stick at the edge of the reeds. Where we went in. For the next boat to find."
Oren looked at it. A tiny, hard, white square, no bigger than a fingernail, with a brown stain on one corner.
"What does it say?"
"I haven't opened it."
Oren looked at him.
Then he took it. Very carefully, between one big finger and thumb. He turned it over. He looked at the folds. Corin saw him see them. Saw his face change, very slightly, in the dim light, the way Corin's own face must have changed in the reeds.
"Kross folds like that," said Oren. "His wrappers. The boy at the Office. Lyra's brother." He looked up. "Juno told us. Rowan's letter came folded like that."
"I know."
Oren unfolded it.
It took a long time. The folds were so tight and so sharp that they did not want to come open. He had to pick at each one with his thumbnail, and smooth it, and pick at the next. Corin held his breath. If it tore—
It did not tear.
Oren held it flat, a small square of notebook paper, creased into a grid of tiny squares like a chessboard. He tilted it toward the gold light from the window. Corin leaned in to look.
There were two lines on it, in pencil. Small, neat, even letters. A clerk's hand.
Long Pond. Second bell past midnight. Ice coming in fast.
Reeds, east side: nothing. V.
They looked at it for a long time.
"Nothing," said Oren.
"He saw the reeds," said Corin. "He held the lantern on them. He looked right at where we'd gone in. For a long time."
"Then he's a fool."
"He's not a fool."
"Then he's lying."
"Yes."
"Why?"
Corin did not know. He looked at the two neat lines. At the small, careful letters. At the V.
Nothing, the gray cape had written. And then he had folded it up small, smaller, into a hard tight square, the way Kross folded his wrappers, and pushed it into a stick where anybody coming after would find it, and read it, and stop looking in the reeds.
And then he had gone to the lock-keeper's house, and knocked three times, softly, and asked to see the shed.
"I don't know," said Corin.
Oren folded the paper up again. Not small. Just in half. And gave it back.
"Then we leave at first light," he said. "All of us. Hessa will know where." He turned toward the cabin. Then stopped, with his back to Corin. "And you will tell Lyra about her brother. Tonight. Before she hears it from anybody else."
He went back along the deck, past the hay, into the gold light, and down the ladder.
Corin stood alone in the bow of the Patient Grace with the paper in his hand and the fog all around him.
After a while he looked down. At his hand. At the cut, where the paper had been. It had opened again when he peeled the paper off. A thin red line, across the middle of his palm. Like a line on a page. Like a line under a name.
