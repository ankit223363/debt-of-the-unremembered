Chapter 33: The Long Way North, Part 3 of 5: Through the Sluice
Through the Sluice
For a moment, neither of them moved.
They stood in the dark shed with their hands on the iron wheel, the old man and the young one, and listened. Under their feet, deep in the earth, the water went on gurgling. Softly. Like somebody drinking.
In the house, a chair scraped. The boy in the kitchen getting to his feet. A young voice said, "Sir!" Too loud. Too quick. The voice of a boy who had been caught with his boots on the fender.
And the plain voice said, "Sit down, Constable. Finish your letter. Your mother will want to hear from you."
Old Jory took his hands off the wheel.
He did it slowly, as if they were very heavy. He looked at Corin. In the swinging light of the lantern on the nail, his walnut face was quite calm. But his bright eyes had gone very still.
"Four turns," he said. Very low. "Four whole turns, and the gate's up enough for a little boat. No more. More, and the water comes through too fast, and it'll take you into the wall." He breathed. "Then go. Don't wait. Don't look back. I'll let it down behind you, when I can."
"When you can?"
Old Jory took his stick from where he had leaned it against the wall.
"He'll want to see the shed," he said. "That sort always wants to see the shed." He took the lantern off the nail. "I'll be slow coming. I'm very old. Old men are slow."
He went to the door.
Then he stopped, and turned back, and looked at Corin once more.
"Tell Hessa," he said. "Davy's girl. In the spring. Tell her she's to come." Something moved under the white beard. "Tell her I said so. Twice."
And he went out, and pulled the shed door to behind him, and Corin was alone in the dark with the wheel.
He heard the stick tap up the brick path. Slowly. Very slowly. Tap. Tap. He heard the old man say something to the dog. He heard the back door. He heard, through it, the plain voice again, closer now, in the kitchen.
"Ah. Mr. Hobday. Thank you. I won't keep you long."
Corin put his hands on the handle of the wheel.
It was dark. Completely dark. Not even the faint glow from the dam came into the shed, with the door shut. He could not see the wheel. He could not see his hands. He could only feel them. The cold iron, worn smooth. The cut on his palm, stinging. The little hard paper square, pressed against the cut.
He pushed.
The wheel was much heavier without the old man. It did not move. He leaned on it. He put his shoulder to the handle and his feet against the dirt floor and pushed until the cords stood out in his neck, and it moved. A finger's width. A groan, far down in the earth.
He stopped. Listened.
In the kitchen, the plain voice was saying something. He could not make out the words. Only the tone of it. Quiet and even and polite. Asking. Waiting. Asking again.
He pushed.
The wheel moved. Another finger's width. Another groan.
One.
He could not count it in turns. He had no way of knowing where a turn began or ended in the dark. So he counted handles. Every time a handle came round to where his hands were, he took his hands off the old one and put them on the new one. One. Four handles to a turn. Four turns. He did not let himself think how many handles that was. Some number beyond nine. A number he could say but not feel.
He pushed.
Two.
It got easier as it went. The wheel had a weight to it once it was moving, like a millstone, and the weight carried it a little way on its own after every push. The groaning in the earth became a steady creak. The gurgling became a rushing. Water, running fast, somewhere under the garden.
Three.
Four.
One turn.
He heard a door open. Not the back door. A door inside the house. The plain voice, suddenly much clearer, as if it had come into the back kitchen.
"—your garden, Mr. Hobday? It goes right down to the Pond, I think. I saw the tree from the dam. A fine old tree."
And Jory's cracked voice, high and slow and wandering, like a very old man who had lost the thread of his own thoughts: "Willow. My father planted it. Or his father. I forget. I forget things now."
"I'm sorry to hear it."
"It's the cold. Gets in your head."
"Yes," said the plain voice. "I find that too."
Corin pushed.
One.
Two.
"And the shed, Mr. Hobday. At the bottom of the garden. With the dog. What do you keep in the shed?"
"Tools," said Jory. "I told the boy. Tools."
"What sort of tools?"
Three.
Four.
Two turns.
"Spades," said Jory's voice. "Forks. My father's scythe. Pots for the beans. I forget. You'd have to look."
A little pause.
"I'd like to," said the plain voice. "If you don't mind. When you're ready. There's no hurry."
Corin's arms were burning. His shoulder, where it pressed the handle, had gone numb. He pushed. He pushed. His boots slid on the dirt floor and he caught himself on the wheel and pushed again.
One.
Two.
"Let me get my coat," said Jory's voice. "It's cold. I'm very old."
"Of course."
Three.
"Where did I put my coat? I had it a moment ago."
"I think it's on the hook, Mr. Hobday. Behind you."
"Is it? Is it. I forget things."
Four.
Three turns.
The water under the garden was roaring now. A deep, hollow, hurrying roar, coming up through the earth, through the floor of the shed, into the soles of his feet. Surely they could hear it in the house. Surely anybody standing in the back kitchen with the door open could hear it.
It sounds like the wind, said Malakor. To anybody who does not know. Push.
He pushed.
One.
The back door. Open. He heard it. And the dog, outside, gave one deep, short bark.
Two.
"Bramble," said Jory's voice. Outside now. In the garden. "Quiet. It's only the gentleman."
Three.
The tap of the stick on the brick path. Slow. So slow. Tap. And beside it, softer, a second set of footsteps. Even. Light. Making almost no sound at all on the frozen bricks.
Four.
Four turns.
Corin let go of the wheel.
It went on turning a little way by itself, creaking, and then stopped. He did not wait. He found the door by feeling along the wall. He pushed it open. It swung out on its leather hinges without a sound.
The garden was there. Gray and frozen in the faint glow from the dam. The dog on its sack. The beehives in their sacking under the lean-to. And at the top of the garden, coming down the brick path between the cabbages, two shapes. One small and bent, with a lantern and a stick, coming very slowly. One upright behind it, in a long coat and a short cape, with its hands folded in front of it.
They were twenty yards away. Fifteen.
The old man was holding the lantern very low. Down by his knees. So that its light fell on the path in front of his feet and nowhere else.
Corin went out of the shed sideways, in the shadow of the wall, past the rain barrel. The dog lifted its head and looked at him and did not make a sound.
He went along the wall. Into the shadow of the lean-to. Behind the beehives. He crouched there, between the last hive and the wall, with the smell of old straw and wax in his nose, and did not breathe.
The lantern came down the path.
"Here we are," said Jory's voice, very close. "Here's the shed. Mind the dog. She doesn't like strangers."
"Thank you."
"Mind the step. There's a step."
The shed door creaked. The lantern light went in. Swung. Lit up the inside. Spades. Forks. Rope. Pots. The great iron wheel on its post.
Silence.
"That's a large wheel, Mr. Hobday," said the plain voice.
"Is it?"
"What does it do?"
A long pause. Corin, crouched behind the beehives, closed his eyes.
"Mill drain," said Jory. "For the old mill. Over the bank. On the other side." His voice wandered, high and thin. "Rusted shut. Thirty years. Forty. My father used to wind it. I can't. I'm too old. I just keep the shed."
"Rusted shut," said the plain voice.
"Thirty years."
Another silence.
Corin opened his eyes. He could see the edge of the shed door from where he crouched. He could see the light inside it. And he could see a hand. A gloved hand, coming into the light. Reaching out. Touching the rim of the great iron wheel.
The hand lay on the iron for a moment.
Then it moved, slowly, along the rim, to one of the handles. The handle Corin had pushed last. And the gloved fingers closed around it.
And stayed there.
Corin did not wait to see what they did next.
He went out from behind the beehives and along the bottom of the lean-to and through the gap in the hedge, low and fast, and down the bank. Not on his hands and knees this time. On his back. Sliding. Feet first through the wet grass and the brambles, with the thorns tearing at his coat and his hair and his face, and the willow branches whipping round him, until his boots hit the stone wall at the bottom and he nearly went into the water.
The boat was there. Wat was there.
And the water under the boat was moving.
It was being pulled. Corin could see it. The black surface between the boat and the stone wall had a dip in it, a smooth dark hollow, like the dip in a sheet when someone pulls it from underneath. And in the stone wall, just below the waterline, where there had been nothing before, there was an opening. A round black mouth, half under the water, with a broken iron grating hanging from the top of it. The water was pouring into it. Fast and smooth and silent.
The sluice.
"Get in," said Wat.
Corin got in. He fell in. The boat rocked and swung and the bow turned toward the black mouth as if it were being called.
"Lie down," said Wat. "Flat. In the bottom. The roof's low."
"Wat—"
"Down."
Corin lay down. Flat in the bottom of the boat, in the cold bilge water, with his face against the wet boards. He felt Wat lie down behind him. He felt the boat let go of the stone wall.
And the dark took them.
It was like being swallowed. One moment there was the willow and the faint glow of the sky. The next, there was nothing. Black. A roaring black. The water took the boat and threw it forward into the tunnel, and the roof came down so close above them that Corin felt the old bricks scrape the back of his coat. Water was everywhere. Pouring in over the sides. Spraying off the walls. Roaring in his ears, so loud that it was not a sound at all any more but a kind of pressure, a weight on his skull.
The boat hit the wall.
It hit hard, on the left side, with a crack of wood on stone, and swung, and Corin felt it turn under him. Sideways. It was going sideways down the tunnel. And then the bow hit the right-hand wall, and the stern hit the left, and the boat stopped.
Stuck. Jammed across the tunnel. Like a stick in a drain.
And the water kept coming.
It piled up behind them. Corin felt it. It came over the stern in a cold black wall and filled the bottom of the boat, and rose, and rose, over his legs, over his back, and the boat began to tip, slowly, the stern going down under the weight of it, the bow rising up against the roof.
He heard Wat shout. One word, lost in the roar.
The water was at his neck. His chin. He lifted his face out of it and breathed, and breathed water, and coughed, and breathed again.
Burn, said Malakor. Very quiet. Very close. Not mocking. Now. Or drown. Choose.
Corin's hand found the rod.
It was in his right pocket, where it always was. He could not see it. He did not need to. He pulled it out, and the silver spiral was cold and slippery in his fingers, and the water was at his mouth, and he did not think about what to burn. There was no time to think. He reached into himself, into the shelves, the way he had been taught, the way he had done a hundred times in a hundred dark places, and he took the first warm thing his hand touched.
A kitchen.
A small kitchen, with a low ceiling, and a range with a fire in it, and a table with a cloth on it. Blue and white checks. And a woman with gray hair and red hands, putting a plate down in front of him. Bread. A thick slice, still warm, with dripping on it and salt. You look like you've not eaten in a week, the woman said. Eat that. Then you can tell me your name.
His first night at No. 14.
He did not want to. Even with the water in his mouth, he did not want to.
He burned it.
It went up like dry paper. A rush of heat, out of his chest, down his arm, into the rod, and out of the rod in a hard bright blow of force. Not light. Push. Like a great hand. It hit the stuck bow of the boat and shoved, and the wood screamed against the stone, and the boat swung, and came free, and straightened, and shot forward down the tunnel with the water roaring after it.
Corin was thrown back against Wat. He felt Wat's good arm come round him and hold on.
They went down the dark.
Fast. Faster than any horse. The roof flying past just over their heads. The walls flying past on either side. The roar of the water. The boat bucking and swinging under them. And Corin lay in the bottom of it with Wat's arm around him and the rod in his fist and the cold water over his legs, and tried to remember what he had just burned.
A kitchen. A woman. Bread.
He could not see it any more. He knew it had been there. He knew it the way you know a tooth has been pulled, by the hole. Mrs. Marta. He knew her. He knew her face, her red hands, her rent tin, the way she said his name. All of that was still there. But somewhere, before all of that, there had been a first time. A first night. A first plate. A first kindness from a stranger, in a city where no stranger had ever been kind to him.
And it was gone.
Then there was light ahead. Gray light. A round gray hole, rushing toward them, getting bigger.
And they came out of the end of the tunnel the way a cork comes out of a bottle, into the open air, into a wide still pond on the far side of the dam, with the sky over them, brown and low and starless, and the water spreading out flat and quiet all around.
The boat spun slowly, once, and stopped.
Behind them, far back up the tunnel, under the earth, under the garden, there was a long, deep, wooden groan.
And the roaring of the water slowed. And dropped. And stopped.
Someone had let the gate down.
A Woman Singing in the Fog
The mill pond was small and round and black, with a ruined mill standing at the far side of it like a broken tooth. Corin rowed across it without looking at the mill. At the far side, a little stream ran out of the pond through a gap in an old stone wall, and Wat pointed at the gap with his chin, and Corin rowed into it, and the stream carried them a little way, slowly, between banks of frozen mud and bare hawthorn. And then the stream ran into the canal.
The upper canal. Above the lock. Above the gray coats and their lanterns.
Corin turned in his seat and looked back. Far behind them now, to the south, over the black hump of the dam, he could see the glow of the five lanterns. Small. Yellow. Still moving their slow beats along the top of the bank. Nobody had shouted. Nobody had come running. No boat with a red lantern was coming after them up the stream.
He did not know what that meant.
He thought about the gloved hand on the iron handle, and the fingers closing round it, and staying there. The handle would have been warm. Warm from his own hands. On a night as cold as this, an iron handle that had been touched in the last minute would be warm.
And the gray cape would have known that.
And then someone had let the gate down. Jory. It must have been Jory. With the gray cape standing beside him in the shed.
Corin made himself stop thinking about it. He put the oars in.
The canal ran north, straight as a ruler, between two high banks. On top of the left bank there was a towpath, a narrow track of frozen mud for the horses that pulled the barges in summer. On the right bank there was nothing but a row of tall bare poplars, black against the sky, standing in a line like soldiers on a parade ground. Every few hundred yards there was a little wooden bridge across the canal, humped up in the middle so that a barge could pass under it. Corin rowed under them one after the other, and counted them, and lost count after nine.
The water here was warmer than the Long Pond. He did not know why. Perhaps it came from deeper down, from springs under the hills. But it was warmer than the air, and the air was so cold now that it hurt to breathe, and where the warm water met the cold air, it smoked.
The fog started as a thin white breath on the surface of the canal. Little curls of it, rising and turning and vanishing, like steam off a cup of tea. Then there were more of them. Then they joined up into a low white blanket lying on the water, a foot deep, so that the oars went down into it and disappeared and came up again dripping. Then it rose higher. Up to the gunwales. Up to Corin's chest. Up over his head.
And then there was nothing but fog.
White. All white. White on every side, white above, white below where the water should have been. He could see the boat, and Wat in the stern, and the oars going out on either side into the whiteness. That was all. The banks had gone. The poplars had gone. The sky had gone. They were rowing through the inside of a cloud.
It was very quiet. The fog ate sound. The clunk-splash of the oars came back to him small and flat and close, as if they were rowing inside a box lined with wool.
"Wat," he said. "Which way?"
Wat did not answer.
Corin looked round.
Wat was sitting in the stern with the steering oar under his good arm. His head had dropped forward onto his chest. His eyes were shut.
"Wat."
Nothing.
Corin shipped the oars and scrambled back over the bench, and the boat rocked, and he caught Wat's shoulder. The good one. He shook it.
"Wat."
Wat's eyes opened. Slowly. They looked at Corin without seeing him. Then they saw him.
"Straight," said Wat. His voice was a thread. "Canal goes straight. Four miles. Then the hay wharf at Brindle. Left bank." He swallowed. "Keep the bank on your left. You'll feel it with the oar."
"You need to stay awake."
"I'm awake."
"You weren't."
Wat looked at him for a moment. Then, slowly, he lifted his good hand off the steering oar and pressed it against his own face. Hard. Over his eyes. And held it there.
"Row," he said through his fingers.
Corin went back to the oars.
He rowed. He kept the left bank close, the way Wat had said. Every few strokes he reached out with the left oar and felt for it in the fog, and found it, a soft scrape of wood on frozen mud, and pulled away a little, and rowed on. It was slow. It was very slow. But they were moving. North. Away from the lock. Away from the gray cape.
One.
Wat began to talk.
Corin did not know, at first, that he was talking to him. Wat's voice came out of the fog behind him, low and slow, in short broken bits, the way a man talks in his sleep. Corin almost told him to save his breath. Then he didn't. He thought it might be keeping Wat awake. He thought it might be the only thing that was.
"She had an oar," said Wat. "The first time. Did I tell you?"
"You told me."
"Saltgate lock. Winter. I was drunk. I was a stupid boy and I was drunk and I fell in." A breath. "She hit me with it. The oar. Right on the head. To make me let go of the gate, she said after. I was holding the gate. You can't hold the gate. The water takes you through."
"I know."
"Then she pulled me out with it." A sound that might have been a laugh, or might have been something else. "Same oar. Hit me. Pulled me out. She was angry. So angry. Shouting the whole time. Shouting it twice. You stupid great lump, you stupid great lump."
Corin rowed.
"I'd go back in," said Wat. Very quietly now. "That lock. Every day. If it was her on the side."
The fog moved around them. White and soft and endless.
"You should tell her," said Corin.
Wat did not answer for a long time.
"She knows," he said at last.
"Does she?"
No answer.
Corin thought of the Ledger. Of the line he had written in it, two days ago, on the dead ship, by the light of the fire. Under Wat's name. He would go back in that lock every day. He has never told her. Tell her, if he can't.
He did not know if he would. He did not know if it was his to tell. He thought about the first night at No. 14, which he could not remember any more, and about Mrs. Marta's face, which he could, and about all the things people meant to say to each other and did not, until the chance was gone. And he did not know.
He rowed.
And then, out of the fog ahead of them, there was singing.
Corin stopped rowing.
It was a woman's voice. Not loud. Not beautiful, exactly. A little rough, a little flat on the high notes, the voice of somebody who had never been taught to sing and had never cared. It came through the fog from somewhere ahead and to the left, soft and close and very clear, and it was singing a song he did not know.
Oh, the river runs down and the river runs wide, And the river don't care who you are. It'll take the king's son and the beggar beside, And it'll carry them both just as far.
There was a pause. Then a small, cross, wordless noise. A baby's noise. A baby that was not asleep and did not want to be.
And the voice said, quite sharply, "Robin. Stop that. Robin, stop that."
And then, more softly, it began again.
So sleep, little fish, while the water goes by, And the stars come and sit on the sail. For your mother's awake, and she's watching the sky, And she'll not let the river prevail.
Corin turned round on the bench.
Wat had lifted his head. His hand had come away from his face. His eyes were open, wide open, looking into the fog toward the sound. And his face, gray and sweating and drawn with pain, had changed. Corin could not have said how. It was the same face. It had not smiled. But something had come into it, the way light comes into a window when someone on the other side lights a lamp.
"Hessa," said Wat.
He said it very quietly. Not to Corin. To the fog.
Corin took up the oars.
He rowed toward the singing. Slowly. Carefully. Feeling for the bank. And out of the whiteness ahead of them, a shape began to come. Long and low and dark. Much bigger than the boat. A wall of black wood, rising out of the fog, with something heaped on top of it, high and pale and shaggy. And above that, a mast. And on the mast, hanging slack in the still air, furled loosely and tied with rope, a sail.
Even in the fog, even in the dark, he could see it was red.
The singing stopped.
"Who's that?" said the voice. Sharp. Hard. All the softness gone out of it at once. "Who's there? I've got a boat hook. I've got a boat hook, and I'll use it."
Corin opened his mouth.
But Wat spoke first.
"It's me," he said. "It's me, Hess."
There was a silence on the barge.
A very long silence.
Then a lantern flared. Somewhere up on the deck, above the black wall of the hull, someone opened a lantern shutter all at once, and the light poured out into the fog, gold and wide and blinding, and turned the whiteness around them into a glowing golden cloud.
And in the middle of the light, leaning over the side of the barge, with a lantern in one hand and a boat hook in the other and a baby on her hip wrapped in a gray shawl, was Hessa Doon.
She was small. Corin had forgotten how small. She was not much bigger than Juno. She had a round face, red with cold, and a mass of brown hair tied up anyhow on top of her head with a strip of cloth, and her sleeves were rolled up to the elbow in spite of the cold, and her arms were thick and strong as a man's. She was wearing a man's coat over her dress. Wat's coat, Corin thought. It came down to her knees.
She looked down at the boat.
At Corin. And then past Corin. At Wat.
At the sacking. At the dark patch on it. At the gray, sweating face.
Her own face did not change. Not at first. She just looked. And Corin, watching her, saw her see it all. Every bit of it. The hook wound. The cold. The long row. How close it had been. He saw her see it, and take it in, and put it somewhere inside herself, and shut the door on it.
Then she put the boat hook down.
"You stupid great lump," said Hessa Doon. "You stupid great lump. What have you done to yourself?"
