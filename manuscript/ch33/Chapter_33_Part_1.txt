Chapter 33: The Long Way North, Part 1 of 5: Under the Sleeping City
Under the Sleeping City
The river at night was not black. Corin had always thought it would be. But it was not.
It was a hundred colors of dark. Near the boat it was brown, thick and slow, with bits of straw and orange peel turning in it. Further out it was the gray of old pewter, where the sky lay on it. And in long, wobbling stripes across it lay the lights of the city. Yellow from the windows of the taverns along Wharf Row. Red from the lamp over a pawnshop door. A cold, pale white from the gas globes on the Chancery Embankment, which the city paid for and nobody else could afford.
The oars broke the stripes every time they went in. Clunk-splash. The yellow and the red and the white shattered into a thousand pieces, and swung, and came back together again behind the boat, as if nothing had happened.
Corin's hands hurt already.
The oars were long and old and gray, and the handles had been worn smooth by other hands, bigger hands, hands that knew what they were doing. His did not. He held on too tight. He pulled too hard with the left and not enough with the right, so that the bow kept swinging toward the wall, and Wat had to lean on the steering oar to bring it back.
Wat did not say anything about it. He just leaned. And the boat came straight. And Corin pulled again, too hard with the left.
Clunk-splash.
He tried to count the strokes. It was a habit from the Archive years, when he had counted everything: steps on stairs, jars on shelves, the bells from the Chancery tower. Counting kept the mind still.
One. Two. Three.
The numbers came easily. They always came easily, at the start.
Seven. Eight. Nine.
And then there was the gap.
He knew the word that came next. Ten. He could say it. He could write it. He could see it in his head, a one and a nought, side by side. But when he reached for the feeling of it, the weight of it, the sense of how many ten was, there was nothing there. Just a smooth flat place, like a missing tooth that the tongue keeps finding.
He had burned that. Some time in the last month. He did not remember when.
So he started again at one. And again. In nines. Nine strokes and then one again, nine strokes and then one, over and over, like a man walking a short corridor and turning at the end.
Clunk-splash.
One.
The city went by above them on both sides.
This part of the river was walled. High walls of dark stone, wet and shining, with green weed hanging off them like hair. Every so often there were iron rings set into the stone for mooring, and iron ladders going up into the dark, and the black mouths of drains. Water came out of some of the drains in a thin, steady trickle. It smelled of soap, or of cabbage, or of something worse.
Above the walls were the backs of the houses. Tall and narrow, five and six floors, all leaning together like tired men holding each other up. Most of the windows were dark. Here and there a candle burned. In one window, high up, a woman sat at a table sewing by the light of a single lamp, with her head bent and her needle going in and out. She did not look down at the river. She did not see the small boat go by beneath her.
Corin watched her until the next house hid her.
He wondered who she was. What she was making. Whether she had a husband, children, a dead mother, a first kiss, a song she liked. All of it in her head, all of it hers. All of it fuel, if she ever learned the trick of burning it.
He looked away.
Clunk-splash.
"Bridge," said Wat quietly.
Corin looked over his shoulder.
Ahead of them, a bridge was coming out of the dark. It was not a big one. Old brick, three low arches, with a wooden rail along the top and a single lamp on a post at the middle. The water went under the arches in three black tunnels.
There was nobody on the bridge. Corin looked hard, to be sure. He looked at the lamp. Under the lamp. On both sides of it.
Nobody. No white gloves. No green coat.
He had looked at every bridge like this since the Lantern Bridge. Since the figure under the first lamp, standing very still, watching the river with its gloved hands folded in front of it. It had been too far to see a face. Too far to see anything but the gloves, pale against the dark, and the stillness.
He had told Juno it was his sister. He had said it out loud. And now, out here on the water, with nobody to say it to, he was not sure whether he believed it or whether he had only wanted something to be true.
She does not want you to know her, said Malakor, very softly, behind his left ear. If she did, she would have come down.
Corin said nothing.
Or perhaps she wanted to see if you would come up.
"Be quiet," said Corin. Out loud. He did not mean to.
Wat looked at him. Wat's face was gray in the dark, and his eyes were hard to see under the heavy brows. He did not ask who Corin was talking to. He had seen Corin talk to the air before. Most of the crew of the Patient Grace had, by now.
"Middle arch," said Wat. "Mind your oars."
They went under.
It was very dark under the arch. The bricks were close above their heads, close enough to touch, and they dripped. The sound of the oars came back from the curved roof twice as loud. CLUNK-SPLASH. Something squeaked and ran along a ledge just above the waterline, a quick gray shape with a long tail. And then they were out the other side, and the sky was there again, low and brown with the glow of the city.
Corin let out a breath he had not known he was holding.
He looked back at Wat.
Wat sat in the stern with the steering oar under his good arm. His left arm. His right arm was tied up against his chest with a strip of sacking, the way Hessa had once shown Corin to tie a broken wing on a bird. There was a dark patch on the sacking, near the shoulder. In the light from the last bridge lamp it had looked black. It was bigger now than it had been at the Ropewalk Stairs.
The Inquisition hook had gone in deep. A long pole with an iron hook on the end, the kind the river constables used to pull drowned men out of the water. Wat had stepped in front of it. He had stepped in front of it for Corin, without thinking, without a word, the way a man steps in front of a child when a cart comes round a corner. And the hook had gone into the meat of his shoulder and come out again, and Wat had made one sound, very low, and kept rowing.
"Let me look at it," said Corin.
"No."
"It's bleeding."
"It'll bleed or it won't." Wat shifted the steering oar. "Can't do much in a boat at night. Hessa'll see to it."
"Hessa's hours away."
"Then row faster."
Corin rowed faster. Too hard with the left. The bow swung. Wat leaned.
One.
They passed a tavern. It was the last one on this stretch, a low, crooked building right on the water with a wooden deck built out over the river on posts. There were lanterns hung along the deck, and men sitting at tables under them, drinking. Somebody was playing a fiddle very badly. Somebody else was singing along, even worse. As the boat slid past in the dark beyond the lantern light, a man stood up from one of the tables and walked to the rail and was sick into the river.
Wat steered wide of him.
The man looked up, wiping his mouth. He saw the boat. For a moment he stared at it, swaying, with his hand on the rail.
Corin held his breath.
Then the man laughed, and waved, and called something. Corin could not hear the words. The man went back to his table, and the fiddle went on squealing, and the boat went on into the dark.
"Drunk," said Wat.
"He saw us."
"He saw a boat. River's full of boats." Wat looked back at the tavern, once. "He'll not remember by morning."
Corin almost laughed. He'll not remember. As if that were a small thing. As if forgetting were the most ordinary thing in the world, that happened to drunk men every night, and meant nothing, and cost nothing.
Perhaps for most people it was.
He thought of the six cuts on the bow of the Margery. Six short, straight cuts, side by side, made with a knife. A tally. He had run his thumb along them on the wreck at Saltgate, and the wood had been soft and wet, and the cuts had been new. Not weeks old. Days.
Six of something.
And the footprints. Bare feet, in the mud on the bank. Going away from the wreck into the reeds. Not running. Not stumbling. Walking. Calm and even, the way a person walks to church, or to the market, or home.
He had not understood it at Saltgate. He did not understand it now. He turned it over and over in his head as he rowed, the way he would have turned a memory jar in his hands at the Archive, looking for the crack.
You will not find it by thinking, said Malakor. You never did.
Then how?
But Malakor did not answer that.
The river began to turn.
It bent to the right, slowly, in a long curve, and the walls on either side began to get lower. The houses on top of them got smaller. Three floors, and then two. And then, on the left bank, the houses stopped, and there was a long, low fence of black planks, and behind it the dark shapes of sheds and chimneys and great round tanks.
The smell hit Corin all at once.
It was a smell he knew, though he had not smelled it in years. Sharp and sour and rotten, like old eggs and wet dog and vinegar all boiled together. It got into his nose and the back of his throat. His eyes watered.
"Dye-works," said Wat. "Tanneries past it."
Corin coughed. "I know."
"We go in here." Wat pointed with his chin, because he had no free hand. "Mill Cut. Behind the dye-works. Then the old weir. Then the Tanners' Ditch."
Corin looked where he was pointing.
In the black fence, low down, almost at the water, there was a gap. A narrow opening, no wider than the boat, with an old iron grille pulled half up above it, rusted in place. Through the gap he could see more water. Dark, still water, with a skin on it that shone in the city glow like oil.
"In there?"
"Kross has men on the river locks," said Wat. "All of them. You heard the woman at the stairs."
Corin had. A woman on the Ropewalk Stairs, coming down with a basket of fish heads as they pushed off, had stopped and said, to nobody in particular, Gray coats on every lock up to Saltgate, they say. Stopping everything. Even the coal boats. And then she had gone on down to the water and thrown the fish heads in for the gulls, as if she had said nothing at all.
"Do they know about the Ditch?" said Corin.
Wat shrugged with his good shoulder.
"Nobody goes in the Ditch," he said. "That's what it's for."
He leaned on the steering oar.
The bow swung slowly round, away from the open river, toward the gap in the black fence. The smell got worse. Much worse. Corin pulled his collar up over his mouth and nose, and felt, through the cloth of the coat, the small square shape of the list in the collar pocket. The one Pegg had sewn shut. THOSE I OWE. He could not read it in the dark. He did not need to. He knew the names.
The boat slid under the rusted grille. Its teeth hung just above his head, brown and flaking, dripping.
And the city closed behind them like a door.
The Tanners' Ditch
The Mill Cut was short and stinking, and the water in it was blue.
Not the blue of the sky. A thick, dead, chemical blue, from the dye-works that ran its waste into it day and night. In the glow of the city, it looked almost black. But where the oars broke it, the drops that flew up were blue as ink, and they left blue spots on the gunwale, and on Corin's sleeves, and on the backs of his hands. He tried to wipe them off. They would not come off.
At the end of the Cut was the weir.
It had been a wall of stone once, built across the water to hold it back for a mill wheel. The mill was long gone. The wall had broken in the middle, and the water poured through the break in a short, loud, white rush, and on either side of the break the old stones stood up out of the water, black and slimy and crooked, like bad teeth.
They could not row through it. The rush would turn the boat over.
"Twenty yards," said Wat. "Over the top. On the left."
They brought the boat in against the left bank, where an old towpath ran beside the weir. Corin climbed out first. The mud came up over his boots, cold and sucking. He held the bow rope and pulled.
Wat climbed out after him.
He did it badly. He had only one arm. He put his good hand on the gunwale and swung one leg over, and the boat tipped, and he went down on one knee in the mud with a grunt. He stayed there for a moment. Corin heard him breathe. Long, slow, deliberate breaths, the kind a man takes when he is counting to keep from making a sound.
Then he got up.
They dragged the boat up out of the water together, Corin pulling at the bow, Wat pushing at the stern with his one good shoulder. It was heavier than it looked. It was much heavier than it looked. The keel scraped and stuck on every stone on the towpath, and every time it stuck, Wat had to put his weight into it, and every time he put his weight into it, Corin heard that long slow breath again.
Twenty yards.
It felt like a mile.
When they slid the boat back into the water above the weir, Wat sat down in the stern very heavily and did not move for a while. Corin could hear the water rushing through the broken wall behind them. Loud and white and endless.
"All right?" said Corin.
"Row," said Wat.
Corin rowed.
The Tanners' Ditch began a few yards on. It was a long, straight cut of water, about as wide as a lane, with walls of old timber on both sides.
The timbers were black and soft with rot. Some of them had fallen in, and lay half under the water like the ribs of a drowned animal, and Wat had to steer around them. Others leaned outward, held up by nothing Corin could see. Weeds grew out of the cracks between them. Pale, thin weeds that had never seen much sun.
And above the timbers, on both sides, were the tanneries.
Corin had been near tanneries before. Every city had them, out at the edges where the wind took the smell away from the rich streets. But he had never been inside one. He had never been under one, in the dark, at the bottom of a ditch, with the whole thing leaning over him.
There were sheds on stilts. Long, low sheds with roofs of tarred felt, and walls made of slats with gaps between them so the air could get through. Through the gaps he could see rows of hides, hanging from poles. Hundreds of them. Cow hides and horse hides and the small curled skins of goats, all hanging in the dark like coats in a giant's cupboard. Some of them were still wet, and dripped. He could hear the dripping all around him, steady and soft, like rain that had forgotten to stop.
There were pits. Big square pits dug into the ground beside the ditch, lined with wood, full of a liquid that was not water. Some were brown. Some were yellow-white and thick. One, as they passed it, was the color of old blood, and steamed faintly in the cold night air.
And there was the smell.
It was worse in here. Much worse. Out on the river, the wind had at least moved it about. Here in the Ditch, the air did not move at all. The smell just sat there, heavy and wet, and pressed down on them like a hand. Corin breathed through his mouth, and then tried breathing through the cloth of his collar, and then gave up and breathed however he could.
Wat did not seem to notice it.
"You're used to this," said Corin. His voice came out thick through the cloth.
"Came through here every week, one summer." Wat pushed them off a fallen timber with the steering oar. "Hessa's father ran hides. Before the barge was hers."
"Hides? On the Patient Grace?"
"She wasn't called that, then."
"What was she called?"
Wat was quiet for a while.
"Can't remember," he said at last.
Corin looked at him sharply. Wat's face, in the dim light, did not change. He was looking ahead, at the water, at the next timber. Just a tired man in a boat, with a hurt arm, who could not remember the old name of a barge.
That was all. People forgot things. Ordinary people, who never burned a single memory in their lives. They forgot names and dates and where they had put their keys. It was nothing.
But Corin found himself watching Wat's face for a long time after that.
Clunk-splash.
One.
It was easier to row in the Ditch. The water was dead still, no current to push against, and the walls were close enough on both sides that he could not swing too far. After a while, he stopped fighting the oars so much. He let them go in and come out. His left hand still pulled harder than his right. But less.
They passed under a footbridge. It was only a couple of planks laid across from one side of the Ditch to the other, with no rail, and it sagged in the middle so that Corin had to duck. As he went under it, he saw, painted on the underside of the planks in white, a row of little marks. Short lines. Some straight, some crossed.
He turned his head to look as they slid past. He could not make them out. Tally marks, maybe. Or a child's game. Or nothing.
You see tallies everywhere now, said Malakor.
Corin did not answer.
They came round a slight bend, and there was a light.
It was on the right bank. A lantern, hung on a nail on the corner of a shed, with its little door half open so the flame showed. It threw a ring of orange on the timbers below it, and on the water, and on a man.
The man was sitting on an upturned bucket at the top of the bank, right at the edge, with his back against the shed wall. He was very big. He had a leather apron on, stiff and stained almost black, and his arms were bare to the elbow, and they were the color of the brown pits. Stained deep into the skin, the way a dyer's hands are stained. He had a clay pipe in his mouth. It was not lit.
His eyes were closed.
Wat stopped steering. He lifted the steering oar a little out of the water, so it would not drip, and held it there.
Corin understood. He stopped rowing. He lifted both oars, slowly, until the blades were clear of the water, and held them out flat on either side of the boat like wings. Water ran off them, down to the handles, and over his fingers. It was cold and it smelled terrible. He did not move.
The boat went on gliding. Very slowly. With nothing pushing it now but what was left of the last stroke.
The ring of lantern light came closer.
The bow went into it. The orange glow ran along the side of the boat, over Corin's knees, over his hands on the oars. He could see every crack in the old wood. He could see the dark patch on Wat's sacking, shining now, wet. He could see the tanner's boots, huge and cracked and stained, and the bucket, and the unlit pipe, and the slow rise and fall of the great chest under the leather apron.
The tanner snored.
It was a short, soft snore, almost a sigh. His head tipped a little to one side.
The boat went on through the light. Slowly. Slowly. Corin's arms began to shake from holding the oars out. He clenched his teeth and held them.
The stern came into the light. Wat sat in it like a rock, gray and still, with his eyes on the sleeping man.
And then a dog barked.
It was somewhere behind the shed. A deep, rough bark, and then another, and then the sound of a chain dragging on stones.
The tanner's eyes opened.
He did not jump. He did not shout. He just opened his eyes, the way a man does who has been woken many times by the same dog, and looked straight down at the boat in the ditch below him.
At Corin. At Wat.
The pipe moved slowly from one side of his mouth to the other.
Nobody said anything. The dog barked again behind the shed, and then whined, and was quiet.
The tanner looked at Wat's arm. At the sacking. At the dark wet patch. He looked at it for a long moment.
Then he looked at Wat's face.
"Hessa Doon's man," he said. His voice was deep and slow and thick with sleep. It was not a question.
Wat said nothing.
The tanner took the pipe out of his mouth and looked at it, as if he were surprised to find it unlit. He put it back.
"There's gray coats on Jory's lock," he said. "Four of them. Come at dusk. Been stopping the coal boats and putting sticks down in the coal." He scratched his stained arm. "Looking for something."
"Thank you," said Corin.
The tanner's eyes moved to him. They were small eyes, very pale, in that huge brown face. They looked at Corin's coat, and his hands on the oars, and his face, and they did not seem to like any of it very much.
"I'm not talking to you," said the tanner.
He looked back at Wat.
"You'll want to be through the Long Pond before the ice skin comes," he said. "Cold enough for it tonight. And you'll not want Jory's dogs." He leaned back against the shed wall again and closed his eyes. "Tell Hessa the hides were short last spring. She owes me a goat."
Wat let the steering oar down into the water.
"I'll tell her," he said.
Corin put the oars in. Clunk-splash. Softly. As softly as he could. And the boat went on out of the ring of light into the dark again, and the lantern got smaller behind them, and the snoring started again before they were round the next bend.
They went on for a long time without talking.
The Ditch got narrower. The tanneries began to thin out. There were fewer sheds, and more empty yards full of broken barrels and piles of bark. The bark had a smell too, sharp and woody, and it was almost pleasant after the hides. Corin took his collar down off his face.
They passed the last tannery.
It was the biggest. A long brick building with a row of tall arched windows, all dark, and a wide wooden landing stage built out over the Ditch on posts. There were crates stacked on the landing stage. Dozens of them. Big square wooden crates, the kind that hides were shipped in, tied with rope and nailed shut.
As the boat slid past under the landing stage, close enough for Corin to reach up and touch the posts, he looked at the crates. He could not help it. Twelve years at the Archive had taught him to look at every label on every box. It was how you found things.
The crates were stenciled in black paint. The same words on every one, in neat square capitals.
NYM & SONS. FINE LEATHER. NOT TO BE OPENED BEFORE DELIVERY.
And under that, smaller, a little mark like a key. Or a bird on a string. In the dark, he could not tell which.
Then the landing stage was behind them, and the crates with it.
Corin rowed on.
He did not think much about the crates. It was a name on a box, at a tannery, at night. The city was full of names on boxes. He let it go the way he let go of most things now, without meaning to, without noticing it slip.
But somewhere at the back of his head, in the part that had once been a very good Archivist, something made a small, tidy note. And put it on a shelf. And waited.
"Wat," he said.
"Mm."
"Who's Jory?"
Wat was quiet for so long that Corin thought he had not heard. The Ditch was opening out ahead of them now. The timber walls were ending. Beyond them, he could see a wide, flat, pale emptiness, gray under the gray sky.
"Jory's a man who owes Hessa," said Wat at last. "Like most of the river."
"What does he owe her?"
"His boy."
Corin looked back at him.
Wat shifted the steering oar. His face was very pale now. Paler than it had been at the Ropewalk Stairs. There was sweat on his forehead, though the night was bitterly cold.
"She was nine," Wat said. "Jory's boy fell in the lock. Middle of winter. Gates open, water going through like a mill race. Grown men on the side, shouting, nobody going in." He took a breath. It sounded wrong. Too short. "She went in."
"She was nine?"
"Small for nine. Hessa's always been small." Something that was almost a smile. "Got him by the hair. Both of them nearly went through the gates. Her father pulled them out with a boat hook." The almost-smile went away. "Same sort of hook."
Corin looked at the dark patch on the sacking.
"Jory never forgot it," said Wat. "Lets her through his garden. Any hour. No questions."
"Through his garden?"
But they had come out of the end of the Ditch. And Wat did not answer, because he was looking ahead, at the wide pale emptiness of the Long Pond under the night sky, and at what lay on it.
Corin turned to look.
The water was going white.
Lanterns on the North Locks
The Long Pond was not a pond. It was a lake.
Or it had been, once. Long ago, before the city came this far north, there had been a wide shallow valley here with a stream running through it, and somebody had built a bank of earth across the bottom of the valley to hold the water back for the canal. And the water had filled the valley up, slowly, over years, until it lay there flat and still and pale, more than half a mile long and a quarter of a mile wide.
Now it was dying. Corin could see that, even in the dark. The edges had silted up. Reeds had come in from every side, tall dead winter reeds, brown and dry, standing in the water in great thick beds that whispered when the wind touched them. Only the middle was still open water. A long gray lane of it, running north between the reed beds toward the far end.
And the water was going white.
Not all of it. Not yet. But all along the edges of the reeds, where the water was shallow and still, there was a thin pale skin on it. Like the skin on milk that has been left to cool. As Corin watched, he saw it spreading. Very slowly. Thin white fingers reaching out from the reeds across the black water, joining up, making lace.
Ice.
"How long?" said Corin.
Wat looked at the sky. It was low and heavy and brown, with no stars. The air had gone very still and very sharp. Corin's breath smoked in front of his face. His fingers on the oars had stopped hurting some time ago, which he knew was not a good sign.
"An hour," said Wat. "Maybe less. Then it's thick enough to stop a boat." He breathed. "Thick enough to hear one, before that."
Corin understood. Ice that was too thin to stand on was still thick enough to crack. And a cracking sheet of ice, on a still night, on open water, would carry sound for half a mile.
He put the oars in.
They went up the middle of the Long Pond, in the lane of open water, with the reeds on both sides. It was very quiet. The city was behind them now. Its glow was a dull brown smudge low in the sky to the south, and its noise was gone. There was only the clunk-splash of the oars, and the drip of water off the blades, and the whisper of the reeds, and, now and then, far away, the cry of some night bird Corin did not know. Long and low and sad. Like a man calling a name and not expecting an answer.
The lock came out of the dark ahead of them, a little at a time.
First there was just a light. A single yellow point, low down, at the far north end of the water. Then another beside it. Then three more, in a line, a little higher. Then, as the boat crept nearer, the shapes behind the lights began to come clear.
The Long Pond ended at a high bank of earth, faced with stone. The dam. In the middle of the dam there was a gap, and in the gap were the lock gates. Two pairs of them, great black wooden gates with long beams sticking out from the tops of them, like the arms of men with their hands on their hips. Between the two pairs of gates was the lock itself, a stone box full of water, long enough to hold a barge. And beside the lock, on top of the dam, was a house.
It was a small house. Old brick, two floors, with a steep slate roof and one tall chimney. Smoke came out of the chimney and went straight up into the still air, gray against the brown sky. There was a light in the ground-floor window. A warm, yellow, kitchen light.
The lock-keeper's house. Old Jory's house.
And all along the top of the dam, on both sides of the lock, there were lanterns.
Corin counted them. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Five lanterns. Some on poles, stuck in the earth of the bank. Some held in hands. As he watched, one of them moved, slowly, along the top of the dam, from one side of the lock to the other, and stopped, and swung. Somebody walking a beat. Somebody looking down at the water.
And by the light of the lanterns he could see the coats.
Long coats. Gray. With the short shoulder capes of the Office of the Inquisition.
"Four," said Wat. "Like the tanner said."
"Five lanterns."
"Then there's one in the house."
Corin stopped rowing.
They drifted. The boat lost its way slowly, and turned a little, and sat on the black water in the middle of the lane, with the reeds on both sides and the ice reaching out of the reeds toward them.
He looked at the lock. At the lanterns. At the small warm window of the house.
"Where's the sluice?" he said.
Wat lifted his chin toward the left side of the dam. Toward the house.
"Under the garden," he said. "Behind the house. There's a little cut in the reeds, on the west side. Goes right up to the foot of the bank. You'd not see it if you didn't know." He breathed. That short, wrong breath again. "Sluice gate's in the bank. Little one. For letting water down to Jory's mill pond on the other side, when the canal's too high. Jory keeps it greased."
"For Hessa."
"For Hessa."
Corin looked at the west side of the dam. It was dark there. The lanterns were all along the top, around the lock gates. None of them were on the west end, where the house stood at the edge of the bank, with its garden running down the back of it toward the reeds.
A garden. In the dark, he could just see it. A low wall. The pale shapes of something growing, or something covered over for the winter. A small shed. And a tree, a big old bare tree, leaning out over the water.
The gray coats were watching the lock. The lock was where boats went. Nobody watched a garden.
Unless they know about the sluice, said Malakor.
Do they?
How should I know? I am only a voice in your head. A pause. But if I were Silas Kross, I would ask the lock-keeper's neighbors about every hole in every bank for a mile.
Corin looked at the house. At the warm window. At the shadow that moved across it, once, and was gone.
There is one in the house, said Malakor. Your friend said so.
"Wat," said Corin quietly. "Can you—"
He stopped.
Because Wat was not looking at the lock. Wat was looking back. Over the stern. Back down the long gray lane of water toward the south. Toward the dark mouth of the Tanners' Ditch.
Corin turned to look.
At first he saw nothing. Just the water, and the reeds, and the brown smudge of the city sky.
Then he saw it.
A light. Very small. Very low. Down at the far south end of the pond, near the mouth of the Ditch. It was not like the lock lanterns, open and yellow and swinging. It was a dim, red, narrow glow, like a coal seen through a crack. A lantern with its shutter almost closed. Somebody who wanted to see where they were going without being seen.
It was moving. Slowly. Up the lane of open water. Toward them.
"Boat," said Wat.
Corin did not answer. He was watching the light.
It came on. Steadily. Not fast. Not slow. The way a man walks who knows exactly where he is going and has all night to get there. And as it came on, Corin began to hear it. Very faint, across the still water. The sound of oars. Clunk-splash. Clunk-splash. But not like his. Even and clean and steady, with no splash at all. Somebody who knew how to row.
And under the oars, something else. A voice. Low. Just a few words. Then quiet.
Two people, then. One rowing. One talking.
And behind the shuttered lantern, as the boat came out of the shadow of the reeds into a patch of open water where the brown sky lay pale on the surface, Corin saw a shape sitting in the stern. Upright. Very still. With something over its shoulders. A short cape. Pale gray in the dark.
He had seen a gray cape before.
On the steps of Mrs. Marta's house, the day the Inquisition took his jars. Constable Pym had told Juno about it, and Juno had told him. A man in a gray cape. Plain face. Asked where the landlady went. Wrote it down very small.
And on the river, once, two nights ago. A boat that went past the burned grain ship at dusk, too slowly, and turned, and went away again.
Kross let you run, said Malakor. Very quietly. Not mocking now. You knew that. You have known it since the sewers. He let you run so that you would run to someone.
And I'm running to them.
You are leading him to them. Yes.
Corin's hands had gone tight on the oars. He made them loosen. One finger at a time.
He thought of the Patient Grace. Black, with a red sail. Somewhere past this lock. Somewhere up the canal in the dark, loading hay. With Hessa on the deck, and Robin asleep in the cabin, and Lord Thorne under the hay, tapping his four numbers. And Lyra.
And behind him, coming up the Long Pond at a steady walking pace, a man in a gray cape with a shuttered lantern, following the only thread that led there.
Him.
"He's seen us," said Corin.
"Maybe," said Wat. "Maybe not. He's got the lantern near shut. He can't see much more than we can." He looked at the lock lanterns, and back at the red glow, measuring. "He's following the water. Not us. Only one way to go, up the Pond. He knows that."
"Then he'll see us when we get to the sluice."
"If we go to the sluice now. Yes."
Corin looked at him.
Wat's face was very gray. The sweat on his forehead had frozen into tiny drops at the edge of his hair, and they glittered. His tied arm hung against his chest. The dark patch on the sacking had stopped growing, or else Corin could not see it grow any more in the dark. But his eyes were steady. Steady and tired and very calm.
"We go back," said Wat.
"Back?"
"Into the reeds. East side. Away from the house." He tipped his head toward the great dark bed of dead reeds on their right. "Let him go by. Let him get to the lock. Let him go and talk to his friends with the lanterns." He breathed. "Then we come out behind him."
"And the ice?"
Wat looked at the white lace creeping out from the reed beds toward them.
"Then we'd best be quick," he said.
He put the steering oar over, hard.
And Corin, with his heart going like a drum under his coat, and the red light coming steadily up the dark water behind them, put his oars in, and pulled with his left, harder than his right, on purpose this time. And the bow swung round, away from the lock, away from the house and the garden and the hidden sluice, back toward the south, toward the man in the gray cape, and into the reeds.
