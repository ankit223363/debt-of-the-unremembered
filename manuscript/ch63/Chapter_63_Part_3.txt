Chapter 63: Six Clockwork Men, Part 3 of 5: Old Rook
Old Rook
He turned round when she was a few steps away, and she saw his face.
It was a very old face. Old and thin and long, with deep lines running down from the nose to the corners of the mouth, and a white stubble on the chin and cheeks as if he had forgotten to shave for several days. He had a little round cap on his head, of black cloth, the kind clockmakers and jewelers wear. Round his neck, on a black ribbon, there hung a lens. A small round magnifying glass in a brass rim, the size of a coin. It rested on his chest, on the front of a long brown apron that was stained with oil and scored with small burns and pocked all over with tiny holes where things had been dropped on it.
His hands were shaking.
That was what Wren saw first, and kept seeing. His hands were held a little in front of him, at the level of his waist, and they shook. Not a small shake. A large, slow, steady tremble, as if there were wind blowing through them that nobody else could feel. They were long hands, thin, very clean, with swollen knuckles and long thin fingers, and they never once stopped shaking all the time she was there, except at one moment she would remember afterward.
"You'll be the cadet," he said. His voice was soft and cracked and very tired. "The one he said."
"Cadet Halloway, sir."
"Don't sir me. I'm nobody." He looked past her, at the door, as if he expected someone else. "Rook," he said. "Thaddeus Rook. Clocks, mostly. And cabinets. In Vellum Row." His mouth moved in something that was not a smile. "Clocks, mostly."
"Are you—" She did not know what to ask. "Are you waiting for the Inquisitor?"
"I'm waiting for him to be ready," said Rook. "Then I'm waiting for them to be ready." He nodded at the crates. "Then I'm waiting to go home." He turned back to the crates. "You might as well see them. Everyone else will."
He went to the first crate, the one nearest the door, and put his shaking hands on its lid.
The lid was not nailed. It lifted on leather hinges. He lifted it, slowly, with both hands, the shaking making the boards rattle a little, and laid it back against the trestle.
Wren looked in.
There was a man in the crate.
That was what she thought, in the first moment, and her whole body went cold, the way it had gone cold on the stair this morning. A man lying on his back in straw, very still, with his arms at his sides. A big man. As tall as Oren Vance, she thought, or taller.
Then she saw that it was not a man.
It was the shape of a man, made of iron and brass.
The iron was dark, nearly black, with a dull sheen to it like the back of a beetle. It was not one piece. It was many pieces. Plates, curved and fitted and riveted together, overlapping each other the way the scales on a fish overlap, or the plates of armor on a knight in an old painting. There were plates on the chest, and the belly, and the long thighs, and the shins. There were plates on the arms, down to the wrists. And where the plates met, at the shoulders and the elbows and the hips and the knees, there were joints of brass, round and bright and complicated, full of tiny wheels and pins that glinted in the lamplight.
The hands were not hands. They were broad flat things like the blades of shovels, with three thick fingers each, jointed in brass, lying curled a little on the straw.
And the head.
The head was the worst. Wren made herself look at it. It was a smooth oval of iron, a little larger than a man's head, with no ears and no nose and no mouth. It was blank. Like a shield. Like the back of a spoon. Polished, so that she could see the lamp in it, a small curved yellow spark, and the shape of her own white face bent over it. Only across the middle of it, where a man's eyes would be, there was a slit. A single narrow slit, straight across, no wider than a finger, and behind the slit there was darkness.
"Eraser," said Rook softly. "That's what they call them. Not me. I never called them anything."
He went along the row of crates, one by one, and lifted all the lids.
Six of them. Six long iron men lying in straw in six pine boxes, side by side in the yellow lamplight, with their blank faces turned up to the curved brick ceiling. All the same. Exactly the same. Wren looked from one to another and could not have told them apart.
"Did you make them?" she said.
Rook did not answer at once. He was standing by the last crate, looking down into it, with his shaking hands held in front of him.
"I made them," he said at last. "Yes."
He put his hand into the pocket of his apron and took something out.
It was a key. A small brass key, no longer than his finger, and the bow of it, the part you hold, was not a plain ring. It was shaped like a bird. A small bird with its wings folded, a wren or a sparrow, beautifully made, every feather cut fine in the brass.
He held it in his shaking fingers and looked at it for a moment.
Then he bent over the first crate, and slipped his free hand under the iron man's shoulder, and with a strength Wren would not have believed he had, he rolled it half onto its side in the straw. On its back, between the shoulder blades, low down, in the small of the back, there was a plate. A square plate of brass, the size of a book, screwed in at the corners. And in the middle of the plate, a small round hole.
Rook put the bird key into the hole.
And his hands stopped shaking.
Wren saw it happen. She would remember it for a long time. One moment his long thin fingers were trembling, the way they had been all along, and the next, the moment the key went into its hole and his fingers closed on the brass bird, they went still. Perfectly still. Steady as stone. As if the trembling had been a sickness that only went away when he was doing the thing he was made for.
He turned the key.
There was a sound. A small, crisp, ratcheting click. Then another. Then another. Click. Click. Click. He turned the key slowly, round and round, and each turn was a click, and the clicks went on and on, a long time, longer than Wren would have thought possible. She counted them without meaning to and lost count after forty.
Then he stopped, and drew the key out, and his hands began to shake again at once.
And the iron man began to tick.
It was a quiet sound at first. A steady, even, patient sound, coming from somewhere deep inside the iron chest. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Like a clock in an empty house. Like a heart.
Rook rolled it back onto its back in the straw. The slit in its blank face stayed dark.
He went to the second crate.
Under Protest
He wound them all.
Wren stood and watched him do it, because she did not know what else to do, and because nobody had told her to do anything else. He went from crate to crate along the row. At each one he slipped his hand under the iron shoulder and rolled the long dark body half over in the straw, and fitted the bird key into the hole in the brass plate, and his hands went still, and he turned the key. Click. Click. Click. A long time. Then he drew it out, and his hands began to shake again, and the iron man began to tick, and he rolled it back.
By the third one, the ticking of the first two had filled the vault.
They did not tick together. That was what made it so strange. Each one had its own pace, a little faster or a little slower than the others, so that the ticks fell over each other and between each other and against each other, like rain on different parts of a roof. Tick tick. Tick. Tick tick tick. Tick. The sound came back off the curved brick ceiling and filled the long dark vault from end to end, so that Wren could not tell anymore which crate it was coming from, or whether it was coming from the crates at all, or from the walls, or from inside her own head.
After the fourth one Rook stopped.
He stood by the fifth crate with the bird key in his shaking hand and did not bend over it. He was looking down into it, at the blank iron face looking up.
Then he put the key back in his apron pocket, and took out instead a small brass screwdriver, and a thin steel tool like a hook.
"This one sticks," he said, not to her. "In the chest. I'll just look."
He bent over the crate, and with his shaking hands he fitted the screwdriver into one of the screws on the iron man's chest, at the top corner of the biggest plate, the one that covered the place where a man's heart would be. The screwdriver slipped twice. The third time it held, and his hand went still on it, and he turned it, and the screw came out, and he caught it in his palm.
Three more screws. Then he lifted the plate.
Wren had not meant to look, but she looked.
Under the plate, the iron man was full of wheels.
Hundreds of them. Brass wheels and steel wheels, large and small, some no bigger than her fingernail, all fitted together, tooth in tooth, so many and so close that her eye could not follow one from another. Little rods ran between them. Little springs, coiled tight. In the middle of it all, low down, a larger brass drum turned very slowly, and everything else turned with it. It was beautiful. She had not expected it to be beautiful. It shone in the lamplight, gold and silver, moving, ticking, like the inside of the finest watch in the world made very large.
Rook bent close over it with his lens to his eye. He poked at something with the thin hook. Then he straightened up.
He did not close the plate at once.
He held it in his hands, the chest plate, turned up so that its inside faced him, and he looked at the inside of it for a moment. It was plain dark iron on the inside, rough and unpolished.
Then he did something with his thumbnail.
Wren almost did not see it. He held the plate close against his apron, tipped toward himself, and his thumbnail moved on the inside of the iron, small and quick. Scratching. A little scratch, and another, and another. His thumb was not shaking at all. It was perfectly still, and perfectly quick, the way it had been on the bird key.
Then he looked up and saw her watching.
For a moment neither of them moved.
Wren could see, past the edge of his thumb, a little of what he had scratched. Very small letters, in the dark rough iron, so faint that they were hardly more than a gleam where the nail had gone. She could not read them all. She could read the last of them, the last word, because the lamp caught it.
...PROTEST.
Rook moved his thumb over it.
He did it slowly, quite deliberately, the way you put your hand over a letter on a table when somebody comes into the room. Then he fitted the plate back onto the iron chest, over the wheels, and put the four screws back one by one with the screwdriver, his hands shaking again now, slipping, catching. When the last one was in, he laid his long thin hand flat on the iron chest for a moment.
"There," he said to the iron man, softly. "There now."
He did not look at Wren again. He went and got the bird key out of his pocket and wound the fifth one, and the sixth.
Wren stood in the ticking dark and found that her heart was going very fast.
She thought about what she had seen. She thought she ought to say something. She was a cadet of the Inquisition. She was in the uniform of the Inquisition, with twelve silver buttons on her coat, and she had seen a man scratch something on the inside of a thing that belonged to the Ministry, a thing that was going to be used by the Ministry, and hide it with his thumb. She ought to ask what it said. She ought to write it down. She ought to tell Kross.
She thought about the cold cup of tea on the fifth step, with a skin on it.
She did not say anything.
When the sixth one was ticking, Rook stood back, and wiped his hands on his apron, though they were not dirty, and looked at the six of them in their crates. Then he said, very quietly, still not to her:
"I made a clock once. For a lord on the Hill. A tall clock, with a moon on its face, and twelve little drawers in the case, one for every month, for him to keep things in. Twelve stones, he wanted, one in each drawer. Blue ones. For luck, he said. For his children." Rook was quiet for a moment. "He paid me fair. He came to the shop himself, to fetch it, and he shook my hand."
Wren did not say anything.
"I heard he died," said Rook. "Yesterday."
He looked down at his shaking hands.
"That was a good clock," he said. "That was the best thing I ever made."
Behind them, at the door of the vault, there was a sound. A footstep. Soft, and light, and very precise, on the wet brick.
Rook's head came up. And Wren saw his face, and saw that he was afraid.
Ferrand
He came down the length of the vault out of the dark between the lamps, walking in the middle of the wet brick floor, and his boots made almost no sound at all.
Wren watched him come. He came into one pool of yellow light, and out of it into the dark, and into the next, and each time he came into the light she saw a little more of him. A slim man. Not tall. In a long gray coat, very well cut, buttoned to the throat, of a gray so pale that it was almost white. Gray kid gloves. A gray hat, which he took off as he came into the last pool of light by the crates, and held against his chest.
His face was pale. Not pale the way a sick man is pale, or a frightened one. Pale the way paper is pale, or the inside of a shell. A smooth, calm, even face, neither young nor old, with fine fair hair combed close to the head and fine fair brows and pale eyes of no particular color at all. A face, Wren thought, that you would not remember. A face you could pass in the street a hundred times and never once see.
Under his arm he carried a small black notebook.
And at his belt, on the left side, where another man might carry a knife, there hung a rod. A slim silver rod, as long as a hand, with a spiral cut round it from the tip to the hilt. Wren had seen harvesting rods before. Every harvester in the Ministry carried one. She had seen the harvester's own, the one he carried in his long coat pocket. But she had never seen one like this. She looked at it for a moment before she understood why it looked wrong.
The spiral went the other way.
Every harvesting rod she had ever seen had its spiral turning to the right, the way a screw turns when you drive it in. This one turned to the left. As if it had been made to undo something instead.
"Cadet Halloway," said the man. His voice was soft. Very soft, and very clear, and very polite, every word finished properly, the way a good clerk finishes his letters. "How kind of you to come."
Wren drew herself up. "Sir."
"Oh, not sir." He smiled. It was a small smile, perfectly pleasant, and it did not touch his pale eyes at all. "Ferrand. Just Ferrand. I am nobody, really. I carry messages." He turned the smile on Rook. "Master Rook. Are they ready?"
Rook did not answer at once. His shaking hands had gone into his apron pockets, both of them, and stayed there.
"They're wound," he said at last.
"Wound," said Ferrand. "But ready?"
"They're wound. They'll go. That's all I can tell you." Rook's voice had gone very flat. "I don't know what ready is, for them. I don't know what they think."
"They don't think, Master Rook. That is rather the point." Ferrand tucked his hat under his arm, beside the notebook, and walked along the row of open crates, slowly, looking down into each one in turn. At each crate he paused. At each crate the slit in the blank iron face seemed, to Wren, to grow a little darker as his shadow fell across it.
He stopped at the end of the row.
"Shall we see?" he said.
He did not wait for anyone to answer. He did not raise his voice. He did not take the rod from his belt, or make any sign with his hands. He only stood at the end of the row of crates, with his hat under his arm and his notebook under his hat, and said one word.
Wren did not catch it.
She would think about that afterward, often. She was standing three paces from him, and the vault was quiet but for the ticking, and her hearing was good. And she did not catch the word. It was short, and soft, and it was in a language she did not know, or in no language at all, a sound like a word with all its edges worn off, like a stone that has been rolled in a river for a thousand years.
And the six iron faces turned.
All at once. All six. In their crates, lying on their backs in the straw, the six blank iron heads turned on their brass necks, with a small grinding sound like a key in a stiff lock, and the six dark slits looked at Ferrand.
The ticking changed. Wren heard it change. It did not get faster. It got even. All six of them, which had been ticking at their own six paces like rain on a roof, suddenly fell into step with each other. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. One sound. One clock, six times over.
Rook made a small noise in his throat.
Ferrand smiled again, his small pleasant smile, and opened his black notebook, and took a silver pencil out of the spine of it, and wrote something. Wren saw his hand move. The letters were square and careful and very upright, each one separate, not joined to the next.
She had seen that hand before.
This morning. On a white card, folded once, sealed with gray wax with no mark in it. The cellars under the Records yard. The third bell after noon. Come alone. Wear your coat.
She felt the card in her breast pocket, against her small book, as if it had suddenly grown heavy.
Ferrand closed his notebook.
He said another word, as soft as the first. The six iron heads turned back, together, grinding, until their slits looked up at the ceiling again. The ticking stayed even. One clock. Six times over.
"Excellent," said Ferrand. "Thank you, Master Rook. That will do very nicely." He turned to Wren. "You will have to forgive me, Cadet. I asked for you particularly. I do hope you didn't mind the card. I find people come more quickly when they don't know why."
"Why did you ask for me?" said Wren.
She had not meant to ask it. It came out by itself. She heard it go out into the ticking dark and wished she could take it back.
Ferrand did not seem to mind.
"Because," he said, "the Inquisitor speaks very highly of you. And because you were there." He tilted his pale head a little. "Yesterday afternoon. In the house on Steeple Lane. When the harvester came. You let him in at the door, I think, and you let him out again. And you watched him go." He smiled. "I find it is always useful to have someone along who knows the face."
Wren said nothing.
Behind her, at the door of the vault, there were more footsteps. Not soft, this time. Quick and uneven and stumbling, as if someone were running down the stair who was not used to running, and had very nearly fallen twice.
