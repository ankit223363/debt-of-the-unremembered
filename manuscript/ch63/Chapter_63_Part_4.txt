Chapter 63: Six Clockwork Men, Part 4 of 5: The Archon's Note
The Archon's Note
It was Scribe Lucian.
Wren knew him by sight. Everybody in the Ministry knew him by sight, though nobody seemed to know him any better than that. He was the Archon's own secretary, a young man, not much older than Wren, thin and pale and anxious, with ink on the cuffs of a good black coat and spectacles that were always slipping down his nose. He carried the Archon's day book. He carried the Archon's letters. He went between the Bank and the Ministry and the Council chamber all day long, half running, with a leather case clutched against his chest, and he always looked as if he had just remembered something terrible and was hurrying to put it right before anyone noticed.
He looked like that now. He came through the door of the vault at a stumbling half-run, with his case against his chest and his spectacles halfway down his nose, and stopped dead in the first pool of light, and stared at the six open crates.
His mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.
The six iron men lay in their straw with their blank faces turned up to the ceiling, ticking, all together, one clock six times over.
"Ah," said Ferrand pleasantly. "Lucian. Good afternoon."
Lucian looked at him. His pale face went paler still. He pushed his spectacles up his nose with one finger, and they slid straight down again.
"I have a message," he said. His voice came out high and thin, and he coughed, and tried again. "I have a message from His Excellency. For the Inquisitor. I was told the Inquisitor would be here. I was told—" He looked round the vault, at the lamps, at the dark, at Wren in her white coat, at Rook with his hands in his apron. "I was told the third bell."
"It is the third bell," said a voice from the door.
Kross came into the light.
Wren had not heard him on the stair. Nobody had. He was standing in the doorway of the vault in his plain dark coat, with his gloves on and his hat in his hand, and he looked exactly as he had looked in the Long Office that morning: neat, and gray, and very tired. He did not look at the crates. He did not look at Ferrand. He looked at Lucian.
"You have a message," he said.
"Yes, Inquisitor." Lucian opened his leather case. His fingers fumbled with the clasp. Wren saw him check inside the case once, and close it, and open it again, and check again, and then a third time, before he took out a folded card. It was a square of thick cream card, folded once, and sealed with dark red wax. In the wax, pressed small and neat, there was no crest and no letter. Only a circle.
Lucian held it out.
Kross did not take it.
"Read it," he said.
Lucian stared at him.
"Inquisitor, it's sealed. It's for you. It's His Excellency's own—"
"I know whose it is." Kross's voice did not change. It was quiet and level and almost kind. "Read it, Lucian. Aloud. Please. I would like Cadet Halloway to hear it, and Master Rook. And Master Ferrand, of course, though I expect Master Ferrand knows already what it says."
Ferrand smiled his small smile and said nothing.
Lucian looked from one of them to the other. Then, with shaking fingers, he broke the red wax, and unfolded the card, and pushed his spectacles up his nose, and read.
He read it three times. Wren saw his lips move, the first two times, silently, as if he were checking that it said what he thought it said. Then the third time, aloud.
His voice shook.
"Word from the north-road toll. A man with gray hands, lame, by the dyke road, in a punt, Thursday night, going north. The fens. Then the hills." Lucian swallowed. "The Inquisitor will take what he needs and follow. Master Ferrand goes with him, to see and to write. Master Rook's six go with him also." He stopped. He looked at the card. He looked up at Kross with his eyes very wide behind the slipping spectacles, and then down again, and read the last line very fast, as if it were hot and he wanted to put it down.
"Bring back the harvester and whatever he finds. The others need not come back."
He lowered the card.
Nobody said anything. The six iron men ticked in their crates.
"Is that all?" said Kross.
"There's—" Lucian looked at the card. "There's no name, Inquisitor. Only the circle."
"There never is." Kross held out his gloved hand at last, and Lucian put the card into it, and Kross looked at it for a long time without reading it. Wren watched his face. It did not change at all.
Then, very slowly, while they all watched, he began to fold it.
In half. In half again. The thick cream card did not want to fold; it creaked and cracked. He folded it anyway. In half. In half. Wren counted, without meaning to, the way the clerks did upstairs when he was not there. Six. Seven. Eight. It was very small now, and very hard, and the creases had gone white.
Nine. Ten.
Eleven.
He put it in his waistcoat pocket.
"Thank you, Lucian," he said. "You may tell His Excellency it was received, and understood."
Lucian stood there a moment longer, with his empty case against his chest. He looked at the crates. He looked at Kross.
"Inquisitor," he said. Very low. "The others. Need not come back. Who are—"
He stopped. He seemed to have heard what he was saying. He went white to the lips, and shut his mouth, and checked his case once, and twice, and three times. Then he turned and went away up the stair at his stumbling half-run, and they heard his feet going up and round and up and round for a long time, getting fainter, until they were gone.
Ferrand opened his black notebook and wrote something in it, in his square, separate letters.
A Bad Liar
When Lucian's footsteps had gone, Kross walked along the row of crates.
He did it slowly, the way Ferrand had done it, stopping at each one. But he did not look into the crates the way Ferrand had looked, with that small pleased smile. He looked at each iron face for a long moment, without any expression at all, the way a man looks at a word on a page that he cannot read, and then he went on to the next. At the end of the row he stopped and stood with his gloved hands behind his back.
"How fast?" he said, without turning round.
Rook took a moment to understand that he was being spoken to.
"As fast as a man walking," he said. "A little faster, on the flat. Slower uphill. They don't run."
"How long?"
"A winding lasts two days. Two and a bit. Then they'll want the key again."
"And at night?"
Rook hesitated.
"They don't see well in the dark," he said. "They go by the light coming into the slit. No light, they go slow. They'll stop if it's black dark. They'll stand where they are till there's light again."
"And on the Glass?"
Rook did not answer.
Kross turned round and looked at him.
"I don't know," said Rook at last. His hands had come out of his apron pockets and were shaking in front of him. "I don't know, Inquisitor. Nobody's ever taken a clock out on the Glass. Nothing burns there. Nothing's taken. That's what they say. They've no Ash in them. Nothing to burn or take." He looked at the iron faces. "I think they'll go. I think the Glass won't know they're there. That's why he wants them."
Kross looked at him a moment longer.
"Yes," he said. "That's why."
He turned to Ferrand.
"You'll take them tonight," he said. "From the Records gate, at midnight. Two closed carts. Four men on horses. The north road, then the dyke road, then the drove road over the hills. I'll give you the men."
Ferrand raised his fine fair eyebrows a very little.
"And you, Inquisitor?"
"I'll follow."
"His Excellency said—"
"His Excellency said I would take what I needed and follow. I am taking what I need. I will follow." Kross's voice did not rise. "On Sunday, at the second bell after noon, Lord Aurelian Thorne will be put in his family's vault at Saint Clement's. I gave my word that I would be there. When it is done, I will ride north. Carts are slow in the hills. I will catch you at Thistle Ford, or thereabouts, by Monday night."
Ferrand looked at him for a long moment with his pale eyes.
Then he smiled, and bowed his head, very slightly.
"Of course," he said. "How very proper."
He opened his notebook and wrote. Wren watched the silver pencil move. She could not see the words. She did not need to.
Kross did not watch him write. He turned his back on him, quite deliberately, and looked at Wren.
"Halloway," he said. "You'll go with the carts."
She had known it was coming. She had known since the card. She had known, perhaps, since this morning on the stair, watching him hold his bare hand over a black cloth. And still it went through her like cold water.
"Yes, sir."
"Go home now. Pack for the cold. A week, perhaps two. Mrs. Oddie will find you what you need." He paused. "Be at the Records gate at the half hour before midnight."
"Yes, sir."
She stood there. He had not dismissed her. She waited for him to, and he did not, and the iron men ticked in their crates, and Ferrand's pencil scratched in his notebook, and at last Kross said, quite quietly, "Walk with me to the stair."
She walked with him.
They went down the length of the vault together, from the crates to the door, through the pools of yellow light and the darkness between them. Their boots made small wet sounds on the brick. Behind them the ticking went on and on. At the door, in the dark just inside it, where the lamplight from the vault did not quite reach and the light from the stair did not quite come down, Kross stopped.
Wren stopped too.
She should not have asked. She knew she should not. She asked anyway.
"Why me, sir?"
Kross did not answer at once.
"Master Ferrand asked for me," said Wren. "He said it was because I knew the face. But anyone could know the face. It's on the Thursday list. It's on every poster on every corner." She heard her own voice go up, and made it come down again. "Why me?"
Kross looked at her in the half-dark. She could not see his face well. Only the shape of it, thin, and the gleam of his eyes.
"Because," he said, "you are a bad liar, Halloway."
She stared at him.
"You are a very bad liar," said Kross. "You lied to me once, in the autumn. On the night of the tenement. You lied very badly. Your face did everything a face can do except tell the truth, and then it told the truth as well." He paused. "I put a line about you in my book that night. You know that."
"Yes, sir."
"And His Excellency reads my book. And he knows it too. He knows that Cadet Halloway cannot lie to save her life." Kross was quiet for a moment. "So what you see, out there, he will believe. Whatever you see. Whatever you write down. Whatever you say, when we come back, standing in front of him with your face doing everything a face can do. He will believe it, because it's you saying it."
Wren did not say anything. She could not.
"Ferrand will write," said Kross. "He writes everything. He will write what he sees and what he thinks he sees and what he thinks I think. And His Excellency will read it." His voice dropped. "And then His Excellency will read what you write, Halloway. And look at your face. And know which of the two is true."
He put on his hat.
"That's why you," he said. "Go home and pack."
He went up the stair ahead of her, and his footsteps went up and round, up and round, steady and unhurried, and she stood at the bottom in the dark with her hand on the iron rope and listened to them go, and behind her in the long brick vault, six iron hearts ticked as one.
What the Word Says
She did not go up the stair at once.
She stood at the bottom of it in the dark, with her hand on the cold iron rope, until Kross's footsteps had quite gone. Then she turned, because she had heard something behind her, and found Rook standing in the doorway of the vault.
He had come down the length of it after them without a sound. He was standing just inside the door, in the edge of the light, with his shaking hands held in front of him and his lens swinging gently on its ribbon against his apron.
"Miss," he said.
"Master Rook."
He looked over his shoulder, back down the vault, to where Ferrand stood far off at the end of the row of crates, a small pale figure in a pool of yellow light, writing in his notebook. Then he looked back at Wren. He came a step closer. His voice dropped so low that she had to lean toward him to hear it over the ticking.
"You'll be with them," he said. "Out there."
"Yes."
He nodded slowly. His hands shook.
"Then listen," he said. "I'll tell you one thing. One thing. I told him. Ferrand." He said the name the way you might pick up a dead mouse, by the tail. "I told him when I built them, and he wrote it in his book, and smiled at me, and I don't think he cared. So I'll tell you."
Wren waited.
"They do what the word says," said Rook. "Not what a man means. You understand me? Not what he means. What he says."
"I don't—"
"If he says stop, they stop. If he says go, they go. If he says bring, they bring." Rook's long face was very close to hers now, and she could smell the oil on him, and see the red rims of his old eyes. "If he says through, miss, they go through. Through a door. Through a wall. Through a gate. Through whatever's standing in the gate." His voice cracked. "I couldn't make them know the difference. I tried. I sat up nights in my shop with the shutters down, trying. A man would know the difference. A dog would know it. A horse. I couldn't put it into them. I don't know how. Nobody knows how. You can't build knowing."
He looked back down the vault at the six crates.
"They're good clocks," he said. "They keep good time. That's all they are."
Then he turned and went back into the vault, toward his crates, and his shaking hands went back into his apron pockets.
Wren went up the stair.
She went home to Steeple Lane through the gray afternoon, through streets that were already starting to darken, and the lamplighters were out on the Hill with their long poles, lighting the gas, and the light came on in each lamp with a small soft pop and a bloom of yellow. She did not look at any of it. She thought about what Rook had said. Through whatever's standing in the gate.
Mrs. Oddie had a bag ready for her when she came in.
Of course she did. She was standing in the kitchen with it on the table: a canvas bag with a strap, already packed. Wren opened it and looked. Two pairs of thick wool stockings. A wool vest. Mittens, and over-mittens of oiled leather. A scarf. A knitted cap that would go under her hat, or instead of it. A tin of goose grease, for her face and hands in the wind. Oatcakes in a paper. A flask, empty, for water. Everything. Exactly right. As if Mrs. Oddie had packed a bag for the cold many times before.
"Thank you, Mrs. Oddie."
Mrs. Oddie nodded. Then, unexpectedly, she put out one of her big ham-like hands and touched Wren's cheek. Just once. Her palm was warm and rough and smelled of bread.
Then she turned back to her range.
Wren went up to her narrow room behind the study and sat down on her bed.
She had very little of her own to pack. Her room had very little in it. A bed. A washstand. The cracked glass on the back of the door. A hook with her spare coat on it. A small shelf with three books on it, all Ministry books, rules and drill and the Inquisitor's Code. That was all. She had lived in the house on Steeple Lane for two years and the room still looked as if nobody lived in it.
She took off her coat and laid it on the bed.
Then she took out of the left breast pocket of her coat the thing she always carried there. A small book. A little thicker than her thumb, and no bigger than her palm, bound in plain brown leather gone soft and dark at the corners. She had bought it on Quill Row, two years ago, the first week she was a cadet, for fourpence. She had written in it every week since.
She opened it.
The pages were full of her small neat hand. Lines and lines of it. Each line a name, or a description where there was no name, and after it, a memory. A short one, mostly. A few words. Woman, Coal Street, debt: the smell of her mother's hair. Boy, about nine, Lower Docks, unlicensed harvesting: his father's laugh. Old man, Watch house, no name: a song his wife sang.
Every one was a memory she had taken.
It was the one thing about her work that nobody knew. Not the other cadets. Not the clerks. Not, she thought, even Kross, though with Kross you could never be sure what he knew. Every time she used her Extraction, every time she put her hand on somebody's head on the Inquisition's orders and drew something out of them, she wrote down afterward, in this book, what it had been. So that somebody would know. So that it would not be quite gone. So that if one day, somehow, somebody wanted to give it back, there would be a record of whose it was.
She did not know if anybody could ever give it back. She did not think so. But she wrote them down.
She turned to the most recent page and read the last line.
Boy, about seven, Fish Stairs, Health cart: his first fish. Given back to nobody.
She looked at it for a long time.
Then she closed the book, and put it back in the left breast pocket of her coat, and buttoned the pocket shut.
And without thinking, her hand went to her right-hand pocket, where for years she had carried a gray stone with a white ring round it. A beach stone. Her stone.
The pocket was empty.
She remembered, and sat still on the bed with her hand on the empty pocket, and did not know why it made her want to cry.
The Fifth Step
At dusk she went down to the kitchen for her supper, and on the way she found him on the stair.
He was sitting on the fifth step again. The one with the dark ring worn into it. He was not in his shirtsleeves now. He was in his coat, buttoned, with his gloves on, as if he were about to go out, or had just come in and had not had the strength to go any further. His hat was on the step beside him. There was no tea.
Wren stopped on the stair above him. She did not know whether to go back up, or to go past.
She stood there long enough that it became impossible to do either without saying something.
"Sir," she said.
"Halloway." He did not turn round. "Have you packed?"
"Yes, sir. Mrs. Oddie packed."
"She would have." A pause. "She packed for me, once. A long time ago. For the north."
Wren waited for him to say more. He did not.
The stairwell was getting dark. The last of the light came in gray through the small high window on the landing, and lay on the scrubbed pale treads, and on the back of Kross's coat, and on the dark ring in the fifth step where so many feet had worn the wood. Below, in the hall, nobody had lit the lamp yet. The house was very quiet. Through the wall she could hear, faintly, the Choir in the Spire, singing for the evening bell. High children's voices, very far away, going up and up.
She found that she had sat down. On the seventh step. Two above him. She did not remember deciding to.
He did not seem to mind.
They sat on the stair in the dark and listened to the Choir.
After a while, Kross said, "Have you ever been north, Halloway? Past the fens?"
"No, sir."
"No." He was quiet. "It's very cold. It's colder than you think, and then it's colder than that. The wind comes down off the hills and goes through your coat as if it were made of paper. You'll want the goose grease Mrs. Oddie gave you. Use it before your face cracks, not after."
"Yes, sir."
"And then you'll come to the Glass."
She said nothing.
"You'll hear it before you see it," said Kross. "A sort of hum. You'll feel it in your teeth. And when you see it, it will look like nothing you have ever seen, and you will want to stand and look at it for a long time." He paused. "Don't stand and look at it for a long time. Keep walking. People who stand and look at it for a long time tend to start walking toward it, and not stop."
Wren swallowed. "Yes, sir."
The Choir finished its song. The last high note went up and up and stopped, and in the silence after it, the house was so quiet that Wren could hear the clock ticking in the study below, slow and steady, like one of Rook's iron men.
"There's one thing about the Glass," said Kross. "One thing you should know before you go. Before any of it."
She waited.
He turned his head, at last, and looked up at her over his shoulder, in the dark. She could see his face now, a little. Thin and gray and lined, with the long lines from the nose to the mouth. His eyes were very tired, and they were looking at her very steadily.
"On the Glass," he said, "nobody can take anything, Wren."
It was the first time he had ever called her by her first name. She did not think he noticed.
"Nobody can burn," he said. "Nobody can take. Not a memory. Not a word. Not a skill. Not a face. Nothing. It's the only place in the world where that's true." He looked at her a moment longer. "Remember that. When you're out there. Whatever happens. Nobody can take anything from you."
He turned back and looked down the dark stairwell.
"And nobody can take anything from me," he said, very quietly, almost to himself.
Then he got up. He did it slowly, with one hand on the banister, like a much older man. He picked his hat up off the step. He stood on the fifth step for a moment, looking at nothing.
Then he went up the stair past her, without a word, and she sat very still on the seventh step and listened to him go. Up past the second floor, past the white room, without stopping. Up again, to the top of the house, where the ceilings were low and the doors were plain.
The footsteps stopped at the last door on the left.
Wren waited for the knock. Two, and then three. The pattern.
It did not come.
There was only silence. A long silence. A minute. More. She sat on the seventh step in the dark with her arms round her knees and listened to the silence at the top of the house, and she knew, as clearly as if she could see him, that Kross was standing outside the door of Room Nine with his gloved hand raised to knock on it, and was not knocking.
Then the footsteps came down again, slowly, and went into the study, and the door shut.
