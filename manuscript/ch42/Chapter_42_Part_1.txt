Chapter 42: Remy's Ledger, Part 1 of 5: Terms
Terms
Remy Castellan came up the rubble slope slowly, the way a man walks into a field where there is a bull.
Corin watched him come. He could see him through the gap in the sacking, from the pallet, if he turned his head. A slight gray figure against the white snow, with the gray hat held against his chest. He came up the slope one careful step at a time, in good gray boots that were not made for rubble, and twice he slipped, and twice he caught himself with his free hand on the broken bricks, and did not swear, and did not hurry.
Oren went out to meet him.
Corin saw the big man stop at the top of the slope, in the mouth of the arch, so that the young man had to stop too, a pace below him. He saw Oren look down at him. Oren did not say anything. He did not need to. He stood with Terrence's coat open, and his sword hand on the hilt of his sword, and let Remy Castellan look at it.
Remy looked at it.
Then he looked up at Oren's face, and said something, too low for Corin to hear. And Oren said something back, short. And Remy nodded. And held out his hat.
Oren took it. He turned it over in his big scarred hand, and looked inside it, and felt round the inside of the band with one finger. Then he gave it back.
Remy held out his arms, a little way from his sides.
Oren went over him. Quickly, the way a guard does it, the way Corin had seen him do it at the Thorne gate to men who came with letters. Down the arms. Under the arms. Down the sides of the coat. The pockets. The back of the collar. The tops of the boots. Remy stood quite still for it, with his arms out and his face turned a little away, toward the river, the way a man stands at a tailor's.
Oren found two things.
Corin saw him find them. A small flat case, from the inside pocket of the gray coat. Silver, perhaps. And a book. A small fat book, black, the size of a man's hand, from somewhere lower down. Not a pocket. Somewhere inside the coat itself. Oren had to feel for it twice before he found where it was, and Corin saw his face change a little when he did.
Oren held them both up and looked at Remy.
Remy said something. Oren looked at him for a long moment. Then he put the silver case into his own coat pocket, and kept it. And gave Remy back the book.
Then he stood aside.
Remy came into the arch.
He stopped just inside the sacking, the way everybody did, while his eyes got used to the dark. Corin watched him look round. At the low round roof of black brick, and the crack with the dead weed in it. At the long dark floor full of sleeping shapes. At the red fire at the end. At Lyra, standing by the fire with her arms folded and her hacked hair sticking up and her face very cold. At Mother Grue, who had gone back to her box by the hearth and sat on it, small and brown and wrapped in her shawls, with her poker across her knees. At Gideon, who had come back from wherever he had been and was sitting cross-legged on the floor at the foot of the pallet, with his good hand inside his coat.
Remy's eyes rested on Gideon a moment longer than on the others.
Then they came to Corin.
"Mister Vane," he said.
His voice was quiet and clear and very even. A clerk's voice, Corin thought. Or a voice that had learned to sound like one. Not high and not low. Not warm and not cold. The kind of voice you would never remember afterwards, except that it had been polite.
"Mister Castellan," said Corin.
"Thank you for seeing me."
"I haven't seen you yet." Corin shifted on the pallet, and felt the leg wake up, and kept his face still. "You said I could set the terms."
"I did."
"Then here they are. You sit on the floor. There. By the fire, where the light is. Where I can see your hands." Corin kept his voice level. "You take off your gloves. Both of them. And you keep your hands on your knees, where everybody here can see them, the whole time you're talking. If you lift them off your knees for any reason, Captain Vance will take them off for you."
Remy looked at him for a moment.
Then he looked at Oren, standing just inside the sacking behind him, with his hand on his sword.
"That seems fair," said Remy.
He went to the fire. He did not hurry. He stepped over a sleeping child on the way, very carefully, and stopped to make sure he had not woken her. Then he took off his gray coat, and folded it, and laid it on the floor, and sat down on it, cross-legged, in the red light, the way Gideon was sitting. A pace from the end of Corin's pallet. With his back to the fire, so that his face was half in shadow and half in light.
He took off his gloves.
He did it the way he did everything. Neatly. A finger at a time. The left, then the right. He laid them on the floor beside him, one on top of the other, smoothed flat.
Then he put his bare hands on his knees.
Corin looked at them.
They were young hands. Thin, and white, and very clean, with long fingers. A clerk's hands. A pen callus on the second finger of the right, hard and yellow. And ink. On the right hand, on the side of the little finger and across the heel of the palm, a smear of blue-black ink. The same as the stain on the cuff of the coat. He had been writing fast, with his hand dragging through the wet line.
And on the back of the left hand, from the wrist to the knuckles, there was a scar.
It was old. White and shiny and puckered, like the skin on hot milk. The shape of it was very plain, once you looked. A square. A small square, the size of a large coin, with its edges very sharp and straight. And in the middle of the square, raised a little, a smaller shape, like a letter. Corin could not read it from where he lay. But he knew what it was.
A brand.
The kind they had used on debt-children, once, in the worst houses of the Lower Docks, where nobody from the Bank or the Watch ever came to look. So that everybody would know whose they were.
Remy saw him looking. He did not move his hand.
"It's a V," he said quietly. "If you were wondering. For Vivienne. She stopped doing it, after me. She said it was common." He looked down at it, with no expression at all. "I was four. I don't remember it. I only remember being told."
Nobody said anything.
Mother Grue, on her box by the fire, had gone very still, with her poker across her knees.
Corin looked at the scar for a while longer. Then he looked up at Remy's face, half in the red light.
"All right," he said. "Say what you came to say."
Remy did not say it at once.
He sat on his folded coat with his bare hands on his knees, and looked round the arch again, slowly. At the sleepers on the floor. At the dead weed in the crack. At Lyra's cold face, and Oren's sword, and Mother Grue's poker. And last, again, at Gideon, sitting cross-legged at the foot of the pallet with his good hand inside his coat.
"I've been here before," he said. "Once. In the autumn. To the bottom of the slope. Not this far."
"I know," said Mother Grue. Her voice was like a gate. "I saw you. You stood in the snow for an hour with your hat in your hands. The day after."
Remy looked at her.
"Yes," he said. "I couldn't come up. I didn't think I'd be let." He looked down at his hands. "And I didn't know what I'd say, if I was."
"And now you do?"
"No," said Remy. "But I've run out of time to wait until I do."
He lifted his eyes to Corin's.
"I came about Tam," he said. "That's true. I wrote it on the card because it was true, and because I knew it was the only thing that would get me up the slope. But I didn't come only about Tam." A pause. "I came because on Sunday night somebody went down into the Bourse cellars through Coker's coal chute, and opened the Under-Secretary's bag on the counting room table, and took one vial out of twenty. And because this morning, the Lady found out."
About Tam
Nobody in the arch moved.
Corin felt Lyra go still by the fire. He felt Oren, behind Remy, shift his weight from one foot to the other, very slightly, the way a guard does before he moves. He felt Gideon, at the foot of the pallet, not move at all.
He kept his own face still. He had had a great deal of practice, the last few days.
"Did she," he said.
"She did." Remy's hands did not move on his knees. "I'll tell you all of it. Everything I know. I came to tell you. But I'd like to say the part about Tam first, if you'll let me. Because if I tell you the rest first, you'll throw me out before I get to it. And I'd understand if you did." He paused. "And Tam's the reason I'm sitting here. Not the vial."
Corin looked at him for a long moment.
"Go on," he said.
Remy looked down at his hands. At the ink on the right one. At the brand on the left.
"I was four when the Lady bought me," he said. "I don't remember before. There's nothing there. Not even a smell. She says my mother sold me for a winter's rent and a bottle, and I've no reason to think she's lying. She doesn't lie about things like that. She thinks they're funny." His voice did not change. "I grew up in the house on the Hill. Not in the cellars. Upstairs. With the rugs and the glass cases. She wanted a boy who could read and write and count and dress well and stand in a corner and remember everything that was said. So that's what she made."
"Her eyes," said Lyra, from the fire.
Remy glanced at her.
"Yes," he said. "Her eyes. And her ears, and her legs. That's what she calls me. To people. Remy is my eyes." A small movement of his mouth, that was not a smile. "Tam was her ears. Mostly. Not as good with a pen as me. Better at standing behind a chair and hearing what was said at the table. He came to the house when I was fourteen. A footman. Nineteen, from the Lower Docks. Big, quiet. Gloves too white. He used to darn his own thumbs."
Corin did not move.
The gloves. Did they fit? Mother darned the thumb.
"He was the only one in that house who ever spoke to me as if I were a person," said Remy. "Not a pen. Not a pair of eyes. He'd come into the little room where I did the books, late, after the dinners, with his collar undone. And he'd bring me something from Mrs. Pratt's warming oven in a napkin. Pie, mostly. Steak and kidney. And he'd sit on the end of my desk and eat his half and ask me what I was reading." He stopped. "Nobody had ever asked me that before. What I was reading. I didn't know what to say. I said the accounts. And he laughed. And the next night he brought me a book. A real one. With a story in it. From a stall on Comb Street. It cost him a week's pay. I've still got it."
The arch was very quiet. The fire hissed.
Mother Grue was watching the young man from her box with her bright black eyes. Not the way she had watched him from the sacking. Another way.
"He was a good lad," she said. "In the end."
"He was a good lad at the beginning, too," said Remy. "He just worked for a bad house. So did I. So do I." He looked up at Corin. "When they put him on the glass in the autumn, in the glass room, after the raid, I was in the corner. Writing it down. I'm always in the corner, writing it down. I wrote it all down. What was taken. How much. How long it took. I wrote down the cold going into him." His voice stayed level. "And when Gant carried him out to the lift, I wrote down the time."
Nobody said anything.
"I didn't know where they'd taken him," said Remy. "Not then. I found out the next day. From the lift-boy. That he'd come here. That he'd died here. That a harvester had sat with him." He looked at Corin. "That you'd sat with him. For three hours. On the stone. And then taken his Ash, because he asked you to. And then put him in the river with an orange."
Corin found that his throat had gone tight.
"Who told you about the orange?"
"Nobody. I watched. From the bottom of the slope. At the turn of the tide." Remy did not look away. "I couldn't come up. I didn't know who you were, then. Not really. Only what the Lady said. I stood at the bottom of the slope and watched the bargeman row him out, and I didn't do anything. I never do anything. I write it down."
He stopped.
Then, more quietly: "I'd like to know what you saw. In his Ash. If you'll tell me. Whatever it was. Even if it's only that he was cold."
Corin looked at him for a long time.
He thought about rule one. About how it was for partners, and this young man in gray with a brand on his hand was not his partner and might never be. He thought about how anything he said in this arch could be in Vivienne's glass room by the ninth bell tonight, written down in this same neat even hand.
And he thought about Tam Hollin, under six blankets, with his slate lips almost smiling, saying Tell her I'm sorry about the gravy.
"I saw the glass room," he said. "The night they put him on the glass. I saw it through his eyes. I saw you, in the corner, writing. I didn't know it was you, then. A young man in gray." He paused. "I saw Lady Vivienne in her chair. And the stones at her throat. And a man standing behind her chair, in the shadow. A tall man, very straight, in a long coat the color of old iron, with a high collar and two rows of iron buttons and red at the cuffs. A soldier's coat. Not from Veridia."
Remy's hands tightened on his knees. Just for a moment. Then they were still again.
"The Colonel," he said.
Corin looked at him.
"That's what she calls him. The Colonel. Never a name. He comes on Thursdays, sometimes. Not every week. Up the lift to the glass room, very late. He stands behind her chair. He never sits. He never drinks. He never speaks to me." Remy was quiet for a moment. "I've written down every night he's come for four years. I've never once written down what he says, because he never says anything when I'm in the room."
"There was another man," said Corin. "Afterwards. Who came up in the lift. Who Tam forgot to look at the wall for. I couldn't see his face. Every time I looked, I slid off it, like a foot off a wet stone." He hesitated. "He had a gold ring. With a seal on it. A dome, with lines going out from it. Like a sunrise."
And Remy Castellan, who had not moved his face once since he came up the slope, went white.
It was not a great change. A man looking at him across a dinner table would not have seen it. But Corin had been looking at faces for twelve years, across intake glasses, waiting to see what they would give, and he saw it. The blood went out of the narrow clever face from the top down, the way water goes out of a basin when you pull the plug. And left it gray, in the red light.
"You saw that," said Remy. "In Tam."
"Yes."
"Tam saw that."
"Yes. Through the lift door. He forgot the wall. He stared."
Remy shut his eyes.
"Then that's why," he said, very low. "That's why it was so much. That's why they took everything. Not just the cold. Everything." He opened his eyes. "I wrote it down at the time. All. By order. I didn't know whose order. I thought it was hers. I thought she was angry about the raid." He looked at his bare hands. "It wasn't her order. It was his. Because Tam looked at his face."
Nobody spoke.
"I won't say his name," said Remy. "I don't think you'd hear it if I did. Nobody does. It slides." He looked up. "But I know who wears that ring, Mister Vane. Everyone who works on the Hill knows who wears that ring. They just can't remember that they know."
And behind Corin's left ear, where for two days there had been only silence and a rustle, there was a sound. Very faint. Very far. Like a man in another room, waking, and turning over, and saying one word into his pillow.
Careful.
Nineteen and a Hole
Corin did not answer Malakor. He did not dare. He held the word the way you hold a coal in a cloth, carefully, so it would not go out and would not burn through, and kept his eyes on Remy's face.
"The vial," he said. "Tell me."
Remy nodded slowly. He looked at the fire for a moment, as if he were reading something in it. Then he began. And his voice, Corin noticed, changed as he did. It went flatter. Neater. Like a man reading out a page from a ledger. As if this were a thing he had written down already, and was only reading back.
"The Under-Secretary keeps a house on Linden Walk," said Remy. "Not far from the Thorne house. A narrow white house, with a black door and a brass knocker in the shape of a hand. He lives there alone. A housekeeper, a cook, a boy for the boots. He goes home from the Bourse on Sunday nights at about the half past midnight, and takes his tea, and goes to bed. On Monday nights, he sits at his kitchen table with his bag, and fills the twists for Friday. Fifty twists. One drop in each sweet. He does it himself. He doesn't trust the dispensary with that part. He likes to do it with his own hands." A pause. "He says it's restful."
Lyra had gone very white by the fire.
"Last night," said Remy, "he opened his tray, and there were nineteen vials. And a hole."
Nobody spoke.
"He sat at his kitchen table for a long time, his cook says. She's a cousin of Mrs. Pratt's. Not looking at anything. Then he wrote a note. Very short. And sent the boot boy running with it up the Hill to the Lady's house, at the first bell of the morning, in the snow." Remy's hands lay quite still on his knees. "I opened the door to him. I read the note before I took it up. I always do. She knows I do. She expects it."
"What did it say?"
"Dear lady. One of mine is missing from my bag since Sunday. I am sure it is nothing. Do look into it. Kindly, A."
The fire hissed.
"Kindly," said Remy. "That's all. Kindly, A. She read it in her bed, by a candle, and then she put it down on the coverlet and sat up and screamed at me for a quarter of an hour." He did not seem to find this strange. "Not because of the vial. She doesn't care about one vial. Because he'd written kindly. Because he'd written I am sure it is nothing. Because that's how he writes when he's very, very angry, and everybody on the Hill knows it. And because whatever is wrong at the Bourse is her fault. Her cellars. Her door. Her people."
"Her people," said Corin.
"Gant was in her room by the second bell." Remy's voice went flatter still. "He'd been through it all by the fifth. Who was in the cellars on Sunday. Mistress Fane. Mister Drabble, the Nym man. The Under-Secretary himself. The two porters. And Coker, on the coal flap, from the sixth bell till the Bell closed and after." A pause. "And the watch. Young Pike. With Tinker. On his round, at the half past eleven."
Gideon, at the foot of the pallet, did not move. His good hand stayed inside his coat.
"Pike had already written it in the watch book," said Remy. "On Sunday night. When he came off. He's a careful boy. He wants to be a sergeant. Half past eleven. Tinker gave tongue at Coker's flap. A coal thief came up out of it. One of Grue's hollow boys. Tinker bit him. Coker let him go. Coker said it was rats."
He looked at Gideon.
Gideon looked back at him with his pale calm eyes.
"It wasn't rats," said Gideon.
"No," said Remy. "I didn't think it was." He looked at the sling. At the black darning showing through the bandage at the elbow. Then back at the boy's face. "You're Big Gideon's son."
"Yes."
"I've seen you. On the back stair. For two years. Sitting on the step outside the kitchen in all weathers." Remy's voice did something. Went a little less flat. "The Lady used to watch you from the window, sometimes. She said you were the only thing in the Lower Docks that never wanted anything."
"I want boots," said Gideon. "I've got some now."
Remy looked at the boots. The good brown boots, too big, with the toes stuffed with rag. Something moved at the corner of his mouth.
"So you have," he said.
Then he looked back at Corin, and the something went away.
"Gant took the watch book up to her room," he said. "And she read it. And she put her finger on the line. One of Grue's hollow boys. And then she put her finger on the line above it. Coker said it was rats." He was silent for a moment. "And she said, Bring me Coker."
The arch was very quiet.
Corin found that his hands had closed on the blanket. He made them open. He could not.
He was kind, Lyra had said. In the doorway on Cinder Lane, shaking, with a penny in her fist. He gave me a penny. He told me to get something hot.
I'm going to have a sleep now, Coker had said, loud, to nobody, on his stool in the frost, while Corin lay in the coal at the bottom of the chute. And if a coal rat comes up out of my flap while I'm sleeping, I'll not see it.
"When?" said Corin.
"The sixth bell. This morning. Gant and two of the Lady's men went down to Cinder Lane and took him off his stool." Remy did not look away. "He didn't run. He didn't fight. He folded up his newspaper and put it under the stool and went with them. Down the kitchen stair." A pause. "To the selling room."
A tall chair with a high back, bolted to the floor. With leather straps on the arms, and a leather strap for the forehead, and a small brass stand beside it with a hook, for hanging a glass.
Lyra made a small sound by the fire.
"No," said Corin.
"Yes," said Remy.
He said it quite gently. As if he were sorry. Corin thought perhaps he was.
"And they know where to look now," said Remy. "For the rest of it. One of Grue's hollow boys." He glanced at Mother Grue on her box, with her poker across her knees, and then round at the long dark arch full of sleepers. "Gant has had Grue's name written in his book for years. He knows where the arches are. He's never troubled to come, because there was never anything here worth the walk. Now there is."
Mother Grue's hand closed on her poker.
"He'll come, then," she said.
"He'll come," said Remy. "Not tonight. Tonight he can't. Tonight every man the Lady has will be at the Bourse, in clean collars, standing behind chairs, because tonight the Bank comes to audit the books." He looked at Corin. "The Auditor of the Fourth Desk. Every Midwinter week. On the Tuesday. She comes at the eighth bell, with two clerks, and sits in the counting room till midnight with every ledger the Bourse has, and Gant stands behind her chair the whole time and smiles." A pause. "Tomorrow, he'll be free. Tomorrow, at first light, he'll come down here with six men and the watch book. Looking for a hollow boy with a dog bite on his arm. And whoever was with him."
Nobody said anything.
Corin lay on the pallet and looked at the young man in gray, sitting cross-legged in the red light with his branded hand on his knee.
"Why are you telling us this?" he said.
Remy looked back at him.
"Because I want you alive," he said simply. "Until Midwinter, at least. And because I want something from you. And because of Tam." He paused. "And because I've spent sixteen years in a corner writing down what happens to people, and doing nothing. I thought I'd try doing something. Just once. To see what it was like."
