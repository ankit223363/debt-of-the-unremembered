Chapter 42: Remy's Ledger, Part 3 of 5: Lot Forty-One
Lot Forty-One
Corin read it once. Then he read it again, more slowly, with his gray finger under the words the way a child reads, because he did not trust his eyes.
Lot 41. A Lord's Last Evening. Tier Four. One jar, sealed, in a case of black wood with a silver clasp. Consigned to the Midwinter Masquerade by C. T., by proxy.
The proxy: an old man. Came to the kitchen gate on the second Thursday of the month, after dark, alone, on foot. A harvester's coat, old, very worn. Gray to the shoulder in both arms; the Ash showing at the cuffs when he lifted the case. His fingers blue. Not cold. Blue, the way ink is blue. He would not give a name. He would not come in. He would not take payment. He said the lady had arranged all that. He said, "Tell your mistress to keep it cold."
Mistress Fane tagged the jar. I copied the tag. Spiral on the seal: turns LEFT.
AFTER: To be sold Midwinter night, by sealed bid. The Lady says whoever buys it will not live to open it. She laughed when she said it.
Corin sat on the pallet with the book open in his gray hands and did not move.
He did not hear the fire. He did not hear Lyra say his name. He did not hear anything at all except a voice, very far back, very dry, from a long way off in time. From the cold room of the Bank, perhaps. Or the Harvesting Section, in his first years, before the gray had reached his wrists.
Linus cut it that way.
Malakor had said that. Once. In the autumn. Over a jar on a table in the Bank, the jar that had held Pell's Ash, when they had both looked at the wax and seen the spiral pressed into it turning the wrong way. Left. Against the sun. Every harvester in the Bank cut his spirals to the right, the way the rod turned, the way the old teachers had always taught. Corin did. Every jar he had ever sealed in twelve years turned right.
Only one harvester he had ever known cut them left. There was one other rod he had seen that turned the same way, against the sun: in Tam's Ash, in a long pale hand, under a face his eyes could not hold. But that man did not walk to kitchen gates on foot, in an old coat, after dark.
Linus.
Linus Hale. Master Linus Hale, of the Harvesting Section. The old man who had taught him. Who had taken a shy gray boy of sixteen into the cold room and shown him how to hold the rod, and how to listen, and how not to listen too hard. Who had sat in a corner of the Lamplighter's Rest on Ferrule Street in the autumn, at the Ink Quarter end of the Lantern Bridge, with his blue fingers round a cup of tea he did not drink, and said I'm sorry it's taken so long. And then had told him a great deal, and not told him a great deal more.
Who had left the Lamplighter's Rest, on Friday, the step women said, early in the morning, with all his books.
And whose name was written in the Carriage Book of the Ministry of Records, in the autumn, in Toby's careful copying hand, on a line Corin had read so many times in the Ledger that he could see it now with his eyes shut. Harvester L. Hale, and one guest. See sealed note 7. From the Thorne house, a sealed case. To Lower Levels.
Two years ago. The night Lord Thorne burned.
Taken whole before the fire.
Somebody had been in the study with Lord Thorne that night, with a rod. Somebody had taken his last evening out of him, whole, before he burned the rest. Corin had known that since Friday, since Lyra read the catalogue aloud on the barge. He had not let himself ask who.
He had not wanted to know.
"Corin."
Lyra's voice. Very close. She was kneeling by the pallet now, with her hand on his arm. He could feel it through the rags. He did not know how long she had been there.
"Corin. What is it? What does it say?"
He gave her the book.
She read it. He watched her read it. He watched her eyes go along the first line, A Lord's Last Evening, and stop. And go on. C. T., by proxy. And stop again. He watched her mouth go thin, the way it had gone thin on the barge on Friday, when she read the same three letters aloud from the catalogue and said I don't know who that is.
She read on. An old man. A harvester's coat. His fingers blue. She did not know what that meant. He could see that she did not. She did not know Linus. She had never met him. She read the line about the spiral, and frowned, and read the last line, and her face went white.
She looked up at him.
"Who is he?" she said. "The old man. With blue fingers. You know. I can see you know."
"He taught me," said Corin. "At the Bank. When I was sixteen."
Lyra stared at him.
"Linus Hale," said Corin. "Master Linus. He cuts his spirals left. Nobody else does. Nobody in the Bank, in thirty years." He swallowed. "His name's in the Ministry's Carriage Book. On the night your father burned. Harvester L. Hale, and one guest. A sealed case. From the Thorne house." He looked at the book in her hands. "He was in the study, Lyra. That night. With your father. With a rod. He's the one who took it."
The arch was very quiet.
Lyra knelt by the pallet with the little black book in her two hands, and looked at him. And Corin watched her take it in. The way you watch somebody take in cold water, a mouthful at a time. Her face did not crumple. It went still. Very still. And very old.
"And C. T.," she said.
Corin did not say anything.
They looked at each other.
Not yet, he had said to her, on the wain, in the dark, on Friday night. When you can.
And she had said nothing, with her face turned away from him in the hay.
"I lied," said Lyra. "On the barge. When I read the catalogue. When Juno brought it and I read out C. T., by proxy and said I don't know who that is." Her voice was very steady. "I knew. I knew the moment I saw it. I've known since I was nine years old what those letters look like at the bottom of a letter. With a hook on the C, like a fishhook."
She drew a breath.
"C. T. is my aunt," she said. "Cressida Thorne. Father's sister. Councillor Thorne, who sits in his seat on the Ministry Council." She stopped. "She's selling my father's last evening at the Midwinter Masquerade. By proxy. And the proxy is your teacher."
Nobody spoke.
Corin looked at her, kneeling in the red firelight with Remy's book in her hands and her hacked hair sticking up and her face very white and very calm.
"Rule one," said Lyra. "There. I've said it." Her mouth moved. Not a smile. "You said yours today, about your sister. So I thought I'd better say mine."
Corin found that he could not speak.
He put out his gray hand and took hers, on top of the book. She let him. Her fingers were very cold.
Remy, sitting cross-legged on his folded coat by the fire, with his bare hands flat on his knees, had not moved through any of it. He had watched them both, with his dark eyes, the way he watched everything. Writing it down, perhaps, somewhere behind them. Corin did not care.
"I didn't know," said Remy quietly, "who the old man was. I've wondered. I wrote down everything about him I could. I thought if I wrote it down well enough, someone would know." He looked at Corin. "I didn't know it would be you."
"Is it there?" said Corin. His voice was hoarse. "Now? At the Bourse? Lot 41?"
"In the vault," said Remy. "Behind the three doors. In its black case, on a shelf of its own. Cold. The way he said." He paused. "I've seen it. Once. When Fane took it down. You can feel it through the case if you stand close. Like standing next to a man who's holding his breath."
He looked at Corin, and then at Lyra, and then back at Corin.
"That's what I came to sell you," he said. "Not the book. The book's a gift. So you'll know I mean it." He paused. "What I came to sell you is the way through the three doors. On Midwinter night. With the key in my pocket."
The Price
"Three doors," said Corin. "And two of them aren't doors."
Remy's eyebrows went up, very slightly.
"Who told you that?"
"The Duke of Marrow," said Lyra, from beside the pallet. "On Saturday. In his gallery. He said the builder of the room was put into a crate."
"He was," said Remy. "In the year I came to the house. I saw the crate go out of the wet door. It was very heavy." He did not seem to think this needed saying in any particular way. "The Duke knows a great deal about that house. He's been coming to it for thirty years. He never buys anything. He only looks."
He was quiet for a moment, with his bare hands on his knees.
"The first door is a door," he said. "Iron. At the bottom of a stair below the glass room. Below the floor of the dome, under the middle, where the hum is. It has no lock. No keyhole. No handle. It opens to blood."
Nobody spoke.
"Hers," said Remy. "Or mine. She taught me, when I was twelve. In case she was ever too ill to go down herself. She said it was the only thing she'd ever taught anybody that she didn't regret." He turned his left hand over on his knee, palm up. There was a small white scar across the ball of the thumb. And another. And another. A row of them, like the marks on a tally stick. "You put your hand on the iron and cut it, and say the word, and the iron drinks, and the door remembers you. It doesn't open for anyone else's blood. Not Gant's. Not Fane's. Not a Bank auditor's, with all her seals." A pause. "It's called a Sanguine Vault. There are three in the city. The Archon has one. The Bank has one. The Lady has the third."
"And the second door?" said Corin.
"Isn't a door." Remy looked at the fire. "It's a passage. Twelve steps long. Very narrow. Lined with glass. And when you walk down it, it reads you. The way Fane reads a man with a glass on his temple. All of you, at once, in twelve steps. And if it finds anything in you that the Lady hasn't put there on purpose, it closes." He paused. "Not the passage. You. It closes you. You stand in the passage with your eyes open, and you can't move, and you can't think, until somebody comes to fetch you. Sometimes it's hours. Sometimes nobody comes."
The fire hissed.
"And you can walk it," said Corin.
"I've walked it every week for eight years." Remy's voice was flat. "It knows me. I'm one of the things she put there on purpose. I can take one other person through it, if they hold my hand the whole way and don't let go and don't think about anything at all." He looked at Corin. "Not anything. For twelve steps. Not your name. Not the girl. Not the boy in the hay. Not your sister. Nothing. If you think of one thing that's yours, it'll close you, and it'll close me with you, because I'm holding your hand."
Corin looked at him for a long moment.
"I can do that," he said.
"Can you?" said Remy.
"I've had practice," said Corin. "Not thinking about things. Mostly they're gone before I can."
Remy looked at him. And then, for the first time since he had come up the slope, something happened to his face that might have been the beginning of a smile. It did not get very far. It came, and stood at the corners of his mouth for a moment, and went away again.
"And the third door?" said Lyra.
"The third door," said Remy, "I'll tell you about on Midwinter night. When we're standing in front of it." He did not look away from her. "Not because I don't trust you. Because I don't trust anybody to keep that one in their head for six days without it showing on their face. Not even me. Especially not me."
Nobody argued with that.
"So," said Corin. "The way through. On Midwinter night. With the key in your pocket. And the price."
"And the price," said Remy.
He sat up straighter on his folded coat. His bare hands lay flat on his knees, the inked one and the branded one, side by side. And his voice when he spoke again was not the reading voice. It was not the polite voice either. It was a voice Corin had not heard him use. Quiet and plain and very careful, like a man setting down something heavy that might break.
"One harvest," he said. "Of Ash. By you. With your rod. Whenever I ask, and wherever, and from whoever I name." He paused. "And you give me the jar. Sealed. And you don't ask me what I'm going to do with it."
The arch was very still.
"Whose?" said Lyra.
"I'll name it later."
"When?"
"When it's time. Not before."
"That's not a price," said Lyra. "That's a blank page with his name signed at the bottom. You could name anybody. You could name a child. You could name—" She stopped. She did not look at Corin. "You could name someone we love."
"I could," said Remy. "I won't."
He turned his head and looked at her.
"Miss Thorne," he said, "I'll tell you three things I'll promise. And you can believe them or not. It won't be a child. It won't be anyone in this arch, or anyone you love, or anyone who's ever done you a kindness." He paused. "And it won't be anyone alive. Not when he does it. He'll never have to stand over a living person with that rod for me. They'll already be gone. He'll only have to be there, after."
Nobody said anything.
Corin lay on the pallet and looked at the young man in gray. At the brand on the back of his hand. At the row of small white scars across the ball of the thumb. At the ink on the side of his little finger. At the narrow, clean, pale face, half in the firelight, that had gone white when he heard about the ring.
He thought about Tam Hollin, under six blankets, in the fifth arch. About the three hours on the stone. About the orange.
He thought about what an Ash harvest was. What it meant. Not memories, the way the glass took memories from the living. Ash was what was left when a mage burned out and died. The last of them. All of them, at once. Everything that had not been spent. You did not take Ash from someone who could miss it. You took it from someone who was gone, and you kept it, and you carried it, and it was the closest thing in the world to carrying a person.
Remy did not want a memory. He wanted a person. Somebody who was going to die. And he wanted to have them, in a jar, afterwards.
And Corin thought he knew who.
He did not say it. He did not let himself think it all the way through. He held it the way he had held Malakor's word, carefully, in a cloth, and did not look at it.
Careful, Malakor had said.
He looked at Remy.
"If I say yes," he said, "and you name someone I won't do, I won't do it. Whatever you've given me. I'll give the key back. I'll give the book back. I'll give you anything else you want. But I won't do it."
"I know," said Remy. "That's why I want you to do it. Because you wouldn't, if it were wrong."
Corin looked at him for a long moment.
Then he held out his hand. His right hand. Gray to past the wrist, wrapped in Hessa's rags and Silas's lint, with the black and red on the knuckles.
Remy looked at it.
Then he lifted his own right hand off his knee. Slowly. Looking at Oren, standing over him with the blade out a hand's width from its scabbard. Oren did not move. And Remy leaned forward from his folded coat and took Corin's hand.
His grip was dry and cool and quite firm. A clerk's hand. Ink on the side of the little finger. A pen callus on the second finger, hard and yellow, pressing into Corin's palm.
"Done," said Remy.
"Done," said Corin.
They let go.
And Remy sat back on his folded coat and put his hands back on his knees. And looked at Corin. And said, in his ordinary, polite, forgettable voice, as if he were mentioning that it had begun to snow again:
"Then you'll want to see the doors tonight."
Tonight
"No," said Lyra.
She said it before anybody else could. She was standing up by the pallet now, with Remy's black book still in her hands, and her face had gone from white to red, the way it did when she was very angry and would not shout.
"No," she said again. "Look at him. Look at his leg. The doctor drew a line on it this morning. In ink. He said if the red goes past the line he'll take the leg off. He said if Corin moves, he'll know." She turned to Remy. "He's not going anywhere tonight. He's not going anywhere for three days."
Remy looked up at her. Then at the leg, out straight on its folded sacks, with the blanket over it. He could not see the line. He looked as if he could.
"I know," he said. "I'm sorry."
"Then why—"
"Because there isn't another night." Remy's voice did not rise. "I wish there were. I've been thinking about it since the first bell this morning, when I opened the door to the boot boy. I've thought about it all the way here, in the snow. I've looked at it from every side. There isn't." He turned his head and looked at Corin. "Tomorrow, at first light, Gant comes here. With six men and the watch book. Looking for a hollow boy with a dog bite. You have to be gone by then. All of you. Somewhere he won't find you. And after that, every day till Midwinter, the Lady's house is full. Workmen. Florists. The Weigher's people, setting the floor. Vespera's masks, being delivered. Gant on every stair." A pause. "And on Midwinter night, the house is full of guests. And the Archon."
Nobody moved.
"Tonight," said Remy, "the Bank comes. At the eighth bell. The Auditor of the Fourth Desk and two clerks, with their ledgers and their seals. They sit in the counting room till midnight, going through every book the Bourse has kept since last Midwinter. And the Lady sits in the room above in her chair and pretends not to care. And Gant stands behind the Auditor's chair the whole time, and smiles, and answers every question, and brings out every book she asks for, because if he doesn't, the Bank can close the Bourse in a day." He looked at Lyra. "For four hours tonight, Miss Thorne, every eye in that house is on one young woman with a gold tooth in the counting room. Nobody is looking at the stair below the glass room. Nobody is looking at the iron door. Nobody is looking at me."
"Then show him a map," said Lyra. "Draw it. You're good at drawing. You drew a flower."
"I can draw the stair," said Remy. "I can draw the iron door. I can't draw the passage. Nobody can." He looked at Corin. "It has to know you. The first time you walk it, it reads you all the way down, and it takes a long time, and it's very bad. The second time, it remembers. It still reads you. But it reads faster. It looks for what's changed. It's easier to stand." He was quiet for a moment. "If the first time is on Midwinter night, Mister Vane, with the Archon upstairs and the house full and the girl on the Duke's arm in the ballroom, it'll take too long. It'll find something. It'll close you. And then it'll close me. And we'll stand there in the glass, the two of us, until Gant comes down and finds us."
Corin lay on the pallet and listened to it.
He did not look at Lyra. He did not look at the line on his leg under the blanket. He looked at Remy's face, and at the hands on his knees, and he listened.
"Twelve steps," he said.
"Twelve steps."
"How long, the first time?"
"For me, the first time, when I was twelve?" Remy did not look away. "A quarter bell. Standing in it. With the Lady's hand on my shoulder, so it would know she'd brought me." A pause. "I was ill for a week afterwards. I didn't know my own name for two days. She said that was normal. She said it was the passage learning me."
The fire hissed.
"And then you walked it again," said Corin.
"And then I walked it again. Every week. For eight years." Remy's mouth moved. "It's quicker now. A breath. Two. Like walking past a dog that knows you."
Corin looked down at the gray hand lying on the blanket.
He thought about a quarter bell, standing in a passage of glass, with something reading him. All of him. At once. The way Fane read a man with a glass on his temple. The way Elara had read him, in the autumn, through the white blaze on her desk, when their hands touched. He thought about what it would find. The Ledger, against his heart. The first page. You have a sister. The barge, and the hay, and the old man in the gold room. Where Hessa owes the goat. Lyra. Lyra in the doorway on Cinder Lane, with her hand flat on his chest.
And behind all of it, at the end of the house, the locked door. The one the man with the ring had felt in Tam's Ash, like a heartbeat behind a wall. When it opens, I want to be the first thing he sees.
What would a passage of glass make of that?
Careful, Malakor had said.
"If I can't walk it tonight," said Corin slowly, "I can't walk it on Midwinter."
"No," said Remy.
"And if I can't walk it on Midwinter, there's no Lot 41."
"No."
"And no last evening. And no way to know what Lord Thorne folded away. Or why. Or who the witness was." He did not look at Lyra. He did not have to. He could feel her standing by the pallet, very still, with the black book in her hands. "And Linus goes on being the man who took it, and we never know why. And your aunt sells it to whoever bids highest, and whoever buys it doesn't live to open it."
Nobody said anything.
"Corin," said Lyra.
He looked at her.
She was standing very straight by the pallet, with Remy's book held against her chest in both hands. The way Nell had held the Ledger in Cinder Lane. The way she held everything she was afraid to drop. Her face was red and wet and furious.
"If you go tonight," she said, "you'll lose the leg."
"Maybe."
"Not maybe. The doctor said."
"The doctor said if I move. He didn't say how much." Corin tried to smile and found that it would not come. "I'll go on the crutch. I'll go slow. Gideon will be with me. Remy will be with me. It's a stair, and a door, and twelve steps. Not two nines and four. Not a coal chute." He paused. "Not on my elbows."
"And if the red goes past the line?"
He did not answer that.
Lyra looked at him for a long time.
Then she looked at Remy, sitting cross-legged on his coat with his bare hands on his knees.
"I want to come," she said.
"No," said Remy and Corin together.
She turned on Corin.
"Gant knows my face now," she said. "Doesn't he? From Coker. A girl. Short hair. A man's cap. A gray coat too good for the Lower Docks. So I can't come." Her voice shook. "So I'll sit here in the fourth arch, on my bucket, with the book against my heart. Again. And wait. Again. While you go down into that house again on a leg that's going to come off." She stopped. "Rule one, Corin. I'm saying it. I hate this. I hate it."
"I know," said Corin.
"Do you?"
"Yes." He looked at her. "I'd hate it too."
Lyra stood there for a moment longer, with her face working.
Then she put Remy's book down on the end of the pallet, very carefully, and went back to her bucket by the fire, and sat down on it with her back to all of them, and put her face in her hands.
Nobody said anything.
Oren, by the sacking, had not moved. He stood with his hand on his sword, and looked at Corin, and Corin saw him decide something, the way a guard decides at a gate.
"I'm going to Ellis tonight," said Oren. "At the Bridewell stables. For Hessa. I can't come with you."
"I know."
"I'll walk you to the Bourse first. As far as the kitchen gate. In the dark. Then I'll go." Oren looked at Remy. "And if he isn't back by the first bell after midnight, Mister Castellan, I'll come into that house and find him. Through the front door, if I have to. And I'll find you too."
Remy looked up at the big man for a long moment.
"I'd expect nothing less, Captain," he said.
