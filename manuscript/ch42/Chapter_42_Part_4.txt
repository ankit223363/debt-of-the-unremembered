Chapter 42: Remy's Ledger, Part 4 of 5: The Notice and the Face
The Notice and the Face
There was a great deal to do before the eighth bell, and most of it was not Corin's to do.
He lay on the pallet and watched it being done. That was the worst part. He had always been the one who did things. Who went down into the cold room and cleared the glasses, who climbed the roofs and crawled the sewers and went down the coal chute. Now he lay on his back with his leg out straight on its folded sacks and a line of ink across his thigh, and watched other people move round him in the red light of the fire, getting ready.
Mother Grue came back from the fifth arch first.
She came back with her poker and without her temper, and stood at the end of the pallet, small and brown in her shawls, and looked down at Remy, still sitting cross-legged on his folded coat with his hands on his knees.
"First light, you said," she said. "Gant. With six men."
"First light."
"Then we'll be gone by the sixth bell." She said it as if she were saying the bread would be ready by noon. "All of us. Every last one. There's forty-one in these arches tonight, counting the babies. They'll go to the old rope-walk on the Saltmarsh, where the Lascar Mary has a fire. They've gone there before. When the Watch comes, or the floods." She sniffed. "Gant can come down here with his six men and his book and find the cabbage pot. And he can have it. It's got a hole in it."
"And him?" said Lyra. She did not look round from her bucket. "Corin. He can't walk to the Saltmarsh. And we can't go with the others. Gant knows my face. Gideon's bitten. They'll look for us there first."
Mother Grue looked at her for a moment.
"No," she agreed. "You can't."
She looked at Corin.
"The doctor," she said. "Saint Bride's. The crypt under the chapel with no roof. Fifty-one beds, and the doctor, and the nurse. And a door at the bottom of the steps that's been bolted every night for twenty years, and the Watch has never once been through it." Her black eyes were very bright. "Nobody looks for anybody in a fever crypt. Nobody wants to go in. You'll be one more on a pallet. And she'll be one more nurse."
Corin looked at Lyra's back.
She did not turn round. But after a moment she nodded, once, into her hands.
"I'll send Nell to the doctor," said Mother Grue. "Now. He'll come at the dark, anyway. He said. He comes every dark." She looked at Corin's leg, under the blanket, and her mouth went thin. "And he'll find you gone."
She went away down the arch to find Nell.
Oren came over to the pallet then.
He had taken off Terrence's coat and was holding it over his arm. He knelt down on the floor beside Corin, slowly, the way a big man kneels, with his sword across his knees. And he felt inside the coat, in the inside pocket, and took out something. Not the oilcloth packet with Cressida's cream paper. Something else. A square of white paper, folded small. Very small. In half, and in half again.
"I tore this off a board," he said. "At the Fish Stairs. On the way back from the bridge." He held it out. "I don't know what it means. I thought you would."
Corin took it. He unfolded it on the blanket with his gray fingers.
Printed. Good paper. A seal pressed into it at the top. A round seal, a rim, an open hand, a small lamp.
NOTICE TO THE RIVER PARISHES.
He read it. All of it. The tonic for the River Fever will be given out FREE, as before, at the head of the Fish Stairs, on FRIDAY MORNING, at the eighth bell, by an officer of the Office in person. All children of the parish are to attend.
By order. A. Pallister. Under-Secretary.
He read it again.
Then he held it out to Lyra. She had turned round on her bucket. She took it and read it, and he watched her face as she read the name at the bottom, and saw her see the stairs at Crown Walk, and a silver box, and an old man with his bad leg out straight and his stick across his knees, doing coin tricks for Ro.
"In person," said Lyra. Her voice was very quiet. "All children of the parish are to attend."
"Friday," said Corin. "Eighth bell. In the open. At the head of the Fish Stairs." He took the notice back from her and folded it again, along its folds. Small. "He's never put up a notice before. Bet said. He just came. Every Friday. With his bag." He looked at the folded square in his fingers. "Now he's put it on every board on the river. Telling them all to come."
"Because Silas stopped the glasses," said Lyra.
"Because one of his vials is gone. And because somebody's been in his bag." Corin put the notice inside his coat, next to the Ledger. "He wants to see who comes. And who doesn't."
Nobody said anything.
Then, from the mouth of the arch, a voice said, "Saints, look at the state of you," and Vespera came in.
She came in shaking the snow off a huge old black cloak that was far too big for her, with a carpet bag in each hand and a third slung across her back on a strap, and stopped just inside the sacking, and looked round the arch with her sharp black eyes. A small, thin, gray-haired woman in a dress the color of ink, with her sleeves pushed up to the elbow. Her hands were stained. They were always stained. Black and blue and a faded red, in the creases of the knuckles and round the nails and in the lines of the palms, so deep that no washing would ever take it out. Forty years of ink.
She looked at Remy, sitting by the fire.
Remy looked back at her.
Neither of them said anything for a long moment.
"Mister Castellan," said Vespera at last.
"Madam Vespera."
"I saw you at the Lady's, once. Years ago. A boy in a corner with a pen." Her eyes went to his bare hands on his knees. To the brand. They stayed there. "She did that to you, then. I'd heard. I didn't believe it."
"She stopped, after me."
"So I heard. Because it was common." Vespera's mouth went very thin. She looked away from the brand, at Corin. "Nell found me in the sixth arch and said you were going back into that house tonight on that leg. I told her she was mad. She said she wasn't. She said I was to bring my bags." She set the carpet bags down on the floor by the pallet with a thump. "So here are my bags. And I still think she's mad. And so are you."
"Probably," said Corin.
"Give me your hands."
He gave her his hands.
She knelt by the pallet and unwrapped them, the rags and the lint and Hessa's neckcloth, very gently, and turned them over in the firelight, and looked at them for a long time without saying anything. The gray, going up past the wrists, past the elbows, under his sleeves. The burns on the knuckles where the Ash lamps had caught him, three times on Sunday night, where the paint had scraped off and the coal had not covered. Raw and red and weeping at the edges.
"You let it burn you," said Vespera quietly.
"I didn't have a choice."
"You never have a choice. That's what all of you say." She opened one of the carpet bags and began to take things out of it. Little pots. Brushes. A tin of grease. A roll of fine pale cloth. "I can't put the paint on raw skin. It won't hold. And it'll hurt like fire. I can put a salve on, and a skin over the salve. A false skin. Thin as an onion's." She looked at him. "It'll pass for hands in a dark kitchen. It won't pass for hands under a lamp. If you go near an Ash lamp tonight, it'll smoke, and so will you."
"There'll be no lamp," said Remy, from the fire.
Vespera looked at him.
"At the iron door," said Remy. "There's a lamp. Ash. In a brass house, like the others. I'm the one who trims the lamps on the north side of the ring. I've done it for two years. Tonight I'll trim that one so short it goes out by the ninth bell." A pause. "Nobody will notice. Nobody goes to the north side of the ring on an audit night. They're all in the counting room, on the south."
Vespera looked at him a while longer. Then she grunted, and went back to her pots.
"And his face?" she said. "What does he want to be?"
"A lamp boy," said Remy. "An old one. The Lady has three. They come and go by the kitchen stair all evening, with their oil and their wicks and their ladders. Nobody looks at a lamp boy. I've never once seen Gant look at a lamp boy." He glanced at Corin's leg. "And a lamp boy with a bad leg would be nothing strange. One of them has a wooden foot."
Vespera nodded slowly.
"A lamp boy, then," she said. "Old. Lame. Soot in the lines. Nobody's grandfather." She took Corin's chin in her stained fingers and turned his face to the firelight, the way she had in her shop in Hook Lane, in the autumn, and looked at it. At the mole under his left eye that she had tattooed into him on Friday, real and permanent, so that Lyra had only known him by his mouth. "We'll keep that," she said. "It suits you. And we'll give you a nose that's been broken twice, and a chin that doesn't quite meet the rest of you. And a squint."
"A squint?"
"People look at a squint," said Vespera, "and then they look away, ashamed of looking. And then they don't look again." She opened another pot. It smelled of tallow and soot. "Lie still. This'll take an hour."
Against the Doctor
Doctor Silas came at the dark, the way he always did.
Corin heard him before he saw him. The crunch of boots on the rubble slope outside. A cough. The sacking at the mouth of the arch pushed aside by a long thin hand in a gray woolen glove with the fingers cut off. And the tall stooped shape of him, in the long coat that was more mend than coat, with the battered hat and the cracked leather bag, coming in out of the snow and straightening up as far as the arch would let him.
He stopped.
Corin was standing.
He was standing by the fire, on his good leg and the ash crutch, with his bad leg held off the floor, bent a little at the knee, the boot just touching the brick. He had been standing for a quarter of a bell. Vespera had made him, so that she could see how the face looked in the light when he was upright. So that she could see how a lame old lamp boy would hold his head.
He did not look like Corin Vane any more. He knew that. Vespera had held up a cracked hand mirror for him, an hour ago, when she had finished, and he had looked into it, and a stranger had looked back. An old man. Sixty, perhaps. With a long face gone gray and loose, and a nose that had been broken at least twice and had set badly both times, and a chin that seemed to belong to somebody smaller. Soot in every line. Soot in the eyebrows, which were thick and white and wild. A mole under the left eye. And a squint. The left eye looked a little to the left, always, as if something over the stranger's shoulder had caught it.
Corin had looked into the mirror, and the stranger's mouth had gone down at the left corner. The way his did when he was thinking.
Lyra, behind him, had made a small sound. And then said nothing.
His hands were in a lamp boy's gloves now. Old leather ones, black, shiny with oil, with the fingers worn through at the tips. Under the gloves, Vespera's salve, and Vespera's false skin, thin as an onion's, and a little paint. Under that, the gray. He could feel it. The salve was cool. The false skin was tight, like a glove inside a glove. The burns underneath it throbbed.
Doctor Silas stood just inside the sacking and looked at him.
He looked at the face. Then he looked at the crutch. Then he looked, for a long time, at the bad leg, held off the floor, the boot just touching the brick.
Then he looked past Corin, at Lyra by the fire. At Oren by the wall. At Vespera, putting her pots away in a carpet bag. At Remy Castellan, standing now, by the end of the pallet, in his gray coat and his gray gloves, with his hat in his hand.
His eyes stopped on Remy. They stayed there.
"I see," said Doctor Silas.
He came down the arch.
He did not hurry. He stepped over the sleepers, the ones who were still there, who had not yet gone off in twos and threes into the dark toward the Saltmarsh and the old rope-walk where Lascar Mary had a fire. He came to the end of the pallet and set his bag down on it. He did not take off his hat. He did not take off his gloves.
"Sit down," he said to Corin.
Corin sat down. On the end of the pallet. Carefully. With the crutch.
Doctor Silas knelt in front of him and put out his hand and took hold of the hem of Corin's trouser leg, the left one. A lamp boy's trousers, from Vespera's bag, old and black and too short. He pushed it up. Slowly. To the knee. And then above it, as far as it would go, folding it over and over. And looked.
Nobody said anything.
The ink line was still there, across the inside of the thigh, a hand's width above the knee. Black. Clear. And under the line, below it, toward the knee, the red. The angry, shiny, swollen red, that he had drawn the line to mark.
And above the line, a hand's width, there was nothing. The skin was gray, with the Ash in it, and pale, and cold. No red.
Corin let out his breath.
Doctor Silas did not. He knelt there and looked at the line for a long time. He laid the back of his gloved hand against the skin above it, and below it, and above it again. Then he pressed, gently, with two fingers, just below the line, and Corin's whole body went rigid on the pallet and he heard himself make a sound through his teeth.
The doctor took his fingers away.
"It hasn't gone past," he said. "Not yet."
He rolled the trouser leg down again. Slowly. Fold by fold. And sat back on his heels on the cold brick and looked up at Corin's face. At the stranger's face. The broken nose and the squint and the soot.
"Mother Grue sent the girl," he said. "Nell. At the fourth bell. She said Gant's coming in the morning. She said you'll all be at Saint Bride's by the sixth bell." He paused. "She didn't say you'd be going into the Bourse first. On that leg. Tonight."
"I have to," said Corin.
"No," said Doctor Silas. "You don't. You want to. There's a difference. I've seen it in a great many men. Usually at the end."
Nobody said anything.
The doctor looked at the leg again. Not at the skin. At the shape of it, under the black trousers. At the way Corin was holding it, even sitting down. A little bent at the knee. Never straight. Never quite on the floor.
"I'll tell you what will happen," he said. "Since nobody else in this arch seems to want to." His voice was quite level. "The iron rod went in at the back of your thigh on Friday. It went through the big muscle there. The one that bends the knee. It cut half of it, Mister Vane. I felt it, on Saturday, when I opened the wound. I didn't tell you, because there was enough to tell you on Saturday." A pause. "The half that's left is holding your leg together. It's swollen, and it's sick, and it's got coal in it from Sunday that I couldn't all get out. It's trying very hard to mend."
He looked up at Corin.
"If you lie still for three days, it might mend. Not well. But it might. You'd limp. But you'd bend the knee. You'd go up a stair." He paused. "If you walk on it tonight, down a kitchen stair and along a stone passage and back again, on a crutch, with your weight going through it every step, it won't. It'll tear. I can't tell you how much. Maybe a little. Maybe all the way. But it'll tear, and it'll heal torn, and when it's healed it'll be short and hard and stiff, like a rope that's been knotted." His eyes did not move from Corin's face. "And you'll never bend that knee again. Not past a little. Not for the rest of your life. You'll walk like a man with a wooden leg. You'll go up stairs sideways. You'll never run again. Not once. Not from anything."
The arch was very quiet.
Corin sat on the end of the pallet and looked down at the doctor, kneeling on the brick in his long mended coat.
He thought about the rooftops. Juno, ahead of him, in the dark, with her brass key on its string, running along a ridge of slates as if it were a road. He thought about the Lantern Bridge, and the Cut, and the coal chute, and all the times in the last two weeks that he had run. From the Watch. From the gray coats. From Gant. From Kross, in the sewers, with the water to his waist.
He thought about the gray in his arms, past the elbows now. About Vespera saying she had only once seen a harvester with the gray past his neck.
He was not going to run very much longer anyway.
"I know," he said.
Doctor Silas looked at him for a long moment.
"And you're going."
"Yes."
"Why?"
Corin did not answer at once. He looked past the doctor. At Lyra, standing by the fire with her arms folded across her chest, very straight, with her wet face. At Remy, with his hat in his hand. At Gideon, sitting cross-legged on the floor by the pallet with his good hand inside his coat, waiting, the way he always waited.
"Because there's a jar on a shelf in that house," he said, "behind three doors. With an old man's last evening in it. Lyra's father. And the only way to it is through a passage that has to learn my face first." He paused. "And because if I don't go tonight, I can't go on Midwinter. And if I don't go on Midwinter, then everything that's been paid for it so far was paid for nothing." He looked down at the doctor. "Coker. Ned. Bramble. Old Jory. The fifty-two glasses. Gideon's cold. Lyra's mother."
He stopped.
"I don't want any of them to have been for nothing," he said. "That's all."
Doctor Silas did not say anything for a long time.
Then he took off his crooked spectacles. And folded them. And held them in his hand, the way he had held them on Monday, on his knees on this same brick floor, when Corin told him he was the best farmer they had.
"Ned," he said quietly.
"Yes."
The doctor looked at the spectacles in his hand. Then he put them back on. And got up off the floor, slowly, stiffly, with a hand on the end of the pallet.
He opened his cracked leather bag. He took out a roll of linen bandage, and a small brown bottle, and the long thin scissors.
"Then I'll bind it," he said. "Tight. So tight you'll curse me. From the hip to the ankle. So it's held straight, and the weight goes through the bone, not the muscle." He did not look at anyone. "It won't save the knee. Nothing will save the knee now, if you go. But it might save the rest. It might keep the tear small." He uncorked the bottle. "And you'll drink this. Two swallows. Not three. It tastes like a wet dog. It'll take the edge off. It won't take off much."
"Thank you," said Corin.
"Don't thank me." Doctor Silas knelt down again on the brick, with the bandage in his hands. "I'm not doing it for you. I'm doing it for Ned."
And he began to bind the leg.
