Chapter 42: Remy's Ledger, Part 2 of 5: Coker
Coker
"Tell me what they did to him," said Corin.
Remy looked at him.
"You don't want to know."
"I didn't ask what I want." Corin's voice came out of him harder than he meant. "I asked you to tell me. You were there. In the corner. Writing it down. You're always in the corner, writing it down. You said so."
Remy did not answer for a moment.
Then he looked down at his bare hands on his knees, and he began. In the flat, neat, reading voice.
"Seventh bell. The selling room. Present: Mr. Gant. Mistress Fane. Two of the Lady's men, to do the straps. R. Castellan, to record." He did not look up. "Coker came in quietly. He sat in the chair when he was told. He didn't need the straps. Fane did them up anyway. She always does. She likes them done up."
Nobody moved in the arch.
"Mr. Gant asked him what he saw on Sunday night. Coker said rats. Mr. Gant asked him again. Coker said rats, and a coal thief from Grue's, and Pike's fool dog. Mr. Gant asked him if he had slept on his stool between the half past eleven and the half past one. Coker said he always did. Every Sunday. Nine years. Everybody knew. Mr. Gant said yes, everybody did know, that was rather the point." Remy's voice did not change at all. "Mr. Gant asked Mistress Fane to put the glass on."
Lyra had put her hand over her mouth.
"She used a reading glass first," said Remy. "Not a drawing glass. Just to look. She held it to his temple and read Sunday night off him like a page. She's very good. She read out what she saw, and I wrote it down." He paused. "The Bell. Three pints and a hot pie. Walking home along the lane with his lantern. A girl, by the coal flap. Thin. Short hair, very short, badly cut. A man's flat cap. A gray coat too good for the Lower Docks. Leaning on a crutch. Lame in the left leg. Very pale. Said she was from Grue's. He gave her a penny."
Lyra did not move. Her face above her hand had gone the color of the snow.
"Then Pike, and the dog, and Big Gideon's boy coming up out of the chute," said Remy. "Coker tying up the boy's arm with his own muffler. Don't come round my flap again. And then the stool, and the newspaper over his face. And his eyes shut. And nothing. Fane said that part was very clear. He really did shut his eyes. He really didn't look. For a whole hour." Remy stopped. "He'd made up his mind not to know. And so he didn't. She couldn't find anything there to read. Only the dark under the newspaper, and the sound of the coal moving down the chute."
"Then that's good," said Lyra. Her voice shook. "Then he didn't see anything. He didn't see Corin. He doesn't know anything. They can let him go."
Remy looked at her.
"Miss Thorne," he said gently, "he let a coal thief go. He gave a lame girl from Grue's a penny on the Lady's wall and told her to get something hot. He shut his eyes on purpose, for an hour, on the night the Under-Secretary's bag was opened. On her door." He paused. "The Lady doesn't let people go for not knowing things. She lets them go when she's finished with them."
Nobody said anything.
"Mr. Gant asked Mistress Fane to change the glass," said Remy. "To a drawing glass. And to draw."
"Draw what?" said Corin. His mouth was dry.
"The Lady had told him what. Before. In her room, at the fifth bell. I was there." Remy's eyes were on his hands. "She said, He's soft. That's the trouble. He's been soft for nine years and I've let it go, because he keeps the door well enough. Take the soft out of him. All of it. I can sell it." A pause. "Kindnesses, small. Suitable for blending."
The fire hissed.
Corin lay on the pallet and could not breathe.
"It took two hours," said Remy. "Fane is careful. She went back and back. Not just the coal thief and the penny. All of it. Every time he'd let a boy off the flap. Every time he'd given a beggar his crust. Every time he'd looked the other way. Every time he'd been kind to anyone, for any reason, since he was a child." His voice had gone very quiet. "It came out in little bits. Yellow. Small. Like sparks off a fire. Into the glass. Fane changed the glass four times. I wrote down every change."
"And Coker?" said Lyra. A whisper.
Remy looked up at her.
"He sat in the chair," he said. "With the straps done up. And his eyes open. And he didn't make a sound. Not once. Not for two hours." He hesitated. "At the end, he looked round the room. At all of us. At me. And he said, Is that it, then? Quite ordinary. As if he'd been waiting at the Bell for his pie, and it had come."
He looked back down at his hands for a while, and did not say anything.
"They took the straps off. Mr. Gant gave him back his newspaper. Mr. Gant said, Back to your stool, Coker. Keep the door. And Coker said, Yes, Mr. Gant. And went back up the kitchen stair and out of the kitchen gate and along the lane to the coal flap. And sat down on his stool. And opened the newspaper." A pause. "He's there now. I walked past him on the way here. He looked up at me and said Good afternoon, Mister Castellan and went back to his paper. Very polite."
"Then he's all right," said Lyra. "He's—he's still—"
"At noon," said Remy, "a boy from the arches came along the lane with a sack, looking for coal round the flap. A little one. Seven, perhaps. One of Grue's." He did not look at Mother Grue. "Coker put down his paper and got up off his stool and took the boy by the collar and threw him in the gutter. And kicked him twice. And went back to his stool. And sat down. And picked up his paper again."
The arch was utterly silent.
"He didn't seem angry," said Remy. "He didn't seem anything. He did it the way you'd move a bucket. Mr. Gant was very pleased. He said Coker would keep the door much better now."
Mother Grue got up off her box.
She did it slowly. She was very old. She stood up in her many shawls, small and brown, with her poker in her fist, and looked at Remy with her bright black eyes. And for a moment Corin thought she was going to hit him with it.
She did not.
"Which boy?" she said.
"I don't know his name. Small. Fair. A cough."
"Peter," said Mother Grue. "That's Peter. He came back at the first bell after noon with a cut lip and said he fell." Her voice was very flat. "He said he fell."
She stood there a moment longer. Then she turned round and went away down the long dark arch, between the sleepers, with her poker, toward the doorway into the fifth. And did not come back.
Corin lay on the pallet and looked at the crack in the roof.
He gave me a penny, Lyra had said. He told me to get something hot.
And he, Corin, had climbed up a coal chute on his elbows and lain on the frozen cobbles beside the man's stool, and looked at him asleep with the newspaper over his face, and wanted to say thank you. And not said it. Because Coker was asleep. Because Coker had said so.
He had not said thank you. And now there was nobody left in that man to say it to.
He felt Lyra's hand on his shoulder. He had not heard her come across from the fire. She was standing by the pallet with her hand on him, and her face was wet, and she was not looking at him. She was looking at Remy.
"It's our fault," she said. "Isn't it. It's because of us. Because I took the crutch. Because he gave me a penny."
Remy looked up at her.
"No, Miss Thorne," he said. "It's because of her. Everything in that house is because of her." He paused. "But yes. It's because of you too."
Nobody said anything to that.
There was nothing to say.
Corin reached up, with his gray rag-wrapped hand, and found the Ledger inside his coat. He took it out. He opened it, without looking, at the page near the front where the edges were soft and gray. THOSE I OWE. And he held out his other hand to Lyra for the pencil.
She gave it to him.
He wrote it at the bottom of the list, in large shaking letters, going downhill.
Coker. The coal flap, Cinder Lane. He shut his eyes for us. They took all his kindness for it. Every bit, since he was a child. He kicked a boy called Peter. It was not him that did it.
And under that, after a long time:
I never said thank you.
The Book in the Lining
It was Oren who brought it up. Corin had forgotten it. He had forgotten everything except the page in front of him and the word kindness going round and round in his head like a moth round a lamp.
"The book," said Oren.
He had not moved from his place inside the sacking. He stood there with his hand on his sword and his eyes on Remy, the way he had stood the whole time. Corin did not think he had blinked.
Remy looked round at him.
"The one sewn into your coat," said Oren. "In the lining. Under the left arm. I gave it back to you because you asked me to, and because I'd felt it and it was only paper. But you've got your hand near it now." A pause. "Your hand's on your knee, but it's on the side of the knee next to your coat. A finger's width from it. It's been creeping there for the last quarter bell."
Remy looked down at his left hand.
It was on his knee. But Oren was right. It had moved. Not much. A little way down the side of the knee, toward the floor, where the gray coat lay folded under him. Toward the left side of the coat. Where, under the lining, a small fat book lay against the cloth.
Remy looked at it for a moment as if it belonged to somebody else.
Then he lifted the hand, very slowly, and put it back flat on the top of his knee. And looked at Oren.
"You're very good," he said.
"I've stood at a gate for twenty-two years," said Oren. "Hands creep. Everybody's hands do. Yours creep toward that book the way a man's creep toward his purse in a crowd." He did not take his eyes off the young man. "What is it?"
Remy did not answer at once.
He looked at Corin. Then at Lyra, standing by the pallet with her wet face. Then at the long dark arch, full of sleepers, and the doorway into the fifth arch where Mother Grue had gone with her poker.
"It's the reason I came," he said quietly. "The real reason. Under the other reasons." He paused. "May I take it out? Slowly? Captain Vance may hold my wrist while I do it, if he likes."
Corin looked at Oren. Oren looked at him.
"Slowly," said Corin.
Oren came across the arch. He did not hold Remy's wrist. He stood over him, very close, with his sword out of its scabbard by a hand's width, so that the firelight ran along the blade. And Remy, very slowly, turned up the left side of the gray coat where it lay folded on the floor, and felt along the lining with two fingers, and found a place under the arm where the stitching was different. Smaller. Neater. He pulled a thread, and the stitching came open, a hand's width. And he slid out the book.
He held it out to Corin. On the flat of his palm. The way he had once held out a blue vial, in Vivienne's glass room, in the autumn.
Corin took it.
It was small. Smaller than the Ledger. Thicker. Bound in plain black leather, very soft and very old, gone shiny at the corners with handling. No lettering on the front. No lettering on the spine. A plain black book, the kind a clerk might buy for his own use from a stationer's on Quill Row, to keep his private accounts in. It was warm from being next to Remy's body. It smelled faintly of ink, and of the young man's coat, which smelled of nothing at all.
Corin opened it.
The pages were thin. Very thin. The kind of paper that is made for bibles and law books, so that a great many pages will go into a small space. And they were full. Every one. Both sides. From edge to edge, from top to bottom, in the same small neat even hand as the card, in black ink. Columns. Lines. Names and dates and figures.
He turned to the first page.
At the top, on its own, in the same hand but younger, a little larger, a little less sure:
What the Lady took. Kept by R. C., from the year he was ten, so that somebody will know.
And under it, columns. Ruled by hand, with a pencil, in faint gray lines. Six of them. The same six columns as the big canvas ledger in the counting room under the Bourse. Corin knew them. NAME. WHERE FROM. TAKEN. HOW MUCH. FOR WHOM. PAID.
But there was a seventh. Added on the right. Ruled in ink, not pencil, as if the boy who made it had wanted it to be harder to rub out.
AFTER.
Corin read the first line.
Jemmy Ott. Cinder Row. His mother's face. All. For the Lady's own glass. Paid: 2 crowns, to his father, for drink. AFTER: Drowned himself in the Mill Cut, the spring after. Aged 11.
He read the second.
Wife of Abel Strand, the cooper. The Hill, back stair. Her singing voice. All. Sold to the Duchess of Ashcombe, for her daughter, who could not sing. Paid: the cooper's debt to the Lady, 40 crowns. AFTER: She works in the laundry at the Ashcombe house now. She washes the daughter's sheets. The daughter sings at dinners. She does not know whose voice it is.
He turned the page.
And the next. And the next.
It went on. Name after name. Year after year. In the boy's hand, and then the young man's, getting smaller and neater and more even as it went, as if the hand had learned to make itself as small and quiet as it could. Childhoods. First loves. A man's skill with a fiddle. A woman's memory of her drowned son, sold to a widow on the Hill who had never had a child and wanted to know what it felt like to lose one. Bulk lots, from the river parishes, by the crate. Kindnesses, small. Courage, common. Cold, for the North.
And always, at the end of the line, in the seventh column, in ink, that word. AFTER. And what had happened.
Went mad. Lives under the Lantern Bridge.
Recovered. Married. Does not remember her first husband. Is happy.
Unknown. Moved away.
Died of the fever, the same winter.
Still on the back stair of the Lady's house. Sits there every day. Does not want anything.
Corin stopped at that one.
He read it again. He looked at the name at the start of the line.
Gideon. No other name known. Cinder Lane. Son of Big Gideon Hollow. His cold, his fear, his sadness, his gladness. All. In parts. Over four winters. H series, 291 to 340. Cold to the North, by the Nym boat. The rest blended. Paid: a bed in the Bell cellar and two meals a day. AFTER: See above.
He looked up.
Gideon was sitting cross-legged at the foot of the pallet with his good hand inside his coat. Looking at the wall, the way he did. Not at the book. He had not asked to see it. He did not seem to know that his name was in it.
Corin looked down again. At the line above.
Big Gideon Hollow, and Saul Hollow, brothers. Lower Docks. Dockers. Carried for the Bourse four years, by agreement. All, by agreement, in the fifth year, to pay their debt and the boy's. Everything but the carrying. Sold in parts. AFTER: Porters. The bench by the lift. Saul still hums.
He closed his eyes for a moment.
Then he opened them, and turned the pages forward. Fast now. Not reading. Looking for something. He did not know what. He only knew that it was there, somewhere, near the end, because a boy of ten who had started to write down what the Lady took, so that somebody would know, would not have stopped at the people he did not love.
He found it four pages from the end.
A Small Drawn Flower
It was the only line in the whole book that had been crossed out.
Corin knew that at once, the way you know a single wrong note in a song you have heard a thousand times. He had turned perhaps two hundred pages, fast, with his gray thumb, and every line on every page had been the same. Neat. Even. Black. Finished. Nothing rubbed out. Nothing changed. A boy, and then a young man, who wrote things down once and did not take them back.
And here, four pages from the end, a line had been crossed out.
It had been done very carefully. Not scribbled. Not blotted. A single straight line, ruled, through the middle of the writing from one side of the page to the other, in black ink, so that every word under it could still be read. The way a clerk crosses out an account that has been closed. Paid in full. Nothing more owing.
Corin read it through the line.
Tam Hollin. The Hill, footman. His cold. All, by order. For the Lady's own glass. Paid: nothing. AFTER: Died at Mother Grue's, under the Dye-Works, the same night. A harvester sat with him. Given to the river at the turn of the tide, with an orange.
And at the end of the line, in the margin, after the word orange, there was something else.
Not a word.
A flower.
A very small flower, drawn in ink. No bigger than Corin's smallest fingernail. Five round petals, drawn with great care, each one exactly the same as the others, and a dot in the middle. And a stem, curving down, with one small leaf on it. The kind of flower a child draws. The kind that grows in cracks in the stone along the river in summer, white and yellow, and is trodden on, and grows again.
It was the only drawing in the whole book.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
Then he looked up at the young man sitting cross-legged on his folded coat by the fire, with his bare hands flat on his knees.
Remy was not looking at him. He was looking at the fire. His face in the red light was quite still. Clean and narrow and pale and still. But the hand on his left knee, the branded one, had closed. Not into a fist. Just closed. The fingers curled in against the palm, the way a sleeping child's hand closes on a blanket.
"He liked them," said Remy, to the fire.
Nobody said anything.
"Those flowers. The little white ones. On the river wall by the Fish Stairs. He used to pick them in the summer on his way up the Hill to the house. One. Just one. And put it in his buttonhole, under his livery collar, where the Lady couldn't see it." Remy's voice was very quiet. "He said it was so that there'd be one thing in that house that she didn't own. I told him that was stupid. That she owned the buttonhole. And he laughed, and said, Then she owns the hole, Remy. Not the flower."
The fire hissed.
"I didn't know what to draw," said Remy. "When I crossed him out. I'd never drawn anything before. I wanted to put something. At the end of the line. So it wouldn't be just a line." He was silent for a moment. "It took me a long time. I did it over and over, on another piece of paper, before I did it there. I wanted it to be right."
Corin looked down at the small ink flower in the margin.
Five petals, each exactly the same. A dot in the middle. A curved stem. One leaf.
"It's right," he said.
Remy did not answer. But the hand on his knee opened again, slowly, and lay flat.
Corin closed the book.
He held it in both his gray hands for a moment. It was warm still. Small and fat and soft. Two hundred pages of names. Sixteen years. Every one written down, so that somebody would know.
"Why did you cross him out?" he said. "Only him?"
Remy turned his head from the fire and looked at him.
"Because he's the only one where it's finished," he said. "The others are still going on. Still being taken. Still under the Lantern Bridge, or in the laundry, or on the back stair, or on the bench by the lift. The after isn't over for any of them." He looked at the book in Corin's hands. "For Tam it's over. He went to the river. With an orange. And a harvester sat with him. Nobody ever sat with anybody else in that book."
He was quiet for a moment.
"So I closed his account," he said. "Paid in full. By you."
The arch was very still.
Corin did not know what to say. He looked down at the closed black book in his hands. Then he looked at Lyra, beside the pallet. She was looking at Remy. Her face had changed. It had not gone soft. Lyra's face did not go soft, Corin had learned. But something had gone out of it. The cold, perhaps. Some of the cold.
"Why show us?" she said.
Remy looked up at her.
"Because you'll want to know whether to trust me," he said. "And I can't tell you. Anything I say, you'll think it might be her saying it through me. That's what I'd think, if I were you." He looked at the book. "But that isn't her. Nobody's ever seen that but me. Not her. Not Gant. Not Tam. If she found it, she'd put me in the chair in the selling room and have Fane draw out everything I've ever written, one page at a time. And then she'd burn the book in front of me, and I wouldn't know what it was." He paused. "So now you have something of mine that could put me in the chair. That's the only thing I've got to give you, that you'll believe."
Nobody spoke.
Corin opened the book again. Not to Tam's page. Forward. To the last pages. To the newest lines, written in the last few weeks, in the smallest and neatest hand of all.
He did not know what made him do it. Perhaps the same thing that had made him turn the pages fast before. Looking for something without knowing what.
The last page but one.
He read down it. Names from the river parishes. Kindnesses, small. A sailor's courage, sold to a banker's son. Bet's brother. Ned, the Fish Stairs. Glass 31, Saint Bride's. AFTER: Forgot how to swallow. Monday. He had to stop on that one for a moment.
Then, near the bottom, a line that was different from the others.
Not a name. A number.
Lot 41.
