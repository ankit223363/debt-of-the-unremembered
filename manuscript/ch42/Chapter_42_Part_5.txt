Chapter 42: Remy's Ledger, Part 5 of 5: By Agreement
By Agreement
It took the doctor most of a quarter bell to bind it, and Corin did curse him, as he had been told he would. Quietly, through his teeth, in words he did not know he knew. Silas did not seem to hear. He wound the linen round and round, from the hip to the ankle, pulling each turn so tight that the leg went numb under it, and then went past numb into a dull deep ache like a tooth, and then went numb again.
While he did it, Gideon got up off the floor.
Corin saw him go. He did not go far. He went round the end of the pallet, past the fire, to where Remy Castellan was standing with his hat in his hand, waiting, a little apart from everyone, by the wall. And stood in front of him. Looking up. The way he had looked up at Saul Hollow from the foot of the three shallow steps.
Remy looked down at him.
"The book," said Gideon.
Remy did not say anything for a moment. Then he looked at Lyra, on her bucket by the fire, with his black book on her knee. Lyra looked back at him. Then she held it out.
Remy took it. He did not open it at once.
"You want me to read you what's in it," he said. "About you."
"About my da," said Gideon. "And Saul. Mister Vane looked at it for a long time. I saw him. His face went the way it goes." He considered. "You can read me the part about me too, if you like. I don't mind. But I want the part about them."
Remy looked at him for a long moment.
Then he sat down. Not on his folded coat this time. On the floor, on the bare brick, with his back against the wall of the arch, so that his face was level with the boy's. And Gideon sat down cross-legged in front of him. And Remy opened the book, and found the page without having to look for it, and held it a little toward the firelight.
Corin watched them over the doctor's bent back, through the haze of the bitter medicine.
"Big Gideon Hollow," read Remy, in his quiet clear even voice, "and Saul Hollow, brothers. Lower Docks. Dockers. Carried for the Bourse four years, by agreement."
Gideon listened, with his head a little on one side.
"All, by agreement, in the fifth year," read Remy, "to pay their debt and the boy's."
He stopped.
"The boy's," said Gideon.
"Yes."
"That's me."
"Yes."
Gideon thought about this.
"I had a debt?"
Remy looked at the page. Then he looked at the boy. And Corin saw him decide something. The way he had seen Oren decide, and Silas, and Lyra. The way you see a man stand at the top of a stair and decide whether to go down it.
"I was there," said Remy. "In the corner. Writing. I was fifteen. You were eight." He did not look at the book now. He looked at Gideon. "The Lady had put you on the glass that winter. The first winter. For your cold. In parts. Every morning and every night. The numbers you know. Two hundred and ninety-one. Two hundred and ninety-two." A pause. "You'd come to the Bell with your da and your uncle, because they were carrying, and there was nowhere else for you. And the Lady saw you on the back stair. And she said you had a very good cold. Very clean. Very young. The North would pay for it."
Gideon said nothing.
"Your da owed the Lady already," said Remy. "From before. From the docks. Everybody on the docks owes the Lady something. And she said the boy's keep was a debt too. His bed in the cellar. His bread. And his cold would pay it off, a little at a time. Over a few winters. And then he'd be square." His voice was very level. "And your da said no."
The fire hissed.
"He came up the lift to the glass room," said Remy. "Big Gideon Hollow. In his docker's coat, with his cap in his hands. I'd never seen him up there before. Nobody came up there who wasn't sent for. He stood on the Lady's rug in his wet boots and said no. He said, Not the boy. Take it out of me."
Gideon did not move.
"The Lady laughed," said Remy. "She said a docker's cold wasn't worth a farthing to anybody. Too old. Too dirty. Too full of drink and fighting. She said there was nothing in him she wanted." He paused. "And your da stood there. And then Saul came up the lift behind him. Without being sent for either. And stood next to him. And they both stood there, on the rug, in their wet boots. And your da said, Then take everything. All of it. Both of us. Everything but the carrying, so we can still work. And let the boy go."
The arch was very quiet.
Even Doctor Silas had stopped binding. He knelt on the brick with the roll of linen in his hands and did not move.
"And she thought about it," said Remy. "For a long time. And then she said yes. By agreement. I wrote it down. Those were her words. She said two strong men who couldn't remember anything but how to carry would be very useful in the cellars. More use than a boy. And she'd sell the rest. In parts." He looked down at the book. "So they sat in the chair in the selling room. One after the other. Your da first. It took three days. And then Saul. And when it was done, they went down to the bench by the lift. And they've been there ever since."
Gideon sat cross-legged on the brick floor and looked at him.
"And me?" he said.
Remy did not answer at once.
"She went on," he said at last. Very quietly. "With you. The next winter. And the next. And the next. Not just the cold. The fear, and the sadness, and the gladness. A little at a time. Four winters." He closed the book. "Because once they'd sat in the chair, there was nobody left who could come up the lift and say no. They didn't remember they had a boy. They didn't remember they'd made an agreement. They carried crates past you on the back stair every day and didn't know you." He paused. "She kept her word for one week. I wrote that down too."
Nobody said anything.
Gideon sat very still.
Corin watched his face. The thin white face, with the pale calm eyes, in the firelight. He watched it the way he had watched it behind the crates, on Sunday night, when the boy had held his father's apron. Waiting for something. For anything.
Nothing came into it.
"Read it again," said Gideon.
Remy looked at him.
"The part where he said it," said Gideon. "Not the boy. Read me that part again."
And Remy opened the book again, and found the place, and read it again. Slowly. In his quiet clear even voice. Not the boy. Take it out of me.
Gideon listened.
"Again," he said.
Remy read it again.
And again, when he was asked. And again. Five times. Six. In the red light, by the wall of the arch, with the snow coming down outside and the doctor kneeling motionless on the brick with the linen in his hands. Not the boy. Take it out of me.
At the seventh time, Gideon put up his good hand. Not to stop him. Just put it up. And laid it flat on the open page of the book, over the words, the way you lay your hand on a warm stone.
And left it there.
"I can't feel it," he said. "That he did that. I know he did. I can hear you say it. But I can't feel anything." He looked at his own hand on the page. "I wanted to see if I could. If I heard it enough times."
"I know," said Remy.
"I'll try again," said Gideon. "Another day. When I've got more of my cold back." He took his hand off the page. "Mister Vane says one's enough to start with."
He got up off the floor, and went back round the end of the pallet, and sat down cross-legged where he had been sitting before, at Corin's feet. And put his good hand inside his coat, over the jar.
Remy sat against the wall with the book shut in his lap, and did not move for a long time.
And Doctor Silas, kneeling on the brick, took a long breath, and let it out, and went back to binding the leg. Round and round. Tight. So tight that Corin felt it in his teeth. And did not curse him any more.
The Kitchen Stair
They went at the seventh bell, in the dark, in the snow.
Four of them. Oren in front, in Terrence's coat with the fur collar up. Remy beside him, in his gray. Then Corin, on the ash crutch, with his bad leg bound from hip to ankle so tight that it was one stiff log, and swung it from the hip at every step, the way Doctor Silas had shown him, so that the weight went through the bone. And Gideon at his side, with his arm in its sling and his good hand on Corin's belt, as if it belonged there.
Lyra did not come to the mouth of the arch to watch them go.
She had said goodbye in the arch, by the fire, with her face set. She had not touched him. She had looked at the stranger's face Vespera had made, the broken nose and the squint and the soot, for a long moment. And then she had reached up and put one finger on the corner of his mouth. The left corner. Where it went down.
"There you are," she had said, very low.
And turned away, and gone back to her bucket, with the Ledger against her heart, and Remy's black book on her knee. And not looked round.
The way to the Bourse was long, and Corin did not remember most of it. He remembered the cold. He remembered the snow, coming down again, soft and steady, out of a sky the color of an old bruise. He remembered the river, black and quick under the bridges, and the fires of the people who lived there, red in the dark. He remembered the doctor's bitter medicine, warm in his stomach and slow in his head, so that everything seemed to happen a little way off, behind glass. And he remembered the leg.
Every step. Swing from the hip. The crutch. The good foot. The stiff log of the bad leg, coming round, coming down. And every step, a pulling at the back of the thigh, deep inside, where the half muscle was. Not pain, exactly. Not yet. A feeling like a rope being stretched over the edge of a stone. A little. And a little more.
He counted the steps. To nine. And then again.
They came up into Cinder Lane from the river end, past the lamp in its bracket, at a little before the eighth bell.
Coker was on his stool.
Corin saw him from the corner. The coal flap in the wall below the kitchen windows, with the snow on it. And beside it, on the low wooden stool, a big man in a long coat with a muffler round his neck and a cap pulled low, with a newspaper open on his knees, and a lantern on the cobbles at his feet. Reading.
He looked up as they came along the lane.
He looked at Oren, a big old man in a shabby coat. At Remy, in his gray. He nodded to Remy, very politely, and said, "Evening, Mister Castellan," in a voice like a man commenting on the weather. He looked at the boy with his arm in a sling, and his eyes went over Gideon's face without stopping, the way they would go over a lamppost. And he looked at the old lamp boy on the crutch, with the squint and the broken nose and the soot.
And went back to his newspaper.
Corin went past him at a hand's breadth. The way he had gone past the Hollow brothers on Sunday night. He did not look at the man's face. He looked at the newspaper. At the big red hands holding it. Quite still. Quite steady.
Thank you, he thought, as hard as he could, at the bent head under the cap. Thank you. Thank you.
Nothing moved in Coker at all.
They went on, to the end of the lane, to the corner of the Bourse, where the great green dome went up into the snow. Round into the back street. And there, under the high arched gate with the lamp burning over it, was the kitchen gate.
Oren stopped in the shadow of the wall.
"Here," he said.
He looked at Remy. Then at Corin. Then down at Gideon. He did not say anything. He put his big scarred hand on Corin's shoulder, once, and gripped it, hard. The way a soldier grips another soldier's shoulder before a line goes forward. Then he let go.
"The first bell after midnight," he said to Remy.
"The first bell after midnight," said Remy.
Oren turned and went away up the back street toward the Bridewell, in the snow, with his collar up. Corin watched him go until he turned the corner. He did not look back. Guards never did.
Remy opened the kitchen gate.
There was a lamp beside it. Inside, on the wall of the yard, by the kitchen door. A brass house, small and square, with glass on four sides, on an iron bracket. Corin saw it as the gate opened, and his whole body went stiff on the crutch.
It was dark.
"Big nights only," said Remy, very low, without looking round. "I told you. Tonight is not a big night. Tonight is only the Bank."
They crossed the yard. It was small and square and walled, and full of barrels and crates and the black humps of things under canvas with the snow on them. A smell of cabbage and ashes. A cat on a wall, watching. And the kitchen door, open a little, with yellow light and noise and heat pouring out of it into the snow.
Remy went in. Gideon went in. Corin went in after them, swinging the stiff leg over the step.
The heat hit him like a wall.
It was a great kitchen. Bigger than any room Corin had ever been in that was not a church or the intake hall of the Bank. Long and high and white-tiled, with a row of black iron ranges down one side, three of them, roaring, and a row of tall windows down the other, misted gray with steam. Copper pans on hooks along the walls, a hundred of them, from the size of a thimble to the size of a bath, all polished so bright that the firelight ran over them like water. A great scrubbed table down the middle, as long as a barge, covered in flour and dough and chopped onions and the carcasses of birds. And people. Everywhere. Cooks in white, scullions in brown, girls with trays, boys with buckets, all moving at once, all shouting, in the steam and the heat and the noise, like bees in a hive that has been kicked.
Nobody looked at them.
Remy walked down the side of the kitchen, along the wall, under the copper pans, the way a man walks down a street he has walked every day of his life. Gideon walked beside him. Corin came after, swinging the leg, with the crutch knocking on the flagstones. Past the ranges, with their heat on his face. Past a girl peeling potatoes into a bucket, who glanced up at the old lamp boy and glanced away. Past a boy turning a spit with three geese on it, who did not glance up at all.
At the end of the kitchen, by a door, there was a woman.
She was standing at the last range, with her back to it, and her hands on her hips. A huge woman. As wide as the door. In a white apron that would have made a sail for the Patient Grace, over a black dress, with her sleeves rolled up over arms like hams, and her red face shining with sweat under a white cap. She was watching the kitchen the way a captain watches a deck. Her small blue eyes went everywhere.
They came to Remy, and stopped.
Then they went to Gideon. And stopped again. To the boy's thin white face, and the sling, and the black darning showing through the bandage. And something in the big red face changed.
Then they went to the old lamp boy on the crutch.
Corin did not look at her. He looked at the floor. At the flagstones, gray and worn, with flour on them.
"Mrs. Pratt," said Remy quietly.
"Mister Castellan." Her voice was deep and hoarse, a voice that had been shouting over ranges for thirty years. She was still looking at the lamp boy. "You're early."
"The lamps on the north side. Before the Bank comes."
"Ah," said Mrs. Pratt. "The lamps."
She looked at Corin for a long moment more. Then at Gideon. Then she turned round to the warming oven set into the wall beside the last range, a black iron door with a brass handle, and opened it, and took something out, and wrapped it in a napkin, and held it out. Not to Remy. To Gideon.
A wedge of pie. Steak and kidney. Steaming.
Gideon looked at it. Then he took it in his good hand.
"Thank you," he said.
"Go on," said Mrs. Pratt. Her voice had gone very rough. "Go on, the lot of you. The lamps won't trim themselves." And she turned her back on them, and went back to watching her kitchen, with her hands on her hips.
Remy opened the door at the end.
Behind it, a stair. Narrow and stone, going down, steep, into the dark. The kitchen stair. With a smell coming up it of cold stone and old fat and onions. And under the smell, faint, so faint that Corin felt it in his teeth before he heard it, a hum.
Remy took a lantern from a hook beside the door, and lit it from a spill at the range, and went down.
Gideon went down after him, eating his pie.
Corin stood at the top of the stair and looked down at it.
It was steep. Very steep. Every step a hand's breadth higher than it should have been, and worn into a hollow in the middle. It turned, a little way down, to the left, and went on out of sight. There was a rope along the wall, on iron rings, for a handrail. He could not bend his knee. He could not put his weight on the bad leg. He would have to go down it the way he had gone down two nines and four. Sitting. One at a time.
He sat down on the top step, with the crutch across his lap and the stiff leg out in front of him, and began.
One.
Two.
He was at the turn of the stair, the ninth step, the edge, when he heard the voices coming down behind him.
From the top of the stair. From the kitchen door. A man's voice, soft and mild, that carried a long way in a stone stairwell. A voice Corin knew. He had heard it at the Thorne gate, and in the glass room, and in a street on a cold morning in the autumn, saying the lady sends her regards.
"This way, Auditor. Mind the step. It's quicker than the front stair, and warmer. The counting room is along the passage at the bottom, round to the south." A small soft laugh. "I always think the Bank should see the kitchen. It's the only honest room in the house."
Gant.
And another voice. A woman's. Quiet. Clear. Cool, like water from a deep well. Not answering him. Only saying, to someone else, someone behind her:
"Bring the green ledger first, please, Mister Harrow. And mind the ink."
And boots on the stair. Coming down.
Corin sat on the ninth step of the kitchen stair, at the turn, with his leg out stiff in front of him across the narrow stone, so that nobody could pass him. With the crutch across his lap. And the rope at his shoulder. And the dark below him, where Remy's lantern had gone round the turn and out of sight.
And he could not get up.
And he knew that voice.
