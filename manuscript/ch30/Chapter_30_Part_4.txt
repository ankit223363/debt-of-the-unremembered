Chapter 30: Down Into the Dark, Part 4 of 5: Three Notes
Three Notes
They hid that afternoon in the ribs of a dead ship.
It lay on the mud at the edge of the Saltmarsh, two miles below the city, where the river began to go wide and flat and brown and the banks turned from stone wharves to reeds. It had been a big ship once. A grain ship, Wat said, in one of his rare short sentences, that had caught fire at her moorings forty years ago and burned to the waterline and been towed down here and left. Now there was nothing left of her but her bones. Great curved black timbers, rising out of the mud in two long rows like the ribs of a whale, with the gray sky showing between them. And at one end, the stern, there was still a bit of deck, and under the deck a sort of cave, dark and dry and smelling of old tar and salt, where the rain could not get in.
Wat rowed the boat in under the ribs at high water and tied it to a timber. And they climbed up into the cave under the deck, and sat down, and for a while nobody said anything at all.
Juno made a fire.
Corin did not know how. Everything was wet. But she went out along the ribs in the rain and came back with an armful of reeds and some bits of old dry wood from deep inside the wreck, and a little tin box from inside her grandfather's coat with a flint and steel in it, and she knelt over them for a long time, striking and blowing. And then there was a small red light. And then a flame. And then a fire. Small and smoky and crackling, on a flat stone at the edge of the cave. The smoke went out between the deck boards. The warmth came in.
Corin sat as close to it as he could get without being in it.
He had taken off his coat. It lay beside him on the boards, black and steaming. He had taken off his boots, and poured the sewer water out of them, and set them by the fire. He was wrapped in the horse blanket. Under the blanket, he had the Ledger in his lap, still in Mrs. Marta's oilcloth. He had not unwrapped it. He was afraid to. Afraid that the water had got in. Afraid that the first page would be a gray smear.
He ate the pie.
All of it. He did not mean to. He meant to share it. He broke off a piece for Juno and a piece for Wat, and they both shook their heads, and he did not argue. It was mutton, and potato, and onion, and the pastry was thick and soft and a little burned at one edge, because Moll's oven was not Mrs. Marta's oven. It was the best thing he had ever eaten. He knew that he would remember it. He made himself remember it. Every mouthful. He put it away in one of the new rooms inside him, carefully, the way you put a coin in a box.
When he had finished, he unwrapped the Ledger.
His hands were shaking. Juno watched him. Wat watched the river.
The oilcloth came away. The Ledger was inside it. Black leather cover, damp at the corners, soft and dark where the water had got at the edges. He opened it.
The first page was there.
The ink had run a little at the bottom, where the damp had crept in. The last line was blurred, the letters swollen and soft. But he could read it. He could read all of it.
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin.
You were born in Oakhaven.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
You used to fear the dark.
He read it twice. Then he closed the book and held it against his chest, under the blanket, and shut his eyes for a moment.
When he opened them, Juno was looking at the pouch on his belt.
He had taken the belt off with his coat. It lay on the boards by the fire. The little leather pouch was still buttoned. Still damp. And the fire was making the leather steam.
"What's in that?" said Juno.
"Stones."
"What kind of stones?"
Corin did not answer. He looked at the pouch. And then he said, without turning his head:
"We're on the river."
Nothing.
"Malakor. We're on the river. You said."
Juno's eyes went wide. She looked at the rod, lying on the boards beside the coat. She had never heard him talk to it out loud before. Nobody had.
The voice came, at last. Very low. Very tired.
So we are.
Corin waited.
I don't know what U.W. means, said Malakor. I want to say that first. I never knew. Nobody I knew ever knew. It was what the clerks wrote on the boxes. Two letters, in black wax, and a number. Some of them said it meant unwanted. Some said unwritten. One old man in the Archive told me it meant unwilling, and laughed, and wouldn't say why.
"What was in the boxes?"
Stones like those. Hundreds of them. Little black wax balls, the size of cherries.
"What's in the stones?"
A long pause.
People, said Malakor.
The fire cracked. Out on the mud, a bird called. A long, thin, lonely cry.
Not the way a jar holds a man, the voice went on. Not one memory. Not one evening, or one face. When they closed a village, in the war—when the Ministry called in an account—they took everything. Every memory of every person. All at once. And most of it burned. Most of it went into the Bank, or the Ministry, or the guns, or the walls. But some of it wouldn't burn. Some of it was too… He stopped. Too together. When a whole village remembers the same thing. The same song. The same tree. The same morning. All at the same time. It doesn't come apart. It sticks. Like wet sand. And you can't burn it, and you can't sell it, and you can't throw it away. So they sealed it in wax. And marked it. And put it in a box. And forgot about it.
Corin looked at the pouch.
"Three," he said. "Pell had three."
Yes.
"From where?"
I don't know. There's no number on them. Somebody took them out of a box, a long time ago, and cut the numbers off, and sewed them up in black cloth, and hid them. And Pell Dorran found them, or was given them, and hid them again. And died.
"And you saw someone open one."
A very long silence.
Yes, said Malakor. In the war. A mage. A good one. Better than me. He was curious. He wanted to know what was in them, and he was strong, and he thought he could hold it. So he cut one open and put his hand on it. The voice went very quiet. He sat down on the floor. He was smiling. He was listening. He said he could hear them singing. A whole village. All their voices, all at once, every one of them remembering the same summer evening. He said it was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard. He didn't get up. We tried to make him. We shouted in his ear. We pulled him by the arms. He didn't hear us. He sat on the floor with his hand on the stone and listened, and smiled, and didn't eat, and didn't drink, and after four days he died. Still smiling.
The fire cracked again.
Juno had pulled her knees up to her chin inside the huge coat. She was staring at the pouch as if it were a snake.
That's why I'm afraid of them, said Malakor. That's all. That's why. It's not that they'll hurt you. It's that they won't. They'll be so kind. And you'll want to stay.
Corin said nothing for a long time.
Then he leaned over, and picked up the belt, and unbuttoned the pouch.
Boy—
"I'm not opening it," said Corin. "I just want to look."
He took out the black cloth square.
It was wet. The black thread was still tight. The stitches were all still there, small and neat and careful. He turned it over in his fingers. And on one side, near the corner, he saw that the cloth had gone thin. Worn. Rubbed almost through. As if someone, a long time ago, had held it in exactly this way, between finger and thumb, and rubbed that corner, over and over, the way you rub a lucky coin.
And through the thin place, he could see a little of the wax inside.
It was cracked.
The heat in the sewer, he thought. The flood. Whatever it was that had made them burn against his hip. One of the three little wax balls had a crack across it. A hair-fine line. And through the crack, so faint he had to hold it close to his eye to see it, there came a light.
Not a bright light. A soft one. Gold. Like the last of the sun on a wall at the end of a summer day.
And a sound.
He held the cloth up to his ear.
Boy, please—
It was very faint. Fainter than in the sewer. Only a thread. Not voices. Not words. Not a whole village singing. Only a hum. A few notes, hummed. Three of them. Low, and then higher, and then low again. Over and over. Slowly. Patiently. The way a mother hums to a child who will not sleep.
Three notes.
And then a pause. A long pause. As if there should be a fourth.
And then the three again.
Corin lowered the cloth.
He sat by the fire with it in his hand. He did not know what was on his face. He did not know what he was feeling. Only that his throat had closed, and his eyes were stinging, and that he knew that tune. He did not remember it. There was nothing left in him to remember it with. But he knew it the way he knew the words on the stairwell. The way his hand knew the place on the wall.
He opened his mouth. And he hummed it.
Three notes. Low, and higher, and low.
Across the fire, Juno went white.
"That's Garran's tune," she whispered. "The carter. The one who comes in from the country with the cabbages. He hums that. All the time. Gran asked him once what it was, and he said it was a song from his village, and nobody knew the end of it. He said the end was only for—" She stopped.
"For what?" said Corin.
Juno swallowed.
"For the end," she said. "That's what he said. The fourth note's only for the end."
Corin looked down at the black cloth square in his hand. At the thin worn corner. At the faint gold light behind the crack.
Then he put it back in the pouch, very gently, and buttoned the flap, and put the belt down on the boards by the fire.
And behind his left ear, for the first time in six years, Malakor said nothing at all. Not because he was afraid. Corin could feel that. It was a different silence.
As if the dead man was listening too.
The Letter
Cadet Rowan Thorne cleaned his sword for an hour and a half that afternoon.
He sat on the end of his narrow bed in the cadets' long room at the top of the Academy, with the rain on the skylight and the other beds empty around him, and the sword across his knees, and a rag, and a tin of oil. And he cleaned it. Every inch. The blade, and the cross guard, and the black leather of the grip, and the little brass cap on the end of the grip. There was coal dust in every crack. Black and fine and greasy. It would not come out. He kept rubbing.
His hands knew how to do it. They had always known. They held the rag at the right angle and drew it along the edge in long smooth strokes, toward the point, never against it, the way a master does. He watched them do it. They did not feel like his hands.
Bought, he thought. In little stones. From the Bank.
He had never thought that word before. Not about himself. He had known it, of course. Everybody at the Academy knew. Half the cadets had bought skills. Riding. Fencing. Old languages. Good handwriting. Their fathers paid for them, and the Bank sent them up in little velvet boxes, and you sat in a white room with a stone in your mouth for an afternoon, and when you came out, you could do it. That was all. It was like buying boots. Nobody thought about it.
But he had heard Harvester Vane's voice in the coal cellar, saying his sister's name. And he had heard the word. That one word, in the language nobody spoke. And something in him had come loose, like a nail coming out of a board. And now he could not stop thinking about it.
He put the sword down.
He looked at the door of the long room. Shut. He listened. Nobody on the stair. Nobody in the passage. Only the rain on the skylight, and far away, down in the yard, the sergeant shouting at the first-years.
He put his hand inside his coat.
The letter was still there. Flat against his chest, where it always was. He took it out. It was worse than before. Crumpled, and damp, and smeared all over with black coal from his fingers. Four black fingerprints on the front of it, over his own name. Rowan. In small fierce writing that slanted hard to the right. And the brown stain on the corner.
He had carried it for weeks. Since the night before the Hollow. He had taken it from the post table in the front hall with all the others, and gone up to the long room, and stood over the stove at the end of the room with the lid open and the fire roaring, the way he did with every one of her letters. Every month. Without opening them. And he had held it over the fire.
And he had seen the stain.
And he had thought, what if she's dead.
And he had put it in his coat instead. And he had not taken it out since. Not once. Not to read it. Not to burn it. He had just carried it. Next to his heart, under his cadet's coat, every day, through every drill and every lesson and every night, like a stone in his shoe that he could not take off.
He sat on the bed and held it.
She's alive, Harvester Vane had said. She's on a barge on the river right now with your father. She got him out.
Rowan turned the letter over.
The back was sealed with a blob of cheap red wax. No seal pressed in it. Just a thumbprint. Her thumbprint. He could see the little lines of it in the wax.
He slid his finger under the edge of the wax.
It came away with a small dry crack, like a twig breaking. He sat very still for a moment, as if the sound might have been heard all over the Academy. Nothing happened. Down in the yard, the sergeant went on shouting.
He unfolded the letter.
It was one sheet. Cheap cream paper, the same as the envelope. Written on one side only, in pencil, in the same small fierce slanting hand. It was not long. He had thought it would be long. All the others had been thick, two or three sheets, he could feel them through the envelopes when he held them over the stove. This one was short. As if she had not had much time. Or as if she had decided, this once, to say only what mattered.
He read it.
Ro,
You won't read this. You never read any of them. I know. I've always known. I write them anyway, because one day you might, and I want there to be something there when you do.
Tomorrow I am going into the Hollow. If I don't come out, this is the last one. So I am going to say it plainly, and you can burn it if you like.
I did not ruin us. I know that is what they told you. I know that is what you believe. But it isn't true. Father was ruined before I ever did anything. Somebody took things from him. From all of us. From you, Ro. There are things you don't remember, about the house, about Mother, about the empty chair at Midwinter. You were very small. They took them out of you while you slept, and paid for your school with them, and you never knew.
I don't know who yet. But I am going to find out.
I am not asking you to forgive me. I am not asking you to come. I am only asking you this. When they tell you who your enemies are, ask them how they know. Just ask. And watch their faces when they answer.
I love you. I always have. You were the one who held my hand on the stairs when I was too small to climb them. You won't remember that either. But I do. So it isn't lost.
Fold it small, Ro. Mother used to say that. Fold it small and keep it close.
Ly.
Rowan read it three times.
The third time, he could not see the words, so he stopped.
He sat on the end of his bed in the empty long room with the letter in his black-smudged hands and the sword on the blanket beside him, and the rain on the skylight, and he did not make a sound. He had been taught not to. At the Academy you did not make a sound. But his shoulders shook. And the tears ran down his face and fell on the cream paper, one, and then another, and made small round gray marks in the coal dust, and he let them.
The empty chair at Midwinter.
He did not remember an empty chair.
But he did. Somewhere. Not a picture. Not a memory. A shape. A gap at a long table, with candles on it, and a place laid, and nobody sitting in it. And his mother looking at it, once, and then looking away. And his own small hand reaching up, and being held.
When they tell you who your enemies are, ask them how they know.
He thought of Silas Kross. Kind, mild, a little sad. Standing at the grating in the silver light, saying: Rowan. Is that where he said he was going?
He thought of the boiled sweets. Barley sugar. The Inquisitor always had a paper bag of them in his coat. He had given Rowan one, that first day, two years ago, when Rowan came to the Office as a new cadet, frightened and alone. And shown him how to fold the wrapper. Small. Very small. Into a hard tight square, the size of a fingernail. A tidy man has a tidy mind, he had said. Fold it small, Rowan. Fold it small and keep it close.
Rowan stopped breathing.
Fold it small.
His mother's words. Lyra's words. His mother's motto, stitched in blue thread on the old embroidery that Lyra had taken when she ran away.
In Silas Kross's mouth.
He sat very still.
Then, slowly, carefully, he folded the letter. Along its old creases. Small. Smaller. Into a hard tight square. And he put it back inside his coat, against his chest, over his heart.
He picked up his sword and put it in its scabbard.
And he took a sheet of paper from the box under his bed, and a pencil, and sat down on the floor with the paper on his knee, and began, for the first time in two years, to write a letter to his sister.
