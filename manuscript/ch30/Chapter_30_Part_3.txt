Chapter 30: Down Into the Dark, Part 3 of 5: The Bricked Door
The Bricked Door
At the very bottom of the Ministry of Records, under the Archive, under the drawers, under the last iron stair, there was a passage that nobody used.
Wren Halloway had found it two years ago, on her fourth day in the Office of the Inquisition, when she had been sent down to fetch a box of old warrants and had taken a wrong turn. She had stood at the end of it with her lamp held up and looked at the wall that closed it off, and felt a draught on her face coming through the cracks in the mortar. A cold wet draught that smelled of the river. And she had thought, that's interesting, and written it down in the little book she kept in her coat, and never told anyone.
She stood in front of it now with Silas Kross beside her and the silver light of his rod on the wall.
It was brick. Old dark red brick, laid in close neat rows, with gray mortar crumbling out from between them. It filled the whole end of the passage from floor to roof. There was no door in it. There was no sign there had ever been a door in it, except for the shape of the passage itself, which was arched at the top, like a doorway, and a little iron ring set into the stone at one side, rusted solid, where a door might once have hung.
Kross looked at it for a long time.
He did not say anything. He stood with the rod held up in his gloved hand and his head a little on one side, the way a man looks at a picture in a gallery that he is not sure he likes. The silver light moved slowly over the bricks. Up. Across. Down.
The draught came through the cracks and stirred the hair on Wren's forehead.
"Well," said Kross at last. Mildly. "That's a wall."
"Yes, sir." Wren kept her voice flat. "I'm sorry, sir. The plans said—"
"Show me."
She took the plans out of her satchel. They were rolled and tied with a black ribbon, in an oilskin case, to keep them dry. She untied the ribbon and unrolled them on her knee, and held the lamp close, and pointed.
"Here, sir. Archive level, sub-cellar three. This passage. And the line here, going through. Door to Main Sewer, reopened by order of the War Office. With the date."
Kross bent over the plans.
He looked at them for a very long time. Longer than he had looked at the wall. He put out one gloved finger and touched the line she had pointed to. The neat black line, going through the wall. And the small neat writing beside it.
He lifted his finger and looked at the tip of his glove.
There was a faint gray smudge on the white kid leather.
"Isn't that strange," said Kross softly. "A plan from the War Office. Eighteen years old. And the ink comes off on my glove."
Wren did not move.
She did not breathe. She did not blink. She kept her face exactly as it was, still and pale and polite, a wall that nobody could climb. And inside the wall, her heart stopped, and started again, and began to beat so hard that she thought he must be able to hear it in the quiet.
Last night, she thought. By candlelight. With the fine pen. I should have used the old ink. I should have baked it. I should have—
Kross straightened up.
He looked at her. Mild, kind, a little sad. And then he did something she had never seen him do in two years and four months.
He laughed.
Not loudly. A soft, short, pleased laugh, through his nose, like a man who has just been told a good joke at a dinner party. He shook his head. He took the plans out of her hands, gently, and rolled them up, and tied the black ribbon round them in a neat bow.
"Oh, Halloway," he said. "Oh, my dear girl. Did you think I didn't know?"
Wren said nothing.
"He went left," said Kross. "Of course he went left. He went left the moment he hit the water. He's halfway to the river by now. Probably drowned, in all this rain, but I don't think so. He's a hard man to drown. I've read his file too." He tucked the plans under his arm. "I knew it at the grating. I knew it when you said right. You have a very honest face, Wren. It's your greatest weakness. When you lie, it goes more still. As if you're holding your breath."
"Then why," said Wren. Her voice came out very small. "Why did you—"
"Come up here? Instead of down there?" Kross smiled. "Because I didn't want to catch him, Wren. Not this morning. Not in a sewer. What would I get? One man. Wet. Half mad. With a dead mage in his pocket and a hole where his childhood used to be." He turned and looked at the bricked-up door again, almost fondly. "No. I want to see where he goes. I want to see who meets him. Who hides him. Who feeds him. Who he trusts. I want all of them, Wren. Every one. And the only way to get all of them is to let him run to them."
He turned back to her.
"And now he will," he said. "Thanks to you."
The draught came through the bricks. Cold. Smelling of the river.
Wren stood very still in the silver light and understood, all at once, the way you understand that you have stepped off the edge of a stair in the dark, what she had done.
She had not saved him.
She had sent him exactly where Silas Kross wanted him to go.
"Don't look like that," said Kross kindly. "You did very well. Better than you know. And you won't be punished, I promise. I don't punish people for being useful." He put his hand into the inside pocket of his gray coat and took out a little book. Small. Black. Bound in soft leather, with a silver pencil tucked into its spine. She had seen it before. Everyone in the Office had seen it. Nobody knew what was in it.
He opened it, and wrote something. She could not see what. It was only one line. Short. He wrote it slowly and carefully, in his small neat hand, and blew on it to dry it, and closed the book, and put it away.
"There," he said. "Now. I think you should go home, Wren. You're wet through. Have a hot bath. Have a good supper. Get a good night's sleep." He smiled. "And tomorrow morning, come to work at the usual time, and sit at your usual desk, and do your usual work. As if nothing happened at all. Can you do that?"
"Yes, sir," said Wren.
"Good girl."
He went away up the iron stair. His footsteps were light and quick on the rungs. The silver light went with him, up and up, round the turn of the stair, and was gone.
And Wren Halloway stood alone in the dark at the bottom of the Ministry, in front of a door that was not a door, and did not move for a long time.
The lamp in her hand shook.
She put her other hand in her pocket. Her fingers found the beach stone. Gray and smooth, with a white line round it, like a ring. She held it so tightly that the edge of the white line bit into her palm.
He wants to see who meets him.
Juno. The boat. Wat Doon, and Hessa, and the baby. The barge called Patient Grace, somewhere up past the north locks, with an old lord hidden in its hold under the hay, and a girl with short dark hair sitting up on deck with a long knife across her knees, waiting.
All of them. Every one.
Wren put the lamp down on the floor.
She took the little book out of her own coat. Not his. Hers. Plain, cheap, gray-covered, with a stub of pencil on a string. The book she kept her secrets in. The book with the draught written in it, from two years ago, and the wrong turn, and a hundred other small things she had never told anyone.
She tore a page out. She wrote on it, fast, crouched over the lamp, in small square letters. Three lines.
Then she folded it. Small. Very small. Into a hard tight square, the size of a fingernail.
And she picked up the lamp, and went back up the iron stair, slowly, in the dark, counting.
Outfall Number Three
The gate had no door in it.
Corin went along it from one side of the tunnel to the other, hand over hand, with the brown water pulling at his legs, and felt every bar. They were as thick as his wrist. They went down into the water and he could not feel the bottom of them. They went up into the dark and he could not see the top. They were set so close together that he could not have pushed his arm between them past the elbow.
He stood in the middle of the gate with his face against the iron and looked through at the gray daylight, ten yards away, and the rain falling on the river. So close. So close he could smell it. Cold and clean and wide, through the stink of the sewer, like a window opening in a sickroom.
Burn something, said Malakor. Burn the bars.
"With what?" said Corin. "I've nothing left that's big enough. Not for iron. Not without burning something I need."
The voice was quiet.
He began to go along the gate again. Slower this time. Feeling lower. Under the water. Crouching, so that the water came up to his chin, and running his hands down the bars as far as they would go, to where they went into the mud.
And at the far left side, near the wall, his hands found a gap.
Two of the bars were bent. Down low. Under the water, where nobody would ever see them from above. Bent outward, away from each other, in a long smooth curve, as if somebody had put a jack between them, or a very strong pair of hands, a long time ago, and pushed and pushed until they gave. The gap between them was narrow. A foot wide, perhaps. Less. But it was a gap.
He felt the edges of it. The iron there was smooth. Rubbed smooth. Not rusty at all. As if bodies had gone through it, many times, for years, wearing it bright.
Children, said Malakor. Mudlarks. They come up the outfalls at low tide, looking for coins and rings that people drop down the drains. That's their door.
"It's under the water."
It is now. It won't be at low tide.
"I can't wait for low tide."
No.
Corin looked at the gap. He looked at the gray daylight. He put the rod in his teeth, crossways, the way a dog carries a stick, with the little blue light shining past his cheek. He took a breath. The biggest breath he could hold.
And he went under.
It was dark under the water. Darker than the drain. He could not see the gap. He could only feel it, the two smooth curved bars, one under each hand. He pushed his head between them. His shoulders. The iron squeezed his shoulders, hard, on both sides, like two hands, and stopped him. He was stuck. His head was through and his shoulders were not, and the water was pushing on his back, and his breath was going, and he could not go forward and he could not go back.
He turned. Sideways. He got one shoulder through, and then the other, scraping, the iron tearing his coat, tearing his skin. And then his chest. His hips. The pouch at his hip caught on the bar, and for one moment he thought, if it tears, if the stones fall, if they fall in the mud here and I can't find them—
And it came free.
And he was through.
He came up on the other side with a great gasp, and the rod fell out of his teeth, and he caught it, just, before the water took it. And he stood up.
The water was only at his waist here. The floor of the outfall sloped up, toward the round mouth, and the water ran over it shallow and fast, spreading out, pouring over the lip of the stone into the river in a long brown fan.
He walked toward the light.
It grew. Gray, and then grayer, and then white, so bright after the dark that it hurt his eyes and he had to put his arm up over his face. And then he was standing in the mouth of the outfall. At the very edge. With the round stone arch over his head, and the brown water pouring past his boots and over the lip, and below him, three feet down, the river.
He sat down.
He did not decide to. His legs decided. They folded up under him like a pair of wet sticks, and he sat down hard on the stone lip of the outfall with his boots dangling over the edge and the sewer water running round him, and he could not have stood up again if the Inquisitor himself had come round the corner.
The rain fell on his face.
It was cold. It was clean. He tipped his head back against the stone arch and let it fall on him, on his eyes and his mouth and his forehead, and washed the sewer off him. He opened his mouth and let it fall in. It tasted of nothing. It tasted of sky. It was the best thing he had ever tasted.
After a long time, he looked at the river.
It was very wide here. Much wider than it looked from the Lantern Bridge. Gray and slow and smooth, pocked all over with rain, with long low curls of mist lying on it like smoke. On the far side, a long way off, there were warehouses, and cranes, and a church tower. On this side, to his right, close above him, there was a great dark wall of stone going up and up. The Customs House. He could see the bottom of its windows, high above, and the iron rings set into its wall for tying boats, and the green weed hanging from the rings. To his left, the wharf. Wooden piles, black and slimy, going down into the water in long rows. Boats tied to them. Barges. Lighters. A coal ship, very low in the water, with its black sails furled.
No little boat. No girl with her hair in a knot.
He did not know what time it was.
And as he thought it, a bell began to ring.
It came from up the hill. From the Upper City. One bell, deep and slow, and then another joining it, higher, and another, until there were five or six of them ringing together across the rain, from all the towers of Veridia, the way they rang every day at the same hour. He knew that sound. He had heard it every day for six years, from his window, from the street, from the Intake Hall. He had never once listened to it.
He listened now.
He counted.
Twelve.
Noon.
The bells stopped. The last one hummed for a while in the wet air, and then that stopped too. And there was only the rain on the river, and the water pouring out of the sewer past him, and somewhere far off a gull crying.
No boat.
He sat and waited.
He waited a long time. He did not know how long. There were no more bells. The rain did not change. The mist on the river moved, slowly, in long gray curls, and opened, and closed again. A barge went past, far out in the middle, very slow, with a man at the long oar at the back of it leaning his whole weight on it, and a dog on the roof of the cabin, barking at nothing. A rowing boat went past, close in, with two men in it, and Corin pushed himself back into the shadow of the arch and held his breath. But they did not look at him. They were arguing about a horse.
Still no boat.
The cold came back.
He had not felt it while he was moving. Now he was still, and it came up out of the stone and into him, slowly, the way water comes up into a cloth. Into his legs. Into his back. Into his chest. He began to shake again. Hard. His teeth knocked together so loudly that he thought the men in the rowing boat must have heard.
She's late, said Malakor.
"Yes."
She might not come.
"She'll come."
The river's high. The rain. The current. Kross. A hundred things. Boy, she might not—
"She'll come," said Corin.
He did not know why he was so sure. He thought about it, shaking on the stone lip with the rain on him. And he found that it was because of a roof. A wet slate roof, on the first morning he ever saw her, with a girl on it in her grandfather's coat, looking down at him through the rain with her chin stuck out, and saying: If you're going to get yourself killed, you might at least let me watch.
She would come. If she was alive, she would come.
He put his hand on the pouch at his hip, without thinking.
The stones were cold now. Quite cold. Just stones.
He took his hand away.
And far out on the river, in the mist, he heard a sound. A small wet regular sound. Coming closer. Clunk. And a splash. Clunk. And a splash. The sound of oars in old iron rowlocks that nobody had greased in years.
And a voice. A girl's voice. High and fierce and hoarse with shouting, carrying across the water.
"—told you it was number three, you great lump, I told you, if you'd only listen—"
Juno and Wat
The boat came out of the mist the way a thought comes out of sleep. First nothing. Then a shape. Then, all at once, a real thing, close, with edges.
It was a small boat. Smaller than he had thought it would be. Long and narrow and low in the water, painted a dark green that had mostly peeled off, so that the gray wood showed through in long patches. There was a coil of wet rope in the front of it, and a tin bailer, and a lantern that was not lit. There was a man in the middle, rowing. And in the back, sitting very straight with both hands on the gunwales and her face turned toward the shore, there was a girl.
She had her grandfather's coat on. It was much too big for her. The sleeves were rolled back four times, and the shoulders came halfway down her arms, and the collar was turned up so high that only her eyes and her nose and the top of her head showed over it. Her hair was in its knot. It had mostly come out of the knot. It hung round her face in wet dark ropes.
She was looking along the wall of the Customs House. Searching. Her eyes went from one outfall to the next, one black round stone mouth after another, quick and sharp and frightened.
And then they found him.
He saw it happen. He saw her eyes stop. He saw her whole face change, all at once, the way a window changes when a lamp is lit behind it.
And then she was on her feet.
"There!" she screamed. "There, Wat, there, number three, I said number three, pull, you great—pull!"
The boat rocked wildly. The man at the oars said something short and deep that Corin could not hear, and grabbed at her coat with one hand and pulled her down onto the seat again, hard, without stopping rowing with the other. And the boat turned. Its nose came round toward the outfall. And it came in, fast, across the gray water, the oars going clunk-splash, clunk-splash in their old rusty rowlocks.
Corin tried to stand up.
He could not. His legs would not do it. He got as far as his knees, on the wet lip of the stone, with one hand on the arch, and then his knees went too, and he sat down again with a bump.
"Don't move!" Juno shouted. "Don't you dare move! Don't you dare fall in!"
The boat came alongside the outfall. It bumped against the stone, hard, under Corin's dangling boots. The man shipped the oars, one and then the other, with a long rattle, and stood up. And the boat did not even rock when he stood. He was that kind of man. Big and slow and solid, like a tree that has decided to stand in a river. He had a wide brown face, and a short gray beard, and no hat, so the rain ran straight down his bald head and into his beard. And he had a long boat hook in his hand, with an iron point and a curved iron claw, the same hook Corin had seen him carry on Sunday night in the den on Cinder Lane.
Wat Doon. Hessa's husband. Who did not talk.
He did not talk now. He looked at Corin for one moment, with small calm gray eyes. Then he put the boat hook down in the bottom of the boat, and reached up with both hands, and took Corin under the arms, and lifted him off the lip of the outfall the way you lift a child off a wall.
And put him down in the boat.
Corin did not have to do anything at all.
He lay in the bottom of the boat, in two inches of rainwater, on top of a coil of wet rope and the tin bailer. He looked up. At the gray sky. At the rain coming down out of it. At the round black mouth of the outfall, going away above him as Wat pushed off from the wall with one oar.
And then Juno's face, upside down, hanging over him.
She looked terrible. White and pinched and wet, with dark rings under her eyes, as if she had not slept at all. Her lips were blue with cold. There was a scratch across one cheek. And she was crying. Not much. Just two lines of tears, running down from the corners of her eyes and getting mixed up with the rain.
"You stink," said Juno.
"I know."
"You stink worse than the time you came down the coal chute."
"I know."
"I thought you were dead." Her voice cracked. "We came at noon, and you weren't there. And we came along every one, every outfall, all the way from the bridge. Number one. Number two. And Wat said you'd have gone in the wrong one, and come out at the gasworks, or you'd drowned, and he wanted to go back, and I hit him, and he said—" She stopped. She wiped her face with the back of her huge sleeve. "He said five more minutes. That's all he said. All day. Five more minutes."
Corin looked at Wat.
Wat was rowing. Slow and steady, out into the river, into the mist. He did not look at Corin. He did not look at Juno. He looked straight ahead, over their heads, at nothing, with the rain running into his beard.
"Thank you," said Corin.
Wat nodded once. Very slightly. And went on rowing.
Juno pulled something out from under the seat. A blanket. A heavy brown horse blanket, rough and smelling of hay and horse and tar, folded up inside an oilcloth to keep it dry. She threw it over him. All of him. From his chin to his boots. And then she sat down on the bottom of the boat beside him, in the rainwater, and tucked the edges of the blanket in under him all the way round, hard, the way you tuck in a very small child who will not stay in bed.
"Gran's safe," she said, while she did it. Not looking at him. "At Moll's. Moll's got her in the back room by the fire with a bottle of something, and she's telling everybody who comes in about her floor. She says the Ministry's going to pay for it if she has to go up the Hill and sit on the Inquisitor's desk till they do." Juno sniffed. "She will, too."
"Yes," said Corin. "She will."
"She sent you this." Juno reached into the front of the huge coat and pulled out a parcel. Wrapped in a cloth. Warm. Actually warm. "Pie. She made it at Moll's. In Moll's oven. Moll said she couldn't, and Gran said watch me. It's mutton."
Corin held the parcel against his chest under the blanket. He could feel the warmth of it going into him.
He did not trust himself to say anything. So he did not.
The boat went out into the river. The mist closed behind it. The Customs House went gray, and then pale, and then it was only a darker shape in the white, and then it was gone. There was only the water, gray and wide and slow, and the rain on it, and the clunk-splash of the oars.
"Juno," said Corin after a while.
"What."
"Were you followed?"
She stopped tucking.
She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "I don't know." And then, lower: "There was a man. On the Customs House steps. When we came round the corner from the bridge. In a gray cape. Just standing there, in the rain, looking at the water. Not doing anything." She bit her lip. "I thought he was a clerk. Waiting for a ship. They do that. Stand on the steps and wait for ships."
"Was he there when you found me?"
Juno did not answer.
She turned round on her knees in the bottom of the boat and looked back. Into the mist. Toward the shore, which was not there anymore.
Wat had stopped rowing.
He sat with the oars held up out of the water, dripping, and he was looking back too. With his small calm gray eyes. For a long time. And then he looked down at Corin, in the bottom of the boat, under the horse blanket.
And for the first time, Wat Doon spoke.
"Barge is north," he said. His voice was very deep and very slow, like a cart going over a wooden bridge. "Past the locks. My Hessa's on it. My boy."
That was all.
Corin understood.
"Then don't go north," he said. "Not yet. Not while they might be watching."
Wat looked at him for a moment longer. Then he nodded. Once. And put the oars back in the water, and turned the boat, slowly, in a long curve through the mist. Not north. Not upstream. Down. Toward the sea.
Juno stared at him.
"Where are we going, then?"
"I don't know," said Corin. "Somewhere they're not looking."
And he lay back in the bottom of the boat, under the horse blanket, with Mrs. Marta's warm pie on his chest and the rain on his face, and closed his eyes.
