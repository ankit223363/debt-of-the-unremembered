Chapter 30: Down Into the Dark, Part 2 of 5: The Fourth Desk
The Fourth Desk
At a quarter past eleven that morning, on the third floor of the Oblivion Bank, the Auditor of the Fourth Desk put down her pen.
She did not know why she had put it down. She looked at it. It was a good pen. Black, with a silver nib, the same pen she had used every working day for as long as her book said she had worked here. It lay on the glass top of her desk beside the ledger she had been writing in. The ink on the nib was still wet. She had been halfway through a word.
She looked at the word. Collect—
She did not finish it.
The room around her was very quiet. It was always quiet. The Fourth Desk was a long, high, white room at the back of the Bank, with three tall windows looking out over Lantern Quay and the river. Rain ran down the windows in long gray lines. Below them, she could see the tops of umbrellas going along the quay, black and brown and one bright red, like beetles on a wet stone. And the river beyond, wide and gray and pocked with rain, and the far bank, and the masts of the ships at the Customs House wharf, standing up in the rain like a forest of bare trees.
The room smelled of ink and wax and lilies. The Bank always smelled of lilies. There were white ones in a tall glass vase on every desk, changed every morning by a girl with no name who came in at six. Elara had stopped smelling them years ago. Or her book said she had.
She sat very still at her glass desk, in her dark green coat, with her white gloves on, and listened to herself.
Something was wrong.
She did not know what. It was not a thought. It was lower than a thought. A feeling in the skin. The feeling you get when you are walking down a stair in the dark and you think there is one more step, and there isn't.
She looked down at the desk.
On the glass, near the left edge, where the light from the window fell, there was a mark. A faint blue shape. A hand. The print of a palm and four long fingers, spread flat, as if someone had leaned on the desk with their whole weight. It had been there since Monday afternoon. She had tried to wipe it off. Twice. With a cloth, and then with spirit. It had not come off. It was not on the glass, she had decided in the end. It was in it.
She had not told anyone about it. Not Mister Gage. Not the Senior Auditor. She did not know why.
She looked at it now.
It was fading.
Not all at once. Slowly. The blue was going pale at the edges, the way a breath fades off a cold window. The fingertips were already almost gone. As she watched, the line of the thumb went thin, and broke, and was not there.
Elara pulled off her right glove.
She did it without thinking. She did not take her gloves off at work. Nobody at the Bank did. But she pulled it off, finger by finger, and laid it on the ledger, and put her bare hand down flat on the glass, over the fading print.
It was cold.
The glass was always cold. That was not it. The print was colder. Much colder. A deep, wet, bitter cold, like river water in winter, coming up out of the glass into her palm. And under the cold, very faint, very far off, something else.
Noise. A roaring. Like water.
And a voice. A man's voice. Saying something over and over, in a whisper, as if to keep himself from forgetting it.
—your name is—
She took her hand away.
She sat back in her chair. Her heart was going very fast. She could hear it. She put her bare hand in her lap and held it with the other one, and they were both shaking.
Ledger-Sight, she thought. I didn't mean to. I didn't open it. It opened on its own.
That had never happened before. Not in her book. Not once in two thousand four hundred and five days.
She looked at the inside of her right wrist. Where the skin was thin and white and the veins showed blue. There was nothing there. There never was. But every time she used the Sight, she lost a day. That was the price. And every night she wrote down the day she had lost, in the small black book she kept in the drawer of her desk, so that at least she would know it was gone. Two thousand four hundred and five lines. She would write one more tonight. She did not know yet which day it would be. She never knew until she reached for it and found the hole.
She opened the drawer of her desk.
Not the top one. The bottom one. The one she kept locked, with the small brass key on the chain round her neck, under her collar. She opened it and took out a card.
It was a plain white card, the size of a playing card, with the seal of the Fourth Desk pressed into one corner in green wax. And under the seal, in her own neat writing: Under audit. Not to be collected or harmed by order of the Fourth Desk. E. She had written three of them on Monday, after he had gone. She had given one to him. She had kept two. They were twins. When one was touched, the others felt it. That was what they were for. So that an Auditor would know if the person under audit came to harm.
She held the card in her bare hand.
It was soaking wet.
Not the card. The card was dry. But it felt wet, in her fingers. Cold and heavy and soaking, the way a thing feels when it has been dragged through deep water. And the green wax of the seal was not green anymore. It had gone gray. Like a bruise.
She put the card down very carefully on the glass, next to the fading blue hand.
Then she rang the bell.
Mister Gage came in.
He did not knock. He never did. He came in through the tall white door with his head bent, because the door was not quite tall enough for him, and he stood in front of her desk and waited. He was the biggest man she had ever seen. Bigger than the doorway. Bigger than any two of the porters on the quay. He wore a black coat that had been made for him and still did not fit, and his hands hung at his sides like spades. And his face was the face she always saw when she looked at him. The face that was not finished. Smooth and broad and pale, with the eyes and the nose and the mouth all there, but somehow too plain. Too simple. As if someone had started to carve a face, and been called away, and never come back to it.
She had asked him once what had happened to his face. He had written on his slate, in his big round careful letters: I sold it. And nothing else.
"Mister Gage," she said. "Order number two thousand one hundred and seven. A collection. From the Office of the Inquisition, filed with us on Tuesday morning. A household at Tallow Street. I want to know if it's been done."
Gage did not move. He did not need to. He knew every paper that came into the Fourth Desk. He had carried every one of them up the stairs himself. He looked at her for a moment with his plain unfinished eyes. And then he took the slate out of his coat pocket, and the stub of chalk, and wrote.
He turned the slate round.
Done at eleven. Not taken.
"Not taken," said Elara. "What does that mean, not taken?"
Gage wiped the slate with the side of his huge hand, and wrote again.
Ran. Down. Under.
She looked at the three words.
Then she looked at the window. At the rain. At the river beyond, wide and gray, moving slowly to the sea. At the masts of the ships at the Customs House wharf. And under the wharf, at the waterline, where she had never in her life thought to look, a row of round black stone mouths, the sewer outfalls of the Lower City, pouring brown water out into the gray.
Down, she thought. Under.
Your name is.
She stood up.
She put her glove back on. She did it carefully, finger by finger, the way she did everything. She put the gray-sealed card in the inside pocket of her coat, against her heart. She closed the ledger, with the half-written word still in it. She did not finish the word.
"Mister Gage," she said. "Get my umbrella. And a cab. We're going to Tallow Street."
Gage looked at her.
He did not write anything. But she knew what he was thinking. She always did. An Auditor of the Fourth Desk does not go to Tallow Street. An Auditor does not go anywhere. The world comes up the white steps to her, under the words FORGET YOUR TROUBLES, and stands in front of her glass desk, and is weighed.
"I know," said Elara. "Get the umbrella anyway."
Gage went.
And behind her, on the glass of the desk, the last faint line of the blue hand thinned to a thread, and broke, and was gone.
Number Fourteen
The cab could not get into Tallow Street.
The street was too narrow, and too steep, and there was a coal cart stuck halfway up it with one wheel in the gutter, and a crowd round the coal cart, and a man on top of the coal shouting at the crowd. So the cab stopped at the bottom, by the pump, and Elara got out into the rain.
She stood on the wet cobbles with Mister Gage holding the black umbrella over her head, and looked up the street.
She had never been to the Lower City before. Or her book said she had not. She had seen it, every day, from her three tall windows. A brown jumble of roofs and chimneys going down the hill to the river, with smoke coming out of all of them, and washing hung between them on lines. It had looked small from the third floor of the Bank. Small and far off and somehow tidy, like a picture of a town painted on a plate.
It did not look tidy now.
The houses leaned. That was the first thing. They leaned out over the street, toward each other, so that the gap of gray sky between their top floors was narrower than the street itself. Their walls were black with soot, and streaked green and brown where the gutters had overflowed. Half the windows had rags stuffed in their broken panes. And they were all joined together, one long crooked wall of houses going up the hill on either side, with no gaps between them, so that it was hard to tell where one ended and the next began.
And the smell. Coal smoke. Wet stone. Onions. Cabbage. Cats. And something under all of it, sour and old, that she could not name.
People were looking at her.
She could feel it. The people round the coal cart. A woman in a doorway with a baby on her hip. Two boys sitting on a step under a broken porch, out of the rain. An old man at a window with a pipe. All looking at the lady in the dark green coat and the white gloves, with the giant holding the umbrella over her. And not one of them was smiling.
"Number fourteen," said Elara.
Nobody answered.
She went up the street. Gage went beside her, with the umbrella. People moved out of his way without being asked. The crowd round the coal cart went quiet as she passed, and the man on top of the coal stopped shouting, and stared.
She did not need to ask again. She saw it from fifty yards off.
There was a Ministry constable standing outside it. A young one, in a wet gray cape, with his hands behind his back and his face very red with cold. And the front door behind him was standing wide open. And the front window beside the door was broken. Not cracked. Broken out entirely, frame and all, so that there was only a big ragged hole in the wall, with splinters of wood and glass sticking out of it, and the rain blowing in.
As if something very big had come out through it. Without bothering with the door.
"Madam," said the constable, as she came up the step. He stood in front of the door. "I'm sorry, madam. Nobody's to go in. Inquisitor's orders."
Elara took a card out of her coat.
Not the gray-sealed one. A different one. Thicker. Cream-colored. With the seal of the Oblivion Bank on it in gold, the white dome with the columns under it, and under that, the words Fourth Desk. Audit of Debts. She held it up in front of the young man's face.
"This house is under audit by the Bank," she said. "The tenant has a debt with us. Anything in it is ours until the debt is weighed. I'm here to weigh it."
The constable looked at the card. He looked at her. He looked up, a long way up, at Mister Gage.
He stood aside.
She went in.
The hall was dark and narrow, and it smelled of onions, and it was a ruin. The walls on both sides had been scraped. Long deep scrapes in the plaster, at shoulder height, as if something too wide for the hall had pushed its way along it. The floorboards were cracked. A picture had fallen off the wall and lay face down on the boards in a mess of broken glass. And at the end of the hall there was a purple door, standing open, hanging from one hinge.
She went to it, and looked through.
A kitchen. Small and low and warm, with a black range against one wall, still giving out a little heat, and a dresser full of blue and white plates, and a scrubbed wooden table. Nothing on the table but a jug with three white daisies in it. Three chairs round the table. One of them was knocked over.
And at the back of the kitchen, through another door, a scullery. Or what had been a scullery.
The floor was gone.
There was a great black hole where it had been. Broken boards sticking up round the edges of it like teeth. A stone sink hanging off the wall, half fallen. Cold air coming up out of the hole, and the smell of earth and coal and old dark places. And in the middle of the hole, going down into the black, the top of an old iron ladder, bent almost double, as if something very heavy had come down it all at once.
Elara stood in the doorway of the scullery and looked at the hole for a long time.
She did not say anything. Gage did not write anything. The rain dripped through the broken window in the hall behind them. Somewhere above, very faint, a door banged in the wind.
She turned round.
On the wall beside the kitchen door, at the height of a woman's head, there was a nail. And on the nail, hanging by a loop of string, a small slate. A child's slate. And on it, in chalk, in big rough capitals, as if somebody had written it in a great hurry: RENT DUE FRIDAY. M.
Elara looked at it for a moment. Then she went back down the hall, past the fallen picture, to the foot of the stairs.
They went up into the dark.
They were very steep and very narrow, and the walls on either side had once been painted a sort of mustard yellow. Now they were brown. Brown with smoke and damp and the grease of a thousand hands, sliding down them in the dark. She did not touch them. She held up the skirt of her coat in one gloved hand and went up, and Gage came behind her, bent nearly double under the low ceiling.
She counted the steps. She did not know why.
Sixteen to the first landing. Doors, shut. Behind one of them, a baby crying. Sixteen more to the second. A cat on the landing, gray, sitting very upright, that watched her pass with yellow eyes and did not move. Sixteen to the third.
On the third landing, she stopped.
She did not mean to. Her feet stopped. And her hand, her gloved right hand, which had been holding up her coat, let go of it and went out to the wall. Low down. Near the floor. To a place on the brown plaster that looked exactly like every other place.
Her fingertips found scratches.
She bent closer. It was too dark to see. She took off her glove, again, for the second time that day, and touched the wall with her bare fingers.
Names. Cut into the plaster with a nail or a knife. DELLA. TOMMY B. KESTER WAS HERE. She could feel the letters. And under them, smaller than the rest, older, worn smooth by something. By touching. By a hand, going past in the dark, night after night, for years.
WE REMEMBER.
She took her hand away.
She stood on the third landing of No. 14 Tallow Street with her bare hand held against her chest, and she did not know why her eyes were stinging.
Then she put her glove back on, and went on up.
Sixteen. Sixteen. Eleven, the last flight, narrow as a ladder.
Ninety-one.
At the top, there was one door. Brown. Plain. Standing a little open. With a new brass lock on it, and the wood round the lock splintered, where someone had put a shoulder to it.
She pushed it.
It swung open, slowly, with a long creak.
And she stood on the threshold and looked at the room where her brother had lived for six years, and did not know whose room it was.
Three Words on a Wall
It was a very small room.
That was the first thing she thought. How small. She could have crossed it in four steps. The ceiling sloped down on one side, under the roof, so steeply that at the low end even she would have had to bend her head. There was one window, in the sloping part, a square of dirty glass with the rain running down it, looking out over a jumble of wet slate roofs and chimney pots. There was a narrow iron bed under the window. A wooden chair. A small table. A washstand with a cracked blue jug on it. A little black stove in the corner, cold, with a kettle on top.
And a shelf. One long plain shelf on the wall opposite the bed, at the height of a man's eyes, running the whole width of the room.
The shelf was empty.
Not just bare. Emptied. She could see the marks where things had stood on it. Round marks, in the dust. Dozens of them, in neat rows, close together. The marks of jars. Glass jars, the kind a Harvester keeps, standing on that shelf for years, each in its own place. And now all gone. Only three of the round marks were fresh, at the end of the row, clean, as if those three had been there until this morning.
The rest of the room had been searched.
She could see that too. Carefully searched. Not smashed, not thrown about, the way the hall downstairs had been smashed by the thing that came through it. Searched by somebody neat. The drawer of the table had been pulled out and put back in upside down. The mattress on the bed had been turned over and slit along one side, and the straw inside it pulled out, and pushed back in. The rug, a thin brown rug with a pattern of faded red squares, had been rolled up and laid along the wall. The boards under it had been tried. She could see the scratches where a blade had gone into the cracks between them, one after another, all the way across the floor.
And on the windowsill, very neatly, side by side, somebody had left two small squares of folded paper. Barley-sugar wrappers. Folded small and tight and hard, the size of a fingernail.
She did not touch them.
She came into the room. Gage did not. He could not. The door was too low and the room too small for him. He stood out on the landing with his head bent under the ceiling and the black umbrella dripping in his hand, and watched her.
She stood in the middle of the floor and turned round slowly.
She did not know what she had come here for. She had told the constable she had come to weigh a debt. That was a lie. She did not know what it was instead. She only knew that a blue hand on her desk had gone cold and faded, and that a card in her pocket had gone gray, and that a voice in a roaring dark had said your name is, and she had put on her gloves and come down the hill in the rain to a place she had never been.
She looked at the bed.
The blanket was folded at the foot of it. A gray wool blanket, old and thin and much mended. And in the middle of it, where it was folded over on itself, there was a hole. A round hole, the size of a coin, with brown scorched edges. As if something small and hot had burned through it.
She looked at that for a long time. She did not know why.
Then she looked at the wall beside the bed.
It was plaster, like the stairwell. Not painted. Just old gray-white plaster gone yellow, with cracks across it like the lines on an old hand. And low down, near the pillow end of the bed, at the height where a man lying on his side would have his face, there was a place where the plaster was darker. Grayer. Smoother. As if somebody had touched it, there, in that one spot, a great many times.
She knelt down by the bed.
She did not think about her coat, or the dust, or the floor. She knelt, and leaned close, and looked.
There were words.
Scratched into the plaster with the point of a nail. Small. Careful. Cut deep, the way the words on the stairwell were cut, but not old. New. The edges of the scratches were still sharp and white. Last night, perhaps. Or the night before. Three lines of them, one under the other, in a hand that sloped hard to the right and pressed so deep that the plaster had cracked a little round each letter.
CORIN VANE.
OAKHAVEN.
ELARA.
She knelt by the bed and read the three words, and the room was very quiet.
She read them again.
Elara.
It was a common enough name. She had always known that. There were four Elaras in the Bank's own staff ledger. There was an Elara who sold flowers on the corner of Lantern Quay. It was the sort of name mothers in the country gave to girls, because it was short and pretty and easy to shout across a field.
Oakhaven.
She had seen that word on Monday. In her own ledger. In red. One account. Called in full at the fourth hour. Do not reopen. And she had seen it inside him, when she touched the black glass. Written in chalk on every door of every empty room. OAKHAVEN. OAKHAVEN. OAKHAVEN.
And she had told herself, on Monday night, sitting alone at her desk after he had gone, that it did not matter. That it was a coincidence. That a Harvester from the Ministry with a hole where his childhood should be had happened to be born in a village that had happened to be closed by the Bank on an order she did not remember signing, because she did not remember anything from before the first line in her small black book. That there was no reason. No reason at all.
She took off her glove.
The third time that day.
Don't, said a part of her. The careful part. The part that kept the black book and wrote the lines in it and counted. Don't. It costs a day. You've already lost one today. You don't know which day this one will take. It might take the wrong one.
She put her bare fingertips on the word ELARA.
And she opened her eyes inside.
It was not like the Bank. At the Bank, when she used the Sight on a debtor, she saw a ledger. Neat columns. Red ink and black. What was owed and what was paid. Clean, and cold, and far away, like looking at the stars.
This was not cold.
She was lying on a narrow bed in the dark. That was the first thing. The bed was hard and the blanket was thin and the room was cold, and there was rain on the roof above her, very close. And she was holding a nail. She could feel it in her fingers. A long iron nail, a horseshoe nail, rough and square. And she was scratching letters into a wall by her face, slowly, carefully, by the light of one candle stub on the floor, because she was afraid.
Not of the Inquisitor. Not of the thing that was coming in the morning. Afraid of waking up and not knowing. Afraid of opening the book, one day, and finding the first page gone. Afraid that one morning there would be nobody left in the world who knew that a girl called Elara had ever had a brother.
So it's on the wall, he thought. She heard him think it. If they take the book. If they take everything. It's on the wall.
And he cut the last letter, the A, and put the nail down, and touched the word with one finger. Gently. The way you touch the head of a sleeping child.
And he said something. Out loud. In the dark.
She could not hear the words. They were in a language she did not know. Soft. Round. With the shape of hills in them. And she did not need to know the words, because she knew the voice. She knew it the way you know a tune you have never heard before, and could hum the end of.
And under the voice, very faint, very far off, so far that it might not have been there at all, a little girl was laughing.
Elara took her hand off the wall.
She was on her knees on the floor of the small room. Her bare hand was pressed flat against her mouth. And she was crying. Not quietly. Not neatly. She was crying the way a child cries, with her shoulders shaking and her breath coming in great ragged gulps, and she could not stop. She did not know why. She did not know what she had lost. She only knew that it was very big. Bigger than the room. Bigger than the Bank. Bigger than two thousand four hundred and five days.
Out on the landing, Mister Gage did not move.
He stood with his head bent under the low ceiling, and the umbrella dripping, and his plain unfinished face turned toward her. And after a long time, he took out his slate, and wrote on it, slowly, in his big round careful letters, and turned it round, and held it out through the doorway so that she could see.
I know, it said.
She looked at it.
She looked at him.
"How?" she whispered. "How do you know? What do you know?"
Gage wiped the slate. He wrote again. He held it out.
I was there.
And then, before she could say anything, before she could even get up off her knees, he wiped the slate clean with the side of his great hand, and put it back in his pocket, and turned his face away. And would not write another word.
Elara knelt on the floor for a while longer.
Then she got up. She put her glove back on. She took her small black book out of her coat, and her pencil, and she wrote in it, standing in the middle of the room, by the light of the gray window.
She wrote the date.
And then she reached inside herself for the day she had lost. The way she did every night. Feeling for the hole.
She found it.
She stood still for a long moment with the pencil above the page.
Then she wrote: A day I cannot name. I think it was a happy one.
And under it, for the first time in two thousand four hundred and six lines, she wrote something that was not a lost day at all.
Corin Vane. Oakhaven. Elara.
She closed the book. She put it away against her heart, next to the gray-sealed card. And she picked up the two small folded barley-sugar wrappers from the windowsill, and put those in her pocket too. Because they were evidence. Because somebody neat had left them there for her to find.
And because she wanted to know who.
