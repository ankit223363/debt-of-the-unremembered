Chapter 30: Down Into the Dark, Part 5 of 5: Do Not Reopen
Do Not Reopen
The Oblivion Bank closed its white doors at six o'clock.
It did it the same way every evening. The porters in their white coats came out onto the top step and stood in a row under the columns, under the words FORGET YOUR TROUBLES cut deep in the stone, and rang a small brass bell, three times. And the last of the customers came down the steps into the rain, with their collars up and their faces strangely smooth and calm, the way faces always were when they came out of the Bank. And the porters went in. And the great doors swung shut, slowly, on their oiled hinges, with a soft heavy sound like a book being closed.
And the Bank went quiet.
Elara sat at her glass desk on the third floor and listened to it go quiet.
She heard the clerks leaving. Their voices on the stairs, going down. Their footsteps in the great hall below. A laugh. A door. Then the night porters coming on, slow and heavy, going round the floors with their lamps, trying the doors. One of them stopped outside hers, and saw the light under it, and went on. They were used to that. The Auditor of the Fourth Desk often worked late.
Then nothing.
Only the rain on the three tall windows. And the lamp on her desk, with its green glass shade, making a round pool of light on the glass. And the lilies in their tall vase, white and still, giving out their thick sweet smell.
She had not gone home.
She had come back from Tallow Street at three. She had come up the white steps with Mister Gage behind her, and gone straight up to the Fourth Desk without speaking to anyone, and sat down, and opened her ledger, and looked at the half-written word, Collect—, and not finished it. She had sat there all afternoon. Doing nothing. Weighing nothing. The clerks had brought her papers, and she had signed them without reading them. Something she had never done in two thousand four hundred and six days.
She had been waiting. For the building to be empty.
Now it was.
She stood up. She took the lamp. She went to the door, and opened it, and Mister Gage was there. Standing in the passage outside, with his back against the wall and his head bent under the ceiling, where he had stood all afternoon. As if he had known.
He probably had.
"The Long Room," said Elara.
Gage looked at her for a moment. Then he took the lamp from her hand, gently, and went ahead of her down the passage.
The Long Room was at the very top of the Bank. Under the white dome. You went up a narrow iron stair from the fourth floor, round and round, until you came out through a trapdoor into a space that was like the inside of an egg. Curved white walls going up on every side to a round window at the very top, where the rain was. And all round the walls, from the floor to as high as a ladder would reach, shelves. And on the shelves, ledgers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Bound in white leather, with gold numbers on their spines. Every account the Oblivion Bank had ever opened, in three hundred years. Every debt. Every payment. Every soul.
It was the only room in the Bank that did not smell of lilies. It smelled of dust, and leather, and very faintly, of cold.
Elara knew where to go. She had been here on Monday night. She went along the curved wall with the lamp, past the white spines, until she came to a place near the floor where the gold numbers changed from four figures to five. And she knelt, and pulled out one ledger, and opened it on the floor in the pool of lamplight.
The page was where she had left it.
OAKHAVEN.
One account. Not a person. A village. Written across the top of the page in old brown ink, in a big clerk's hand, with a flourish under it.
And below that, the columns. Debt. Empty. Not zero. Empty. No figure at all. As if there had never been a debt. As if there had been nothing owed.
Paid. Empty too.
Called in full. Fourth hour. And a date. Eighteen years ago this winter.
Collector. And then a name. Or the place where a name had been. Scratched out. Not crossed through with a line, the way a clerk crosses out a mistake. Scratched. Gouged. With the point of something sharp, over and over, so hard that the paper had torn in two places, and the leather of the page beneath showed through.
And at the bottom, in red, three words. And a red wax seal. The seal of the dome.
Do not reopen.
Elara looked at it for a long time.
On Monday she had read these words and been afraid, and closed the book, and put it back, and gone home, and told herself it did not matter.
She was not afraid now. Or she was, but it did not matter. She took her right glove off. The fourth time that day. And she laid her bare hand flat on the gouged place, on the torn paper, where the collector's name had been.
Don't, said the careful part of her. That's three in one day. You've never done three in one day. You don't know what—
She opened her eyes inside.
There was no ledger. No columns. No red and black. There was only dark. A great dark, very cold, like the inside of a well. And in the dark, a hand. Writing. A man's hand, long and pale, in a dark sleeve. Writing a name in a ledger by lamplight. She could not see the name. Every time she looked at the letters, they slid away. Like water. Like a face in a dream.
And on the hand, on the third finger, a ring. Gold. With a seal on it. A dome.
The hand finished writing. It put the pen down. And then it picked something else up. Thin. Silver. A rod. With a spiral cut into it, going round and round. Going round the wrong way. Turning to the left.
And the hand laid the point of the rod on the name, and scratched.
And scratched.
And scratched.
And a voice. Soft. Gentle. Very kind. The kindest voice she had ever heard. Speaking to someone who was not there. Or someone who was, and could not hear.
There. Now nobody will ever know it was me. Not even you, little one. Especially not you.
Elara took her hand off the page.
She sat on the floor of the Long Room with the lamp beside her. She was not crying. She was past crying. She felt cold, all the way through, the way the hand on her desk had felt cold that morning. Cold and very clear and very quiet.
And she reached inside herself for the day she had just lost.
She found it at once. The hole. Small and neat and new.
She did not know what had been in it. She never did. But it was near the beginning. She could feel that. Near the very first line in her black book. A day from the very start.
She closed the ledger.
No. She did not close it. She had been going to close it. Her hands had already begun to fold it shut. And then they stopped.
She opened it again. Flat. To the page.
She took the black pen with the silver nib out of her coat pocket.
And under the red words Do not reopen, in her own small neat black hand, she wrote:
Reopened. By audit. Fourth Desk.
And the date. And under that, her initial.
E.
She blew on the ink. She waited for it to dry. And then she closed the ledger, and put it back in its place on the shelf, between the white spines, near the floor. Exactly where it had been. So that nobody, looking, would see that anything had changed.
She stood up.
Mister Gage was standing at the top of the iron stair, by the trapdoor, holding the lamp. He had watched it all. He did not write anything on his slate.
Elara went to him.
"You said you were there," she said quietly. "This afternoon. You said, I was there. Where, Mister Gage? Where were you?"
Gage looked at her for a long moment with his plain unfinished face.
Then he took out the slate. And the chalk. And he wrote, slowly, in his big round careful letters.
He turned it round.
Not yet.
"Why not?"
He wiped the slate. Wrote again. Turned it.
He is listening.
Elara looked at the words. Then she looked up. At the round window at the top of the white dome, high above them, black with night, with the rain running down it. And for one moment, just one, she thought she felt something. Not a sound. Not a sight. The feeling of being looked at. The feeling you have in an empty house that it is not empty.
She looked down again.
Gage had wiped the slate clean.
She went down the iron stair after him, round and round, in the swinging lamplight. And all the way down, she did not look back up at the dome. Not once.
But she felt it watching her go.
The Last Page of the Week
Night came down on the Saltmarsh slowly, the way it does in flat country, with nothing to hide behind.
First the gray sky went darker gray. Then the reeds went black, one long black line along the edge of the water, like a drawing in ink. Then the water itself went dark, all except a long pale strip down the middle, where the last of the light lay on it. And then that went too. And there was only the dark, and the sound of the river moving past the ribs of the dead ship, and the rain, which had thinned at last to a fine soft drizzle, so light that you could only hear it when everything else was still.
Far away, up the river, the city was a glow. A low orange smear on the bottom of the clouds. And in the middle of the glow, very small, like a pin stuck in a map, one light brighter than all the rest.
The first lamp on the Lantern Bridge.
The one that never went out.
Corin sat at the mouth of the cave under the deck and looked at it.
The fire had burned low behind him. Juno was asleep beside it. She had fallen asleep sitting up, with her back against a timber and her knees under her chin inside the huge coat, and then slid down sideways, slowly, until she was lying curled on the boards with her cheek on her arm. She was snoring a little. A small, soft, whistling snore, like a kettle a long way off.
Wat was not asleep. Wat was sitting in the boat, under the ribs, with the boat hook across his knees. Keeping watch. He had been sitting there since dark, without moving, a big black shape against the faint pale water. Corin did not think he would move until morning.
Corin had the Ledger open on his knee.
He had a pencil. Juno's. A short stub of one, from the pocket of the huge coat, chewed at one end. And he had the lamp from the boat, lit now, turned down very low and set on the boards beside him, with Wat's coat hung on a timber behind it so that no light would show from the river.
He turned the pages.
Not to the first page. Past it. Past all the pages he had written in the last six years. Past the lists, and the rules, and the names. Past FOR THE DARK. Past the page he had rewritten on Tuesday night, at Mrs. Marta's table, with his hand shaking. Past the line in somebody else's shaking hand that he had found there, that he did not remember writing. The dark is the day with its eyes shut. —K.
He stopped at that line. He looked at it for a while.
Then he turned to the first empty page.
And he wrote.
He wrote slowly. The pencil was short and blunt, and his fingers were stiff, and the page was still a little damp at the edges, so that the lead dragged on it. But he wrote it all. Everything from the week. Everything he could hold.
Wednesday. Night.
They came for you at eleven. Kross, and Wren Halloway, and Cadet Rowan Thorne, and one Eraser. Wren warned you. Wren lied for you. Wren sent them the wrong way. Do not forget what she risked. Bring her stone back.
Mrs. Marta is safe at Moll's. Rent is due Friday. Her floor is broken. That is your fault. Pay for it.
Rowan Thorne let you go. He has a letter from his sister. He said he would read it. Tell Lyra. Tell her gently.
You burned the oldest thing in you. Your first language. The words of Oakhaven. You burned it to knock a sword out of a boy's hand. You do not have the word for mother anymore. You do not have the true name of the place you were born. You will never have them again. You chose. Do not pretend you did not choose.
He stopped. He looked at that for a long time. His eyes stung. He did not cross it out.
He went on.
Under the city there is a wall. Near the Customs House outfall, in the big sewer, in a round room where the old gray stone is. It is covered in WE REMEMBER. And names. And little birds hanging on strings. And low down, near the floor, OAKHAVEN, and an oak tree, and a long row of marks.
Someone is still counting. Some of the marks are new.
Find out who.
He stopped again. He held the pencil above the page.
Then he wrote, smaller:
The U.W. stones are people. Too many people remembering the same thing at once, so it would not burn. One of them is cracked. It hums. Three notes. Garran the carter knows them. The fourth note is only for the end.
Do not open them.
Malakor is afraid of them because they are kind.
He looked at that for a while too. And then, after a long time, under it, very small:
I think I was afraid of the dark because I could not hear them anymore.
He did not know if that was true. He did not know where it had come from. It had come out of the pencil before it came into his head. But he left it.
The drizzle whispered on the deck over his head. Juno snored. The river went past.
Boy, said Malakor softly.
"Mm."
You left something out.
Corin looked at the page.
"I know," he said.
He turned back. All the way back. To the very first page. READ THIS FIRST. He looked at the five lines, with the last one blurred by the water.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
He thought about a desk made of glass. A gold tooth over a gap. A pair of white gloves, and dark eyes, and a voice saying I lost two thousand four hundred and five days, and a slice of orange peel, kept by a giant with an unfinished face, and given to him in a passage without a word. He thought of a girl with a gap in her teeth, saying his name. In a language he did not have anymore.
He did not know. He did not know. There was no memory. There was nowhere for a memory to be. There was only the word, faded, over his heart. And a feeling. Very small. Like a stone at the bottom of a well.
He turned back to the empty page. And he wrote, very slowly, pressing so hard that the pencil nearly went through the damp paper:
The Auditor of the Fourth Desk is called Elara.
She has a gap in her teeth.
She does not remember anything from before six years ago either.
He put the pencil down.
He sat with the Ledger open on his knee and his hand flat on the page, and he looked up the river. At the orange glow on the clouds. At the one small bright light in the middle of it.
It never goes out, Linus Hale had said. The first lamp on the bridge. Nobody knows who lights it. It's been burning since before the war. People say it's for somebody who never came home.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
Then he picked the pencil up again. And under the three lines, he wrote one more.
Go home.
And he closed the Ledger.
At that same hour, in the back room of the Watch house on Rope Street, where Sergeant Moll had put a mattress on the floor by the stove and a bottle of plum brandy on the table, Mrs. Marta was not asleep either.
She was sitting in Moll's best chair with her feet in a basin of hot water and her mother's old shawl round her shoulders, telling the night constable about her floor. She had been telling him about her floor for an hour. He had stopped trying to leave.
And so she was the one who heard the small sound at the door.
Not a knock. A scratch. A little dry scrape on the wood, low down, like a cat.
She stopped talking. She took her feet out of the basin. She went to the door, in her wet stockings, and opened it.
There was nobody there. Only Rope Street, black and empty and shining in the drizzle, with one lamp at the far end. And on the step, at her feet, a small square of paper. Folded. Very small. Into a hard tight square, the size of a fingernail.
Mrs. Marta picked it up. She unfolded it, slowly, with her blunt old fingers, under the lamp in the doorway. It took her a long time. It had been folded many times.
There were three lines on it. Written small and square and fast, as if by someone crouched over a lamp in a hurry.
HE LET HIM GO ON PURPOSE.
HE WANTS TO SEE WHO HE RUNS TO.
DO NOT GO TO THE BARGE. —W.
Mrs. Marta read it twice. Then she looked up and down Rope Street, at the black empty shining road and the one lamp. For a long time. With her small black currant eyes.
Then she went back inside, and shut the door, and locked it. And bolted it. And put Moll's best chair under the handle.
And she sat down at the table, and pushed the brandy away, and said to the night constable, in a voice he had not heard her use before:
"Fetch me Sergeant Moll. And a pen. And be quick about it, young man. We have a great deal to do."
And far out in the dark on the Saltmarsh, in the ribs of a dead ship, Corin Vane lay down beside the dying fire, with the Ledger against his chest and a girl's beach stone in his fist and three warm stones at his hip that hummed, very softly, a song that had no end.
And for the first time in six years, he was not afraid of the dark.
He was listening to it.
End of Arc One.
