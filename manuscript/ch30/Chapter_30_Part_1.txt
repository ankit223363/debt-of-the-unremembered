Chapter 30: Down Into the Dark, Part 1 of 5: The Ledge
The Ledge
The ledge was eighteen inches wide.
Corin knew that because he measured it. Not on purpose. His boots measured it for him, every step. The outside edge of his left boot was on the crumbling lip of the stone, over the water. The outside edge of his right boot was against the foot of the wall. And between them, eighteen inches of old black slimy stone, cracked and broken and furred with something soft and green that squeaked under his heels.
He went slowly.
He held the rod up in his right hand, high, so that its small blue-white light went ahead of him along the ledge. He kept his left hand on the wall. The bricks were cold and wet and rough, and every few steps his fingers found a gap where a brick had fallen out, or a soft place where the mortar had turned to mud. Once his whole hand went into a hole up to the wrist, and something in the hole moved away from his fingers with a dry little scuttle, and he pulled his hand back so fast that he nearly fell.
Rats. Only rats.
He went on.
The big sewer was the oldest thing he had ever been inside. He could feel that. It was not like the Ministry, which was old too, but old in a tidy way, with every stone in its place and every door with a number on it. This was old the way a cave is old. Old and wild and forgotten. The brick of the roof curved over him in a great black arch, so high that his light could only just find it. Twenty feet. Maybe more. And it was not one kind of brick. It changed as he walked. Here it was small and red and neat. Then, for a stretch, it was big and yellow and rough, laid in a different pattern, as if a different set of men had built it in a different hundred years. Then gray stone, great square blocks of it, with moss in the joins. Then red brick again.
Patched, he thought. Built and broken and built again. For hundreds of years. By people who had all forgotten each other.
And always, below him, the water.
It was brown in the blue light. Brown and fast and loud. It filled the whole bottom of the tunnel, from wall to wall, and it went past him in a long smooth rush, like the back of some great animal running. Things went by on it. Leaves. Sticks. A cabbage, turning slowly. A shoe. A dead pigeon with its wings spread out flat. A wooden spoon. A child's rag doll, face down, its yellow wool hair spread out round its head on the water. He watched that go past for a long time, until the dark took it.
The smell was very bad. He had stopped noticing it after the first hundred steps, the way you stop noticing a noise. But every so often a breath of air would come up off the water, warmer than the rest, and it would hit the back of his throat like a hand, and he would have to stop and lean on the wall and wait until his stomach settled.
Breathe through your mouth, said Malakor.
"I am."
Breathe through it more.
He almost laughed. It came out as a cough.
He was very cold. He had been cold in the drain, but then there had been no time to think about it. Now there was time. He was wet to the skin, all over, from his hair to his boots. His coat was so heavy with water that it hung on his shoulders like a sack of wet sand. His fingers had gone white and stiff and stupid. He could not feel his toes at all. And deep in his chest, behind his ribs, there was a shaking that would not stop. Not his hands. Not his knees. Something deeper. As if his whole body had become a bell, and somebody had struck it, and it was still ringing.
Keep walking, said Malakor. If you stop, you'll sit down. If you sit down, you won't get up.
He kept walking.
He counted steps, the way Wren counted. It helped. It gave the front of his mind something to hold on to, so that the back of his mind did not go wandering off into the dark after the word he had burned. A hundred. Two hundred. At three hundred the ledge broke off, suddenly, in a gap where the stone had fallen into the water, and he had to stand and look at it for a long time.
The gap was four feet wide. On the other side, the ledge went on. Below it, the water ran fast and brown and deep.
He jumped.
He landed on the far side on his knees, and one knee came down on the green slime and slid, and for one long terrible moment his whole left leg was over the edge, in the air, and the water was reaching up for it. He threw himself at the wall. His fingers found a crack. He hung on. And slowly, slowly, he pulled his leg back up onto the stone and lay there with his cheek against the wet brick and his heart going like a drum.
Well, said Malakor, after a while. That was very ugly.
"Thank you."
But you're on the right side of it.
Corin got up.
It was then that he noticed the stones.
He had forgotten them. He had forgotten everything that was in his pockets, in the cellar and the drain. The Ledger, flat against his chest, in the oilcloth Mrs. Marta had wrapped it in. Wren's beach stone, in his left coat pocket. The flask of tea and the bread and the cheese, in the bag across his back, probably ruined. And at his hip, in the little leather pouch on his belt, the black cloth square from Pell's drawer.
The three U.W. stones.
He had carried them for days now. He had never opened the cloth. He had never even taken it out of the pouch, except once, in his room, on the first night, to look at it. A square of black cloth, folded tight, sewn shut on all four sides with black thread in small careful stitches. And inside it, three hard round lumps, each the size of a cherry, each sealed in black wax. He had held the square up to the lamp, and turned it, and felt them move against each other inside, with a small dry click. And then he had put them away again. Because Malakor had gone very quiet when he took them out. Not his ordinary quiet. A different kind. Like a dog that goes still when it smells a wolf.
And he had not asked why. Because he had been afraid of the answer.
Now, on the ledge, in the dark, he could feel them.
They were warm.
Not warm the way a thing is warm when it has been in your pocket a long time, against your body. He had no warmth left in his body to give them. Warm on their own. A small, steady, living heat, coming through the wet leather of the pouch, through his wet coat, through his wet shirt, into the skin of his hip. Like three coals. Like three small creatures, curled up asleep, breathing.
He put his hand on the pouch.
The heat came up into his palm. And for a moment, just for a moment, he was not cold at all.
Don't, said Malakor.
The voice was sharp. Sharper than Corin had ever heard it.
"I'm not doing anything."
You're touching them. Don't touch them.
"They're warm."
I know they're warm, said Malakor. They've been getting warmer since we came into the big sewer. I've been listening to them. Leave them alone, boy. Please. Just leave them alone, and walk.
Corin took his hand away.
He stood on the ledge, with the water roaring past below him and the blue light shaking in his fist. He had never heard Malakor say please. Not once. In six years.
"What are they?" he said.
No answer.
"Malakor. What are they? What is U.W.?"
A long silence. Only the water. Only the drip of the roof, and the scuttle of rats, and somewhere far off ahead, a deep, slow, hollow booming, like a great door being shut, and shut, and shut again.
Walk, said the voice at last. Very quietly. Ask me on the river. If we get to the river, I'll tell you what I can.
Corin walked.
Birds on Strings
The booming grew louder as he went.
It came every thirty breaths or so. A deep hollow boom, far ahead, that he felt in his feet before he heard it with his ears. And after it a long rolling echo, going away up the tunnel behind him toward the Hill, like thunder going over a valley. He did not know what it was. He did not like it.
The river, said Malakor. Or the tide gates. The river comes up the sewer mouths at high water. They have iron gates to keep it out. When the tide pushes on them, they bang.
"Is it high water now?"
I don't know. I've been in a drawer for twelve years and a pocket for six. Nobody tells me the tides.
Corin went on.
The ledge got worse. In some places it was only a foot wide, and he had to go sideways, with his back flat against the wall and the rod held up by his face. In other places it was gone altogether for a few steps, and there were only the tops of old broken stones sticking up out of the water, black and round and slimy, like the backs of turtles, and he had to step from one to the next with his arms out and the brown water snatching at his boots.
And the water was higher. He was sure of it now. When he first climbed onto the ledge it had been a foot below the edge. Now it was eight inches. Now six. In some of the low places it was already lapping over the stone, so that he walked in it to his ankles, and he could feel it pulling at his feet.
Faster, said Malakor.
"I can't go faster."
Then go the same speed, but more.
The tunnel bent. It went round to the left in a long slow curve, and the brick changed again, from red to something older, a dark gray stone in long thin slabs, laid one on top of another with no mortar at all between them. And here the ledge got wider. Suddenly. Three feet. Four. Flat and dry and solid under his boots, like the floor of a room. And the roof came down lower, so that his light could reach it easily, and he saw that he was walking along the side of a sort of chamber. A wide place in the tunnel. Round. Like a room built into the sewer, with the water running through the middle of it.
He stopped.
He did not mean to stop. His feet stopped for him. And his hand, the hand on the wall, went still on the stone.
There was writing under it.
He lifted the rod and turned it toward the wall, and the blue-white light went across the old gray stone. And the wall was covered.
Covered. From the floor of the ledge to as high as a man could reach, and higher, as if people had stood on each other's shoulders to cut the top ones. Marks. Letters. Shapes. Thousands of them. Cut into the soft gray stone with knives and nails and chisels and the points of keys. Old. Very old. Some of them were so worn that his fingers could hardly find them. Some of them had green moss growing in the cuts. Some of them were sharp-edged and clean, as if they had been made last year. Last month.
And all of them, everywhere, again and again, the same two words.
WE REMEMBER.
He stood very still.
He knew those words. He knew them the way he knew the shape of his own front door. They were on the stairwell at No. 14. On the third landing. Low down, under the paint, so small and faint that he had walked past them for six years without seeing them, and then one morning his hand had found them in the dark, as if it had touched them a thousand times. He touched them every night, going up. The way you touch the head of a dog.
And here they were. A thousand times. Ten thousand. On the wall of a sewer, under the city, where nobody came.
He went closer.
The words were not alone. Round them, between them, under them, there were other things cut into the stone. Names. Hundreds of names. MERRIT. ADDY. JOSS AND HIS BROTHER. OLD TAMSIN WHO SANG. Short names. Country names. Not city names. And dates, some of them. And places. FROM HARROW FORD. FROM LITTLE WEIR. FROM THE MILL AT ASKE. Places he had never heard of. Places, he thought suddenly, that perhaps nobody had heard of anymore. Because perhaps they were not there.
And birds.
That was the strangest thing. Little birds. Cut into the stone between the names. Very simple. A round body, a small round head, a beak, a tail. The kind of bird a child draws. And each one hung from a line. A thin straight cut going up from the bird's back to nowhere. A string.
Birds on strings.
Hundreds of them. Hanging all over the wall, among the names, like the decorations on a tree.
He put out his hand and touched one. The cut was old and smooth, and the moss in it was soft and cold. And something moved in him. Not a memory. There was nowhere left for a memory to come from. Just a feeling. A little twist, low down, under his ribs. The feeling you get when you hear a tune and you cannot remember where you heard it, and you know that you did.
A tree. Hung with birds.
He had read that somewhere. Not long ago. In a letter. A letter from a dead man's sister. A tree hung with carved birds. We remember.
Pell. Pell Dorran's sister. Nan.
He stood in the blue light with his fingers on the little stone bird and could not breathe.
Boy, said Malakor. The water.
He did not hear.
He was looking at the wall. Moving the light along it, slowly. Name after name. Bird after bird. He did not know what he was looking for. He only knew that he was looking. The way you look through a crowd for a face, before you know whose face it is.
And then he found it.
Low down. Near the floor. So low that he had to crouch to see it. Cut small and deep and careful, by somebody who had taken a long time over it, and had known exactly how. In letters that had been gone over and over again, so that they would last.
OAKHAVEN.
And under it, a bird on a string. Bigger than the others. With a little round tree cut beside it. An oak, with its leaves done as tiny curved lines.
And under that, nothing. No names. No dates. Just a long row of short straight cuts. Tally marks. In groups of five. He counted them without meaning to. Ten. Twenty. Fifty. A hundred. More. He stopped counting. They went along the bottom of the wall in a long neat row, under the word, until the light could not follow them.
One mark for every one of them, he thought. One for every person.
And the cuts were not old.
That was what made his skin go cold all over, colder than the water. The word OAKHAVEN was old. The bird was old. But some of the tally marks, at the far end of the row, were new. Sharp. Clean. The stone inside them pale and fresh, not gray. As if somebody had come down here, into the dark, not so long ago, and crouched where he was crouching, and cut one more mark.
And one more.
And one more.
Counting.
Who? he thought. Who comes down here? Who's still counting?
Boy.
The voice was very sharp now. And it was right. Because Corin looked down, and the water was over his boots.
Not at the edge of the ledge. Over it. Right across the floor of the chamber, wide and flat and brown, an inch deep, two inches, running past his ankles toward the far side of the room. And from up the tunnel behind him, from the Hill, from the dark, there was a new sound coming.
Not a boom. A roar. A long, rising, rushing roar. Getting louder.
Corin stood up.
He put his hand flat on the word OAKHAVEN, just for a moment. The way you put your hand on a door you are leaving. And he took it away.
And he ran.
The Flood
He got as far as the end of the chamber.
The wide flat ledge narrowed again, and the gray slabs gave way to red brick, and the roof came curving down. And there, where the tunnel closed in, he looked back.
He should not have looked back. He knew that even as he did it. But his head turned on its own.
The water was coming.
He saw it in the blue light of the rod. A wall. Not a tall wall. Three feet high, perhaps. Four. But it filled the whole tunnel from side to side, brown and white and boiling, with a long curling lip on the front of it like the edge of a wave on a beach. It came round the curve of the chamber with a roar that shook the stones, and everything in front of it went under. The flat ledge. The wall of names. The little stone birds. The word OAKHAVEN, low down by the floor.
All of it. Gone under in one breath.
And then it was on him.
It hit him in the back, the way a horse hits you. He felt his feet leave the ledge. He felt the wall go away from his left hand. And then he was inside it. Inside the water. In the dark, in the cold, in the roar, turning over and over with no up and no down and no air, and something hitting his shoulder, and something hitting his knee, and the water pushing into his mouth and his nose and his ears, heavy and brown and tasting of rot and iron.
He held on to the rod.
It was the only thing he knew. Hold on to the rod. His fingers were locked round it. They were too cold to open. The little blue light at its tip was still burning, somewhere, a blur in the brown dark, going round and round. He could see it flashing past his face. Up. Down. Gone. Back.
Boy! Malakor was shouting. A thin far shout, like a man on a cliff calling to someone in the sea. Boy, kick! Kick up! Up is where the light goes when—
He did not hear the rest.
His head hit the roof.
Not hard. The water had carried him up, and the roof of the tunnel was there, curved and slimy, and his head bumped along it, and his mouth came out into a gap of air between the water and the brick. Two inches of air. Less. He sucked at it. He got half a breath and a mouthful of water, and coughed, and went under again.
The water threw him round a bend. Into a wall. Off it. He felt his coat catch on something and tear. He felt the bag on his back go, the strap snapping, the weight of it sliding away. Mrs. Marta's flask. Mrs. Marta's bread. Gone.
And under him, at his hip, something was burning.
He felt it even through the cold. Even through the roar. A heat. A real heat, not like before. Not three small coals. A fire. Pressing against the bone of his hip, through the leather of the pouch, so hot that it hurt, the way a hot pan hurts when you pick it up without a cloth.
The stones.
And with the heat, there was a sound.
Not the water. Under the water. Inside it. A sound he heard not with his ears but with the bones of his face and the roots of his teeth. Voices. Many voices. Hundreds of them. Very faint and very far away, like people singing in a house at the other end of a valley, on a summer night, with the windows open. He could not make out words. He could only hear that they were together. All at once. Saying the same thing, over and over.
And he wanted to listen.
That was the terrible thing. He was drowning, in a sewer, in the dark, and he wanted to stop and listen. He wanted to put his hand on the pouch and open it, and let the voices out, and hear what they were saying. Because he knew, in some deep place under all the empty rooms, that if he could only hear it, he would understand everything. Oakhaven. The man with the face that slid away. The locked door. The word that was gone. Everything. It was all there, in the voices. All he had to do was open the cloth.
His free hand went to his hip.
NO!
It was Malakor. And it was not a shout anymore. It was a scream. Corin had never heard the dead man scream. He had not known that he could. It went through his head like a nail through a board.
No! No, boy! Leave it! LEAVE IT SHUT! If you open it here, in this, you'll never come up again. You'll go down listening. They'll pull you down and you'll go gladly. I've seen it. I've SEEN it. Leave it shut!
Corin's fingers were on the pouch. On the flap. On the little horn button that held it closed.
Please, said Malakor.
Corin took his hand away.
It was the hardest thing he had ever done. Harder than burning the word. Harder than dropping into the drain. He took his hand away from the pouch, and made a fist, and pressed the fist flat against his chest, against the Ledger, where the word Elara had faded. And he held it there. And the voices went on singing, very far away, and he did not listen. He made himself not listen. He thought of the first page instead. READ THIS FIRST. Your name is Corin. He said it in his head, over and over, louder than the voices. Your name is Corin. Your name is Corin. Your name is—
And his back hit iron.
Hard. So hard that the last of the air went out of him in a great bubble. Bars. He could feel them across his back. Thick iron bars, set across the tunnel, and the water pouring through them and pinning him against them like a leaf against a fence. He turned. He got his hands round two of the bars. He pulled himself up. Up. Hand over hand, with the water pounding on his chest, and the bars slimy and cold and rough with rust. Up, and up.
And his head came out into the air.
Real air. A lot of it. He hung on the bars with his face turned up, and breathed, and breathed, and it was the sweetest thing he had ever tasted, even with the stink of the sewer in it. He breathed until his chest hurt. Until he was dizzy. Until the shaking in him was too big to hold and he hung there on the bars and shook and coughed and cried all at once, and did not care.
The flood went past him, under him, through the bars. Roaring. But lower now. Lower every moment. The front of the wave had gone on, and what came after it was only a fast high river, wide and brown, already beginning to drop.
He was alive.
Boy, said Malakor. Very quietly. Boy. Are you there?
"Yes."
Your name.
"Corin. Corin Vane."
A long breath. Like a man setting down something heavy.
Good, said the voice. Good. Don't ever do that to me again.
Corin laughed. It hurt.
He looked down at his hip. At the pouch. It was still there. Still buttoned. And it was cooling. He could feel the heat going out of it, slowly, the way the heat goes out of a stove when the fire dies. And the voices were going too. Further and further. Fainter and fainter. Until he could not tell if he was still hearing them, or only remembering that he had.
"You said you'd seen it," he said. "Somebody going down listening. You said you'd seen it."
Malakor did not answer.
"Malakor."
On the river, said the voice. I said on the river.
Corin lifted the rod. It was still lit. Still burning, small and blue, as if nothing had happened. He held it up, and looked through the bars, and saw where he was.
The bars were a gate. A great iron gate, set across the end of the big sewer, rusted brown, hung with weed and rags and sticks and straw. And beyond it, on the other side, there was no more tunnel. There was light. Gray daylight. Coming in through a wide, low, round opening, a stone mouth the size of a barn door, ten yards away. And through the mouth, he could see rain falling on water.
Not sewer water. Wide gray water, all pocked and dimpled with rain. Moving slowly. Going away, very wide, to a far gray shore with gray buildings on it and the masts of ships.
The river.
And above the round stone mouth, carved into the arch, in big square letters gone green with weed: CUSTOMS HOUSE OUTFALL. NO. 3.
