Chapter 10: The Address in the High Quarter, Part 5 of 5: The Height Chart
The Height Chart
They did not run. Footmen do not run, and ladies do not run, and in any case the stairs were too narrow and too steep to run on without breaking a neck. But they went quickly. Lyra went first, with the little blue box held tight against her chest under her cape. Corin went behind her, with the tall black hat under his arm, and his eyes on the passage behind them, and his ears on the house below.
The house below was silent. Nobody yet.
They had reached the head of the narrow stairs when Lyra stopped.
She was looking at the second of the three small white doors along the passage under the roof. Corin had not looked at it on the way up. It was the same as the other two: plain, low, painted white, with a white china doorknob. But Lyra was looking at it as if it had spoken her name.
"The nursery," she said.
"Lyra. They're coming."
"I know." She did not move. "One minute. Just one."
Corin looked at her face and knew that if he said no, she would go in anyway. He thought that it would take a woman in narrow heels and a big man in heavy boots a long time to cross the garden and climb five floors. He thought that one minute was probably safe. He thought that he did not, in fact, have any idea whether it was safe at all.
"One minute," he said.
Lyra opened the door.
It was a low room under the slope of the roof, wider than the sewing room, with two small square windows in the slope, both shuttered. Thin bars of gray light came round the edges of the shutters and lay across the floor. The room was empty, of course. Everything had gone. But the walls had been painted, a long time ago, with a picture that went all the way round the room: a green hill with sheep on it, and a blue river, and a castle with red flags, and a great white moon in a dark blue sky. Somebody had painted it for children. It was faded now, and cracked, and in places the plaster had fallen away and left gray holes in the sky. But the moon was still there. It was large and round and kind-faced, with two eyes and a smile, looking down from over the door.
"Rowan cried if you put him to bed without saying goodnight to the moon," said Lyra. "Every night. Goodnight, moon. Even when he was ten." She looked up at it. "Goodnight, moon."
The painted moon smiled down and said nothing.
Lyra had turned round. She was looking at the door frame. The inside of it, on the edge, where the door met the wall when it was shut. The paint there was white, like the door, and old, and a little yellow.
And it was covered with lines.
Pencil lines. Short ones, straight across the wood, each about the length of a finger. Dozens of them, one above the other, going up the frame from the height of Corin's knee to the height of his chest. And beside each line, in small pencil writing, there was a name and a date.
Corin knew what it was before Lyra said it. He did not know how he knew. Perhaps, somewhere in a life he did not remember, in a village with a big oak and a schoolhouse with a bell, somebody had once stood him against a door frame with a book flat on his head and drawn a line.
"Our heights," said Lyra. "Every birthday. Father did it. He'd stand us against the frame with his hand flat on our heads and draw a line with his pencil, and write the date, and then he'd say, Well, you'll be taller than me by Midwinter, every single year, even when we weren't." She laughed, a small cracked laugh. "Rowan used to stand on his toes."
She touched the lines. There were two sets of them. Side by side.
On the left, the lower set went up in neat steps, close together. Beside each one: Lyra. And a year. Lyra, two. Lyra, three. All the way up to Lyra, twelve, at about the height of Corin's shoulder. And then they stopped. There were no more after twelve.
"That was the last birthday before Mother went," said Lyra. "After that, Father didn't want to."
On the right, the other set went up beside them. Rowan, two. Rowan, three. Up and up, to Rowan, eight, in the same hand as Lyra's marks. And they did not stop there. They went on, in a different hand, a hand that was smaller and more careful and not Lord Thorne's at all. Rowan, nine. Rowan, ten. Up and up again, to Rowan, thirteen. Rowan, fourteen. The last one was the highest mark on the frame.
"I did those," said Lyra. "After Father stopped. Rowan wanted to keep going. So I did them. With his pencil." She touched the top line. Rowan, fourteen. "He's sixteen now. He'd be taller than this. Much taller. I haven't seen him in—" She stopped.
Corin was not listening.
He was looking at the third set.
It was not beside the others. It was on the other side of the door frame, the outside edge, the edge that faced into the passage. You would not see it unless you stood in the doorway and turned your head. It was older. The pencil had gone fainter, grayer, sinking into the wood the way old pencil does.
And it had been destroyed.
Not rubbed out. Not painted over, like the label on the bell board in the kitchen. Gouged. Somebody had taken a knife, a sharp knife, and cut into the wood beside every line, again and again, deep, hacking out the name next to each mark until there was nothing left but rough splintered grooves, like claw marks. The paint had come away in long curls. The pale wood underneath showed through, raw, and it had gone gray with years of dust, so the gouges were old too. Very old. Older than anything else on the frame.
But the lines themselves were still there. The height lines. The knife had gone for the names and the dates and left the little pencil lines alone, as if whoever held it could not bring themselves to cut those. Or had not thought they mattered.
Corin counted them. There were five.
The lowest was at about the height of Lyra's two. The highest was at about the height of Lyra's six.
And then nothing.
"Lyra," he said quietly. "Come here."
She came. She stood in the doorway beside him and looked where he was looking, and he heard her breath catch.
"What is that?" she said.
"Somebody else," said Corin. "Another child. Measured on this door. Five birthdays."
Lyra stared at the gouges. She put out her hand and touched the deepest one, the one beside the highest line, and drew her finger back as if the splinters had bitten her.
"No," she said. "There wasn't. There was only me and Rowan. I was the oldest. Rowan came four years after me. There was nobody else. There was never anybody else."
"Then who measured them?"
"I don't know." Her voice was rising. "I don't know. Maybe it was a cousin. A visitor. Somebody's child who stayed for the summer—"
"For five summers?" said Corin. "Five birthdays? On your nursery door?"
Lyra did not answer.
Corin looked at the five lines. He looked at how they sat beside Lyra's own marks, on the other side of the frame. He thought about it the way he thought about a page of the Ledger, slowly, line by line.
The third child's lowest mark was at the height of Lyra's two. But it was older. The pencil was grayer. It had been drawn first, while Lyra was still too small to stand against a door.
"Older than you," he said. "Lyra. Two years older, perhaps. This child was measured on this door while you were a baby in a cradle. Two years old, and three, and four, and five, and six." He looked at the highest gouge, beside the last line. "And then not here anymore."

Corin thought.
A pencil pressed into wood. A man's hand, pressing hard, writing a name beside a line on his child's birthday. Pressing so hard, perhaps, because he was proud. Pressing the lead into the soft grain of the old pine. Not just on top of it. Into it.
And a knife, years later, cutting the pencil away. Cutting off the top of the wood, the gray marks, the letters.
But not, perhaps, cutting all the way down. Not to the bottom of the pressure.
He took out the Ledger. He tore a page from the very back, a blank one, thin and soft. He laid it flat against the door frame, over the highest gouge, the one beside the last of the five lines, and held it there with the flat of his left hand. With his right hand he took the stub of pencil from the Ledger's spine, and turned it on its side, so the long edge of the lead lay against the paper, and he began to rub.
Very lightly. Back and forth. Back and forth. The way a child rubs a coin through a sheet of paper to make a picture of the king's head.
The paper went gray under the pencil. The rough splintered grooves of the gouge came through it as white shapes, ragged and jagged, like cracks in ice. The edge of the frame came through as a straight white line.
And in the bottom of the deepest groove, very faint, there was something else.
Not white. Darker than the gray. A place where the paper had pressed down into something under the splinters, a dent that the knife had not quite reached.
It was a letter.
Corin stopped rubbing. He held his breath. He lifted the paper away from the wood very slowly, and turned it to the thin light coming round the edge of the shutter.
The letter was faint. Broken. Half of it was lost in the jagged white of the knife cuts. But it was there. One single letter, at the beginning of where the name had been. The first one. Pressed deepest, because the first letter of a name is always the one you press hardest, when you are proud.
It was a W.
And beside it, a little way off, beside the end of the height line itself, where the knife had not bothered to cut at all because it was not part of the name, there was something else. Something small. A little shape that Lord Thorne, or someone, had drawn next to the last line, the way some fathers draw a star or a heart.
A leaf.
An oak leaf. Five round lobes on each side, and a little stem.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
Oak leaves, he thought. Oak-leaf buttons from the village past the end of the maps. And a W.
He did not know any W. He went through the Ledger in his head, every name he could remember from its pages, and he did not know any name that began with W. Wilkin, at the gate on Archive Lane, one-armed and old. That was all. And Wilkin was not a child who had been measured on a nursery door on the Hill.
There is another, said Malakor, very quietly. In the Ledger. A white coat. A notebook with something brown pressed in the front of it. A girl who hummed your three notes back to you, and knew them.
Corin's hand went cold on the paper.
Wren, said Malakor.
Corin did not say anything. He could not. He folded the paper with the rubbing on it, small, smaller, into a tight square. He put it in the secret pocket under his left arm, inside Abel's coat, with his list and the bell button, close to his heart.
Lyra was watching him.
"What is it?" she said. "What did you find? Corin. Tell me."
"A letter," said Corin. "One letter. And a leaf." He looked at her. "I don't know what it means yet. When I know, I'll tell you. I swear it."
She looked at him for a long moment. He saw her want to argue. He saw her decide not to. Not yet.
"Before the Midwinter dinner?" she said.
"What?"
"The Midwinter dinner. When I was five. When my aunt came." Lyra's voice was slow and strange. "I told you. The only time I ever saw her. I remember a lot of white silk and a smell I didn't like." She swallowed. "And I remember being angry. All through dinner. Because there was an empty chair at the table, next to mine, with a place set, a plate and a cup and everything, and nobody sat in it. And I asked who it was for. And Mother said, for someone who couldn't come." She looked at the five little lines on the door frame. "And my aunt laughed."
Below them, far below, five floors down, a door opened.
They both heard it. A heavy door, with a heavy lock, turning with a long grinding sound. Then the sound of it swinging inward. Then voices. Faint. A man's voice, low. Corin had not noticed it on the way up: a narrow gap in the wall, no wider than his shoulders, with no door, only a dark square opening and the top of a ladder-steep staircase going down into blackness.

Below them, on the great staircase, there were footsteps now. Coming up. Two sets. One heavy and slow, the stairs creaking under it. One light and quick, with a sharp little tick at each step, like a clock. They were on the first floor. The long gallery. They had stopped there.
The woman was talking. Corin could not make out the words yet. Only the voice. Light and high and clear, and very pleasant, and very sure of itself.
Like a kettle, Madame Hesper had said, when it starts to scream. But it was not screaming now. It was singing, almost. Chatting. The voice of a lady paying a call.
Corin put his finger to his lips. Lyra nodded.
