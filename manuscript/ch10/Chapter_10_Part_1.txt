Chapter 10: The Address in the High Quarter, Part 1 of 5: Sunday
Debt of the Unremembered · Arc 1
Sunday
He woke with something sharp against his neck.
It was the first thing he knew. Before the fish-shaped stain on the ceiling. Before the crack that ran from the stain to the corner like a river on a map. Before the weight of the book on his chest, rising and falling with his breath. Something small and hard and folded, pressing into the soft skin under his left ear, inside the collar of his shirt.
He sat up very fast. His hand went to his neck. His fingers found it: a little square lump, inside the doubled cloth of the collar, where some of the stitches had been picked open and then pushed roughly closed again.
Somebody had put something in his clothes while he slept.
His heart began to bang. He did not know where he was. He did not know whose room this was, with its sloping ceiling and its one small window full of pale gray light and its narrow bed that creaked when he moved. He did not know whose shirt he was wearing. He did not know his own name. He knew only that his body had woken up afraid, and that the small hard thing at his neck had made it more afraid, and that his right hand had already closed into a fist without being told.
Then he saw the board.
It was nailed to the wall above the bed, a square of old cork gone brown with age, and it was covered with papers. Pinned in rows. Some old and yellow and curling at the corners. Some new. All of them in the same big untidy hand. And at the very top, above the others, in letters so large that they filled the whole paper:
READ THE BOOK BEFORE YOU DO ANYTHING ELSE.
Under it, smaller: BOOTS FIRST. And under that, smaller still, a new one, pinned crooked, as if somebody had pinned it in the dark:
THE THING IN YOUR COLLAR IS YOURS. YOU PUT IT THERE. DON'T TAKE IT OUT. READ THE LAST PAGE.
He looked at that one for a long time.
Then he looked down at the book on the blanket. It had fallen off his chest when he sat up. It was small and thick and bound in dark brown leather, almost black, still stiff and new, and low on the cover, pressed into the leather, it said GROUNDING LEDGER. B. HOLT, STATIONER. LOWER DOCKS. And under that, VII.
He opened it. The first page said:
READ THIS FIRST Your name is Corin You were born in Oakhaven You had a younger sister named Elara You used to fear the dark
He read it. And something in his chest that had been clenched like a fist slowly opened.
Corin, he thought. I am Corin.
He read the rest. All of it. It took a long time, the way it always took a long time, though he did not know that it always did. The light in the little window went from gray to a paler gray. Somewhere below him in the house, a door banged and a woman's voice called out sharply, and a child's voice called back, and then there was the smell of porridge coming up the stairs, thin and warm and faintly burnt.
He read about the Ministry of Records and the Third Basement and the gray coat hanging on the hook behind the door. He read about a rod with a word on it and a voice inside it. He read about Lyra Thorne and her father who tapped numbers on the wall, and Juno who ran over the roofs, and Toby who called him Inspector. He read about a Bank with glass counters and a woman with a gold tooth who read people through their hands. He read about a dead man called Pell and a red sock and a girl called Nan.
And then he came to the last pages, the ones written last night, and he slowed down.
They were long. Very long. Crowded edge to edge, as if the hand that wrote them had been afraid of running out of paper before it ran out of things to say. A shop with a bell like a church. A tall thin woman with pins in her mouth. A green coat. A pocket for a list. Those We Owe. A card in violet ink with a C on it that had a hook on top. A pattern book. Healthy as a horse. Why the sea?
And at the very end:
Wrote my own list. THOSE I OWE. Nine names. Folded small. Sewed it into my shirt collar, left side, so it is not in the book. If I lose the book, I still have the list. On Monday, move it to the secret pocket in the green coat. DO NOT LOSE IT.
Sunday. Eighth bell. Under the Lantern Bridge. Toby. The button woman. He's bringing the receipt.
Corin put his fingers to his collar again. The little hard square was still there. He did not take it out. The board said not to, and the book said not to, and both of them were in his own hand, and he had decided, a long time ago, though he did not remember deciding, that his own hand was the only thing in the world he would always believe.
He got up. He put his boots on first, because the board said so. Then the rest.
The gray coat on the hook was heavy when he took it down. He felt the weight of it in different places as he put it on: something long and hard along the right side, under the arm; something small and warm low on the right, near the hip; and an empty pocket over his heart, waiting. He slid the book into the empty pocket. It fit exactly. The cloth there was worn into the shape of it.
Good morning, said a dry old voice, from the long thing at his ribs.
He went very still.
Malakor, said the voice. The rod. Page four. You always stop like that. Every morning. As if I were a snake in your coat. A pause. Go and eat something. The tall woman told you to. It is in the book.
He went down the narrow crooked stairs.
The house was waking up around him. On the fourth-floor landing there were words scratched on the wall, very old, that he could not read in the dim light. On the second-floor landing there was an empty chair by a round dirty window. At the bottom, in the dark hall, a door stood half open, and through it he could see a small warm kitchen with a black range, and a girl of about fourteen with a sharp face and a cap pulled down over her ears, sitting at a table and eating porridge very fast. A brass key on a string hung round her neck, outside her shirt.
She looked up and saw him.
"You've read it," she said, with her mouth full. It was not a question.
"Yes."
"You're Corin."
"I know."
"I'm Juno." She swallowed. "Gran says there's porridge if you want it, and you still owe her one silver, and she's not forgetting, and you're to sit down before you fall down."
A small bent old woman in a black shawl came out of the back of the kitchen with a pot in her hands, looked at him with one sharp gray eye, and put a bowl down at the end of the table without a word. Then she went back to the range. Corin saw that there was an old brown coat folded over the back of a chair by the fire, very neatly, the way you would fold something you meant to keep. He saw the old woman's hand touch it as she passed. Just once. Lightly.
He sat. He ate the porridge. It was thin and a little burnt and very hot, and it was the best thing he had ever tasted, though of course he could not remember tasting anything else.
When he had finished, Juno pushed her own empty bowl away and looked at him across the table with her head on one side.
"Gran read it," she said, quietly, so the old woman at the range would not hear. "The thing in Grandpa's pocket. Last night. After you went up."
Corin waited.
"She didn't tell me what it said." Juno's sharp face was very serious. "But she cried. And then she went and sat with Grandpa in his room for a long time, and she held his hand, and she sang him Gran's song. The one you hum." She looked at him. "And Grandpa smiled. He hasn't smiled at Gran in eight years, Corin. Not at Gran. He smiles at everybody, but not like that. Not at her."
She got up from the table and put her bowl in the basin.
"So whatever it said," said Juno, with her back to him, "it was a good thing."
Corin did not know what to say. So he said nothing. He wrote it in the Ledger instead, quickly, at the bottom of the new page: Mrs. Marta read the paper in Terrence's pocket. She cried. She sang him the three notes. He smiled at her. Then he put on his hat and went out.
The city was quiet. It was always quiet on a Sunday morning, the Ledger said, because the Ministry was closed and the Bank was closed and the factories on the far bank had banked their fires, and the only people about were the ones who had nowhere else to be. A thin cold mist lay on Tallow Street. The cobbles were wet from yesterday's rain, and black, and shining. A cat sat on a step washing its face. A milk cart went past at the end of the lane, its horse's hooves going clop, clop, clop very slowly, and the milkman was asleep on the seat with his chin on his chest, and the horse was finding its own way.
Corin walked down toward the river.
He could hear the bells as he went. Not the hour bells. The Sunday bells. From all over the city, from the tall white temples on the Hill and the small brown chapels of the Docks, the bells were ringing, each one a little out of step with the next, so that the whole sky seemed to be full of slow iron voices calling to each other through the mist and never quite agreeing. He counted the strokes on the nearest one, the one from the chapel on Tar Street. Seven. Then a long pause. Then the great bell of the Archon's temple, far up on the Hill, so deep that he felt it in his feet more than he heard it: eight.
He came round the last corner, and there was the river.
It was wide and brown and slow, and the mist lay on it like a sheet on a bed. On the far bank, the factory chimneys stood up out of the mist with no smoke coming out of them, like a row of dead trees. And across the water, from this bank to that one, there ran the Lantern Bridge.
It was old. Much older than the Ministry, the Ledger said, older than the Bank, older perhaps than anything else in the city. It was built of gray stone gone green with moss and black with soot, in seven great arches, each one taller than a house. Along its walls, on both sides, every few paces, there stood a little lamp in the shape of a house, with a stone roof and tiny stone windows. In the evenings the windows glowed blue. Now, in the morning, they were dark, and each little house had a crust of pigeon droppings on its roof like snow.
At the near end of the bridge, where it came down to meet the street, there was a wide flight of stone steps going down. The Lantern Bridge steps. Found by a bargeman below the Lantern Bridge steps, said a line in the Ledger, about a doctor called Aske who had drowned. Corin looked at the steps and did not go near the edge.
And under the bridge, in the shelter of the first great arch, there was the market.
He could hear it before he could see it. A low, busy hum of voices, and the clatter of things being set down on boards, and somebody laughing, and somebody else shouting about the price of a kettle. He went down the steps, and round the foot of the arch, and there it was.
The rag market.
It filled the whole space under the arch and spilled out onto the muddy shore on either side, where the river had gone down and left a wide brown beach of mud and stones. There were no proper stalls. There were blankets laid on the ground, and boards laid across barrels, and handcarts with their sides let down, and people sitting on upturned crates with trays on their knees. And on the blankets and the boards and the trays there was everything. Everything that anybody in the city had ever thrown away, or lost, or sold because they were hungry.
Old boots, tied together in pairs by their laces. Hats with no brims and brims with no hats. Coats. Hundreds of coats, hanging from a rope strung between two iron rings in the wall of the arch, so many that they looked like a crowd of headless men waiting for something. Chipped plates and cracked cups. Bent spoons. A birdcage. A box of doll's heads with no bodies, all looking up at the arch with their painted blue eyes. A pile of books with no covers. Keys, by the bucketful, keys to doors that nobody would ever open again. Spectacles with one lens. Clocks with no hands. Bottles. Rags, rags, and more rags.
It smelled of river mud and wet wool and old leather and fried onions, because at the far end of the arch there was a man with a brazier and a pan frying onions and sausages for the market folk, and the smoke from it went up into the curve of the arch and hung there, and made the whole place blue and dim, like a church full of incense.
And standing at the foot of the steps, hopping from one foot to the other in the mud, with a sack over his head against the damp and his thin pale face looking anxiously in every direction at once, there was Toby.
He saw Corin and his whole face lit up.
"Inspector!" he said. "You came! You remembered!"
"I didn't remember," said Corin. "I wrote it down."
"That's the same thing," said Toby. "For you." He patted the inside left pocket of his coat. "I've got it. The receipt. And the sock. I didn't want to leave the sock at home in case Mother washed it. She washes everything. She'd wash the cat if he'd let her."
He looked round at the blue, smoky, crowded arch, with all its blankets and boards and murmuring voices, and his face grew a little less bright.
"There's a lot of people," he said.
"Yes."
"How do we find her? The button woman?"
Corin looked at the market. He let his eyes go the way the tall woman had taught him yesterday, the way the Ledger said she had taught him. Not looking at anything. Letting everything come in from the edges.
And from the edges, after a moment, came a small sound. A very small sound, under all the voices. A dry, steady, clicking sound, like rain on a tin roof, or like somebody letting a handful of something small and hard run slowly through their fingers, over and over.
"That way," said Corin.
The Button Woman
She sat right at the back of the arch, where the stone curved down to meet the ground and the light was dimmest. It was the coldest place in the market, and the dampest, because the river seeped through the old stones there and ran down them in thin dark streaks. Nobody else had wanted it. She had it to herself.
She sat on a low three-legged stool, very upright, with a long wooden tray across her knees. Over her head and shoulders she wore a hood, a big, deep, brown hood like a monk's, made of some heavy cloth that had been patched with so many other cloths that it was more patches than hood. The hood hid her face. All Corin could see of her, as he and Toby came closer, was a pair of hands.
They were small hands, very old, with brown spots on the backs and swollen knuckles and skin as thin and shiny as the paper you wrap fish in. And they were moving. All the time. They dipped into the tray and came up full of buttons, and opened, and let the buttons fall back down through the fingers, click, click, click, click, like dry rain. Then they dipped again. And again. It was the sound Corin had heard from the steps.
The tray was the most beautiful thing in the market.
It was an old printer's case, the kind printers use to keep their little metal letters in, long and shallow, made of dark wood, divided into dozens and dozens of small square boxes. And in every box there were buttons. Each box had its own kind. Corin had never thought there could be so many kinds of buttons in the world. Horn buttons, brown and cream and striped like a tabby cat. Bone buttons, white and plain. Glass buttons in red and green and blue that caught the dim light and glowed like sweets. Jet buttons, black and shining, for mourning. Mother-of-pearl buttons, which shimmered pink and silver when the hands moved over them, like the inside of a shell. Wooden buttons. Leather buttons, woven like little baskets. Brass buttons with anchors on them, from sailors' coats. Brass buttons with crowns. Brass buttons with eagles, with stars, with lions, with ships. And tin buttons, cheap and gray, by the hundred, in a big box at the end.
The hands stopped moving.
"Gray coat," said a voice from inside the hood.
It was a thin, cracked, old voice, but sharp. It was not afraid. It was simply saying what it saw, the way you might say rain when it starts to rain.
"Yes," said Corin.
"Got nothing for a gray coat. Nothing burned, nothing bottled. Buttons, that's all. Buttons don't remember anything." The hands dipped into the tray again. Click, click, click. "Go on, now. You're in my light."
There was no light to be in. Corin stepped back anyway.
Toby did not. Toby came forward, past him, and crouched down in front of the tray in the mud, so that his face was level with the hood. He had taken the sack off his head. His fair hair was sticking up in wet tufts. Behind his thick spectacles he looked about twelve years old, and he looked at the buttons with such open delight that he seemed to forget, for a moment, why they had come.
"These are wonderful," Toby said. "Are they all sorted? By kind? Did you do all of this?"
The hands paused.
"Who else would do it?" said the voice.
"But there's so many. How do you keep them straight? Do you have a system? I work in a records office," said Toby, "well, a sort of records office, and we have a system, but it's terrible, honestly, nobody can find anything, and the forms are all printed wrong, and—" He stopped himself and went pink. "Sorry. I talk too much. Mother says."
Inside the hood, something happened. A small dry sound. It took Corin a moment to understand that it was a laugh.
"By the coat," said the voice.
"Sorry?"
"My system, boy. I sort them by the coat they came off." One old finger came out and tapped a box full of tin buttons. "Those are workhouse coats. Those," tap, a box of brass anchors, "are sailors. Those are Ministry," tap, a box of black-painted iron, chipped and rusty, and Corin looked at them and felt the buttons on his own coat suddenly heavy. "Those are Bank porters. Those are footmen, from the big houses on the Hill. Those are soldiers." Tap, tap, tap. "Every coat is somebody. You cut the buttons off, you still know who they were. That's my system."
"That's beautiful," said Toby, with complete sincerity.
The hood turned toward him. Slowly. And for the first time Corin saw a little of the face inside: a sharp chin, brown and wrinkled as a walnut, and a thin mouth with no lips to speak of, and, higher up, in the shadow, the glint of two very bright small black eyes, like a bird's.
"What do you want, records boy?" said the button woman. "You and your gray coat. You didn't come under the bridge on a Sunday to tell an old woman her tray is pretty."
Toby glanced back at Corin. Corin nodded.
So Toby told her. He told it the way he told everything, too fast, with his hands going up and down, and he got some of it in the wrong order and had to go back. A clock-mender called Faber on Hook Lane. Three brass buttons in a tin, with oak leaves on them. A watch mended in trade. The village with the big oak. Up past the end of the maps. A cart that comes down twice a year.
The old hands had gone very still in the tray.
"Faber talks too much," said the button woman.
"He didn't mean any harm," said Toby. "He liked the buttons. He wears them on his cardigan. He's very proud of them."
"Then he should keep his mouth shut and be proud quietly." The black eyes moved past Toby and found Corin, standing a little behind. "And you. What's a gray coat want with oak-leaf buttons? Going to take the cart for the Ministry? Going to burn the carter for what's in his head?"
"No," said Corin.
"Why should I believe you?"
Corin thought about that. He thought about the tall woman in the plum-painted shop, and the card in her window. No questions asked. He thought that the button woman had probably been asked a great many questions by men in gray coats, in her long life, and had probably not liked any of them.
So he did not ask her a question. He took off his hat instead, and crouched down in the mud next to Toby, so that he was lower than her, and he said:
"There was a man called Pell Dorran. He was a scribe. He died in a lane in the Ink Quarter, not long ago. He owed money to the Bank. If nobody paid it, the Bank was going to send a man to find his sister and make her pay it instead. His sister is twelve. Her name is Nan. She lives in a village that's not on any map, and she's practicing her letters, because she wants to come to the city and be a scribe like him." He looked at Toby. "Toby paid the debt. With his own money. He has the receipt. But nobody has told Nan. She doesn't know her brother's dead. She doesn't know she's safe. And we don't know where she is."
The button woman did not say anything.
Toby reached into his coat and took out the red sock. He held it out, on his flat palm, in the dim blue light under the arch. It was small and old and darned at the heel with blue thread, and there was nothing inside it now. Pell's money had gone into the Bank and out again. But the sock was still a sock.
"This was his," said Toby. "He kept his savings in it. In his boot. For her."
The old hands came out of the tray. Slowly. One of them reached out and touched the sock, just with the tip of one finger, the way you would touch a sleeping baby's cheek.
"Blue thread," said the button woman. "On a red sock." The finger traced the darn. "That's how they mend, up there. Always blue on red. They say red is for blood and blue is for the river, and you put the river on the blood to wash it." She took her hand back. "Stupid. Pretty. But stupid."
She sat for a long time without moving.
Then she said, very quietly, so quietly that Corin had to lean close to hear her: "His name is Garran. The carter. Big man. Doesn't talk. Comes down at the thaw and at the end of the harvest, with a cart full of whatever they've got that the city wants. Wool. Cheese. Honey. Old coats off the dead." Her mouth tightened. "He came three weeks ago. He won't come again till spring."
Toby's face fell.
"Spring," he said. "But that's months. That's—she'll be waiting all winter. She'll think he's forgotten her."
"Then she'll think it," said the button woman. "Lots of people up there think it. They're used to it." But the black eyes had softened a little in the shadow of the hood. "I keep a box. Under the tray. Folk in the city who came down from there, years back, sometimes they leave a letter in it. For their mothers, their brothers. Garran takes the box up when he goes. Brings it back empty." She looked at Toby. "You want to write to the girl, you write. Put it in the box. In the spring it'll go."
Toby looked as if somebody had given him the moon.
"I've already written it," he said. "Last night. Just in case." He pulled out a folded paper from the other pocket, a little crumpled, with For Nan Dorran written on the outside in his careful round clerk's hand. "I told her about Pell. I tried to tell it nicely. I told her he talked about her. And I told her the debt's paid and nobody's coming. And I copied out the receipt, the words on it, so she'd know it's true." He hesitated. "And I told her that if she still wants to come to the city when she's older, and be a scribe, she should come to the Ministry of Records and ask for Toby, and I'll help her."
The button woman held out her hand. Toby gave her the letter. She did not look at it. She reached under the tray, and Corin heard the scrape of a small wooden lid, and the letter was gone.
"Spring," she said.
"Spring," said Toby. He looked happy and sad at once.
Corin had been looking at the tray all this time. At the boxes and boxes of buttons. At the one marked, in the old woman's system, soldiers. He looked for oak leaves in it and did not find any.
"The oak-leaf buttons," he said. "Faber's. Do you have any more?"
"No." She said it too fast.
"Were they off a soldier's coat?"
The button woman said nothing. Her hands had gone back into the tray, but they were not moving.
She is lying, said Malakor softly, from his ribs. Or not lying. Holding something. Her left hand is closed. Look.
Corin looked. The old woman's left hand lay in the corner of the tray, over one particular box, right at the end of the top row. A box by itself, a little apart from the others, with a scrap of black cloth tucked over it like a lid. Her fingers were curled over the cloth. Holding it down.
He did not ask about it. He sat back on his heels in the mud and looked at the river instead, through the great stone curve of the arch, where the mist was beginning to lift off the water in long white rags. He did not know what to say next. He was tired, suddenly. Tired the way he was always tired, the Ledger said, by the middle of the morning, from carrying so much of himself around on paper.
And without knowing he was doing it, he began to hum.
Three notes. Very soft, through his nose. Up. And up. And up. And stopping, as if they were asking a question.
The button woman's head came up so fast that the hood fell back.
Her face was very old. It was brown and wrinkled all over, like a winter apple, and she had almost no hair, only a few white wisps flat against her skull, and her black bird's eyes were wide open now, wide and shining. She was staring at him.
"Where did you get that?" she said.
Corin stopped humming. "What?"
"That. What you just did. The tune." Her voice had gone high and thin. "Where did you learn that?"
"I don't know," said Corin. "I don't remember. I hum it. I've always hummed it, the book says. I don't know what it is."
The old woman looked at him for a long, long time. Toby looked from one of them to the other with his mouth open.
"Garran hums that," said the button woman at last. "The carter. When he's loading. When he thinks nobody's listening. All of them do, up there. The old ones." She swallowed. "They hum it to the babies. It's the first thing they teach them, before mother, before bread. Three notes going up." She was quiet. "And then there's a fourth. Coming down. But they don't sing the fourth to the babies. Only at the end."
"At the end of what?" said Toby.
The button woman did not answer him. She was still looking at Corin. And slowly, very slowly, her left hand lifted off the little box at the end of the tray, and twitched away the scrap of black cloth.
There was only one button in the box.
It was brass. Old, and worn smooth at the edges, and dark with years of being handled. It was a little larger than the others. And stamped into the face of it, very small and very clear, there was a shape. Not an oak leaf.
A bell. A little bell, the kind that hangs in a frame on the roof of a schoolhouse, with a rope going down from it.
Corin's chest went tight. He did not know why. He wrote nothing down. He could not move his hands.
A schoolhouse. A bell. An oak. A line in his own handwriting, in the Ledger, from a warm bright hall that smelled of polish and crying.
"Garran's mother sent it down," said the button woman, "years ago. The first year he drove the cart. Off a coat. A teacher's coat, he said. The teacher up there. She said to give it to anybody who came asking about the oak." She looked at the button in its box. "Nobody ever came. Not in all these years. Folk from up there who come to the city don't ask about home. They don't dare. They're afraid that if they say the name out loud, somebody will hear."
"What name?" said Corin. His voice did not sound like his own. "What is the name of the village?"
The old woman's face closed like a door.
"No," she said. "Not here. Not under a bridge, in the middle of a market, with a gray coat on you and the Ministry three streets off. Names get heard." She picked the bell button up out of its box between her thumb and her finger, and held it out to him. "You want the name, you go and ask Garran in the spring. Or you find it yourself, wherever you put it. It's in you somewhere, gray coat. You hum it."
Corin held out his hand. She dropped the button into his palm.
It was warm. Warm from her fingers, he told himself. Only from her fingers.
But in the black square at his hip, the three stones marked U.W. grew warm too. All at once. As if something had answered them.
"How much?" he said.
"One copper," said the button woman. "Same as everything." She held out her other hand. "I don't give things away, gray coat. People don't value what they don't pay for."
He gave her a copper. He had six left until Friday, after paying Juno, and no silver at all, and he still owed Mrs. Marta one. Now he had five.
She put it in a pocket somewhere in the depths of the patched brown hood, and pulled the hood back up over her head, and her hands went back into the tray. Click, click, click, click. As if nothing had happened.
They were three steps away, at the foot of the great stone arch, when she spoke again, without looking up.
"Records boy."
Toby turned. "Yes?"
"Your letter," said the button woman. "It's a good letter. I can tell by the weight." Click, click. "She'll keep it. They keep letters, up there. They've got nothing else to keep."
Toby went very red and said thank you three times, and then fell over a pile of boots on the way out, and said sorry to the boots.
Outside, on the stone steps, in the thin gray light of the Sunday morning, Corin sat down and took out the Ledger and wrote it all. The printer's case. Every coat is somebody. Garran the carter, at the thaw and the harvest. The letter box. The three notes. All of them do, up there. There's a fourth, coming down. Only at the end. The bell. A teacher's coat. Give it to anybody who came asking about the oak.
Names get heard.
He looked at the bell button in his palm for a long time.
Then he did something he did not understand. He did not put it in the black cloth square with the stones, though the stones were warm and seemed to want it. He did not put it in the Ledger. He worked open the stitches in his shirt collar again, on the left side, and slid the little brass bell in next to the folded square of his list, so that the two of them lay together against his neck.
Those I owe, he thought. And whoever this was.
Toby sat down next to him on the cold step and looked out at the river.
"Inspector," he said, after a while. "What happens at the end? When they sing the fourth note?"
"I don't know," said Corin.
But his chest hurt, the way the Ledger said it hurt when the busker played on the Lantern Bridge. And he thought, though he did not say it, that he could guess.
