Chapter 10: The Address in the High Quarter, Part 3 of 5: Crown Walk
Crown Walk
The Hill steps began at the top of Market Row, where the street ran out against a wall of old gray stone, and they went up.
They went up for a long time. They were wide steps, shallow and worn, made for ladies in long skirts and gentlemen with walking sticks, and every fifty steps there was a flat landing with a stone bench where you could sit and get your breath back and look down at the city you had left behind. Corin did not sit. Footmen did not sit. He walked two steps behind Lyra and a little to the left, holding the duck-handled umbrella closed under his arm because it was not raining, and he watched her shoulders, and he did not look at anything else.
But he saw everything anyway. From the edges of his eyes. The way the tall woman had taught him.
He saw the city fall away below them as they climbed. First the roofs of Market Row, slate and tile and tar paper, patched and crooked, with smoke coming out of a hundred little chimneys. Then the Docks beyond them, a brown smudge along the river, all cranes and masts and warehouses. Then the river itself, wide and slow and the color of tea, with the Lantern Bridge laid across it like a gray belt with seven buckles. And on the far side, the factories, and the far side's own crowded streets, and past them, very faint in the haze, low brown hills going away and away.
He saw how the steps changed as they went up. At the bottom they were cracked, and green with moss in the corners, and there was rubbish blown against the walls. Halfway up, the moss was gone, and the rubbish was gone, and somebody had swept them. By the top, they were so clean that they shone, and there were iron lamps on posts at every landing, and the posts were painted black and the lamps had brass tops, and in each lamp there sat a gemstone as big as a hen's egg, unlit now in the daylight, but waiting.
And he saw the air change. That was the strangest thing. At the bottom, the air smelled of coal smoke and fish and wet streets. Halfway up it smelled of nothing. At the top, it smelled of cut grass and roses. As if the people up here had bought the air too, along with everything else.
They came out at the top onto a wide street lined with trees.
The High Quarter.
Corin had read the words in the Ledger. The High Quarter. The Hill. He had read that Lyra had lived here until she was eighteen. He had read that her house on a street called Crown Walk had been sold at auction, with everything in it. But reading about it was not the same as seeing it, and he had not seen it in daylight, or if he had he did not remember.
The street was very quiet. That was the first thing. There were no carts here, no shouting, no children running. The houses stood back from the road behind tall iron railings, and the railings were painted black, and the top of every iron bar ended in a little gold spear point. The houses were made of white stone. All of them. Pale smooth stone the color of milk, so clean that it hurt his eyes a little in the gray light. They were tall, four storeys and five, with long windows in rows, and every window had a window box under it, and every window box was full of flowers. Red flowers. White flowers. Yellow. Even now, in the autumn, even after the rain.
At the end of the street, where it met another street, a man in a yellow coat with silver buttons was walking two dogs, small white fluffy dogs with blue ribbons in their hair. A nursemaid in a long gray dress and a white cap was pushing a big black pram along the pavement, slowly, with her eyes on the ground. A carriage went past, very slow, very quiet, its wheels rolling on rubber so that they made almost no sound at all, and a gentleman inside it with a newspaper did not look up.
And far away, at the very top of the Hill, above all the white houses and the trees, there rose the Archon's temple. Corin could see it over the rooftops. A great white dome, and above the dome a tall spire, so tall and so thin that it seemed to go up into the low clouds and disappear, like a needle going into cloth. At the top of the spire, the Ledger said, there was a great stone eye, the same as the eye carved over the doors of the Ministry. Corin could not see it from here. But he felt it. He felt the whole Hill looking down from under it.
Lyra walked through all of this as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world. Head up. Hands folded. Not fast. Not slow.
She turned left, and then left again.
"Crown Walk," she said quietly, without turning her head. She did not need to say it. The name was cut into a white stone tablet set into the corner railing, in letters picked out in gold.
Crown Walk was a crescent. A curve of tall white houses, fourteen of them, all joined together in one long bending terrace like the inside of a horseshoe, every one the same height and the same width and the same pale stone. They looked across a narrow cobbled road at a garden. The garden was in the middle of the curve, and it was round, and it was full of old trees, plane trees and limes, with their leaves going yellow, and lawns of short, very green grass, and gravel paths, and a little white stone fountain in the very center that did not seem to be running. The whole garden was closed in by more of the black railings with gold spear tips, and there was one gate, and the gate was locked with a big brass padlock.
"Only the houses have keys," said Lyra, very low, as they walked along the pavement on the house side. "One key each. We had ours on a ring by the front door. I used to play there with Rowan. Under that tree. The big one." She did not point. She did not look. "Rowan fell out of it when he was six and broke his arm, and Father carried him home, and I ran behind and cried louder than Rowan did."
Corin looked at the big tree, from the edge of his eye. It was a lime tree, very old, with a fat crooked trunk and branches that came down low enough for a child to reach. Under it the grass was worn a little, in a patch. As if other children played there now.
The houses had numbers on them, in brass, on the fanlights over the doors. They passed Number 14 and Number 13 and Number 12. Each one had a different color of door, and a different brass knocker, and a different set of flowers in the window boxes. On the steps of Number 12 a maid in a black dress was scrubbing the white stone with a brush and a bucket. She looked up at Lyra, and then, quickly, at Corin's green coat, and then down again at her bucket. But her brush had stopped.
Then they came to Number 11.
Corin knew it before he saw the number. He knew it by the smell.
Lilies. A great wave of lilies. It came out from the house and down the steps and across the pavement, so thick and sweet that it was almost like walking into a warm wet cloth. In every window box, all along the front of the house, on every floor, there were lilies. White lilies, tall and stiff, with long curling petals and yellow tongues in their throats. Hundreds of them. Far too many for one house. They crowded the window boxes so tightly that they leaned out over the edges and dropped their orange pollen on the white stone underneath, so that every windowsill had a little stain of orange dust under it, like rust.
The front door of Number 11 was painted white. The knocker on it was brass, in the shape of a lily. And on the top step, beside the door, very straight and very still, there stood a footman.
He was all in white. White coat, white breeches, white stockings, white gloves. Even his hair was white, though he was not old; it had been powdered, in the old-fashioned way, so that it looked like a wig. Only his shoes were black, and they had been polished until they shone like glass. He stood with his heels together and his hands behind his back and his eyes on a spot just above the street, and he did not move.
As Lyra passed, his eyes did not move either. They did not move to Lyra.
They moved to Corin. To the green coat. Just for a moment. Just a flick, like a lizard's tongue. And then back to the spot above the street.
Corin did not change his face. He walked on. Two steps behind. A little to the left.
But in his ribs, Malakor said very softly: He saw. He knows that green. By tonight, your lady's aunt will know that a footman in Thorne green walked past her door at noon.
That's what Lyra wants, Corin thought.
Is it? Then she is braver than she looks. A pause. Or more foolish.
Number 10. Number 9. Number 8. Lyra did not slow down. Number 7. Number 6. Number 5. Corin saw her hands, folded in front of her, tighten a little on each other. Number 4.
And then Number 3.
Lyra stopped.
She did not mean to, Corin thought. Her feet stopped by themselves. She stood on the pavement in her mother's gray gown and looked up at the house where she had been born, and Corin stood two steps behind her, and they did not say anything.
It was the same as the others. The same pale stone, the same height, the same long windows in rows. But it was not the same at all. It was dead.
Every window had its shutters closed. Not the pretty painted shutters of the other houses, which were only for show and were folded back against the walls. Real shutters, heavy wooden ones, the kind that are meant to keep things out, and they had been closed and barred from the inside, so that the house seemed to have its eyes shut. The paint on them had once been green, Thorne green. It had faded to the color of an old leaf, and peeled, and in places it hung in long curling strips.
The window boxes were empty. Not empty of flowers. Empty of everything except earth, gone gray and hard, with a few brown stalks sticking up out of it like the bones of very small birds.
The white stone was not white. Two years of rain and soot had run down it from the roof and left long dark streaks, like the marks of tears down a dirty face. In the corner by the front steps, a little tree had seeded itself in a crack and was growing there, a thin stubborn sapling with three yellow leaves on it.
The front door was painted green, too. Or had been. There was no knocker on it. There had been one; Corin could see the four small holes where it had been screwed to the wood, and the pale shape on the paint where it had hung for so many years that the sun had not been able to reach underneath. The shape of a rose. With a thorn wound round it.
And under the pale rose, screwed to the door with four bright new brass screws, there was a plate.
It was brass too, and new, and very well polished. Somebody came to polish it, Corin thought. Somebody came to this dead house and polished this one thing and nothing else. The letters on it were deep and black and very clear.
HELD IN TRUST. THE OBLIVION BANK. FOURTH DESK. NO ADMITTANCE WITHOUT WRITTEN AUTHORITY.
Lyra read it. Corin saw her read it. He saw her shoulders go stiff and her chin go up.
"Fourth Desk," she said, very quietly. "That's her. The woman with the gold tooth. The Auditor. Isn't it? That's her desk."
"Yes," said Corin.
"She owns my house."
"She's holding it," said Corin. "For somebody. In trust. That's what it says."
"For who?"
Corin did not answer. He was looking at the lock.
It was an old lock, big and black, set into the green door under the brass plate. And around the keyhole, in the faded green paint, there were scratches. Fresh ones. Bright little lines in the paint where the green had been scraped away and the pale wood showed through. The kind of scratches a key makes when the person holding it is in a hurry, or in the dark, or does not know the lock well, and misses the hole, and misses it again.
They were new. A week old. Perhaps less.
Someone has a key, said Malakor. And they do not come often enough to know the lock.
At the end of the crescent, behind them, a door opened. Corin heard it. He heard the soft click of a latch, and then the sound of two women's voices, light and high and polite, coming down a set of steps onto the pavement. Ladies. Going out to make calls. He heard their skirts whisper on the stone.
He heard them stop.
And then he heard one of them say, not quite quietly enough: "Look. Is that—? That's Thorne green. On a footman. In front of Number 3."
And the other one said: "It can't be. They're all gone. The girl lives over a stable, Harriet, I told you."
And the first one said: "Well, she's not over a stable now."
Lyra did not turn round. She did not move at all for a long moment. Then she lifted her chin, very slightly, and she turned, not toward the ladies but away from them, toward the end of the crescent, where a narrow lane ran off between Number 1 and the next street.
"The mews," she said, in a low clear voice, as if she were speaking to her servant about nothing at all. "I want to look at the mews, Vane. Come."
"Yes, my lady," said Corin.
And behind them, the ladies' voices started up again, faster now, and higher, like birds when a cat has walked past.
The Mews and the Third Stone
The mews was another world.
It ran along the back of Crown Walk, behind the great white houses, a narrow cobbled lane with a gutter down the middle of it. On one side were the high back walls of the houses' gardens, old brick, dark red and crumbling, with ivy hanging over the tops. On the other side were the stables and the coach houses: low buildings of the same red brick, with big double doors painted in the colors of the houses they belonged to, and little windows above, where the grooms and coachmen slept. It was the place where the High Quarter kept its horses and its carriages and the people who looked after them. It was not white. It was not quiet. It smelled.
It smelled wonderful, Corin thought, after the lilies. It smelled of horses and straw and leather and saddle soap and wet cobbles, and of somebody's dinner cooking in one of the rooms upstairs, onions and bacon. A row of old iron horseshoes hung from nails on one of the stable doors, rusting. A cat sat in a window licking its paw. Somewhere a horse stamped, and blew through its nose, and shifted in its stall with a creak of wood.
Lyra stopped at the end of the lane and breathed in, and for a moment her face changed. It went soft and young and tired.
"It smells like Patience," she said. "Like home." She laughed, a small, odd laugh. "Isn't that strange? I was born up there," she tipped her head back toward the white houses, "and I've only lived over the stable for two years. But this is the part that smells like home."
Halfway down the mews, outside a coach house with doors painted a deep cherry red, a groom was washing a carriage. He was a short broad man with his sleeves rolled up past his elbows and a bucket at his feet, and he was sloshing water over the wheels with a big sponge and whistling. He stopped whistling when he saw them. He looked at Lyra's gray gown and Corin's green coat, and his sponge stopped halfway to the wheel.
Corin felt the moment go tight, the way a rope goes tight.
Then Lyra did something he did not expect. She did not walk past the groom with her eyes on the cobbles, the way the ladies on Crown Walk would have done. She walked straight up to him. And she said, "Good afternoon, Mr. Tibbs. Is Dumpling still with the Langleys? Is her leg better?"
The groom's mouth fell open.
Then he dropped the sponge in the bucket with a splash, and pulled off his cap, and said, "Miss Lyra?"
"Hello, Mr. Tibbs."
"Miss Lyra Thorne. Well, I never. Well, I never." He stood twisting his cap in his wet red hands. "Look at you. All grown up. And in your mother's—well. Well, I never did." He swallowed. "Dumpling's leg's right as rain, miss. She's out in the paddock behind the Langleys' summer house, eating her head off. Fat as butter." His eyes went past her to Corin, to the green coat, and something went over his face, quick, like a cloud over a field. "And who's—?"
"This is Vane," said Lyra. "He walks with me now."
Mr. Tibbs looked at Corin for a long moment. At the green. At the dark band on the left sleeve. He knew what it meant. Corin could see him know it.
"Abel's coat," said Mr. Tibbs, very low.
"Yes," said Lyra.
Mr. Tibbs nodded slowly. He put his cap back on. He picked up his sponge. He turned back to the cherry-red carriage, and he began sloshing water over the wheel again, and he said, without looking at either of them, in a voice loud enough to carry along the mews: "Shocking weather we've been having. Shocking. Can't see a thing in it. Couldn't tell you who's been up and down this lane all week, not if you paid me."
And he started whistling again.
Lyra walked on. Corin followed. Two steps behind.
Interesting, said Malakor. The servants still love her. That is worth more than a house.
The back wall of Number 3 was the oldest-looking wall in the mews. The ivy on it had not been cut in two years, and it had grown wild, so thick and dark and heavy that it pulled at the top of the wall like a weight, and some of the bricks up there had come loose and fallen into the lane. They lay among the cobbles, half buried in old leaves. In the middle of the wall, deep in the ivy, there was a door.
It was a small door, narrow and arched at the top, made of thick planks of wood bound with black iron. It had been painted green once, like everything else at Number 3, and the green had gone gray. It was set high in the wall, so that you had to climb up to it, and in front of it, rising out of the cobbles of the lane, there were three stone steps.
They were worn in the middle, the way old steps are, into a shallow dip like the inside of a spoon. Moss grew in the cracks between them. The bottom one was cracked right across.
"The third stone," said Lyra.
She stood looking at the steps. Her hands were folded in front of her very tightly.
"Bertram told me," she said. "The night before he left. We were in the kitchen at the stable. Father was asleep. Bertram had his coat on, and his bag by the door, and he said, Miss Lyra, if you ever need to go back, the garden door on the mews side. The key is under the third stone of the step. They will never think to look." She swallowed. "And then he said, I'm sorry, Miss Lyra. For all of it. I'm sorry. And I didn't know what he meant. I thought he meant he was sorry for leaving. And he went out, and he didn't come back."
"Which is the third stone?" said Corin. "From the top or the bottom?"
Lyra looked at him. Then she gave a small, shaky laugh.
"I don't know," she said. "I never asked him. I never thought I'd dare to come."
Corin looked up and down the mews. Mr. Tibbs was still washing his carriage, with his back to them, whistling loudly. The cat had gone from the window. There was nobody else.
He went down on one knee on the wet cobbles, in Abel's beautiful green coat, and took hold of the edge of the top step.
It was a single slab of stone, about the length of his arm and the width of his two hands, set into the wall at the back and resting on the step below at the front. He pulled. It did not move. He pulled harder. Nothing.
He tried the bottom step instead. The cracked one. This one moved. Just a little. When he pushed on one end, the other end lifted a fraction of an inch out of the cobbles, with a small wet sucking sound.
"Bottom," he said. "Counting up from the lane. If you were coming in from the house, it would be the third."
"Bertram always came in from the house," said Lyra.
Corin got his fingers under the lifted end. The stone was cold and slimy with moss. He braced his knee and heaved. The slab came up, slowly, heavily, grinding against its neighbor, and he tipped it back against the step above.
Underneath was a hollow. Somebody had dug it out of the earth under the stone, a long time ago, and lined it with flat bits of slate to keep the wet out. And in the hollow, there was a package.
It was wrapped in oilcloth, the black waxy cloth that sailors use for keeping things dry, and tied with tarred string. It was small, about the size of a man's hand. Corin lifted it out and gave it to Lyra without opening it. It was hers.
She stood holding it in both hands for a long time. Then she untied the tarred string, slowly, because her fingers were not quite steady, and folded back the oilcloth.
Inside there was a key. A big iron key, old and black, as long as her hand from wrist to fingertip, with a loop at the top and a heavy toothed blade. Tied to the loop with a bit of green thread there was a little card label, the kind you tie to luggage. On it, in an old man's careful upright hand, it said:
Garden door. For Miss Lyra. B.
And under the key, folded small inside the oilcloth, there was a slip of paper.
Lyra unfolded it. Corin watched her face as she read it. He saw it go still, and then puzzled, and then pale.
She held it out to him.
It was the same careful upright hand. Only a few words. Written quickly, as if Bertram had been afraid somebody would come into the kitchen and see.
Miss Lyra — They will tell you the clock was sold. It was not sold. I took it back before the auction. It is with N. on Gutter Street. I could not pay to get it out again. Forgive me. Do not let her have it. — B.
Corin read it twice. Then a third time.
The clock was withdrawn before sale, said a line in the Ledger, copied from an auction book in a shop on Market Row. By the vendor's agent, B.
Bertram had taken the clock. The clock with the twelve stones, the one Lyra's grandfather had built, the one that stood in the long gallery. He had taken it out of the house before the auction men could sell it, and he had put it somewhere. With somebody called N. On a street called Gutter Street. And then he had needed money, or could not pay, or something had happened, and he had not been able to get it back.
Do not let her have it.
"Her," said Lyra. Her voice had gone thin. "Don't let her have it. Who is her?"
Corin did not answer. He was looking past her, down the mews, toward the far end of the lane, where it came out onto the street behind the crescent. From here, if you leaned a little, you could see the corner of the back of Number 11. You could see its own back wall, taller than the others, and newer, and its own garden door. White. And over the top of the white wall, leaning out, nodding a little in the wind, there were the heads of lilies.
Lyra followed his eyes.
She did not say anything. She folded the slip of paper small, very small, and put it inside the collar of her gray gown, against her neck. The same place, Corin thought, where he had kept his list yesterday. As if she had known.
Then she climbed the three stone steps, two of them and the empty hollow where the third had been, and put the big iron key into the lock of the garden door.
It was stiff. She had to use both hands. It turned with a long grinding shriek that made Mr. Tibbs, far down the lane, stop whistling for a moment, and then start again, louder.
The door opened inward, scraping on stone.
Beyond it, there was the garden.
Corin had read about the rose garden in the Ledger. The room at the top of the house, over the rose garden. Gold light. He had imagined it, he supposed, the way you imagine any garden: neat beds, and gravel paths, and roses in rows.
It was not like that now.
It was a wilderness. The roses had not been pruned in two years, and they had gone mad. They had climbed everything there was to climb. They had climbed the walls, and the old sundial in the middle of the lawn, and the wooden bench under the pear tree, and each other. They had made great tangled thickets taller than a man, all thorns and long green whips and dead brown leaves, and in among the thorns, here and there, even now in the autumn, there were roses still flowering. Small ones. Wild-looking. Red and white and a deep dark crimson nearly black, their petals spotted with rain. The lawn had grown into a meadow, knee-high, gone to seed, and gray-gold and wet. The gravel paths were green with weeds. A little stone fishpond by the wall was full of black water and dead leaves, and a stone girl stood in the middle of it with a jug on her shoulder, and the jug had once poured water into the pond, and now it poured nothing, and the girl's face was green with moss.
And up above it all, at the far end of the garden, there rose the back of the house. Five storeys of pale stone streaked with dirt. Every window shuttered. And right at the very top, under the slates of the roof, one small round window, like an eye, with no shutter on it at all.
Lyra stood in the doorway in her mother's gown and looked at it all.
Corin did not look at the roses. He looked at the path.
The main path ran from the garden door straight up the middle of the garden toward the house, between two great banks of tangled roses. It was overgrown with weeds and moss, and in the middle of it, where the moss was thinnest, the earth showed through, soft and black and wet from the rain.
And in the black earth there were footprints.
Not old ones. The rain of Saturday would have washed old ones away. These were new. They went up the path toward the house, and came back down, and went up again, so that they crossed over each other. Two kinds. One was broad and heavy and square at the toe: a man's boots, a big man, walking slowly. The other was small and narrow, with a sharp little round dent at the back, like the mark of a coin pressed into clay.
A woman's heel. A lady's heel. A fine shoe, with a narrow heel, the kind you could only walk in if you never had to walk far.
And lying on the path beside one of the small prints, as if it had fallen from somebody's sleeve or somebody's hair, there was a petal. Long and white and curling, gone a little brown at the edges.
Corin did not need to pick it up. He could smell it from where he stood.
Lilies, said Malakor. Very softly.
Lyra saw it too. Corin saw her see it. And he saw her lift her chin, and fold her hands, and step through the garden door onto the path, carefully, so that her own small feet went beside the other prints and not on them.
"Shut the door, Vane," she said. "And lock it behind us."
"Yes, my lady."
He shut it. The key turned, shrieking. And the garden closed around them like a fist.
