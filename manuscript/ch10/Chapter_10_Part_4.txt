Chapter 10: The Address in the High Quarter, Part 4 of 5: Dust Sheets
Dust Sheets
The footprints went to the scullery door. But the scullery door was locked.
Corin tried it. It was a heavy door at the bottom of four stone steps, below the level of the garden, with a small barred window beside it, and it did not move when he pushed. He bent and looked at the keyhole. Bright scratches around it, the same as the front door. Whoever had come in this way had a key to this door too, and had locked it again behind them when they left.
"The boot room," said Lyra.
She was standing a little way along the back wall of the house, by a low window half hidden behind a water butt. It was a small window, four panes, with a wooden frame gone soft and gray. Its shutter was closed, like all the others, but the shutter was on the outside here, a plain plank one, held shut with a simple iron bar that dropped into a hook.
"The latch never shut properly," Lyra said. "Rowan and I used to climb in this way when we'd been out in the garden after we were supposed to be in bed. Cook knew. She never told. She said a boot room was meant for muddy things coming in, and we were muddy things." She lifted the iron bar out of its hook. The shutter swung open with a groan. Behind it the window was dark and filthy. She pushed on the bottom of the frame with the heel of her hand, the way you would push on something you had pushed a hundred times before.
It swung inward.
"It still doesn't shut," she said. And her voice did something strange, as if it wanted to laugh and cry at the same time and could not decide which.
She went first. She did not ask him to. She gathered her mother's gray gown up in one hand, put her other hand on the sill, and climbed through, quick and neat, like a girl of ten. Corin heard her land on the other side, softly. Then he took off the tall black hat and handed it through to her, and climbed after.
The boot room was small and cold and smelled of old leather. There were hooks on the wall with nothing on them, and a long wooden rack along the floor where boots had stood in rows, and now it was empty except for one small boot, a child's boot, lying on its side in the corner. Nobody had bought it. Nobody had wanted it. Lyra looked at it and did not pick it up.
Through the boot room there was a passage, and through the passage there was the kitchen.
It was enormous. Corin had not known that a kitchen could be so big. It was bigger than the whole of the boarding house's ground floor, bigger than the Needle and Thimble, front and back together. The ceiling was high and arched, made of whitewashed brick, and hooks hung down from it on chains for hams and copper pans, but there were no hams and no pans on them now, only cobwebs, long and gray, swaying very gently in the draft from the boot-room window. Along one wall there was a range, a great black iron cooking range with six ovens and two fireboxes and a spit for roasting, and it was cold. So cold that when Corin passed close to it, he felt the cold come off it, the way you feel the cold come off a block of ice. In the middle of the floor stood a long table, scrubbed white, so big that it must have been built in the room, because it could never have fitted through the door. That was why nobody had bought it, Corin thought. You could not get it out.
There was a light in the kitchen. Thin and gray, coming down from small high windows near the ceiling that looked out at the level of the garden. They were not shuttered. They were too small to bother with. The light came down through them in long pale bars, and the dust floated in the bars, slowly turning, like tiny fish in a stream.
And in the dust on the floor, there were the footprints. Coming in from the scullery door. A man's square boots and a lady's small sharp heels. Going across the kitchen. Going out of the far door.
Lyra had stopped by the long white table. She put her hand flat on it.
"Cook let me roll pastry here," she said. "On Fridays. She'd give me the scraps and I'd make little men out of them, and she'd bake them with currants for eyes." She took her hand away. There was a clean print of her palm in the dust on the wood. "I don't remember her name."
Corin looked at her.
"I've been trying," said Lyra. "All the way across the garden. I've been trying to remember her name and I can't. She was here my whole life. Every day. And I can't remember her name." She shook her head. "I didn't burn it. I know I didn't. I only burned the one thing, in the Inkwell. It's just gone. The way things go."
"Mrs. Hesper would know," said Corin. "Or Mr. Tibbs."
"Yes," said Lyra. "Somebody always knows. That's the only good thing about this city."
On the wall by the far door, high up, there was a board. A long wooden board, painted cream, with a row of little brass bells hanging from it on curled springs. Under each bell there was a painted label in neat black letters. Front Door. Drawing Room. Dining Room. Library. Study. Long Gallery. Lady Thorne's Room. Lord Thorne's Room. Blue Room. Rose Room.
And at the end, a little apart, three more.
Miss Lyra. Master Rowan.
And the third label had been painted over. Not cleanly. Somebody had taken a brush full of cream paint, the same cream as the board, and slapped it across the label, again and again, so thickly that the paint stood up in ridges. Under it Corin could just see the ghost of black letters. He could not read them. He could only see that there had been a word there once, perhaps two, about as long as Master Rowan.
The bell above it still hung on its spring. It was smaller than the others. A child's bell.
Lyra had not seen it. She had already gone out of the far door, following the footprints.
Corin stood under the bell board for a moment longer. Then he took out the Ledger and wrote, very quickly: Kitchen. Bell board. Lyra. Rowan. And a third, painted over. Small bell. He put the Ledger away and went after her.
The back stairs were narrow and dark and very steep, made of plain scrubbed wood, worn hollow in the middle of every step by the feet of servants carrying coal and water and trays up and down for a hundred years. At the top there was a green baize door, the kind that keeps the noise of the kitchen from the people who live upstairs. It had been grand once. Now the green cloth hung off it in tatters, and there were moth holes in it like stars. Lyra pushed it open.
And they were in the house.
The front hall was tall and wide and full of gray light. The light came in through a great fan-shaped window over the front door, high up, the only window in the hall that had no shutter, and it lay across the black and white marble floor in a long pale slant. The marble was laid like a chessboard. It was very dirty. Dust lay over it like a gray carpet, so thick in the corners that it had made little drifts, like snow against a fence. And everywhere, on the dust, on the marble, on the walls, there were the marks.
Chalk marks. White chalk. Numbers. They were written on the floor in big careless figures, inside chalk circles. Lot 63. Lot 64. Lot 65. They were written on the walls, at the height of a man's chest. Lot 29. Lot 30. And around each number on the walls, there was a pale shape. A rectangle. A square. An oval. Places where pictures had hung for so long that the wallpaper underneath them had kept its color while the rest of the wall faded around them, so that now, with the pictures gone, the shapes were left behind like the ghosts of pictures. Pale green rectangles on a gray-green wall. And in the middle of each one, a single nail. Or a hook. Or a hole where the hook had been.
The furniture was gone. Almost all of it. Where it had stood, there were more pale shapes on the floor, cleaner squares in the dust and dents in the marble where heavy feet had pressed for years. In one corner, under the stairs, a few things had been left behind. Things too big, or too ugly, or too broken for anybody to buy. They had been covered with dust sheets, big gray-white sheets of cotton thrown over them, so that they stood in the corner like a family of ghosts huddled together. One tall and thin. One short and square and wide. One with a strange humped shape, like a crouching animal.
The great staircase went up from the middle of the hall, wide and curving, with a banister of dark polished wood. There had been a carpet on it. The carpet was gone too. Only the brass rods that had held it to the steps were left, one rod on every stair, dull and greenish, all the way up into the shadows.
It was very quiet.
Not the quiet of an empty room. The quiet of a room where something has died. Corin knew that quiet. He did not remember knowing it, but his body did. He had felt it in a hundred houses, the Ledger said, in a hundred rooms where a person had been emptied out and their family stood around them not knowing what to say. This was the same. The house had been emptied. Everything it remembered had been taken out of it, numbered, sold. And what was left stood around in the gray light, not knowing what to say.
Lyra stood in the middle of the chessboard floor.
"The clock stood on the landing," she said. Her voice echoed. It went up the curving stairs into the shadows and came back down, small and thin. "No. That's wrong. That was a different clock. The tall one with the moon on its face. The twelve-stone clock was in the long gallery." She turned. "This way."
The long gallery ran along the whole back of the house on the first floor, over the garden. It was the longest room Corin had ever seen. Or remembered seeing. It was long and narrow, with a polished wooden floor that went on and on, and a plaster ceiling high above with flowers and leaves and little fat babies with wings carved into it, all gone gray with dust. One whole side of the room was windows, tall ones, five of them, all shuttered, but with thin lines of daylight leaking in round the edges of the shutters like light round the edges of a door. The other side was wall. Paneled wall, in dark wood, from the floor to the height of Corin's shoulder, and above the paneling, the same faded green paper as the hall.
And on that wall, all along it, at even spaces, there were the ghosts of pictures. Dozens of them. Large and small. Pale rectangles on the green, each with its chalk number beside it.
Lot 38. Lot 39. Lot 40.
Lot 41.
Lyra stopped in front of that one. It was a tall pale rectangle, taller than she was, with a heavy brass hook still screwed into the wall at the top.
"That's her," Lyra said quietly. "That's where she hung. The portrait. Mother." She looked at the empty shape. "Bertram put her in the sale with everything else. I paid the auctioneer two silver to take her out again. It was all I had that month." She stood there a moment longer. Then she turned away, quickly, and walked on down the gallery.
Near the far end, where the gallery turned a corner into a shorter passage, there was a pale shape on the wall that was not a picture.
It went from the floor almost to the ceiling. Tall and narrow. With a curved top, like the top of a church window. The pale paper inside the shape was cleaner than any of the others, and it had a little dark line running round its edge, the way a shape has when something has stood against a wall for a very, very long time, and the soot from the candles has drawn around it like a pencil.
There was no chalk number beside it.
In the floor in front of it, in the polished wood, there were two deep square dents, a little way apart, where something very heavy had stood on short feet for many years.
"Here," said Lyra.
She did not need to say what. The shape said it.
Lot 12. Long-case clock. Twelve stones. Made by Lord Edmund Thorne. Withdrawn before sale by the vendor's agent, B.
"My grandfather built it," said Lyra. "Before I was born. He was a clockmaker. Not for money. For love. He made clocks the way other men make gardens." She touched the pale shape on the wall with two fingers. "This was the best one. The last one. Twelve stones in the face, one for each hour, and they glowed at night. Father said they glowed different colors depending on what kind of day it had been. Gold for a good day. Blue for a sad one." She took her fingers away. "I used to sit here and watch them change. I don't remember what color they were the night he burned."
Corin was not looking at the shape.
He was looking at the paneling under it. The dark wooden paneling that ran along the bottom of the wall, from the floor to the height of his shoulder. Where the clock had stood, the paneling had been hidden behind the clock's tall wooden case for years. It had not been polished. It had not been touched. It was a slightly different color from the rest, lighter, less worn.
And in the middle of that lighter patch, at about the height of his knee, there was a seam.
A thin straight line in the wood. Then another line, at right angles. Then another. A square. About the size of a big book. So neatly fitted into the paneling that if you had not been looking for it, if you had not spent your whole life being trained to look for the thing that did not belong, you would never have seen it.
Corin knelt down in Abel's green coat on the dusty floor.
There was no handle on the little square. No hinge that he could see. But near the top edge of it, there was a hole. A keyhole. Small, and set deep into the wood, and lined with a thin rim of dull silver.
It was not the shape of an ordinary keyhole.
It was the shape of a mask. A little half-mask. Two round eyes, and the curve of a nose between them. The same shape that Madame Hesper had drawn in her pattern book. The same shape, Corin thought, as the brooch on Lady Thorne's collar in the portrait in Lyra's narrow room.
The three stones at his hip went hot. Not warm. Hot. So hot, all at once, that he gasped, and his hand went to the place over the green wool, and he felt the heat of them through Madame Hesper's eleven tiny stitches.
"Lyra," he said. "Come and look at this."
She came and knelt beside him, the gray gown spreading out around her on the dusty floor. She looked at the little square in the paneling. At the silver keyhole in the shape of a mask.
She did not say anything for a long time.
"I never saw this," she said at last. "Never. It was always behind the clock. The clock was always here." She put out her hand and touched the mask-shaped hole, very gently, with one fingertip. "How long has it been there?"
"As long as the clock," said Corin. "Longer, maybe. The clock was standing in front of it to hide it."
"Then my grandfather made it. Or my mother." Lyra's finger traced the edge of the silver. "Or both."
It is Marrowmere work, said Malakor. His voice was very low, and very careful, the voice of something that is trying not to wake a sleeper. That lock. The silver. I have seen locks like that. In the canal city, in the old houses on the water. They are not made to open with keys, Corin. Not iron ones.
Then with what?
The rod did not answer at once.
With a mask, it said. With the right mask. Pressed into the hole, the right way up. A pause. A small silver one. That a lady might wear on her collar.
Corin looked at Lyra. Lyra was looking at the keyhole.
Lady Thorne's brooch. Marrowmere work. Must make a small loop of silk inside every collar to take the pin. DO NOT PIN THROUGH THE EYES.
She had taken it to the coast with her. She had been wearing it in every gown, Madame Hesper said, on every collar. She would have been wearing it the day she left.
Wherever the brooch was now, the key to this little door had gone with it.
Corin stood up. His knees cracked, the way Madame Hesper's did. He took out the Ledger, and he drew the keyhole as carefully as he could, the two eyes and the curve of the nose, and he wrote beside it: Long gallery. Behind where the clock stood. Knee height. Mask lock. Marrowmere. Malakor says the brooch is the key. And under that: Bertram moved the clock. Did he know what it was hiding?
Then he looked down the long gallery, back the way they had come, along the floor.
The footprints were here too. In the dust. A man's square boots and a lady's small sharp heels. They came along the gallery from the far end, past all the pale ghosts of pictures, past Lot 41, right up to the place where the clock had stood.
And they stopped there. In front of the little square door. Close together. As if two people had stood here for a long time, looking down at the mask-shaped keyhole, just as he and Lyra had.
Then they turned round, and went back the way they had come.
They found it too, said Malakor. And they could not open it either.
"Not yet," said Corin, very quietly.
Lyra had heard him. She looked up from the floor. Her face, in the thin gray light leaking round the shutters, was white and fierce and very much like her mother's.
"Then we'll have to find it first," she said. "Won't we."
The Room Over the Rose Garden
At the end of the long gallery, where it turned the corner into the shorter passage, there was a door standing half open.
Lyra stopped beside it. She did not go in. She only looked through the gap.
It was a small room, hardly bigger than a cupboard, with a single shuttered window and a fireplace no wider than a bread oven. The walls were papered in pale blue with little white birds on it, faded nearly to nothing. The room was empty except for one thing. A mirror. A long mirror, as tall as a man, in a plain gilt frame gone dark, fixed to the wall opposite the window with four big iron bolts. Too heavy to take down. Too plain to be worth the trouble. So the auction men had left it.
It was the dressing room. Corin knew it before Lyra said anything. The little dressing room off the long gallery, in front of the long mirror, Madame Hesper had said, and I was on my knees with my mouth full of pins.
Lyra pushed the door open a little more and went in. Corin stood in the doorway.
She went and stood in front of the mirror. It was dusty, and spotted with dark marks where the silver behind the glass had begun to fail, so that her reflection looked as if it were standing behind a thin gray veil. A young woman in a gray gown with pearl buttons, with a gray lace cap over her short black hair and one dark lock loose by her left ear.
She looked at herself for a long time.
"She stood here," Lyra said. Not to him. To the glass. "In this gown. With me inside her. And Madame was on the floor, pinning her hem, and through that door," she did not turn her head toward it, "my father and his sister were saying things that nobody would ever tell me." She put her hand flat on the front of the gray wool, low, just below her waist, where it had once been let out and then taken in again. "You cannot keep them hidden forever, Aurelian."
The words sounded strange in the small blue room. Too big for it.
"Keep who hidden?" Lyra said to her reflection. "Who were they hiding?"
The reflection did not know either.
She stood a moment longer. Then she took her hand away from the gown, and turned, and walked out past Corin into the passage without looking back at the glass. He heard her breathe in once, hard, through her nose. Then she said, in a perfectly ordinary voice: "The top of the house. The stairs are at the end."
The stairs at the end of the passage were narrower than the great staircase, and plainer, and they went up and up, round and round, like the inside of a shell. Corin counted the landings. Second floor. Third. Fourth. On each one there were doors, closed, and he did not open any of them, and Lyra did not stop. The air grew colder as they climbed, and drier, and the dust on the steps grew thinner, because there were fewer drafts up here to carry it.
And the footprints went up too. The square boots and the small sharp heels. Up and up. All the way.
At the very top, the stairs ended at a short passage under the roof. The ceiling here sloped down on both sides, the way the ceiling in Corin's room on Tallow Street sloped, and he had to bend his head. There were three doors along the passage, small and plain and painted white. At the end there was a fourth.
Lyra went to the fourth door.
She stood in front of it with her hand on the knob and did not turn it.
"I don't remember this," she said, very quietly. "I mean, I do. I know it. I know it's the sewing room. I know it looks over the rose garden. I know it's where she taught me. I know there's a window seat with a blue cushion." She swallowed. "But I know it the way I know the name of the river. Because somebody told me. Because Madame told me on Saturday. I don't have the day. I don't have her hand on mine. I don't have in, through, pull. I burned it." Her hand tightened on the knob. "So if I go in there, it will just be a room. Won't it. It will just be an empty room in somebody else's house."
Corin did not know what to say to that. He thought of the Ledger in his pocket, and every room in it that he had been told about and could not remember. Every face. Every name. A whole life of empty rooms in somebody else's house.
"Yes," he said. "It will."
Lyra looked at him.
"But you'll know it was yours," said Corin. "That's not nothing. The tall woman said that. I wrote it down."
Lyra stood very still. Then she let out a breath that was almost a laugh.
"Which is not the same," she said. "But it is not nothing." And she turned the knob and opened the door.
The light hit them.
It was the only room in the house with no shutters. The round window at the far end, the little round window Corin had seen from the garden, like an eye under the roof, was clear. Dirty, and streaked with old rain, and webbed across one corner with a spider's silk. But clear. And as Lyra opened the door, the clouds outside, which had lain over the city all day like a gray lid on a pot, chose that moment to come apart. Just for a little while. Just a crack. And the late afternoon sun came through the crack and through the round window and into the room, and the room filled up with light.
Gold light. Thick and slanting and warm, like honey poured from a jar. It lay across the bare floorboards in a great bright oval. It lit up the dust in the air so that every speck of it glowed, turning, floating, like the tiny sparks that go up from a bonfire. It climbed the pale walls and lit up the old wallpaper, which was covered with roses, small faded pink roses on cream. It touched the window seat under the round window, a deep wide seat built into the wall, with a cushion on it. A blue cushion. Faded, and dusty, and sagging in the middle where somebody had sat on it a thousand times. But blue.
The auction men had not taken the cushion. Nobody had wanted it.
Lyra stood in the doorway in the gold light, in her mother's gray gown, and did not move.
Corin did not move either. He stood behind her, two steps back, in Abel's green coat, and he let her stand there as long as she needed.
It was a long time.
Then she walked across the floor, through the gold oval of light, and sat down on the window seat. On the blue cushion. In the middle, where it sagged. She sat the way a girl sits, with her feet tucked up under her gown and her back against the side of the window, and her face turned toward the glass. Below her, through the dirty round pane, the wild garden lay spread out in the sun. The great tangled banks of roses. The meadow that had been a lawn. The stone girl in the black pond with her empty jug.
And Lyra's hands, lying in her lap, began to move.
Corin saw it. He did not think she knew she was doing it. Her right hand was holding something that was not there, between the thumb and the first finger, something very small and thin. Her left hand was holding something round and flat. And her right hand went down, and through, and up. And down, and through, and up. Slowly. Steadily. A small careful movement, over and over, in her lap, in the gold light, like a girl of six with her tongue in the corner of her mouth.
In, through, pull.
Her face did not change. She was looking out of the window. She did not know her hands were sewing.
Corin stood very still in the doorway and watched, and his throat hurt.
The hands remember, said Malakor, so softly that Corin almost did not hear him. Even when the head does not. I have seen it a thousand times. You burn the day, but the hands were there too, and nobody thinks to burn the hands. A pause. Do not tell her. Not now. Let her have it without knowing.
Corin did not tell her.
After a while, her hands stopped. They lay still in her lap. She turned from the window and looked down at them, and frowned a little, as if they had been doing something they should not. Then she looked round the room.
It was empty. Except for the window seat and the cushion, there was nothing in it at all. Pale shapes on the rose wallpaper where things had stood: a tall narrow shape that might have been a cupboard, a low wide one that might have been a table, a square one high up that had been a picture. And on the floor, near the window seat, a ring. A pale ring in the dust and the boards, about as wide as a dinner plate, where something round had stood on a stand, on one leg, for years and years.
"Her frame," said Lyra. "Her embroidery frame. It stood there. On a stand, by the window, where the light was best." She looked at the pale ring. "Somebody bought it. Lot something. Somebody's wife is doing her needlework on my mother's frame in a house somewhere on the Hill and doesn't even know."
She got up from the window seat.
And as she got up, the old blue cushion, which had sagged for so many years in the middle, slid a little on the seat, the way a cushion does when the weight comes off it. And underneath it, in the wood of the seat, there was a small dark gap.
Corin saw it. Lyra saw him see it.
She lifted the cushion. Under it, the seat of the window seat was a lid. A long wooden lid, hinged at the back, with a little brass finger-ring at the front to lift it by. One of the hinges had broken long ago, which was why the lid sat crooked and left a gap. Lyra put her finger in the brass ring and lifted.
Inside, there was a box under the seat, the length of the whole window. It was the kind of box you find under every window seat in every old house: a place to keep things you do not want to look at but cannot throw away. Spare blankets, old curtains, outgrown shoes.
It was empty. The auction men had looked in here too. Corin could see the clean places in the dust where they had lifted things out.
All but one.
Right at the back, in the far corner, where the lid did not open wide enough for a big man's arm to reach without bending, there was a little box. A small flat box made of thin wood, painted blue, with a lid that slid in grooves. It was the size of a child's pencil box. It was so small, and so plain, and so far back in the dark corner, that a man in a hurry with a list in his hand and fifty lots to number before dinner would never have noticed it.
Lyra reached in. She had to lie half across the window seat to do it, with her arm down inside the box up to the shoulder. When she came up again, she had the little blue box in her hand. She sat down on the edge of the seat with it on her knees and looked at it.
On the lid, painted in white, in a hand Corin had never seen before, small and quick and slanting, there was one word.
Lyra's.
She slid the lid open.
Inside there was a square of white linen. Small, no bigger than her palm. The edges were frayed. In the middle of it, someone had tried to sew something. Corin could not tell what. A flower, perhaps, or a letter, or a bird. There were a few red stitches, uneven, big and small, going in all directions. And then there was a knot. A terrible knot. A great tangled nest of red thread, wound round and round on itself, pulled so tight that it had puckered the linen all around it into little ridges like the skin on hot milk. A child's knot. A bird's nest. A knot that had made somebody throw a hoop on the floor and say she would never sew again as long as she lived.
Under the linen there was a slip of paper, folded small. Into a little square. Very small.
Lyra unfolded it with fingers that were not steady.
It was written in ink. Violet ink. In the same small quick slanting hand that had painted Lyra's on the lid of the box.
My darling —
This is your first. I am keeping it for you, because one day you will be very good at this, and you will not believe that you were ever this bad, and I want you to have the proof.
When something is too big, fold it small. Put it somewhere safe. Carry it anyway.
And try again tomorrow.
— M.
Lyra read it. She read it again. Then she sat on the edge of the window seat, in the gold light that was already beginning to fade as the clouds closed back over the sun, with the little blue box on her knees and the knotted square of linen in one hand and her mother's note in the other.
She did not cry. Corin had thought she would. She did not.
She laughed.
It was a small laugh, and wet, and it shook. But it was a laugh. She held up the square of linen with its terrible red knot to the round window, so the last of the light came through it.
"Look at it," she said. "Look how bad it is. Corin, look. I was awful. I was really, truly awful."
"Yes," said Corin. "You were."
She laughed again, and wiped her face with the back of the hand that held the note, and the violet ink did not smudge, because it was old and had dried long ago.
"I don't remember making it," she said. "I'll never remember making it." She looked down at the knot. "But it's mine. It's the proof. She kept it for me. She knew I'd need it." She folded the note small again, very carefully, along its old creases, and put it back in the box, on top of the linen, and slid the lid shut. "She always knew what people would need. Didn't she. Before they did."
She held the little blue box against her chest.
Corin had gone to the round window. He had not meant to. His feet had taken him there while she was laughing, the way his eyes always went to the thing that did not belong. He stood beside the window seat and looked out through the dirty glass.
From up here, from the top of Number 3, he could see over the garden wall. Over all the garden walls, all along the back of the crescent. He could see the gardens of Number 4 and Number 5 and Number 6, neat and green and clipped. He could see the mews beyond, and the coach houses, and Mr. Tibbs's cherry-red doors.
And further along, he could see the garden of Number 11.
It was white. Everything in it was white. White gravel paths, raked into lines. White stone urns. A white bench. And at the bottom of the garden, against the back wall, a glasshouse. A long glasshouse with a curved roof, its panes misted on the inside with warmth, so that you could only see shapes through them, pale and tall and crowded together.
Lilies. A whole house of glass, full of lilies, growing in the warm wet air in the middle of the autumn.
And as Corin watched, the door of the glasshouse opened, and two people came out.
A woman. Tall, and slender, and dressed all in white, with a white shawl around her shoulders and something white and shining in her hair. She walked slowly along the white gravel with her head bent, the way people walk when they are listening to someone.
And beside her, a man. A big man. Broad in the shoulders and heavy in the body, in a dark coat, with a dark hat on his head. He walked slowly too, a little behind her, his hands clasped at his back. Corin could not see his face. Only the shape of him, square and solid against the white.
They stopped by the white bench. The woman said something. The man nodded. The woman lifted one white hand and pointed.
She pointed along the backs of the houses. Along the gardens. Toward Number 3.
Corin stepped back from the window so fast that he knocked his shoulder on the sloping ceiling.
"What?" said Lyra. She was on her feet. "What is it?"
"Your aunt's garden," said Corin. "A woman in white. And a man." He took a breath. "A big man. In heavy boots."
Lyra looked at him. Then she looked down, at the floor of the sewing room, at the dust. At the footprints that had come up all those stairs, and across this floor, and stood for a while by the window seat, and gone away again.
A man's square boots. A lady's small sharp heels.
"They're coming back," said Lyra.
It was not a question.
Then do not be here when they do, said Malakor.
