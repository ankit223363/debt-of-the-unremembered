Chapter 10: The Address in the High Quarter, Part 2 of 5: Monday at the Intake Hall
Monday at the Intake Hall
On Monday he woke again, and did not know his name again, and read the board and the book again, and found the hard little square in his collar again. This time there were two things in it. The folded list. And something round and flat and cold, like a coin.
He took that one out, for a moment, against the board's advice, and looked at it in the gray light. A brass button, with a little bell on it.
The Ledger told him what it was. He read the page about the button woman twice. Then he put the button back, and sewed the stitches closed with a needle and thread he found, by the instructions on the board, in a tin on the windowsill marked MENDING, and went to work.
The line outside the Bank on Lantern Quay was already long when he got there, though the great doors had not yet opened. It stretched along the riverfront in the early morning mist, gray and quiet, people standing with their collars up and their hands in their pockets, waiting to sell an afternoon or a song or the smell of their mother's kitchen for a handful of coppers. Corin walked past them, the whole long length of them, and they looked at his coat, and looked away.
At the top of the white steps, under the four tall columns and the gold words FORGET YOUR TROUBLES, two Bank porters in dark green with gold piping were unlocking the doors. They saw him and nodded, the small cold nod you give to a man you work beside but do not like.
Inside, the hall was cold. The gemstone lamps had not been lit yet. The long counter stretched away into the gloom, all its glasses dark, like a row of graves with their stones laid flat. The clerks were arriving one by one through a side door and taking off their coats and putting on their white gloves. Nobody spoke.
Warden Briggs was at the far end, by the door to the slag-room stairs, with a big ledger open on a stand in front of him and a pen behind his ear. He was a heavy gray man, gray in the face and gray in the hair, in a dark green coat a size too small, so that the brass plate on his chest, WARDEN. INTAKE. BRIGGS., stuck out over his stomach like a shelf. He looked up when Corin came, with his small, tired, pale eyes.
"Vane," he said. He did not say good morning. "You're early."
"I need to leave at noon today," said Corin. "I'll make up the time."
Briggs looked at him. "Leave at noon. For what?"
"Family business."
It was not a lie, exactly. Lyra had said, last Saturday, in the shop, the Ledger said: the only family I have left in this city. He thought that if she had no family and he had no family, then perhaps for one afternoon they could be each other's.
Briggs grunted. "Harvesters don't have families," he said. "Not that remember them."
"No," said Corin. "But the families sometimes remember us."
Briggs looked at him for a long moment with an odd expression. Then he took the pen from behind his ear and turned a page in his big ledger.
"Slag doesn't care what time it is," he said. "Four glasses. Clear four glasses by noon and you can go dancing for all I care. Three glasses and you stay till six." He made a mark. "And Vane."
"Yes?"
"Don't hum down there."
Corin blinked. "I don't—"
"You do. Friday. All afternoon. I could hear it up the stairs." Briggs did not look up from his ledger. His voice was flat and gruff. "Don't. The glass picks it up. Old glass, cloudy glass, it's thin in the head. You hum near it long enough, it starts humming back. Then you've got a sheet of slag that sings, and nobody'll touch it, and I've got to send it to the grinder." He made another mark. "Lost three good sheets that way, years back. Bucket man used to sing. That's why he went odd."
He waved his pen at the stairs. Go on.
Corin went down.
The slag room was as the Ledger had said it would be. A long low cellar with a brick ceiling and no window, cold as a well, lit by a single gemstone lamp in a cage on the wall that gave off a thin blue light like moonlight on snow. And all around the walls, on wooden racks, stood the old glasses. Dozens of them. Each one as big as a tabletop, standing on its edge like a slice of bread in a rack. Each one clouded from inside, gray and milky, as if someone had breathed on it in winter and the breath had frozen there.
He went to the first one. He put his hand flat against it, through the gray glove. It was very cold. Under his palm, deep inside the cloud, something moved. A small slow shape, turning. The corner of a window, with rain on it. Then gone.
He took out the rod.
It was long and silver and cold, and the word MALAKOR ran down the length of it in old letters, and around the very tip a spiral of fine lines had been carved, winding round and round the silver to the right, like the inside of a seashell. He held the tip flat against the clouded glass. And he let it listen.
Crumbs, said Malakor, sourly. Again. Do you know what I was, boy, before Hale put me in your hand? I was the right arm of the greatest Harvester the Ministry ever had. I took the whole of a general's war out of him in a single night. I have tasted kings. And now I am a broom.
"You're a very good broom," said Corin.
Do not be kind to me. I cannot bear it.
The cloud came out of the glass and into the silver, slowly, thread by thread, the way water soaks into a cloth. Behind it the glass cleared, a little at a time, like a window clearing when the sun comes up. And the bits went past him into the rod. He felt them as they went. Tiny. Broken. Scraps. A smell of soap. A man's laugh, cut off halfway. A dog's wet nose. Somebody saying I'm sorry in a voice like a cracked bell. A red door. The taste of salt. A hand pulling a blanket up. None of them whole. None of them anybody.
The first glass took an hour. When it was clear, it stood in its rack like a sheet of water, and he could see the brick wall through it, and his own face, faint, looking back. He did not look at his face for long. He moved on.
The second glass was quicker. The third was slow and sticky, as if the slag had been there for years and had grown into the glass like moss into stone. He worked at it with the rod for nearly two hours, and his arms ached, and his eyes stung from staring into the gray, and the bits that came out of it were older and stranger than the others. A horse-drawn tram, the kind they did not have in the city anymore. A woman in a bonnet. A song in a key that sounded wrong.
He was halfway through the fourth glass, and the bells across the city were ringing the eleventh hour, when he caught it.
It was only a scrap. Smaller than the others, if anything. A breath of something. But it was not like the others. The others were cold. This one was warm. It came through the rod and into his hand and up his arm like the warmth of a cup of tea through a glove, and it was a voice. A girl's voice. Young. Singing.
Three notes. Going up. And up. And up.
And then, before the scrap broke, before it slid away into the rod and was gone, a fourth. Coming down.
Corin's hand jerked. The rod came off the glass with a small high squeal, like a wet finger on a window.
He stood there in the cold blue light with his heart pounding so hard that he could feel it in his teeth.
Did you hear that? he said. Not out loud. In his head. Malakor. Did you hear it?
The rod was silent for a long moment.
I heard it, it said at last.
The fourth note.
Yes.
Whose was it? Who sold it? Can you—can we find out who sold it? There must be a record. Upstairs. The glass has a number. Briggs keeps a book.
Corin. The old voice was very quiet. Quieter than he had ever heard it. That glass has been in this cellar for years. Look at the dust on the rack. The scrap was old. Older than the bucket man. Whoever sold it did so long ago, at that counter, for a copper or two, and walked out into the street and did not miss it. There will be no name. The Bank does not keep the names of people who sell crumbs.
But it was the fourth note.
Yes. A pause. And now it is in me. And I will keep it. I promise you that, for whatever a broom's promise is worth.
Corin stood a long time in front of the half-cleared glass. Then he put the tip of the rod back against it, very gently, and went on. He did not hum. He did not dare.
But when the fourth glass was clear, a little before the noon bell, he did a thing he did not quite understand. He took out the Ledger, and on a fresh line, he tried to draw the four notes. Not with letters, because he did not know their names. With a line. A line that went up a step, and up a step, and up a step, and then down.
It looked like nothing. It looked like a child's drawing of a hill.
He wrote under it: Slag room, glass 4, rack on the left by the door. A girl's voice. The fourth note, coming down. Malakor kept it. And then: Ask the button woman what happens at the end.
When he came up the stairs, Briggs was at his ledger stand by the door, and the hall was full of light and noise. The gemstone lamps were blazing cold blue on their long chains. The glasses all along the counter were waking and going dark, waking and going dark, under the bare hands of the people from the line. Clerks murmured. Stamps thumped. Coins clinked into palms. And the sweetish smell of the hall, the smell of a room where a great many people have been crying, was so thick that Corin could almost see it.
Briggs looked up. "Four?"
"Four."
Briggs grunted and made a mark. "You weren't humming."
"No."
"Good." He made another mark. He did not look up again. "Go on, then. Family business."
Corin went past him toward the front doors. Halfway there, by the end of the long counter, he had to stop and step aside, because a Bank runner was coming the other way: a thin young man in dark green, with a leather satchel on a strap across his chest, walking fast. He was going to the dispatch shelf by the side door, where the Bank's parcels and letters were stacked to go out across the city. He set a small packet on the shelf and went off again without a glance.
Corin looked at the packet as he passed. He did not mean to. But his eyes went where they always went, to the thing that did not belong.
It was small, square, wrapped in brown paper and tied with green string, and sealed with a blob of dark green wax pressed with the Bank's mark, a pair of scales. And on the front, in a clerk's neat hand, it said:
No. 3, Crown Walk. To await collection. Fourth Desk.
Corin did not stop walking. He did not turn his head. He let his eyes go flat and empty, the way the tall woman had taught him, and he walked out through the great doors and down the white steps into the noon, past the long patient line, and he did not look back.
But the three stones at his hip were warm all the way to Market Row.
Number 3, said Malakor quietly. The house nobody lives in. And the Bank sends it parcels.
"To await collection," said Corin, under his breath.
Then someone, said the rod, is coming to collect.
The Back Door
The alley behind Market Row was so narrow that Corin could touch both walls at once if he put his arms out. It was paved with broken bricks and puddles, and it smelled of cabbage water and cats, and along both sides of it ran the back doors of the shops: the baker's, with a heap of burnt loaves by the step; the butcher's, with a bucket that Corin did not look into; the candle-maker's, with drips of yellow wax hardened on the threshold like small frozen waterfalls.
The back door of the Needle and Thimble was painted the same plum color as the front, and it had the same tired look, as if it had been waiting a long time for somebody to paint it again and had given up hoping. There was no bell. There was a knocker in the shape of a hand holding a ball. Corin lifted it and let it fall, once.
The door opened before the sound had finished.
"You are four minutes late," said Madame Hesper.
She looked as if she had not slept. Her long face was grayer than it had been on Saturday, and there were shadows under her pale eyes like thumbprints in clay. The iron-gray hair was still pinned tight in its knot with the two long pins crossed through it, but a few strands had come loose and hung down by her ears. There were threads all over her black dress. Green threads. Dozens of them, clinging to her sleeves and her apron and the front of her skirt, as if she had been walking through a field of long green grass.
"I'm sorry," said Corin. "The Warden—"
"I do not want to hear about any Warden. Come in. Quickly. Mrs. Pellow is hanging out her washing two yards up and she has eyes like a hawk and a mouth like a drain."
He came in. She shut the door behind him and slid the bolt.
They were in the back room of the shop, behind the red velvet curtain. It was smaller than the front, and darker, and even more full. A big worktable took up most of it, covered with paper patterns weighed down with flat irons. There was a sewing frame in one corner with a half-made quilt stretched on it. There was a narrow cot against the wall, with a gray blanket thrown over it, and a pillow with the dent of a head still in it. Corin looked at the pillow. Madame Hesper saw him look.
"I lay down for an hour," she said. "At four. I am sixty-five. I am allowed." She pushed past him toward the curtain. "Do not look at my bed, Mr. Vane. Look at your coat."
She pulled the curtain aside.
In the front of the shop, in the gray noon light from the window, there was a dress form. And on the dress form, there was the coat.
Corin stopped where he was.
It did not look like the coat he had seen on Saturday. That coat had been old, and tired, and creased from two years in a trunk, and spotted with moth holes at the collar, and it had smelled of lavender and dust. This coat looked new. The green was so deep and clean that it seemed to drink the light from the window, the way a forest pool drinks the sky. The wool had been brushed until it had a soft glow on it, like the skin of a plum. The tails hung straight and even down the back of the dress form. The high collar stood up stiff and proud, lined now with fresh green silk. The wide cuffs were turned back to show their pale leaf-green facing.
And the buttons. All the brass buttons, in their double row down the front, had been polished until they shone like little suns. On every one of them, sharp and bright, there was the rose. And the thorn wound around it.
The dark band on the left sleeve, the band that meant I belong to her, was still there. Madame Hesper had not taken it off. She had sewn it back on with new thread, so close and so neat that the stitches could not be seen at all.
"Well?" said Madame Hesper.
Corin did not know what to say. "It's—" he began, and stopped.
"It is a coat," said Madame Hesper. "That is all it is. Take off the gray one. Quickly. I want to see it on you before she comes."
He took off the gray coat. He did it carefully, the way the Ledger said he always did, folding it so the inside pockets lay against each other. He laid it on the long scarred table, next to the great shears. Madame Hesper lifted the green coat off the dress form and held it up behind him, open, the way a servant holds a coat for a gentleman, and he put his arms into the sleeves.
It was heavy. Heavier than the gray. The wool was thick and close and warm, and it settled over his shoulders like a pair of hands. The collar came up under his jaw and held his head high. The sleeves were exactly long enough. He moved his arms. Nothing pulled. Nothing pinched. Not one pin. It fitted him as if he had been born in it.
Madame Hesper walked round him once, slowly, the way she had on Saturday. She tugged the hem straight at the back. She turned down one corner of the collar, and then turned it up again. She stood in front of him and looked at the line of the buttons, and frowned, and moved him one half-inch to the left by the shoulders so that the light from the window fell on him better.
Then she stood back, and folded her long thin hands together in front of her apron, and said nothing for a long time.
"Abel would have liked it," she said at last, quietly. "He was always telling me his collar was too low."
Then she was brisk again.
"Now. The pockets." She pulled open the right-hand front of the coat. "Here. Inside. A long one, from the ribs to the hip, cut deep and lined with leather, so that whatever you keep in it will not wear through the silk. It is the same shape as the one in your gray coat. I took the measure from the stretch in the seam." She did not look at the gray coat on the table. She did not ask what was in its long pocket. "Here," she touched the left-hand front, over the heart, "a pocket for a book. Square. Exactly the size of the worn place on your waistcoat. With a flap and a button, so it cannot fall out if you bend. And here," low on the right, near the hip, "a little flat place. A double piece of cloth with an opening at the top. For a thing that is warm when it should be cold."
She looked at him.
"I did not know what it is, and I do not wish to," she said. "But I will sew it in for you, if you bring it to me. You need not open it. I will sew it in shut."
Corin looked at her for a long moment. Then he went to the gray coat on the table and felt inside the right side of it, down low, near the hem of the lining. His fingers found the black cloth square. Sewn shut. The three stones inside it, small and hard, warm against his fingertips even through the glove.
He took out a little knife from his trouser pocket and cut the threads that held the square to the gray coat's lining. Very carefully. Not touching the square itself. When it came loose, he held it in his palm for a moment. It was the size of a small biscuit, and it weighed almost nothing, and it was warm. Like a small animal sleeping.
He gave it to Madame Hesper.
She took it without a word. She did not weigh it in her hand. She did not press it to feel what was inside. She just turned and slid it into the double piece of cloth in the green coat, and took a needle from her collar, already threaded with green, and sewed the opening closed in eleven tiny stitches, so fast that he could hardly follow the needle.
"There," she said. And bit the thread.
Then he moved the rest. The rod, out of the long pocket of the gray coat and into the long pocket of the green. It slid in easily. The leather lining fit it like a sheath.
This is better, said Malakor, from inside. He sounded surprised. This is a good pocket. She is a clever old woman.
The Ledger, out of the pocket over his heart in the gray coat, and into the square pocket over his heart in the green. It fitted exactly. He did up the flap and the small horn button.
And last of all, the list.
Madame Hesper turned her back. She did it without being asked. She went to the window and stood looking out at Market Row, with her hands folded in front of her, and she did not turn round.
Corin undid the top of his shirt. He found the doubled cloth of his collar, and the stitches he had sewn closed that morning, and he picked them open again with the point of the little knife. Out came the folded square of paper, warm from his neck. THOSE I OWE. And out came the brass button with the bell.
He opened the green coat. Inside, on the left, under the arm, close to the heart, behind the place where the lining met the wool, there was the little secret pocket. The one Madame Hesper had shown them on Saturday. The one she sewed into every coat. He slid the folded list into it. It went in easily, as if it had always belonged there.
Then he held the bell button for a moment between his fingers.
He put it in too. Beside the list. The two of them together, close to his heart, where nobody would ever find them.
"You can turn round," he said.
Madame Hesper turned round. Her eyes went to the left side of the coat, just once, very quickly, and away again.
"Good," she said. That was all.
She put a pair of gloves into his hands. White cotton. Very clean. "Footmen wear white," she said. "Always. If you have reason to wear gray ones underneath, then wear gray ones underneath, and nobody will know. White over gray. The white will look a little thick, but in the High Quarter they will only think you have fat fingers." Then she gave him a hat: a tall round hat, black, very stiff, with a little rosette of green ribbon on one side. "And this. You will carry it under your arm indoors and wear it out of doors. You will never, ever wear it inside a house. If you wear a hat inside a house in the High Quarter, Mr. Vane, they will know you are not a servant. They will think you are a lunatic."
Corin put on the gray gloves, and then the white ones over them. They were tight. His hands looked strange: large, and pale, and stiff, like the hands of a statue.
There was a soft knock on the back door. Twice, and then once.
"That is her," said Madame Hesper. "She knocks like her mother."
She went through the curtain and drew back the bolt.
Corin heard the back door open, and a voice say, "I'm sorry I'm late, Madame, Patience threw a shoe and Mr. Pruett—" and then stop.
There was a silence.
Then Lyra came through the red curtain, and stopped too.
She was not wearing her second-best brown. She was not wearing any of the coats Corin had read about in the Ledger. She was wearing a gown he had never seen. It was gray. A soft dove-gray, like a pigeon's breast, in a fine thin wool that fell from her shoulders to the floor without one wrinkle. It had a high neck and long close sleeves and a small row of pearl buttons down the front, and it was so plain that it was beautiful. Over it she had a short cape of darker gray, with a hood. Her short black hair, the hair she had cut off herself with a knife, had been brushed until it shone and smoothed flat under a little gray lace cap, and one dark lock had been left loose by her left ear, the way her mother wore a curl in the portrait.
She looked older. She looked like the portrait in her narrow room.
And she was staring at him.
"Oh," said Lyra. Very softly. She put her hand up to her mouth. "Oh, it's—" She stopped. "For a moment I thought—"
"You thought it was Abel," said Madame Hesper, behind her.
Lyra nodded. She could not speak.
Madame Hesper came round and stood beside her, and looked at the two of them, Corin in the green coat by the window and Lyra in the gray gown by the curtain, and something moved in her long stern face again.
"The gray gown," said Madame Hesper, to Corin, "is your lady's mother's. One of them. The only one I could get back." She smoothed a fold of it at Lyra's shoulder, very lightly. "When the auction men took the house, all of Lady Thorne's gowns were sold in one lot. To a dealer on Silk Street who cuts up good gowns and sells them by the yard to make cushions. I went to him the next morning with every coin I had, and I said, Sell me one. Just one. The gray. He said it was not worth anything. It had been let out at the waist. Badly altered, he said." Her mouth twisted. "I did not tell him that the alteration was mine. Or why it had been made."
Lyra looked down at the soft gray wool falling over her body.
"Evening gown, gray," she said slowly. "Let out at waist. She is expecting. Do not mention."
"I took it in again," said Madame Hesper. "Last night. To fit you." She stepped back. "You are the same size she was, child. The year before you. Almost to the quarter-inch."
Lyra did not cry. Corin saw her decide not to. She lifted her chin, and folded her hands in front of her, and stood the way her mother had stood in the portrait: straight, and still, as if she were listening to something far away.
"Thank you, Madame," she said. Her voice was steady. "I'll bring it back."
"You will not," said Madame Hesper. "It is yours. It was always going to be yours." She turned away briskly and began to sweep threads off the table into her palm. "Now go. Both of you. Before I start being sentimental and stick a pin in somebody. The gray coat stays here. I will put it in the trunk upstairs with the others. Nobody will look for a Ministry coat among the Thorne liveries." She looked at Corin over her shoulder. "Come back for it tonight. The back door. Knock like a Thorne."
"How does a Thorne knock?"
"Twice, and then once," said Madame Hesper. "As if they were sure of being let in, but too polite to say so."
Corin put the tall black hat on his head. He went to the back door and opened it, and stood aside, the way Lyra had shown him on Saturday, the way Abel must have stood ten thousand times: one hand on the door, his eyes on a spot just above her head.
Lyra went past him into the alley, gathering the gray skirt up out of the puddles.
Corin followed. Two steps behind. A little to the left.
Behind them, before the plum-colored door swung shut, Madame Hesper said one more thing. She said it to nobody, or to herself, or to the empty shop. But Corin heard it.
"Keep sewing pockets," said Madame Hesper. "One day somebody will need one."
