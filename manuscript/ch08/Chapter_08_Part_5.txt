Chapter 8: The Auditor, Part 5 of 5: The Intake Hall
The Intake Hall
The form had said from tomorrow. Tomorrow had come. So at the first bell after noon, with half of Toby's bread and dripping in his stomach and the empty jar wrapped in a handkerchief in his coat, Corin went down the great black stairs at the front of the Ministry, under the huge stone eye, and through the city, and down to the river.
He did not need the Ledger to find the way. He needed only to follow the line.
It began a long way from Lantern Quay. He saw it first on the Docks road, at the top of the Salt Stairs. A thin gray thread of people, standing still against the wall while the rest of the street moved round them. Then thicker. Down the Salt Stairs, two by two, three by three, pressed against the railing. Along the riverfront, past the pie sellers and the fish carts and the coils of rope, a long patient gray snake of people, so long that he walked beside it for a quarter of an hour and did not reach the end.
They did not talk much. That was the first thing he noticed. A line that long, in the Lower Docks, should have been loud. It was not. It was quiet in the way a church is quiet. People stood with their arms folded, or their hands in their pockets, or holding a child by the wrist. Old women in shawls. Young men in patched coats with their caps pulled down. A girl younger than Juno, alone, with her hair in two plaits and her bare feet blue with cold. A man with one leg on a crutch. A mother with a baby wrapped in a gray blanket against her chest, rocking it, very slowly, from side to side, and singing to it under her breath. Corin slowed down as he passed her, and heard the song, and did not know it, and was glad.
They looked at his coat as he went by. At the long gray Ministry wool. And they looked away.
The line ended at a building.
It stood on Lantern Quay, right on the edge of the river, with its back to the water. It had been a warehouse once. You could see that. Great blank walls of dark brick, and a roof of black slate, and high narrow windows, barred. But someone had built a new front onto it. White stone. Columns, four of them, tall and smooth and clean, holding up a wide stone roof over the doors, like a temple. And carved into the stone above the columns, in letters as tall as a man, gilded, so that they caught the gray afternoon light and shone:
THE OBLIVION BANK. LANTERN QUAY BRANCH. INTAKE.
And under that, smaller, also gilded:
FORGET YOUR TROUBLES.
Corin stood at the foot of the white steps and looked up at the words for a long time.
Then he went up the steps, past the head of the line, past two Bank porters in dark green with gold piping who looked at his coat and did not stop him, and through the great doors, and in.
It was warm inside. That was the second thing. Warm and bright and smelling of polish and lamp oil and something else, something faint and sweetish, like the smell in a room where a great many people have been crying. The old warehouse had been gutted and floored with white tiles, and its high walls painted cream, and great gemstone lamps hung from its beams on long chains, blazing cold blue. And down the whole length of it, from the doors to the far wall, there ran a counter.
It was long. As long as the line outside, almost. Polished dark wood, waist high, divided every few paces by a thin brass rail into little stalls. And in every stall, set into the wood of the counter, there was a sheet of glass.
Corin's breath stopped.
He had seen a glass like that this morning. In a silver frame, under a bare hand, blazing. Smaller than these. These were as big as a tabletop. Clear, and so clean that they seemed not to be there, and inside each one, very fine, the thousands of silver lines, like frost on a window, like the veins of a leaf.
And behind every stall, on the far side of every glass, there sat a clerk of the Bank. In dark green. With white gloves. With a little brass stamp, and a pad of ink, and a wooden box of small square cards. And in front of every stall, on the near side, there stood a person from the line.
He watched the nearest one.
It was an old man. Thin, bent, in a coat that had been mended so many times it was mostly thread. He put his bare hand flat on the glass. The clerk behind it laid her white-gloved hand on top of his. The glass woke, faint and blue, a few inches round the old man's palm. The clerk looked down into it. Her lips moved. And then she reached under the counter and brought out something small and hard and clear, like a drop of water that had frozen, and pressed it against the glass beside the old man's hand.
The blue glow in the glass flowed into it. Slowly. Like water running downhill. The little stone filled with light, faint and cold, and the glow under the old man's hand went out.
The old man took his hand off the glass. He looked at it. He looked at the clerk. He looked puzzled, for a moment, the way you look when you walk into a room and forget why you came.
The clerk stamped a card. She slid it across the counter to him, with two coppers on top. Next.
The old man took the coppers. He put them in his pocket. He walked away, slowly, toward a door at the far end of the hall, where a porter in green held it open. He did not look back. He did not look at anything. He looked like a man who has put something down somewhere and cannot think where.
The next person in the line stepped up to the glass.
Corin did not know how long he stood there. Long enough to watch it happen four times. Five. A young man who sold, the card said, a fight he won. A woman who sold a song her mother sang. A boy of perhaps ten, standing on a little wooden box so that he could reach the counter, who sold a dog. Just that. A dog. Two coppers. He walked away toward the far door with his small fists in his pockets, and his face very calm, and Corin could not breathe.
"You're the Ministry man."
The voice came from his left. Gruff. Tired. Bored.
Corin turned.
A man was standing beside him. He had come up without a sound, the way men do who have walked the same floor every day for years. He was perhaps fifty. Middle-sized, thick through the chest and the belly, in a dark green coat that was not quite a Bank coat and not quite a guard's, with brass buttons instead of gold, and a stick at his belt, short and black, with a brass cap. His face was broad and gray and heavy, with deep lines down from the nose to the corners of the mouth, and a gray stubble on the chin. His eyes were small and pale and very tired. Not unkind. Not anything. Just tired, the way a road is tired, from having so many feet go over it.
On the breast of his coat there was a small brass plate. WARDEN. INTAKE. BRIGGS.
"Harvester," said Corin. "Yes."
"Got a paper about you. From the Archon's office. Came this morning." The man called Briggs did not take it out. "Says you're mine now. Says I'm to find you something to do." He looked at Corin's coat. At his gloves. At the long narrow pocket on the right. "Don't know what. Don't need a harvester here. Nobody dies here." He jerked his chin at the long counter, the glasses, the line. "They just go home a bit lighter."
Corin did not say anything.
Briggs looked at him for a moment longer with his small tired eyes. Then he sighed, a long gray sigh, and turned, and began to walk slowly along the counter, behind the people in the line, and Corin understood that he was supposed to follow.
"Rules," said Briggs, as they walked. He did not look round. He said it the way a man says something he has said a thousand times, to a thousand new faces, and no longer hears. "One. You don't talk to them. Two. You don't look at them, not in the face. They don't like it. Makes them change their minds, and then they go back out and stand in the line again tomorrow, and it's all to do twice." A pause. "Three. You don't touch the glass. Not ever. Not unless you're Bank. Ministry men touch the glass, all sorts of things happen. I've seen it."
"What things?"
Briggs did not answer that. He walked on.
"Four," he said. "Whatever they sell, it's theirs to sell. Not yours. Not mine. Not the Archon's. Theirs. They come in of their own feet. They put their own hand on. Nobody makes them." He stopped by a stall where a woman was standing with her hand on the glass, and her eyes shut, and her mouth trembling, and he looked at her without any expression at all. "It's a free city."
The woman at the glass was perhaps thirty. Thin, with red hands, and a shawl over her hair. Her other hand, the one not on the glass, was holding the hand of a small boy who stood beside her, three or four, sucking his thumb and looking round at the bright lamps with enormous eyes.
The clerk behind the counter was writing on a card.
Corin read it upside down. He could not help it. His face. Her husband's. Drowned. Two years. And a price. One silver.
"She's selling his face," said Corin.
"Rule one," said Briggs.
"She'll go home and she won't know what he looked like. The boy's father."
"Rule two." Briggs did not raise his voice. He did not need to. "And she'll go home and she'll have one silver. For the rent. And the boy will eat this week." He looked at Corin, finally. His small pale tired eyes. "And she won't wake up every night seeing it under the water. That's what they sell, mostly, Harvester. Not the good things. People think it's the good things. It's not. It's the things that keep them up at night." He shrugged. "The good things are just what the Bank pays best for."
The clerk pressed the little clear stone against the glass. The blue flowed into it. The woman's mouth stopped trembling. Her face went smooth and calm. She opened her eyes. She looked down at the little boy holding her hand, and smiled at him, a small, tired, pleasant smile, and took the silver, and the card, and went away toward the door at the far end, with the boy trotting beside her.
The boy looked back. Just once. At the lamps.
Corin's gloved hands were fists.
"You'll get used to it," said Briggs.
"Have you?"
Briggs looked at him for a long moment.
"Twenty years," he said. "Near enough. I don't remember not being used to it." He turned away. "Come on. There's a room at the back where they keep the slag. That's what's left in the glasses at the end of the day. Bits that didn't go into the stones. It builds up. Clouds the glass. Somebody's got to clear it out. The Bank's been using a man with a bucket and a cloth." He walked on. "Reckon a harvester's better than a bucket."
Corin did not move.
"Warden Briggs," he said.
Briggs stopped. He did not turn round.
"The paper," said Corin. "From the Archon's office. About me. What did it say? My name. Did it say my name?"
"Course it did." Briggs half turned. "Harvester Vane. Corin. Senior. Third Basement. To be kept busy and out of the way." He snorted, very softly. "That's the Archon's office for you. Out of the way. As if there's any out of the way in this place."
He started to turn back.
And stopped.
It was a small thing. Corin would not have seen it if he had not been watching the man's face, the way he had learned that morning to watch faces, as if they were rooms he had to search. Briggs had stopped halfway through turning, with his weight on one foot, and his heavy gray face had gone very still.
"Vane," he said.
Not to Corin. To himself. Slowly. As if he were tasting it.
"Vane," he said again. And frowned. His small pale eyes had gone far away, unfocused, looking at nothing, at the cream-painted wall, at the high barred windows, at a place a long way past both. "Knew a Vane once. I think. Long time back." His frown deepened. "Teacher, was it? A teacher. In a… in a schoolhouse. With a bell. And a big tree outside, an oak, and…"
He stopped.
His hand had gone up to his own chest. Flat. Pressed there, over the brass plate. WARDEN. INTAKE. BRIGGS. As if something under it hurt.
Corin did not breathe.
Then Briggs blinked. Hard. Twice. And the far-away look went out of his eyes like a lamp being blown out, and he was just a tired gray man in a dark green coat again, in a warm bright hall that smelled of polish and crying.
"No," he said. Gruffly. To nobody. "No. Never knew nobody. Don't know where that came from." He took his hand off his chest and looked at it, puzzled, the way the old man at the glass had looked at his. Then he shook his head, the way a dog shakes off water. "Come on. Slag room. Back here."
He walked away down the long bright hall, behind the long patient line, past the glasses waking and going dark, waking and going dark, one after another, all the way to the far wall.
Corin followed him.
But before he did, he took out the Ledger, and wrote, very small, with the gray glove on, so that the pencil skidded:
Warden Briggs. Intake Hall, Lantern Quay. Twenty years here. "I don't remember not being used to it." When he heard my name: "Knew a Vane once. A teacher. A schoolhouse with a bell. A big oak tree." His hand went to his chest. Then: "Never knew nobody."
And under it:
A schoolhouse. A bell. An oak.
He did not know why those three things together made his hands shake. He wrote them down anyway, so that someone would have them.
Rent Day
The slag room was a cellar under the back of the old warehouse, with a low brick ceiling and no window, and it was full of glass.
Not new glass, like the sheets on the long counter upstairs. Old glass. Spent glass. Dozens of sheets of it, stacked on wooden racks along the walls, each one the size of a tabletop, each one gone cloudy. Not dirty. Clouded from inside. As if somebody had breathed on a window in winter, and the breath had stayed, and frozen, and would not wipe off. In some of them, if you looked closely, you could see faint shapes moving in the cloud. Very small. Very slow. A hand. A window. The corner of a smile. Turning, and fading, and coming back.
"Bits," said Briggs, from the doorway. He did not come in. "Bits that didn't go in the stones. Every glass gets a bit of slag on it, every day. After a month it's too cloudy to read. They bring it down here. Man with a bucket used to wipe it." He sniffed. "Never came out properly. Bucket man went a bit odd, too, in the end. Started crying at nothing. Had to let him go."
He left. His footsteps went away up the brick stair, slow and heavy.
Corin stood in the cold cellar among the clouded glass.
Then, because there was nothing else to do, and because the paper from the Archon's office said he was to be kept busy, he took out the rod.
It was easy. That surprised him. Easier than the roof above the Cut, easier than the grate in Lord Thorne's empty room. He held the rod against the first clouded sheet, flat, the tip of it just touching the glass, and let it listen. And the cloud came. Slowly. Thread by thread. Out of the glass and into the silver, the way water goes into a cloth. The glass cleared behind it, like a window clearing when the sun comes up. And the rod took the bits in.
He felt them go past. Tiny. Broken. A smell of bread. Half a face. A word in a language he did not know. A dog barking, very far off. The edge of a song. A hand on a child's hair. None of them whole. None of them anybody. Just the scraps, the crumbs, the leftover corners of a thousand things that a thousand people had sold for coppers at a long bright counter upstairs.
Scraps, said Malakor. His voice was thin and sour with disgust. They've got you sweeping the floor, boy. Picking up crumbs from under a rich man's table.
It's work.
It's an insult. A pause. To them. Not to you.
Corin did not answer. He moved the rod to the next glass, and the next, and the next. He worked until his arm ached and the rod was heavy and cold with crumbs, and the racks along one whole wall stood clear and clean and empty, like windows with nothing behind them. Then he went to the jars that Briggs had left on a shelf by the door, small ones, Bank jars, with gold lids, and emptied the rod into them, one after another, and labeled them, in his big untidy hand, because nobody had told him not to.
Slag. Lantern Quay. Friday. Pieces of many people. Nobody's.
And under each one, very small, because it seemed wrong not to:
Somebody's.
It was dark when he came out onto Lantern Quay. The line was gone. The great white columns of the Bank stood empty against the black river, and the gilded letters over them caught the light of the gemstone lamps along the quay and shone faintly gold. FORGET YOUR TROUBLES. He did not look at them twice.
He walked home.
He walked slowly, up the Salt Stairs and through the narrow wet streets of the Lower Docks, with his hands in his pockets, his right hand round three silver and seven coppers, and his left hand round the empty jar in its handkerchief. He did not hurry. He did not want to get to No. 14 Tallow Street. He knew what was on his door.
RENT: 4 SILVER A WEEK. YOU OWE 4 SILVER. DUE FRIDAY.
He went in anyway.
The onion-smelling passage. The purple door at the end of it. He stood in front of it in the dark for a long moment with the coins in his fist. Then he knocked.
It opened at once. As if she had been standing behind it.
"Well?" said Mrs. Marta.
He held out his hand, and opened it.
Three silver. Seven coppers. On his palm, in the light from the doorway, dull and worn.
Mrs. Marta looked at them.
She looked at them for a long time. Then she looked up at his face, with her small black currant eyes, and said nothing at all. And he understood that she was waiting for him to tell her a story. And that if it was a lie, she would know.
So he told her the truth.
He told it badly. Standing in her doorway in the dark, with his hand held out. A dead scribe called Pell. A red sock. A twelve-year-old girl with no address who was practicing her letters. A woman from the Bank with a gold tooth, who said the Bank does not buy, it lends. A debt that would go looking for the girl if nobody paid it. Toby's two silver for a new cup. His own five. A stamp. PAID IN FULL.
When he had finished, Mrs. Marta did not say anything for a long time.
Then she reached out with one small dry hand like a bird's claw, and took three of the silver coins off his palm. Not the coppers. She pushed the coppers back at him with one finger.
"Eat," she said.
"I owe you four."
"You owe me one." She tucked the three silver into the pocket of her black dress. "Next Friday." She looked at him. "And if you pay another dead man's debt before then, you'll owe me two, and I'll put it on your door in letters so big Old Odding can read them from his bed."
She started to close the door. Then she stopped, the way she always stopped, with her hand on the purple wood.
"Boy," she said.
"Yes?"
"That girl. The twelve-year-old." She was not looking at him. She was looking at the wall of the passage, at the dark. "She'll never know, I suppose. That you did it."
"No."
Mrs. Marta was quiet for a moment.
"Terrence would have done that," she said. "When he was Terrence." And she shut the door.
Corin stood in the dark passage.
Then he went up the stairs. Past the old man in the chair by the round window, who was not there now, whose chair stood empty in the dark. Past the little girl's door with the cat's saucer outside it. Past the scratched words on the fourth-floor landing, and the scratched bird beneath them, hanging from its scratched string. Up to the top. To the door with the papers round it.
There was a new one.
It was pinned at the very top of the frame, with one of the beaded hatpins, a green bead. Brown wrapping paper. Purple ink. Small spiky letters leaning hard to the right like thorns in a wind.
OWES 1 SILVER. NEXT FRIDAY.
And under it, smaller, as if she had added it after, and had pressed so hard on the pen that it had scratched:
HE IS A FOOL. A GOOD ONE.
He stood in front of it for a long time.
Then he went in, and lit the little brass lamp on the crate, and turned it down to a small blue bead, and did not put it out. And he sat down on the edge of the narrow iron bed and took out the Ledger and wrote it all. The slag room. The crumbs. Somebody's. Mrs. Marta. Three silver. Terrence would have done that, when he was Terrence. The note on the door.
When he had finished, he sat with the pencil in his hand and the book open on his knee, and did not move.
Boy, said Malakor.
The voice was very quiet. Not sour. Not bored. Not thin with disgust, the way it had been in the slag room. Just quiet. Like an old man speaking in the dark, in a room where somebody is trying to sleep.
Malakor.
The Auditor. A long pause. This morning. When she put her hand on yours. On the glass.
Yes.
I felt her too. The voice was slow. Careful. As if each word were being taken out of a box where it had been put away a long time ago. I'm in the rod, boy. I feel what you feel. And when the glass went white, I felt her hand. Through yours. A pause. I've felt that hand before.
Corin did not breathe.
Where?
On a glass. The voice was very low. A long time ago. A small hand. Smaller than it is now. Much smaller. Slapped down flat on a sheet of glass on an iron frame, in a long room with tall windows and a bell tower, and an oak tree outside the windows, full of birds, settling for the night. A pause. Before anyone could stop her.
The lamp hissed softly on its crate.
And the glass went white, said Malakor. Just like this morning. Whiter than anything I'd ever seen. Whiter than the boy before her.
What boy?
No answer.
Malakor. What boy?
For a long time there was nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, in the house, a baby was crying, thin and angry, and a woman's voice was singing to it, off-key. Out in Tallow Street, a cart went by.
Linus said a word, said Malakor at last. When the glass went white. For her. One word. I heard it through his hand. I was in his rod then. I've been in a lot of hands.
What word?
The old voice was very soft.
Blazing.
Corin sat on the edge of his narrow bed in the little gray room, with the Ledger open on his knee and the blue bead of the lamp beside him and the cold rod against his side, and could not move.
Ask me another night, said Malakor. I'm tired. I've been carrying crumbs all afternoon. A pause. And then, gentler than Corin had ever heard it, so gentle that it hardly sounded like the same voice at all: But write her down, boy. Before you sleep. Big. On a page by herself. So you'll see her in the morning.
And then he was gone.
Corin turned to a clean page. Near the front of the new Ledger. After the pages that told him who he was, and where he lived, and who Juno was, and who Lyra was. A page by itself.
He wrote it in the middle. In capitals. As big as the letters on the first page that said READ THIS FIRST. Pressing so hard that the pencil went through the paper on the last letter.
ELARA.
And under it, smaller:
Auditor, Fourth Desk, the Oblivion Bank. Short dark hair, cut straight. Dark eyes. A gold front tooth, over a gap. She reads people by touch. Every reading costs her a day. She has nothing before the Bank. When she read me, the glass went white, and I saw a pool and a girl with a gap in her teeth who called me Cor. When she was small, she put her hand on a glass in a schoolhouse with an oak tree outside, and it went white, and Master Hale said "Blazing." Malakor heard it.
She says the song isn't finished.
She doesn't know who I am.
I don't know who I am.
He looked at the page for a long time.
Then, at the very bottom, he wrote one more line. And this time his hand let him put the question mark at the end.
Is she?
He closed the book. He lay down on the narrow bed in his clothes, with the book on his chest, the way he always did, and his hand flat on its stiff new cover. The little blue bead of the lamp burned beside him.
The three notes came into his throat. Up. And up. And up. He hummed them, softly, in the dark.
And then he tried to find the fourth. The one that came down. The one Wren Halloway had sung him in the Third Basement. The one Elara had said was missing.
He could not find it. He reached for it, and reached, and it was not there. As if it belonged to someone else. As if somebody else were holding it, somewhere in the city, in the dark, the way a small hot hand holds on to a bigger one.
He fell asleep still reaching.
And in the morning, he would not remember any of it. But the page would be there. By itself. With the pencil gone through the paper on the last letter.
Waiting.
