Chapter 8: The Auditor, Part 2 of 5: The Account of Pell Dorran
The Account of Pell Dorran
She did not turn round. She lifted one white-gloved hand from the glass, a little way, and held it out to her side, palm up.
The big man by the door moved. Corin had not seen him move before. He did it without a sound, without seeming to hurry, and yet he was across the room and kneeling by the brass-bound case before Corin had properly seen him go. He opened the case. Inside, Corin saw velvet, dark green, and things lying on it in shaped hollows, the way the rod lay in its long case at home. The big man took out three of them, one by one, and put them into the Auditor's open hand. Then he closed the case, and went back to the door, and stood there again with his hands folded.
The first thing was a jar.
Corin knew it at once, though he did not remember it. A small glass jar with a brass lid, the same as the empty ones on the shelves, the same as the one in his own pocket. It had a paper label on it. The label was in his own big untidy hand.
DORRAN, P. Last minutes. Stable. Do not open.
He hummed.
The seal was still on the lid. A twist of gray wax, unbroken. And the jar was empty. Not a thread. Not a wisp. Clean glass, as if it had never held anything at all.
Toby made a small sound by the wall.
"Collected this morning," said Elara, "from the vault of the Inquisition, under the east tower, by order of the Fourth Desk. Mister Gage signed for it." She set it down on the sheet of glass, in the very middle, very carefully, with its label facing Corin. "Sealed. Unbroken. Empty."
The second thing was a book. Small, thin, bound in dark green leather with gold edges to the pages. It looked like a little prayer book, the kind a child might be given on its naming day. She laid it on the desk beside the glass and opened it. The pages were ruled in columns, in faint gold lines. On the open page, in a small neat brown hand that was not hers, there were five or six entries. Corin could read the top of the page upside down.
DORRAN, PELL. ACCOUNT 41-7716. LOWER DOCKS BRANCH.
The third thing was a pen. Silver, plain, with a gold nib. She laid it beside the book, exactly parallel to the edge of the page.
Then she took off her gloves.
She did it slowly. One finger at a time, the left hand first. She undid the tiny pearl button at the wrist, and drew each finger loose, little finger first, then the ring finger, then the next, then the thumb, and drew the glove off and laid it flat on the desk beside the pen. Then the right. The same. When she had done it, she laid the two gloves together, one on top of the other, palm to palm, and smoothed them flat.
Her hands were thin and long and very pale. The nails were cut short and square. And on the tips of the first three fingers of each hand, the skin was different. Smoother. Shinier. Almost silver in the lamplight, like the skin over an old burn, or like the inside of a shell. As if they had touched something very cold, or very hot, a great many times.
"Pell Dorran," she said, "opened an account with the Bank two years and four months ago, at the Lower Docks branch, on Lantern Quay." She did not look at the little green book. She did not need to. She was looking at Corin. "He made five deposits. The first, a little over two years ago. A birthday. His own. His seventh. A cake with plums in it, and his grandmother singing, and a wooden horse with a string tail. He was paid two silver." She said it the way a clerk reads out a column of figures. "The second, eight months later. The taste of the first apple from a particular tree. One silver and four. The third. His first day at this Ministry. Being shown his desk. Feeling proud. One silver." A pause. "The fourth and fifth were smaller. Coppers. A dream he had once of flying. The smell of his grandmother's kitchen on a baking day."
Toby had put his hand over his mouth.
"Seven silver and some coppers, in all," said Elara. "Over two years. All of it withdrawn on the day of deposit. In coin." She looked at Corin. "Do you know what he did with it, Harvester?"
Corin did. It was in the Ledger. A red sock, darned at the heel with blue thread, stuffed into the toe of a boot. That's his money. For his sister.
He did not say so.
He did not look at Toby either. He kept his eyes on the Auditor's face. It was the hardest thing he had done that morning. Harder than dragging the desk. Because Toby's coat pocket, on the other side of the room, was clinking very faintly every time Toby breathed, and Corin could hear it, and he did not know if she could.
She waited. When he did not answer, the dry thing moved at the corner of her mouth again, and she nodded, as if he had told her something.
"No," she said. "Of course you don't."
She laid her bare left hand flat on the sheet of glass. Beside the jar. Not touching it.
The glass woke up.
It was not like a lamp being lit. It was like a breath going into a cold window. The thousands of fine silver lines inside the glass, the ones Corin had seen when she came in, like frost, like the veins of a leaf, began to glow. Faintly. A cold pale blue, spreading out from under her palm in every direction, like ink dropped in water, like frost growing across a pane on a winter night. It hummed. Very softly. A thin high note, right at the edge of hearing, that Corin felt more in his teeth than in his ears.
And something in him went very still, and very cold, and very small.
He did not know why. He had never seen a glass like that. He was sure he had never seen a glass like that. But his right hand, flat on the desk in its gray glove, had curled into a fist again, so hard that the leather creaked, and the ache under his ribs had stopped being a thumb pressing and become a hand. A whole hand. Pressing flat. Pressing in.
Elara did not look at him. She was looking at the glass. At the place where the cold blue glow ran under the empty jar.
And under the jar, the lines were different.
Everywhere else on the glass, the silver lines ran smooth and even, branching like the veins of a leaf. But under the jar, in a circle exactly the size of its base, they did something else. They curled. They had been bent round, all of them, into a pattern. A spiral. Fine and tight and perfect, going round and round and round toward the middle, like the inside of a seashell. Or the thread of a screw.
Or the carving on the tip of a silver rod.
"Do you see it?" said Elara.
Her voice was quite soft now. Almost interested. The voice of someone who has found something unusual in a column of figures.
"Ash leaves a mark," she said. "When it is moved. On everything it passes through. Glass most of all. Most people cannot see the mark. I can." Her fingers moved very slightly on the glass, and the spiral under the jar brightened. "This residue did not leave its jar through the lid. The lid was never opened. It was drawn out through the glass. Pulled. Slowly. Thread by thread. The way a magnet pulls iron filings across a table. By someone holding a rod against the outside of the jar. A rod with a spiral at its tip."
She looked up from the glass. At Corin. At his face. And then, slowly, deliberately, down. At the right side of his coat. At the long narrow pocket.
"There are not many rods like that left in Veridia, Harvester," she said. "The Bank keeps a list. The old kind, with the spiral. Made before the Ministry was the Ministry. Most of them were melted down, years ago, when the new rods came. There are seven on the list." A pause. "One of them is registered to this Section."
The rod in his pocket was cold. The ordinary cold.
And then not ordinary.
It went cold the other way. The deep way. The cellar way. It came through the wool and through his shirt and into his side like a hand of ice, and he did not gasp, he did not let himself gasp, but his whole body went rigid in the creaking chair, and he felt the frost come into the corners of his eyes.
Not me, said Malakor. Very quiet. Very fast. Behind his left ear. I'd know my own marks, boy. That's not my spiral. Look at it. Look at the turn of it. It goes the wrong way.
Corin looked.
The spiral under the jar went round toward its middle. Round, and round, and round. He made himself look at which way it turned. Left. Against the sun. Like water going down a drain on the other side of the world.
Mine goes right, said Malakor. Linus cut it that way. Thirty years ago. With his own hand. He said a rod should turn the way the clock turns, so that it's always going forward. A pause. And then, lower, so low that it was hardly a voice at all: I only ever knew one other harvester who cut his spiral to the left.
Who?
No answer.
Malakor. Who?
The cold stayed. The voice did not.
Elara was watching his face. She had seen the frost come into his eyes. He knew she had. She had not looked away for a second. She sat very straight on the front edge of her chair with her bare hand flat on the glowing glass and her dark careful eyes on him, and her face did not change at all.
"Your eyes have gone pale," she said. "At the edges. Like frost on a window." A pause. "Someone is talking to you, Harvester. Not your clerk. Someone in your coat."
Corin did not answer.
"It doesn't matter," said Elara. She took her hand off the glass. The glow drained out of it, back toward the place where her palm had been, and lingered there for a moment in a small pale patch shaped like a hand, and went out. The spiral under the jar faded with it, until there was only clear glass and silver lines and an empty jar with a label that said He hummed. "I do not need you to tell me. I only need you to show me your rod."
"No," said Corin.
She looked at him.
"It's not the same rod," he said. His voice was rough. He made it steady. "Look at the spiral. It turns to the left. Mine turns to the right."
The room was very quiet.
Elara looked down at the glass. At the place where the spiral had been. Her bare fingers moved on it, very lightly, not quite touching, as if she were tracing the shape of something she could still see and he could not.
She did it for a long time.
Then she looked up.
"You know which way your rod turns," she said slowly, "without looking at it."
"Yes."
"Most harvesters don't. Most of them couldn't tell you which end is which without a label." Her dark eyes rested on him, very steady, very careful. "Who told you?"
Someone in my coat, Corin did not say.
He said nothing at all.
Elara watched him say nothing. Then she picked up the silver pen from beside the little green book, and dipped it in an inkwell that Mister Gage had put on the desk at some point when Corin had not been looking, and wrote something in the book. Small. Upright. Perfect. He could not read it upside down.
"Then we will leave the rod," she said, without looking up. "For now. And go on to the living." She blotted the line. "Because a dead man's account cannot be closed until we know where his residue went. And if it did not go into your rod, Harvester, then it went into someone else's. And the last two people who touched this jar before it reached the Inquisition were you." She looked up. "And your clerk."
Ungloved
Toby went white.
Corin heard it happen. Not saw. Heard. A small sharp breath by the wall, and then nothing, the way a candle goes out.
"He's a clerk," said Corin. "He fills in forms. He didn't harvest anything. He stood at the end of the lane with his back turned."
"He carried the jar," said Elara, "from the lane to the corner of Needle Street. Inquisitor Halloway's report says so. She is very thorough." She did not look round at Toby. She kept her eyes on Corin. "Anyone who touches deposited residue may be read by an Auditor of the Bank. Charter of the Oblivion Bank, clause eleven. Signed by the Archon's grandfather. Countersigned by the Council." A pause. "Your clerk knows his Regulations, Harvester. I know my Charter."
"You're not reading him."
"I can read you instead." She held out her bare left hand across the desk, palm up, over the scarred wood, over the landslide of papers, toward him. Not far. Not threatening. Just there. Waiting. "Take off your glove, and put your hand in mine, and I will leave your clerk alone."
The room was very quiet.
Corin looked at the hand.
It was thin and long and pale, with the nails cut short and square, and the first three fingertips smooth and shiny and faintly silver, like the inside of a shell. It was quite steady. It did not shake at all.
He did not know why he wanted to take it.
That was the strangest thing. Stranger than the glass, stranger than the spiral. He was frightened of her. He was more frightened of her than he had been of anything he could remember. And he wanted, very badly, to take off his glove and put his hand in hers. Not because she had asked. Because of something else. Something that had nothing to do with clauses or Charters or jars. Something that came up from under his ribs, where the ache was, and pulled.
GLOVES. ALL DAY. DO NOT LET THE AUDITOR TOUCH YOU.
In his own hand. Torn through the paper.
He did not move.
Elara waited. Her hand stayed out across the desk. One breath. Two. Five. Then, slowly, she drew it back, and laid it flat on the glass again, and the dry thing moved at the corner of her mouth, and the gold tooth gleamed.
"Then I will read your clerk," she said.
"Inspector."
It was Toby.
He had come away from the wall. He was standing at the end of the big desk, in the gap where you could squeeze past, with his thin hands hanging at his sides and his spectacles crooked and his face as white as the salt crusts on the stone. His big ears were red. His voice was shaking so badly that the one word had come out in three pieces.
"It's all right," said Toby. "It's all right, Inspector. Let her."
"Toby—"
"No. Listen." He swallowed. "I'm only a clerk. There's nothing in me. Not like you. I've got forms and Regulations and pigeon pie and a landlady who's going to be furious about my door. That's all." He looked at Elara. "You can read that. You're welcome to it. You can read all of it." His voice wobbled, and he pushed it flat. "But you leave him alone."
Elara looked at him for the first time.
She looked at him the way she had looked at the room when she came in. Quickly. Carefully. All of him, from his polished boots to his tuft of hair. She looked at his hands. At his face. At his coat. At the inside pocket on the left of his too-big gray coat, buttoned shut, where something had made the wool hang a little lower than on the other side.
Her eyes stayed on that pocket for one breath longer than on anything else.
Then she nodded.
"Sit, Junior Clerk," she said. Not unkindly. Not kindly either. The way you would say sit to a patient. "There. On the end of the desk. Where I can reach you."
Toby sat on the corner of the big desk, at the end by the gap, with his knees together. He was so close to Corin that Corin could hear him breathing. Short, quick breaths, through his nose, like a small animal in a trap.
Elara stood up. She came round the end of the chair and stood beside Toby, by the corner of the desk. She was taller than him. She looked down at him. Then she lifted the sheet of glass in its silver frame from the desk, and held it flat in her left hand, like a tray.
"Put your right hand on the glass," she said. "Flat. It doesn't hurt."
Corin's whole body went cold.
It doesn't hurt.
He did not know why those words did that to him. He did not know where he had heard them before. He did not remember ever hearing them. But they went into him like a needle, and something at the very bottom of him, under everything, under the Ledger and the ache and the cold rod and the dark, jerked once, hard, like a sleeper kicking in a dream.
Put your hand on it. Like on a cold window in the winter. And then we go home and have supper.
A woman's voice. Very calm. Very far away.
He did not know whose.
Toby had put his hand on the glass.
He did it slowly, as if he were putting it into cold water. Thin and pale and inky, the fingers spread, flat on the clear glass over the fine silver lines. Elara laid her own bare right hand on top of his. Palm to the back of his hand. Fingers lying along his fingers. The silver-shiny fingertips resting on his inky knuckles.
And the glass woke up.
The cold blue light came up out of it, out of the silver lines, round Toby's spread hand, and spread. Not far. A few inches. A soft cold glow, like moonlight on frost. It lit Toby's face from below, his round spectacles, his pale pointed nose, his wide frightened eyes. It lit Elara's face. Her straight dark brows. Her narrow chin. Her dark careful eyes, looking down into the glass, not at Toby, but at the light, the way you look at a page you are reading.
And she read.
"Junior Clerk," she said. Her voice had gone soft and level and very quick, the voice of someone reading a column of figures aloud. "Harvesting Section. Five years. Balance, in good order. Deposits with the Bank: none. You have never sold anything." The faintest pause. "That is rare. In a clerk. On your wage."
Toby did not move.
"Debts," said Elara. "Small. You owe your landlady for a door. You owe a man called Brightwater a Form three-R. Two years overdue." Toby's ears went from red to scarlet. "You lent your Harvester one silver in the spring. He has not paid it back. You will not ask."
She was quiet for a moment, reading.
"A friend," she said. More slowly. "A friend you ate with. On a warm step. Bread and dripping. He had a laugh like a…" She paused. Her eyes moved on the glass, as if she were looking for a word. "Like a donkey. He sharpened your nibs. He showed you how. Nobody else would." Another pause. "He is gone. Four days. You have not slept properly since."
Toby's face had crumpled. Just a little. He pressed his lips together hard, and it uncrumpled, and he stared straight ahead at the shelves of jars with his eyes very wide and very wet.
"A lie," said Elara.
The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well.
"Tuesday," she said. "The tenth bell. A form. Twelve-B. Content of residue. Summary. Four sentences." Her voice did not change at all. "You knew what should have gone in that box. A book. A red one. A word. You left them out. You signed your name under four true sentences, knowing there should have been a fifth." A pause. "It is the first lie you have ever told on a form. In your whole life."
Toby made a sound. Very small. Like something tearing.
"And another," said Elara. "Wednesday. The eighth bell. A man in a white coat. What red book? Your voice went up at the end." She almost, very nearly, sounded sorry. "It does that. When you lie. It is the most honest thing about you."
Corin's hands were fists on the desk. He did not remember making them.
"Stop," he said.
She did not look at him. She was looking at the glass. At the cold blue light round Toby's hand. And her face had changed, very slightly. Her dark brows had drawn together. As if she had come, in her column of figures, to a number that did not add up.
"A sock," she said slowly.
Toby went absolutely still.
"Red," said Elara. "Darned at the heel. Blue thread. You carry it. You have carried it every day since Tuesday. It clinks." Her eyes moved on the glass. "Coins. Seven silver and some coppers." A pause. "And a letter. In pencil. On brown paper. Dear Pell." She was reading it. Corin could see her reading it, there in the light, in the glass, in Toby. Her lips moved a little. "Gran says to tell you the apples are in. A girl. I am going to be a scribe too. A girl called…"
She stopped.
Not because she could not read it. Corin saw that. She had read it. She had read the name, there in the glass, at the bottom of the letter. Your sister, Nan. And something had happened to her face.
It was very small. You would not have seen it unless you had been watching her face the way Corin had been watching it, since the moment she walked in. A flicker. At the very bottom of her dark careful eyes. Like a fish turning, once, in deep water.
Your sister.
Then it was gone.
And her right hand jerked on Toby's. Hard. As if the glass had bitten her.
The cold blue light went out.
All at once. Not draining back toward her palm this time. Just gone, like a lamp knocked over in the dark. Toby snatched his hand back off the glass and pressed it against his chest, against the left side of his coat, against the buttoned pocket and the faint clink beneath it, and sat there on the corner of the desk shaking from head to foot.
Elara had not moved.
She stood beside the desk with the dead glass held flat in her left hand and her bare right hand lifted a little way above it, frozen, fingers spread, the way your hand stays when you have touched something very hot and snatched it back before you have had time to think. Her face had gone very pale. Paler than before. And her eyes were not on Toby, or on Corin, or on the glass. They were on nothing. On the middle of the air. Wide and dark and empty, as if she were looking for something on a shelf that was not there anymore.
Her lips moved. No sound.
Then she blinked.
And she was back. Her face closed over, smooth and pale and careful. Her hand came down. She laid the glass on the desk again, in front of her chair, and squared it with the edge of the wood with both hands. And she sat down, on the front edge of the chair, very straight, with her knees together.
She did not look at anybody.
She picked up the silver pen from beside the little green book. But she did not write in the green book. She reached into the inside of her dark green jacket, on the left side, over her heart, and took out a different book. A very small one. No bigger than the palm of her hand. Bound in black leather, worn soft and shiny at the corners, with a gold clasp. She opened the clasp. She opened the book. And she wrote in it, very small, very fast, with the silver pen, two lines, and blotted them with her thumb, and closed it, and fastened the clasp, and put it back inside her jacket.
Then she looked up. At Corin.
Her face was perfectly calm. But the hand holding the silver pen was not quite steady.
"Your clerk is honest," she said. "Except twice. Both times for you." A pause. "And he is carrying the dead man's estate in his coat."
