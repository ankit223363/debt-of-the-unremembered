Chapter 8: The Auditor, Part 4 of 5: The Debt
The Debt
She sat down at last.
She did it the way she had done it before, on the front edge of the chair, very straight, with her knees together. She drew the little green book toward her across the desk and opened it at Pell Dorran's page, and picked up the silver pen, and dipped it. Her hands had stopped shaking. Or nearly.
"Seven silver," she said, "and six coppers."
She wrote it at the bottom of the column, under the five deposits, and drew a line under it, very straight.
"That is the balance. Lent to Pell Dorran by the Bank against five memories, over two years, and now called in. Clause four." She looked up. "The account cannot be closed until it is paid. If it is paid today, from the estate, it is closed today, and the Bank has no further claim on anybody. Not on the estate. Not on the next of kin. Not on a girl in a village with a tree." A pause. "If it is not paid today, the claim passes. And the Bank begins to look."
Toby had come round the end of the desk. He was standing beside Corin's chair now, close, with one hand on its back, the way Lucian had held his black book. Corin could feel the hand trembling through the wood.
"The estate is a red sock," said Corin. "The estate is a twelve-year-old girl's way to the city."
"I know what the estate is, Harvester. I read it."
"Then you know what you're asking for."
"I am not asking for anything." Her voice was perfectly level. "I am telling you what the Charter says. The Charter does not care about socks. It cares about columns. A column has to add up." She laid the pen down beside the book, parallel to the edge. "It does not say whose money adds it up."
Corin looked at her.
She looked back. Her face did not change. Her dark eyes did not move. But somewhere at the corner of her mouth, the dry thing was there again. Very small. Not a smile. Something that knew exactly what it had just said, and was not going to say it a second time.
"Whose money," said Corin slowly.
"The Charter requires that the balance be paid against the account," said Elara. "By the close of business. In coin. It does not require that the coin come from the deceased." She folded her bare hands on the dead glass. "It is very old, the Charter. It was written by men who thought that nobody would ever pay a dead man's debt who did not have to."
The lamp hummed.
Toby's hand had stopped trembling on the back of the chair.
"I get paid today," said Corin. "At the noon bell."
"I know. The Ministry pays its officers on a Friday. In silver. By the pipe." She did not look at the brass hatch above Toby's desk. "I have seen your wage, Harvester. It is in your account. Nine silver a week." A pause. "You owe four to a landlady. You owe two to a stationer. You owe one to your clerk, which you will not pay because he will not ask." Her eyes went, very briefly, to Toby, and back. "That leaves two."
"Seven and six is more than two."
"Yes," said Elara. "It is."
She did not say anything else. She waited.
Corin sat in the creaking chair and did the sum. He did not need the Ledger for it. Nine silver. Seven silver and six coppers. That left one silver and four coppers. For a week. And four owed to Mrs. Marta on her purple door, in her small spiky handwriting, DUE FRIDAY. And IF YOU FORGET ME AGAIN I WILL NOT BE PLEASED.
He thought of a nice young couple who would remember her name.
Then he thought of a girl at a window, practicing her letters.
"I'll pay it," he said.
"Inspector—" Toby's hand had tightened on the chair.
"At noon. When the pipe comes." Corin did not look away from Elara. "Seven silver and six coppers. Against the account of Pell Dorran. And then it's closed. And the sock is nobody's business. And the Bank never goes looking for anybody."
Elara looked at him for a long moment.
"The Bank will accept a pledge," she said. "From an officer of the Ministry. Against his wages. Signed now. Paid at noon." She turned a page in the little green book, to a blank one, and wrote on it quickly, small and upright and perfect, and turned the book round on the desk so that it faced him, and laid the silver pen beside it. "Read it first. All of it. Three times, if you like."
He read it.
I, Corin Vane, Senior Investigator, Harvesting Section, Ministry of Records, pledge to the Oblivion Bank the sum of seven silver and six coppers, from my wages, at the noon bell this day, against the account of Pell Dorran, deceased. On payment, the account is closed, and no claim of the Bank shall pass to any person.
If the sum is not paid by the close of business, the debt passes to me, with the Bank's interest, and I consent to be read by the Bank at any time, until it is paid.
He read the last line again.
I consent to be read by the Bank at any time.
He looked up at her. She was watching him. She knew which line he was reading. Her face did not change.
"That's the price of a pledge," she said. "If you break it. Not if you keep it."
"And if I keep it?"
"Then you are a man who paid a dead stranger's debt from his own wage," said Elara, "and the Bank will think you are a fool, and write it down, and never think of you again." A pause. "Except me."
He picked up the pen.
"Wait."
It was Toby.
He had let go of the chair. He was digging in the pockets of his too-big gray coat. Not the left pocket, the one with the clink. The right one. And the inside of the right one. And his trouser pockets. He came out with a handful of things and dropped them on the desk beside the little green book. A stub of pencil. A bit of string. A crumpled handbill. A brass button that was not from anywhere special. And coins. Two silvers, bright and worn. And three coppers.
"My wage is Friday too," he said. His voice shook. "But I've got these. From last week. I was saving them for a new cup. For the Section. Since they cut the crockery allowance." He pushed the coins across the desk toward Elara with one inky finger. "Two silver and three. Against the account. Toward it. So it's not all him."
The room was very quiet.
Elara looked at the coins. Then at Toby. For a long moment, she did not move. And then, very slowly, she reached out with one bare hand and drew the coins toward her across the desk, and stacked them, two silver and three coppers, in a neat little pile beside the green book.
"Junior Clerk," she said. "I read you less than an hour ago."
"I know."
"You have never sold anything to the Bank. Not once. In five years. On your wage." A pause. "Do you know how rare that is?"
Toby's ears went red.
"I don't need to read you again," said Elara, "to know that this is the last money you have until noon."
"I know," said Toby. "I'll have bread." He swallowed. "Pell always said bread was enough."
Elara did not say anything to that.
She picked up the silver pen. She crossed out seven silver and six coppers in the pledge, with one very straight line, and wrote above it, small and upright: five silver and three coppers. Then she turned to Pell Dorran's page, and under the balance she wrote: Received, against the account, from T., Junior Clerk: two silver, three coppers. And blotted it with her thumb.
Then she turned the pledge back round to Corin.
"Sign," she said.
He signed. C. Vane. In his big untidy hand that sloped hard to the right and pressed too deep, under her small upright perfect one.
She looked at his signature for a long time. Longer than she needed to. As if she were reading something in the shape of the letters that was not there.
Then she closed the green book.
"Noon," she said.
"Noon."
"I will wait." She stood, and went to the end of the room, to the shelves, as far from Corin's desk as she could get without leaving, and stood there with her back to the jars and her hands folded in front of her and the dead glass held flat under her arm. "Mister Gage will wait with me. Neither of us will speak. You may ignore us." A pause. "Everybody does."
And so they waited.
It was the strangest hour Corin could remember. Which was not saying much. But it was strange. The Auditor of the Fourth Desk stood by the shelves of jars like a statue in dark green, with her bare hands folded and her face turned toward the wall, and did not move, and did not speak. The big man stood by the door like a wardrobe. Toby sat at his neat desk with his hands in his lap and looked at the empty place where his two silvers had been, and every now and then he took off his spectacles and cleaned them and put them back on. And Corin sat behind his wall of a desk with his gloved hands flat on the wood and looked at nothing.
Once, about halfway through, he looked up and found that she was looking at him.
Not at his hands. Not at his coat. At his face. From across the room. With her head a little on one side, the way you look at a word you have seen before somewhere and cannot remember where.
She looked away at once.
He did too.
At the noon bell, the pipe went whoosh.
And thump.
And a brass cylinder dropped out of the hatch onto Toby's blotter. And then, a moment later, another one. Two. One for the Inspector. One for the clerk. Toby opened his own first, with fingers that fumbled, and tipped it, and three silvers and some coppers slid out onto the blotter, and he looked at them as if he had never seen money before. Then he handed the other cylinder to Corin without a word.
Corin opened it.
Nine silver. And a slip of gray paper with his name on it, and SENIOR INVESTIGATOR, HARVESTING SECTION, and WEEK ENDING, and a date.
He counted out five silver and three coppers onto the desk. His hand was quite steady. It surprised him how steady it was.
That left three silver and seven coppers.
RENT: 4 SILVER A WEEK. DUE FRIDAY.
Not enough for Mrs. Marta. Nothing for Bram. Just enough, if he was careful, to eat.
He pushed the five silver and three coppers across the desk.
Elara came back from the shelves. She looked down at the little pile of coins. She did not count them. She did not need to. She swept them up in one bare hand and dropped them into a small leather purse that Mister Gage held out to her, and the purse vanished into the big man's coat.
Then she opened the green book at Pell Dorran's page one last time. Under the balance, under the crossed-out line, under received from T., she wrote, slowly, in her small upright perfect hand:
Received, from C. Vane, against the account: five silver, three coppers.
Balance: nil.
Account closed. No claim passes.
And under it, a seal. Not wax. A small round brass stamp that Mister Gage put into her hand, and she pressed it into an inkpad, and pressed it onto the page, firmly, once. A pair of scales. A coin. An eye. And around the edge, in tiny letters, two words.
PAID IN FULL.
Paid in Full
She tore the page out of the little green book.
Corin had not thought that anybody from the Bank would ever tear a page out of anything. She did it very neatly, along the fold, with one bare hand holding the book flat and the other drawing the page away, slowly, so that the edge came clean. Then she folded it in half, and in half again, and held it out across the desk.
"The receipt," she said. "The account is in the Bank's books now. This is the copy for the estate."
He took it.
On the outside, in her small upright perfect hand, she had written: DORRAN, PELL. Closed. Paid in full. No claim. And the brass stamp, the scales and the coin and the open eye.
"Give it to the next of kin," said Elara. "If you ever find her. If anyone from the Bank ever comes to her door, she shows them that, and they go away." A pause. "They will go away. The Bank does not argue with its own stamp."
Corin looked at the folded paper in his gloved hand. Then he held it out to Toby.
Toby took it as if it were made of glass. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he unbuttoned the inside pocket on the left of his coat, where the clink was, and put the folded receipt in beside the red sock, and buttoned the pocket again, and laid his hand flat over it.
He did not say anything. He did not need to.
Elara had picked up her gloves. She put them on the way she had taken them off. One finger at a time. The left first. Little finger, ring finger, middle, first, thumb. The tiny pearl button at the wrist. Then the right. When she had done, her hands were white again, and smooth, and you could not see the silver-shiny tips at all, and she looked exactly the way she had when she walked in. Straight and narrow and cold and perfect, in dark green and gold.
Except that she did not look at him.
She picked up the sheet of glass in its silver frame. She looked down at it for a moment, the way you look at a window you have just seen something through. Then she held it out to her side without turning, and Mister Gage was there, and took it, and laid it in its velvet hollow in the brass-bound case, and closed the lid.
She picked up the little green book and the silver pen, and he took those too.
She did not pick up the empty jar.
It stood on the desk where Mister Gage had put it, upright, with its label facing Corin. DORRAN, P. Last minutes. Stable. Do not open. He hummed.
"You're leaving that," said Corin.
"The Bank has no use for an empty jar." She was looking at the door. "It is yours, Harvester. Your label. Your hand."
He looked at it. He hummed.
"Thank you," he said.
She did turn her head then. Just a little. Just enough to look at him sideways, from under her straight dark brows, with an expression he could not read at all.
"Do not thank the Bank," she said. "The Bank closed an account. That is all. It closes ten thousand a year." A pause. "The Bank closed the account of Pell Dorran. The Archon has not closed anything. I would not forget that, if I were a man who could remember things."
She went to the door.
Toby went with her, to draw the bolts. One. Two. Three. She waited while he did it, very straight, with her white-gloved hands folded in front of her and her face toward the iron. Mister Gage stood behind her with the brass-bound case, like a wall.
The door swung open on the cold dark of the stair.
She did not go through it.
She stood on the threshold with her back to the room. And Corin saw her tongue touch her front tooth again. The gold one. Once. Quickly. He could not see it, from behind. But he saw her jaw move, the small movement of it, and he knew. He did not know how he knew.
"Harvester," she said, without turning.
"Yes."
"The song you hum."
The room went very still.
"Three notes," said Elara. "Up. And up. And up." Her voice was low and even, but the evenness was the kind that is held there by force, like a door held shut against a wind. "You hummed it the whole time I was reading you. Under everything. I could hear it in the glass. In you."
He did not say anything.
"It's wrong," she said.
"Wrong?"
"It isn't finished."
She stood there for a moment longer, very straight, on the threshold of the cold stair, with her white-gloved hands folded and her short dark hair cut level with her jaw. He waited for her to sing the fourth note. The one that came down. The one Wren Halloway had sung him yesterday, in this same room, and made his chest hurt.
She did not.
"I don't know how I know that," said Elara quietly.
And she went out, and up the stair, with the big man behind her, and her footsteps went up and up, quick and precise and even, and did not stop, and did not slow, until they were gone.
Toby bolted the door. All three bolts.
Then he turned round and leaned against it, the way he always did now, and looked at Corin across the long cold room.
Corin had not moved from the big chair. He was sitting with his gloved hands flat on the desk on either side of the empty jar, and his eyes on the label. He hummed.
"Inspector," said Toby.
Corin did not answer.
"Inspector. Are you all right?"
"I don't know."
Toby came away from the door. He came and stood by the corner of the big desk, where he had sat to be read, and looked down at Corin. His face was very pale, and his eyes behind the thick lenses were red, and his tuft of hair was standing straight up. But his hand was flat over the pocket of his coat, over the sock and the receipt, and it was quite steady.
"You paid it," he said. "Pell's debt. Five silver and three."
"We paid it."
"You paid five. I paid two." Toby swallowed. "You don't have any money now. You owe your landlady. I know you do. She'll put it on your door. She'll put you in the street."
"Maybe."
"Why?"
Corin looked up at him.
"Because he hummed," he said. It was the only answer he had. "In the dark. With his last minutes. So that she couldn't take it. And I've got it in my throat now, and it won't go away, and I can't give it back to him." He looked at the jar. "So I gave his sister this instead."
Toby looked at him for a long time.
Then he pulled the chair Elara had sat in round to the end of the desk, close, and sat down on it. Not on the front edge, the way she had. All the way back, slumped, with his long legs stuck out in front of him and his hands in his lap, like someone who has been holding himself up for a very long time and has just let go.
"Inspector," he said, to the ceiling. "Can I say something? Not as a clerk."
"Yes."
"When she was reading you. When the glass went white. I couldn't look at the glass. It was too bright. So I looked at you." He was quiet for a moment. "And then I looked at her."
"And?"
"You had the same face," said Toby.
Corin did not move.
"Not the same face," said Toby. "I don't mean that. I mean the same… look. On your faces. At the same time. When you made that sound." He pushed his spectacles up his nose with one inky finger. He was still looking at the ceiling. "Like when you've been looking for something in a drawer for a long time. And you put your hand on it. And you don't know yet what it is. But you know it's the thing."
The lamp hummed. Water dripped somewhere in the walls.
Corin took out the Ledger.
His hand was steady now. It surprised him, again, how steady it was. He opened the book at the back, at the place where yesterday stopped, and wrote. For a long time. All of it. The glass. The spiral, turning the wrong way. I only ever knew one other harvester who cut his spiral to the left. Toby, on the corner of the desk. A sock. Red. The debt. Clause four. Nan. It does not say whose money adds it up. His hand on the glass. White. Fifteen people who were not him. Something older than the Bank. Watching me. Withdrawn. Everything. Twenty years. Sealed. The Archon's own seal. The little black book. Lost: a day from before. I do not know which. The pledge. Toby's two silver and three. PAID IN FULL. The receipt in Toby's pocket. The Archon has not closed anything.
And then he stopped.
He sat with the pencil over the page for a long time.
Then he wrote the rest. Slower. Smaller. As if each word cost him something.
When she read me, I saw a pool. Flat gray stones round the edge. Clear water. Dragonflies. A smell of mint. A small hot hand holding mine. A girl. Two short dark braids. A gap in her front teeth. She said: "Cor, look, it's so pretty."
It isn't my memory. I don't have any. It came through her hand.
Her front tooth is gold. Over a gap. "As long as I can remember. It never grew in."
She says the song isn't finished. She doesn't know how she knows.
She has nothing before the Bank. She lost a day from before. She doesn't know which.
He stopped again.
Then he turned, slowly, to the very front of the book. To the inside of the cover. To the big black letters, pressed so deep they had cut into the paper.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
He looked at it for a very long time.
Then he turned back to the last page, to the end of what he had written, and under it, very small, so small that only he would ever be able to read it, he wrote one more line.
He did not write a question mark at the end of it. He tried. His hand would not do it.
It's a common name.
