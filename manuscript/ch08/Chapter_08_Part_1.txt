Chapter 8: The Auditor, Part 1 of 5: Friday
Debt of the Unremembered. Arc 1: The Siphon's Ledger.
Friday
There was a note pinned to the board above his bed that had not been there the morning before. It was the first thing he saw when he sat up, after the fish-shaped stain and the crack in the ceiling and the book on his chest. It was written in his own big untidy hand, so big and so hard that the pen had torn the paper in two places, and it had been pinned at the very top of the board, above BOOTS FIRST, above everything, so that nobody could miss it.
GLOVES. ALL DAY. DO NOT LET THE AUDITOR TOUCH YOU.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
He did not know what an Auditor was. He did not know why one would want to touch him. But the paper had been torn by a hand that was frightened, and he knew it was his hand, and so he believed it.
He read the Ledger. All of it. The first page and the second, the lines in other people's writing, the long crowded pages that came after. It took a long time. The gray light in the window became a little less gray. When he came to the last pages, the ones from yesterday, he slowed down, and read them twice, and then a third time, and by the end of the third time his hands were cold.
Carriage 9. Harvester L. Hale. One guest, see sealed note 7. To Lower Levels.
A FRIEND OF THE MINISTRY. FROM A FRIEND. THE SAME.
Master Hale sends his regards.
Tomorrow's Auditor reads people by touch. DO NOT LET HER TOUCH YOU.
And at the very bottom of the last written page, in a hand that had been shaking:
Auditor. Fourth Desk. Signed: Elara.
He turned to the front. To the inside of the cover. You had a younger sister named Elara.
The ache came. Just under his ribs. The thumb, pressing.
He sat there for a while with the book open on his knees, looking from the one Elara to the other. Then he did what the first page told him. He turned the page. He got up. He put on his boots, and then his coat. And then, because the note on the board said so, he looked for gloves.
They were in the left coat pocket, with the keys and a small empty jar and a heavy little knife with a bone handle. Gray leather. Old and soft and worn shiny at the fingertips. He pulled them on. They fit as if they had been made for his hands, and they probably had, a long time ago.
Outside his door there was a bowl, and a plate on top of the bowl, and steam. And beside it, propped against the wall, a small folded square of paper with a name on the outside in a neat, clear, slanting hand that leaned like grass in a steady wind.
C.
He ate the stew first. The first page said eat something. Then he picked up the paper and unfolded it, crouching in the doorway in the gray light from the stairwell, with the long cold rod pressing against his side.
C.
Your girl brought your note at the first bell. She would not leave until I had read it all twice and told her I had understood it. Then she ate two of Oren's apples and went over the roof.
I have read it many times since. I will not pretend I am not frightened. Oren has not sat down all evening. He is standing at the top of the stairs as I write this, with his arms folded, and I do not think he will sit down tonight at all.
You asked about C.
There is only one C in my family that I know of. My father's sister. My aunt Cressida. I do not know her. I have not seen her since I was very small, perhaps five, at a Midwinter dinner, where I remember only a great deal of white silk and a smell I did not like. She and my father quarreled before I was born. Nobody would ever tell me why. When the house fell, she did not write. When my father burned, she did not come. I have been told she sits on the Council now, but I have not seen her name in the broadsheets, and I have not looked.
I do not know her hand. I do not know if her C has a hook.
I am going to find out.
Corin read that line twice. The pen had pressed harder on it than on the others.
One more thing. And then I will stop, because Juno says you must sleep, and I think she is right, and I also think she will throw a slate at my window if I do not let her go.
This evening, after you left, I sat with Father as I always do. And when he began to tap, I tapped back. Four. Only four. Slowly. The way you did.
He stopped. He looked at the wall. And then, Corin, he tapped again. Not the whole number. Only one part of it. Four. Back to me. As if he were answering.
And then he smiled at me. Not the new-girl smile. A different one.
I have not cried. I want you to know that. Oren will tell you I have not cried.
Rule five. The same day. Even if it's good.
L.
Corin read it all again. Then he folded it up very carefully along its creases and put it inside the back cover of the Ledger, beside a cream envelope that smelled of flowers and a gray slip with a red stamp. And he took out the stub of pencil from the spine and wrote, under the last line of yesterday:
Friday. Lyra's answer. The only C she knows: her father's sister, Aunt Cressida. On the Council. Quarreled with Lord Thorne before Lyra was born. White silk. A smell she did not like. Lyra does not know her hand. She is going to find out.
Lord Thorne tapped back. Four. Then smiled. "A different one."
He sat looking at the last line for a long moment. He did not know Lord Thorne. He did not know Lyra, except as a careful slanting hand that told him he had chosen her. But the line made something in his chest feel warm and loose, like a knot coming undone.
Then he went downstairs.
He said good morning to an old man in a brown suit by a round dirty window, who said good morning back, very politely, with his thumb moving back and forth across the side of his first finger. He stepped round a little girl with a cat on the third-floor landing, who told him he looked like a ghost. And at the bottom of the stairs, in the onion-smelling passage, a purple door opened before he could knock, and a very small, very old woman with a face like a dried apple stood in it with a wooden spoon in her hand.
"It's Friday," she said.
He looked at the Ledger. He did not need to. It was on his door. RENT: 4 SILVER A WEEK. YOU OWE 4 SILVER. DUE FRIDAY.
"It's Friday," he agreed.
"And you get paid on a Friday. At the Ministry. At the noon bell. In silver."
"Yes."
"Then I'll expect you tonight," said Mrs. Marta, "with four of them in your hand, and no stories." Her small black eyes went over him, slowly, from his boots to his face. They stopped at his hands. At the gray gloves. She looked at them for a moment, and her mouth went thin, and he thought she was going to say something. But she only said: "You look peaky. Eat your dinner. And stay off my roof. My Juno says you were up there twice this week, crawling about like a cat with three legs."
"Twice," said Corin. "Yes."
"Once is foolish. Twice is a habit." She pointed the spoon at him. "Don't make it three."
And she shut the door.
Out in Tallow Street, the lamps were still burning cold and blue, and a girl sat on a gutter four stories up eating a heel of bread. She told him who she was, once, and he read it in the book, and she took him up the hill the quick way, by the alleys and the yards and a plank over some geese, to a little iron gate in a great black wall. An old man with one arm let him in and did not say anything, but watched him go, the way you watch a man walk out onto a frozen pond.
At the boiler-room step, Corin stopped. He looked down at the gray leather on his hands. At the soft worn fingertips. He flexed them, once.
Then he went on, up the stone steps, along the dim corridor, down the cold spiral, to the black iron door.
Toby opened it before he knocked.
He did not say oh, thank the saints. He did not ask if it was him. He just looked at Corin, and then at the gloves on his hands, and nodded once, very small, as if something he had been worrying about all night had turned out to be all right after all.
"You've got them on," he said.
"I wrote it on my wall."
"I know." Toby stood aside to let him in. His face was gray with tiredness, and there were dark marks under his eyes behind the thick lenses, and his tuft of hair was standing straight up as if he had been pulling it. He had on a clean collar. He had polished his boots. He had swept the floor. Every jar on every shelf had been turned so that its label faced the room. "I wrote it on mine too," he said. "On the inside of my front door. In ink. My landlady will be furious."
The clock on the wall said a quarter past the eighth bell.
They had three quarters of an hour.
The Ninth Bell
They spent the three quarters of an hour moving furniture.
It was Toby's idea. He had thought about it all night, he said, lying in bed with his eyes open, and he had decided that if an Auditor of the Bank was going to come into the Harvesting Section and try to touch his Inspector, she was going to have to get past a desk to do it. A big one. So they dragged the big desk, Corin's desk, the scarred old one with the landslide on top, out from the far end of the room where it had always stood and turned it sideways across the middle of the floor, like a wall. It took both of them. It weighed as much as a cart. Papers slid off it as they pushed, and an old teacup, and a half-eaten apple that had gone brown and soft, and a jar with a little mist in it that Toby caught just before it hit the floor and held against his chest with his eyes shut for a long moment before he put it carefully back on its shelf.
When they were done, the desk stood across the room from wall to wall, almost, with a gap at one end just wide enough for a person to squeeze past. Corin's creaking chair was behind it. In front of it, on the door side, Toby had put the other chair, the one Lucian had sat on, very straight, facing the desk.
"She sits there," said Toby. "You sit here. The desk's between. She'd have to climb over it to get to you." He looked at it doubtfully. "Do you think she would? Climb over it?"
"I don't know."
"I don't think she would. I don't think Auditors climb over things." Toby pushed his spectacles up his nose with one inky finger. "I think they just ask."
Then he went and sat at his own small neat desk, by the shelves, behind the end of the big one. He squared every paper on it. He lined up every pen. He moved the dying plant from the left corner to the right corner and then back again. He took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his sleeve and put them back on, and took them off again.
Corin sat in the creaking chair behind the wall of the desk with his gloved hands flat on the scarred wood, and the Ledger inside his coat over his heart, and the rod cold on his right, and the sewn black square warm in his inside pocket, and waited.
The little clock on the wall moved toward the ninth bell.
He did not know what she would look like. He had nothing in the Ledger about her face. Only a name, signed in a small, perfect, upright hand at the bottom of a cream-colored letter. And a line Wren Halloway had said to him in this room yesterday, copied down while his hand was still shaking: They say she can read a man's whole life in the time it takes to say good morning. And they say she has never once forgotten anything she read.
And a line on the inside of his front cover, in his own hand.
He did not look at that one. He did not need to.
Far above, through the rock, a bell began to ring.
One. Two. Three.
Toby stopped polishing his spectacles.
Six. Seven. Eight.
On the ninth stroke, exactly, as if whoever was outside had been standing there with their hand raised, waiting for it, there was a knock on the iron door.
Not a fist. Not knuckles. Something hard and small and metal. Brass, perhaps. Tock. Tock. Tock. Three times. Evenly spaced, like the ticking of a clock.
Toby looked at Corin. Corin nodded.
Toby went to the door. He did not ask who it was. They both knew. He drew the three bolts, one, two, three, and pulled the door open, and stepped back against the wall beside it, very fast, as if the door had been hot.
A man came in first.
He was enormous. Not like Oren, who was big the way an old oak is big, knotted and scarred. This man was big the way a wardrobe is big. Square. Smooth. Very clean. He had no hair at all, not on his head, not on his face, not even eyebrows, so that his face looked unfinished, like a face drawn by someone who had stopped before they got to the details. He wore a long coat of very dark green, so dark it was almost black, with gold piping down the front and round the cuffs, and on the left breast a little badge embroidered in gold thread. A pair of scales. On one side of the scales, a coin. On the other, a tiny eye. Open.
He was carrying a case. Brass-bound, leather, the size of a small trunk, and he held it in front of him in both hands as if it weighed nothing at all. He did not look at Toby. He did not look at Corin. He did not look at the jars. He walked to the chair in front of the big desk, the one Toby had put there, and looked at it. Then he put the case down on the floor beside it, very gently, without a sound. Then he turned, and went back to the door, and stood beside it, opposite Toby, with his huge hands folded in front of him and his unfinished face looking at nothing.
And then she came in.
She was not what Corin had thought. He did not know what he had thought. Something cold. Something old. Something that went with a letter on cream paper in a hand that had never made a mistake.
She was young.
That was the first thing. Younger than him, he thought. Perhaps twenty-four. Perhaps less. She was slim and quite tall, almost as tall as he was, and she stood very straight, the way people stand who have been told to stand straight all their lives and have long ago stopped needing to be told. She wore the same dark green as the big man, the green that was almost black, with the same gold piping. But hers was not a coat. It was a long fitted jacket, buttoned high to the throat with small gold buttons, over a long dark skirt, and the gold scales on her breast were bigger than his, and finer, worked in gold thread so fine and so close that they seemed to catch the light from the one gemstone lamp and hold it. She wore white gloves. Very white. Very thin. Buttoned at the wrist with tiny pearl buttons.
And under one arm, held flat against her side, she carried a sheet of glass.
It was in a frame. A thin frame of silver, plain, without any pattern, and the glass inside it was perhaps the size of a large book, and so clear that at first Corin thought it was not there at all, that she was carrying an empty frame. Then the lamp caught it, and he saw the glass, and he saw something else. Inside the glass, very fine, so fine that you could only see them when the light fell at the right angle, there were lines. Thousands of them. Silver. Like the veins in a leaf. Like frost on a window.
He did not know why his breath stopped when he saw it.
He did not know why his right hand, flat on the desk in its gray glove, curled up all by itself into a fist.
She stopped just inside the door and looked round the room.
She did it the way he had seen himself do it, in a room he did not know. Quickly. Thoroughly. One thing at a time. The shelves. The jars, every row, with their labels turned to face the room. The small neat desk with its squared papers and its lined-up pens and its dying plant. Toby, pressed against the wall beside the door. The big scarred desk turned sideways across the floor like a barricade, with its landslide of papers. The chair in front of it.
And Corin, behind it.
Her eyes came to him last. And stopped.
They were dark. Very dark. Almost black, in that light. Careful eyes. Eyes that looked at a thing as if they were trying to read something written on it very small. Her face was narrow and pale, with a straight nose and a firm, rather stubborn chin, and dark straight eyebrows. Her hair was dark too. Almost black. Cut short, very short, level with her jaw, as straight and even as if it had been cut along a ruler. Not a single hair out of place.
She looked at him for a long moment without any expression at all.
Then she looked at the desk between them. At the way it had been dragged across the room. At the scrape marks on the stone floor where its legs had gone. At the chair placed in front of it, alone, for her.
And the corner of her mouth moved.
Not a smile. Something drier than that. Something that knew exactly why the desk was there, and was not going to say so, and found it a little bit funny.
"Harvester Vane," she said.
Her voice was low and clear and very even, every word set down exactly in its place, with nothing extra. When she spoke, her lips parted a little over her teeth, and Corin saw, just for a moment, a flash of something bright in the front of her mouth. Gold. One of her front teeth, the left one, was capped in gold. It caught the lamplight, a small hard yellow gleam, and was gone again as her mouth closed.
"Auditor," said Corin.
He did not stand up. He did not know if he was supposed to. He did not think he could have stood up if he tried. His legs had gone strange and heavy, and the ache under his ribs, the thumb pressing, had come back, and was pressing harder than it had all week.
She did not seem to mind that he did not stand.
She walked to the chair in front of the desk. She did not sit in it. She stood beside it, with one white-gloved hand resting on its back and the sheet of glass held flat under her other arm, and looked at him across the scarred wood.
"My name is Elara," she said. "Auditor, Fourth Desk, the Oblivion Bank. This is Mister Gage." She did not look round at the big man by the door. "He carries things. He does not speak. You may ignore him. Everybody does." A pause. "I am here to close the account of Pell Dorran, deceased, scribe of this Ministry. And to examine the Harvester who was last in contact with his residue."
She looked down at his hands on the desk. At the gray leather.
"My letter," she said, "asked that you be ungloved."
"I read it."
"And yet."
"And yet," Corin agreed.
She looked at the gloves for a moment longer. Then up at his face. And the dry thing moved at the corner of her mouth again, very slightly, and the gold tooth gleamed and was gone.
"Someone has been talking to you," said Elara.
He did not answer.
She did not seem to expect him to. She drew the chair out from the desk, exactly one hand's width, and sat down in it, very straight, on the front edge, with her knees together. She laid the sheet of glass on the desk in front of her, flat, on top of the landslide of papers, and squared it with the edge of the wood, carefully, with both hands, the way Toby squared his papers. Then she folded her white-gloved hands on top of it.
"Then we will begin," she said, "with the dead. They are easier. They do not wear gloves."
