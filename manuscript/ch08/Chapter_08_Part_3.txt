Chapter 8: The Auditor, Part 3 of 5: Corin's Hand
Corin's Hand
"It's his sister's," said Corin.
He said it before he could stop himself. Then he did not want to stop himself. The words had been sitting in his throat since the moment she said sock, and they came out hard and rough and much too loud for the small cold room.
"It's a twelve-year-old girl's," he said. "Every copper. He sold his own birthday for it. He sold his gran's kitchen. He ate bread and dripping on a step for two years and kept it in his boot so the rats wouldn't get it. It was going to bring her to the city. So she could be a scribe." He was standing up. He did not remember standing up. His gloved hands were flat on the scarred wood, and he was leaning on them, across the desk, toward her. "It isn't the Bank's. It isn't anybody's. It's hers."
Elara looked up at him from her chair.
She did not lean back. She did not flinch. She sat on the front edge of her chair with her bare hands folded on the dead glass and looked up at him with her dark careful eyes, and her face was perfectly calm. But the gold tooth was not showing. Her mouth had closed into a straight line.
"The Bank does not buy memories, Harvester," she said. "Nobody has ever told you that, I think. It is the most common mistake in the city. People think they are selling. They are not. The Bank lends. Against the memory. It holds the memory, and it pays coin, and the coin is a loan." Her voice was very even. "While the depositor lives, the loan sleeps. When the depositor dies, the loan wakes. And it is called in. From the estate. Clause four."
"There is no estate. He had a sock."
"Then the sock is the estate." She did not look at Toby. "And if the sock is not enough, or if it is hidden, or if it goes missing, then the loan passes to the next of kin. Clause four, part two."
The room went very still.
Toby, on the corner of the desk, had stopped breathing again.
"The next of kin," said Corin.
"A girl called Nan," said Elara. "Twelve. Who lives with her grandmother, in a village with a tree hung with carved birds, and practices her letters, and ate a whole pie by herself and did not get caught." She said it in exactly the same voice as everything else. A column of figures. "There is no address on the letter. There is no address in his staff file, I imagine, or you would not still have the sock. But the Bank has its own ways of finding next of kin, Harvester. It is very good at it. It has had a long time to practice."
Corin looked at her.
And she looked back.
And for a moment, a very long moment, neither of them moved, across the scarred desk, across the landslide of papers and the dead glass and the empty jar with He hummed on its label in his own big untidy hand.
He thought of a red sock. Of a girl he had never met, at a window in a village that was not on any map, waiting for a letter that would never come. Of a man in a black coach who would come instead, with a paper in triplicate, and a pair of scales embroidered in gold on his breast.
Then he thought of Toby, sitting on the corner of the desk, shaking, with his hand pressed over his pocket.
And he pulled off his right glove.
He did it fast. Not one finger at a time, the way she had. All at once, with his teeth, the way you pull off a glove in the cold when you need your hand in a hurry. The gray leather came away and dropped on the desk. His hand was bare. Broad, with long fingers, and a hard little lump on the side of the second finger where a pen rested, and scars across the knuckles he did not remember getting.
He held it out across the desk. Palm up. Over the glass.
"Read me," he said. "Not him. Me. Whatever you need for your account, take it from me." His voice was not steady anymore. He did not care. "And leave the girl alone."
Toby made a sound like a cry. "Inspector—"
Corin did not look at him.
Elara was looking at his hand.
She looked at it for a long time. At the scars on the knuckles. At the lump on the finger. At the palm, lined and rough and open. And something happened to her face again. The thing from before. The fish turning, once, in deep water. And this time it did not go away at once. It stayed. For one breath. Two.
She did not want to touch it. He saw that. He did not know how he saw it, but he did. Her own right hand, lying on the dead glass, had curled up a little, the fingers drawing in toward the palm, the way a hand draws back from a stove.
Then her face closed again.
"Very well," she said. "Put your hand on the glass."
He did.
It was cold. Colder than it looked. Smooth and hard and very slightly damp, like a window on a winter morning, and under his palm he could feel the fine silver lines inside it, thousands of them, the tiniest ridges, like the grain of a leaf.
It doesn't hurt.
The woman's voice again. Very calm. Very far. You put your hand on it, like on a cold window in the winter.
He shut his eyes.
And Elara laid her bare hand on top of his.
The first thing he felt was how cold her fingers were. The silver-shiny tips of them, resting on his knuckles. Cold as the glass. Colder.
The second thing he felt was the glass waking up.
It did not wake the way it had for Toby. Not a soft glow spreading a few inches, like moonlight on frost. It woke all at once. He felt it under his palm before he saw it. A jolt. A rush. As if he had put his hand flat on the surface of a river and the river had suddenly started to run. Cold, and fast, and enormous, pouring out from under his hand in every direction at once.
He opened his eyes.
The glass was blazing.
Not blue. White. The whole sheet of it, edge to edge, every one of the thousands of silver lines inside it flooded with light so bright that he could not look at it straight. It lit the whole long low room from below, like a frozen lake with the sun under it. It threw his shadow and hers up onto the curved stone ceiling, huge, two dark shapes leaning toward each other over a square of fire. It turned every jar on every shelf into a little white lamp. It hummed. Not a thin high note at the edge of hearing. A great high singing note, like a finger run round the rim of a wineglass, so loud that he felt it in his teeth and his bones and the soles of his feet, so loud that the jars rattled on their shelves and Toby, somewhere far away, cried out.
And Elara was reading.
He could feel her. That was the strangest part. He could feel her reading, the way you can feel someone's eyes on the back of your neck. A cold quick touch, going through him. Not through his body. Through him. Down. Like a hand running down the pages of a ledger, fast, the way only a clerk's finger can go down a column.
"Crowded," she said.
Her voice was strange. Far off. Breathless.
"You are… crowded, Harvester. There are… there are people in you. Fourteen. Fifteen. Not you. Not yours. A boxer. Big. Angry. Red at the edges, like a wound that's half healed." Her breath caught. "A woman who cries about a dog. A dog called… Biscuit. A sailor, a boy, no, a hole where a boy was, very fresh, very cold. A clerk who hummed. Three notes. Over and over." Her fingers tightened on his. "And something else. Something under them all. Old. Older than the Bank. Cold as… cold as a cellar. Watching me."
In his coat, against his side, the rod was ice. Malakor did not speak. But Corin felt him there, very still, very close, the way you feel someone standing in a dark room with you, holding his breath.
"Your own balance," said Elara. Faster now. Her voice had gone thin and high and tight. "Your own deposits. None. Nothing. You have never sold anything. Not once. Not a copper." A pause. "Your withdrawals."
She stopped.
The white light pulsed under their hands. Once. Twice. Like a heart.
"Everything," she whispered. "Withdrawn. Everything. Not sold. Not spent. Not burned. Taken. All at once. A long time ago. Twenty years, near enough." Her fingers were digging into the back of his hand now, hard, the cold silver tips pressing into his knuckles. "Someone withdrew you, Harvester. Your whole account. And then…"
She stopped again.
And Corin, with his eyes open on the blazing white glass, saw it.
Not in the glass. In him. Behind his eyes. All at once, as if a door had blown open in a wind.
A pool.
Wide and shallow and round, with flat gray stones round its edge, and water so clear you could see every pebble on the bottom. Dragonflies hanging over it, blue and green, like little bits of colored glass. Sunlight. A smell of wet grass and mint.
And a hand in his. Small. Very small. Hot and sticky and fierce. Holding on.
And a voice. A child's voice, high and bright and delighted, right beside him, laughing.
Cor! Cor, look, it's so pretty!
And a face. Turned up to his. Two short untidy dark braids sticking out from the sides of its head like the handles of a jug. A wide grin. And in the middle of the grin, at the front, a gap. A small dark gap where a tooth should have been.
Corin made a sound.
He did not know what sound. It came up out of the bottom of his chest, from under the ache, from under everything, and it was not a word, and it hurt coming out.
And across the blazing glass, Elara's whole body jerked.
She tore her hand away from his.
She did it so hard and so fast that the silver frame of the glass skidded on the desk and knocked the empty jar over and sent it rolling. Mister Gage was across the room before it reached the edge. He caught it. He did not seem to have moved. He was simply there, with the jar in his huge hand, and his unfinished face turned toward the Auditor.
The white light went out.
The room was dark. Darker than it had ever been. The one gemstone lamp overhead seemed to have shrunk to a dim blue bead. Corin's eyes were full of swimming spots of white, and he could not see. He could only hear. The jars, still rattling faintly on their shelves, settling, one by one. Toby, breathing in short sobbing gasps somewhere to his left. And close in front of him, across the desk, a sound he did not understand at first.
Then he did.
It was Elara, breathing.
Fast. Shallow. Uneven. Like someone who has been held underwater.
His eyes cleared. Slowly. The spots faded. The room came back, gray and dim and ordinary, the shelves, the jars, the curved stone ceiling.
She was standing.
She had pushed her chair back so hard that it had gone over, and it lay on its side on the stone floor behind her. She was standing with her back almost against the shelves, with her right hand held against her chest, pressed flat over the gold scales on her jacket, as if it hurt. Her face was white as paper. Her dark eyes were very wide and fixed on Corin, and there was something in them he had not seen there before. Not coldness. Not care.
Fear.
And under the fear, very deep, something else. Something that did not know what it was. Something lost, and looking.
"Stop it," she said.
Her voice shook.
"Stop what?"
"Humming."
Corin had not known he was. He stopped. He had been humming the three notes. Up. And up. And up. Through his nose, softly, over and over, the way he always did, without knowing.
Elara stood against the shelves with her hand pressed to her chest and stared at him, breathing hard. She did not say anything else for a long time.
Then, slowly, she took her hand away from her chest. She looked at it. At her own palm, and the silver-shiny tips of her fingers. As if she expected to see something there. A mark. A burn.
There was nothing.
She closed her hand.
"Your account," she said. Her voice was steadier. Not steady. Steadier. "Is sealed."
Corin did not understand.
"At the bottom," said Elara. "Under everything. Under the people who aren't you, and the withdrawals, and the twenty years. There is a seal. On the account itself. On you." She swallowed. "An open book. A closed eye. And a crown." A pause. "The Archon's own seal. Not the Ministry's. His. Personal."
The room was very quiet.
"I have read four thousand, one hundred and twelve people," said Elara. "Living and dead. I have seen accounts that were empty, and accounts that were overdrawn, and accounts that were forged. I have seen the Archon's seal on dead men's accounts, many times, when an estate is taken for the State." She was looking at him as if he were a column of figures that would not add up, no matter how many times she ran her finger down it. "I have never once seen it on a living man."
She bent and picked up her chair, and set it upright, and squared it with the edge of the desk. She did not sit in it.
"Who are you, Harvester Vane?" she said.
Corin did not answer.
He did not know.
The Price of a Day
Nobody spoke for a long time.
Mister Gage set the empty jar down on the desk, upright, with its label facing Corin, exactly where it had been before. Then he went back to the door, and stood beside it with his huge hands folded, and his unfinished face turned toward his Auditor, and did not move again.
Toby had slid off the corner of the desk. He was standing by his own small neat desk with both hands pressed flat on its top, holding himself up. His face was wet. He did not seem to know.
Corin sat down. Slowly. In the creaking chair behind the wall of the desk. His right hand was still bare. He looked down at it, lying on the scarred wood, and it did not look like his hand. It looked like a stranger's hand that someone had left there. The palm was tingling. Not cold anymore. Warm. As if it had been held, for a long time, by something warm.
He pulled the gray glove back on. His fingers did not want to go into it. He made them.
Elara had not sat down either. She was standing beside her chair, with one hand on its back, and she had taken out the very small book again. The black one, worn soft and shiny at the corners, with the gold clasp. She was holding it in both hands, closed, against the gold scales on her breast. As if it were a thing she held on to when the floor moved.
"What is that?" said Corin.
He did not expect her to answer. She looked at him as if she had forgotten he could speak.
Then, slowly, she opened the clasp.
"My account," she said.
She did not hold it out. But she did not hide it either. She opened it in her two hands, at a page near the back, and looked down at it, and he could see, across the desk, that the pages were covered in writing. Tiny writing. Small and upright and perfect, the same hand as the letter on cream paper. Line after line after line, close together, from the top of every page to the bottom, so small and so close that from where he sat the pages looked gray.
"Every reading costs," said Elara. Her voice was low and even again. She was reading from the little book, he thought. Or saying something she had said so many times that it had worn a groove in her. "Every account I open. Every hand on the glass. It costs one day." A pause. "Of mine."
Toby made a small sound.
"One whole day," said Elara. "From my own past. It goes. Not the reading. The reading I keep. I never forget a reading. That is what makes me useful." She did not look up. "But for every account I read, one day of my own life goes out of me, like a coin out of a purse. A day I lived. A real one. Gone." She turned a page. "I don't get to choose which day. It just goes. So I write them down. Here. What I read, and what it cost."
She held the book a little toward the lamp, and read, very quietly, from the top of the page.
"Monday. Read: a cooper on Rope Street, for the Lower Docks branch. Lost: a Tuesday in the rain, when I was eleven. I think I had a cold."
She turned the page.
"Wednesday. Read: a widow, for her husband's estate. Lost: a morning in the Bank school garden. There were bees. I think there were bees."
Another.
"Yesterday. Read: the Dorran jar, under the east tower. Lost: the day I was given my glass. I was fourteen. I remember that I remember being proud."
She stopped. She looked at the next line. The last line on the page. The one she had written, small and fast, with a hand that was not quite steady, standing by the corner of the desk while Toby sat shaking with his hand on his pocket.
"Today. Read: a junior clerk. Lost: an afternoon at the Bank's seaside house. Age nine. Sand."
She was quiet for a long moment.
Then she took the silver pen from the desk, and dipped it, and held it over the next empty line. And did not write.
Corin watched the pen. It did not move. It hung over the page, the gold nib catching the lamplight, a drop of ink gathering slowly at its point.
"What did you lose?" he said. "Reading me."
Elara did not answer.
The drop of ink fell. It made a small round black spot on the empty line. She looked at it. She did not blot it.
"I don't know," she said.
Her voice was perfectly flat. It was the flatness of something that has been pressed down very hard to keep it from moving.
"I always know," she said. "Every time. Four thousand, one hundred and twelve times. I feel it go. A day. I feel the shape of it, going, the way you feel a tooth come out. And then I look for the hole, and I find it, and I know which day it was. A Tuesday. A morning with bees. A day at the sea." She was looking at the black spot of ink. "It's always a day I had. A day after the Bank took me in. Because that's all I have. I have nothing before the Bank. Nobody does, in the school. We come to the Bank with nothing. That's why the Bank can teach us." She swallowed. "So whatever goes, it always goes from after."
"And this time?"
She was quiet.
"This time," said Elara, "something went that wasn't there."
The lamp hummed.
"I felt it go," she said. "When you made that sound. When the glass went white. I felt a day go out of me. A whole one. A big one. Bigger than any of them." Her hand, holding the little book, had begun to tremble. She pressed it against her chest to make it stop. "And I looked for the hole. Afterward. Just now. And it isn't after. It isn't in the school, or the seaside house, or the garden with the bees. It's before. Before all of them. Down at the very bottom. Where there isn't anything. Where there's never been anything." She looked up at him, and her dark eyes were wide and frightened and fierce. "How can I lose a day, Harvester, that I never had?"
Corin could not answer.
He was thinking of a pool. Of dragonflies. Of a small hot sticky hand holding on to his. Of two short dark braids sticking out like the handles of a jug, and a grin, and a gap in the grin.
He did not say any of it. He did not know how. He did not know if it was his, or hers, or nobody's. He only knew that it had come into him through her hand, on the glass, and that it was not his memory. He had never been to a pool like that. He was sure he had never been to a pool like that.
You were born in Oakhaven.
He was not sure of anything.
Elara was still looking at him. Waiting. When he did not answer, something in her face hardened again, slowly, like water freezing at the edges.
She looked down at the black spot of ink on the empty line. And then she wrote. Small. Upright. Perfect. But slower than before, as if each letter cost her something.
Today. Read: Harvester Vane, Harvesting Section. Account sealed. Archon's private seal. Lost: a day from before. I do not know which. I do not know how.
She blotted it with her thumb. She closed the book. She fastened the gold clasp. She put it back inside her jacket, over her heart.
And then, without seeming to know she was doing it, she touched her tongue to the front of her teeth. To the left one. The gold one. Just once. Quickly. The way someone touches a sore place, to see if it still hurts.
Corin saw it.
"Your tooth," he said.
She went still.
He had not meant to say it. It had come out by itself, the way the humming did.
"What about it?"
"Does it hurt?"
She looked at him for a long, strange moment. As if no one had ever asked her that. As if it were the oddest question anybody had ever asked her, in four thousand, one hundred and twelve readings.
"No," she said at last. "It doesn't hurt. It's only gold." A pause. "There was a gap there. Always. As long as I can remember. The tooth never grew in. The Bank's dentist capped it when I was twelve, so I would look correct at the counter." Her mouth moved, very slightly, the dry thing at the corner. "It's the only thing about me that the Bank had to fix."
Corin's chest hurt.
Not the thumb. Not the hand. Something bigger. Something like a door, very heavy, very old, swinging shut somewhere deep inside him, and the whole of him shaking with it.
She has a hole in her teeth.
He did not know where the words came from. They were not in the Ledger. They were not anywhere. They were in a voice he did not know, a boy's voice, very small, very serious, saying them as if they were the most important thing in the world.
My sister is Elara. She is four. She has a hole in her teeth.
He gripped the edge of the desk with both gloved hands.
Across from him, the Auditor of the Fourth Desk stood very straight, with her hands folded over the place where the little black book lay against her heart. She was watching him. She had seen something go across his face. He knew she had. She did not know what it was. Neither did he.
"You've gone white, Harvester," she said quietly.
"So have you."
She did not deny it.
She looked at him for a moment longer. Then she turned away, toward the desk, toward the dead glass and the empty jar and the little green book with DORRAN, PELL at the top of its page. And when she turned back, her face had closed again, smooth and pale and careful, like a door shutting on a lit room.
"The account of Pell Dorran," she said, "is still open."
