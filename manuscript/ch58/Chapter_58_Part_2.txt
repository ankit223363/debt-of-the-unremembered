Chapter 58: A Lord's Last Evening, Part 2 of 5: The Carrying Tune
The Carrying Tune
Gideon woke at noon.
Corin saw him do it. He had not slept himself, or not much, a little toward the eighth bell, sitting up against the pillar with the Ledger in his lap, and he had woken with a start out of a dream of a fish market and ice and a knife too big for his hand, and not known for a long moment where he was or whose hands those were in his lap with white linen on their black fingertips. And then he had known. And he had stayed awake after that.
The crypt was gray with daylight now. Not much of it. It came down the long flight of steps from the church above, through the door at the top, which Silas had left open a hand's width for the air. A long thin wedge of gray on the stone steps, and a little spilling out across the floor at the bottom. The brazier had burned low. Silas had kept it in all morning, as he said he would, and now he was asleep himself, at last, in a chair by the steps, with his long legs stretched out and his crooked spectacles in his hand and his chin on his chest.
Oren was still in the dark at the far end, against the wall, with the black case on his knees. Corin did not think he had slept at all. He had not moved since he put his hand over his eyes.
The girl in the gray gown was asleep on her blankets by the pillar. Really asleep, now. Corin could tell.
Juno was asleep curled up against Gideon's back, on the same heap of blankets, with her whistle still clutched in her fist and her frostbitten feet wrapped in a piece of sacking.
And Big Gideon was standing by the fire.
He had not moved. Not once. Not since he put them down. He had stood there all night and all morning, by the brazier, with his great gray hands hanging at his sides and his head almost touching the stone of the arches, looking at nothing. Nobody had told him to sit. So he had not sat.
Gideon woke all at once. The way he always woke, Corin thought, though he did not remember how he knew it. No stirring. No stretching. One moment his eyes were shut and the next they were open, and his hands had gone to the front of his coat, both of them, flat, over the place at his chest.
The jars were there. Corin saw his face as he felt them. Two shapes, under the coat. One the size of a fist. One no bigger than a hen's egg.
Gideon lay still with his hands over them for a long moment.
Then he coughed. A long, hard, tearing cough, from deep in his chest, that went on and on and bent him double on the blankets and woke Juno, who sat up with her whistle in her fist and said "What? What?" And then the cough stopped, and Gideon lay on his back on the blankets, breathing hard, and looked up at the stone arches.
And then he turned his head and saw his father.
He looked at him for a long time.
At the huge gray barefoot shape standing by the low red fire, with its hands hanging and its pale eyes fixed on nothing. Corin watched Gideon's face while he looked. It did not change. It never did. Gideon's face was a shut door, and had been since he was eight.
But his hand, on the front of his coat, over the smaller jar, had closed.
"Where's Saul?" said Gideon.
Nobody answered.
Juno looked at Corin. Corin looked at Juno.
Gideon sat up. Slowly. With his hand still closed over the front of his coat. He looked round the crypt. At the fire. At the pillars. At the dark at the far end, where Oren sat. At the steps going up to the church. He looked at all of it, carefully, the way he looked at everything, the way a boy looks who has had to know where every door in a room is for most of his life.
Then he looked back at his father.
"Where's Saul?" he said again.
And Juno told him.
She told him the way she had told Corin and the girl on the roof. Flat. Quick. Without stopping. How the two of them had come up the chute together carrying him between them like a crate, long before the light. How they had put him down in the snow. How the big one had sat down and taken him on his knees. And how the other one had stood there, in the snow, for a long time, looking at the chute. And then looked at Gideon. And hummed. And turned round, and gone back down. Feet first. Like he was going to work.
"I whistled," said Juno. "Three short. Over and over. He didn't look up." Her voice cracked. "And then the dome came down. And the light came up. Out of the chute, too. And he didn't come out."
Gideon listened.
He sat on the blankets with his knees drawn up and his hand on his coat and listened to every word, with his thin gray face turned toward Juno, and did not interrupt. And when she had finished, he did not say anything for a long time.
Then he said, "He went back to the drum."
"Yes."
"Because nobody rang the bell."
Juno did not answer.
"Stair's shut till the bell," said Gideon. "He'd have gone back to the drum to wait for the bell. Because that's where you wait. That's where he always waited." His dry voice did not change at all. "Nine years. Every night. He sat on the bench by the drum and waited for the bell. And then he turned the handle. That's all he had. The bench and the drum and the bell. And the tune." He looked at the fire. "So he went back to it. Because it was his."
Nobody said anything.
Gideon sat very still.
And then, slowly, he began to shake.
Not crying. He could not cry. That was in a jar somewhere, on a shelf, or gone. It was the other thing. The thing that had happened in the cold room, at the foot of the pillar, when he took the small white jar off its shelf and held it to his chest. His shoulders shook. Hard. Small. All through him, from the neck down. Like a wet dog. Like a boy who has come in out of the snow.
He shivered.
He sat on the blankets by the fire in the crypt of Saint Bride's with his hand over the small jar at his chest and shivered and shivered, and his teeth knocked together, and he did not seem to know he was doing it. His face did not change at all. Only his body. As if his body had found a way to do the thing that the rest of him could not.
Juno put her arms round him.
She did not say anything. She just put her arms round him from behind, the way you hold somebody who is cold, and held on. And her own face was wet. And she put her chin on his shoulder, and held on, and he shivered against her, and she let him.
And by the fire, Big Gideon Hollow did not move.
Corin watched him. The great gray shape. The empty eyes, fixed on nothing. The great hands, hanging. Three paces from his son. Who was shaking so hard his teeth knocked together. And he did not look round. He did not know. There was nothing in him to know with.
Gideon stopped shivering at last.
It went out of him slowly, the way a fever goes. And he sat with Juno's arms round him and looked at his father.
"Sit down," he said.
Big Gideon did not move.
"Sit down," said Gideon again. Louder. Not much. His voice was hoarse with the smoke. "Da. Sit down. You've been stood there all night. Sit down."
Nothing.
Then Gideon got up.
He got out of Juno's arms, and got up off the blankets, and walked the three paces across the stone floor to the fire, in his boots with no big toes in them. And stood in front of his father. He came up to the leather apron. He had to tip his head right back to see the face.
And he reached up, and took one of the great gray hanging hands in both of his own white curled ones. And pulled. Down. Gently. The way you pull a horse's head down to put the bridle on.
"Sit," he said.
And Big Gideon sat.
He did it the way he did everything. Slowly. In stages. Like a great stone door swinging. His knees bent. His great body came down, and down, until he was sitting on the stone floor by the fire with his legs stuck out in front of him and his back against the pillar, and his head, at last, below the arches. And his hand still in his son's.
Gideon sat down beside him.
Close. Against his side. Against the leather apron. The way he had lain across his knees in the snow. And did not let go of the hand.
And after a while, very softly, under his breath, he began to hum.
The carrying tune. Slow and heavy and going-on, with no end to it. The tune a man hums when he has a load on his back and a long way to go. The tune Saul had hummed on the stair, in the dark, under the burning Bourse, while his brother stood aside. Gideon hummed it now, by the fire, in his thin dry smoke-hoarse voice, sitting against his father.
Big Gideon did not hum. He never had. Couldn't hold a note, Coker said.
But his head moved.
Just a little. The way a dog's head moves when it hears its name from a long way off. It turned, very slowly, on the great gray neck. Away from the fire. Down. Toward the small thin boy beside him. And the pale empty eyes came down off nothing and found the boy's dark head, against his arm. And stayed there.
He did not know who it was. Corin was sure of that. There was nothing behind the eyes. Nothing at all.
But he went on looking.
And Gideon went on humming, on and on, with no end to it.
Juno sat on the blankets with her arms round her knees and her face wet and watched them. Corin sat against his pillar with the Ledger in his lap and watched them.
And across the brazier, on her blankets, the girl in the gray gown had woken. Corin saw her. She was lying on her side, quite still, with her eyes open, watching Gideon and his father by the fire. And her lips were moving. Very slightly. Counting.
When the tune had gone round four times, Gideon stopped.
He did not let go of his father's hand. He looked across the fire at Corin.
"What did you burn?" he said.
Corin did not answer.
Gideon looked at him. And then, the way he looked at everything, carefully, a door at a time, he looked at the girl on the blankets. Awake, now, watching. And then down. At the floor. At the clear space by the fire where nobody had swept.
At the word in blue chalk.
LYRA.
Corin saw him understand. It took him less time than it had taken Oren.
"I wrote that," said Gideon. "On Thursday. With my blue chalk. So Oren could learn the shape of it. A boot, and a fork, and a gate with a belly, and a tent." He looked at it. "It's the only word I know. Apart from V."
He was quiet for a moment.
"I'll write it again," he said. "Bigger. Every morning. On the floor. Wherever we are. So you can see it when you wake up." He looked at Corin. "It's not much."
Corin looked at the word on the floor.
A boot. A fork. A gate with a belly. A tent.
"It's not nothing," he said.
Thorne Blood
They opened it at the second bell after noon.
Oren brought it out of the dark at last. He came across the crypt with it held in his good arm and set it down on the stone floor by the brazier, in the middle of them all, very carefully, square, the way you set down a thing that might go off. And stood back. And did not sit down.
They sat round it in a ring. Corin against his pillar. Silas, awake now, with his spectacles back on, crooked. Juno with her whistle. Gideon, still against his father's side, with his father's great gray hand in his lap. Mrs. Doyle, who had come down the steps at noon with a broom and a basket of bread and a jug of milk and had looked at all of them and said nothing whatever and begun to sweep, and had swept round the word in blue chalk without touching it, and now sat on the bottom step with her broom across her knees.
And the girl in the gray gown. Kneeling. Closest. With the case in front of her on the stone.
She had washed. Silas had brought her a basin, at noon, and she had washed the soot off her face and her hands and her arms, and combed her short burned hair flat with her fingers. She still had on the gray gown. There was nothing else for her to wear. It was black at the hem and torn at one sleeve and stained across the front, and it had been her mother's. She knelt in it on the stone in front of the black case and looked at it.
Corin looked at her.
Washed, she was younger than he had thought. Twenty, perhaps. Thin, and pale, with dark eyes too big for her face, and a sharp chin, and a small worried line between her brows that he had not seen through the soot. Her mouth was set. Her hands were very still in her lap.
She did not look at him.
Her father, he thought. It's her father in there.
He knew that. From the Ledger. From Oren. From the brass plate on the Lady's cupboard, from Remy's book, from the Weigher's voice coming down the lift shaft. A Lord's Last Evening. Taken whole. Before the fire. Lord Aurelian Thorne. Her father. Who lay somewhere on the Hill tonight, in a room the Inquisitor had found for him, with a better nurse. One who listens.
He knew all of it. He had read it. And none of it was his.
"Linus said," said the girl, "it opens only to Thorne blood."
She said it to the case. Not to anybody.
"He said the hooded man closed it, in the hall, the night he took it. And it closed itself. And it would only open again for blood. Mine, or Ro's." She looked at the silver clasp. "Ro isn't here."
"Lyra," said Oren.
It was the first word he had said since the dawn.
She looked up at him.
"Whoever buys it," said Oren slowly, "won't live to open it. That's what the boy wrote. In his book. Remy." He did not take his eyes off her. "That's what everybody says."
"Nobody bought it," said the girl. "It was stolen."
Oren looked at her a moment longer. Then he let out a long breath through his nose, and said nothing more.
"Doctor," said the girl. "Your knife."
Silas took it out of his bag. The same knife. Corin knew it, from the Ledger. SHE CUT HER HAIR OFF WITH SILAS'S KNIFE. A short, plain surgeon's knife with a bone handle, very sharp. Silas wiped it on a clean cloth and held it out to her, handle first. And she took it.
She did not hesitate. She held out her left hand over the case, palm up, and drew the point of the knife across the ball of her thumb. Quick. Clean. The way Remy did it, Corin thought. The way you do a thing you have decided to do, before you can think about it.
The blood came up in a thin dark line.
She gave the knife back to Silas. And reached out with her cut thumb. To the clasp.
It was shaped like a closed hand. Corin had looked at it in the cold room, by the light of Remy's lantern. A small silver hand, a child's hand almost, with the fingers curled shut over the palm, the way you hold a thing you do not want anybody to see. And tucked under the curled silver fingers, so small you could miss it, the little square of folded paper. Folded and folded and folded, until it was no bigger than a fingernail.
She pressed her bleeding thumb to the back of the silver hand.
Nothing happened.
For a breath. Two. The blood lay on the silver, dark and wet. The fire crackled. Nobody moved.
And then the silver hand opened.
Corin saw it. They all saw it. Slowly. Like a real hand. Like a sleeping child's hand, opening in its sleep. The small silver fingers uncurled, one by one, the little finger first, then the next, then the next, then the first, and last of all the thumb. Until the hand lay open, palm up, flat on the black wood of the lid. Empty.
And the little folded square, with nothing to hold it any more, fell.
It fell off the open silver palm, and off the edge of the lid. And the girl caught it. Without thinking. In her cut left hand. The way you catch a thing that falls. And closed her fingers over it.
And the lid of the case lifted.
Not much. A finger's width. With a small soft click, somewhere inside, like a key turning in a well-oiled lock. And stopped there. Waiting.
Nobody breathed.
The girl knelt on the stone with her cut hand closed in her lap, round the folded square, and looked at the case with its lid lifted a finger's width. Her face had gone very white.
"Lyra," said Silas. Quietly. "Your hand."
She looked down at it.
At her closed fingers. At the blood from her thumb running down between them, onto the gray silk of her mother's gown. And she opened her hand, slowly, and looked at what was in it.
The folded square lay in her palm. Small and tight and white, with a smear of her blood on one corner.
"He folded it," she said. "Linus said. While it was being taken. With his free hand, under the table, where she couldn't see. He thought it was a habit." She looked at it. "Like a fish going under a boat. That's what he said. Something went under, and he let it go. The only brave thing he'd done in twenty years."
"Don't open it," said Corin.
She looked at him.
It was the first time she had looked at him since the dawn. Straight at him. With her dark eyes too big for her face.
"Why not?" she said.
He did not know.
He did not know why he had said it. The words had come out of him the way the house is on fire had come out of him in Cinder Lane. Before he thought. As if something in him knew a thing that the rest of him did not.
Boy, said Malakor, very quietly. Good.
"Because he folded it to hide it," said Corin slowly. Working it out as he said it. "Your father. On the last night. While they were taking everything else. He couldn't stop them taking the evening. So he took one thing out of the evening and folded it so small that they wouldn't see it go. And he let it go under the boat." He looked at the square in her palm. "If you open it, here, now, whatever he hid is out again. In the air. Where anybody can see it."
The girl looked at the folded square.
"He wanted it hidden," said Corin. "So it stays hidden. Until you know what it is. And who's looking."
She was quiet for a long time.
Then she closed her hand over it again. And reached into the bodice of the gray gown, at the side, where there was a pocket sewn flat into the seam. A list pocket. The kind a lady keeps her list of dances in. Corin knew it, from the Ledger. The evidence goes in Lyra's list pocket. The vial. The letter. The strip from the ledger. She put the folded square in there, with the rest. And smoothed the silk flat over it with her bleeding hand.
"All right," she said. "Not yet."
Not yet. When I can. That was in the Ledger too. On the wain. Under the hay. In her small quick hand.
She turned back to the case.
She put both hands on the lid, the cut one and the other. And lifted it.
It came up easily. On silver hinges. All the way back, until it lay open against the stone. And the inside of the case was lined with velvet. Black. Very old, worn smooth in places, the nap gone. And in the middle of the black velvet, in a hollow shaped exactly to hold it, there was a stone.
It was the size of a pigeon's egg. Round, but not quite round. Smooth. Cloudy. The color of smoke, and of honey, and of the sky an hour before a storm, all at once, swirling together in it. And deep inside it, so deep you had to look and look to see it, a light.
Not like the jars. The jars in the cold room had glowed. Gray and blue and green, faint, all through the glass. This was not like that. This was one light, very small, very far down in the middle of the stone. Gold. Warm gold. Like a candle in a window at the end of a long road, seen from a great way off, at night, in the rain.
And it was moving.
Not fast. Not slow. Steadily. Round and round. Like something walking. Like a man walking up and down a room, in the evening, by a fire, waiting for somebody to come.
The girl looked at it for a long time.
Then she put out her cut hand and touched it. Just with one fingertip. On the smooth cloudy surface, over the place where the light was.
And the light stopped walking.
For a moment. Just a moment. It stopped, deep in the stone, as if it had heard something. And turned. Toward her finger.
And then went on walking, round and round, as before.
The girl took her hand away.
She did not say anything. She knelt in front of the open case with both hands in her lap and her head bent, and her short burned hair fell forward over her face so that nobody could see it. And her shoulders did not shake. And she made no sound at all.
Oren turned round and walked away into the dark at the far end of the crypt.
Nobody else moved.
After a long time, the girl lifted her head. Her face was quite dry. Quite white.
"How do we see it?" she said. "How do we see what's in it?"
And she looked across the case at Corin.
And so did everybody else.
