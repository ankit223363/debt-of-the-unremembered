Chapter 58: A Lord's Last Evening, Part 4 of 5: Witness K
Witness K
The visitor came in by the garden way.
Corin did not see how. There was a door, he thought, behind the books, on the left of the fireplace. A narrow door, painted to look like shelves, with the spines of books painted on it in brown and gold, so that you would never know it was there. The man in the chair knew it was there. He had known all his life. It let out into a narrow passage, and the passage let out into the garden, behind the house, where there was a wall, and a gate in the wall, and an alley beyond. His grandfather had made it. For visitors who did not wish to be seen coming in at the front.
The painted shelves swung open.
And somebody stepped through.
And Corin could not see them.
He tried. He was inside the man in the chair, looking out through the man's eyes, and the man was looking straight at the visitor, and Corin tried with all of himself to see what the man saw. And he could not. There was a shape. A person, standing just inside the painted door, in a long dark traveling cloak, wet through, dripping onto the rug. About the height of a man, or a tall woman. With a hood. He could see the cloak. He could see the water running off the hem of it onto the worn red and blue rug. He could see a hand come up, and push the hood back.
And where the face should have been, there was—
Paper.
That was the only word he had for it. Not paper. But like it. As if somebody had taken a picture of a face, and folded it. And folded it again. And again. Until there was nothing left to see but the creases. Pale. Flat. Folded in on itself, over and over, the lines crossing and crossing. Where the eyes should be, a fold. Where the mouth should be, a fold. Folds all the way down, into nothing.
He folded it, thought Corin. Afterward. While Linus was taking the rest. He folded this.
He took the face out of his own last evening, so that nobody who ever walked this stone would see it.
The man in the chair could see it. Corin felt that. The man was looking at the visitor's face, and knew it, and the knowing was warm and sad and very old. But Corin, inside the man, behind his eyes, saw only folds.
"K," said Lord Aurelian Thorne.
Just the letter. The way you say a name you have not said aloud for a long time, and are not sure you should.
The visitor did not answer at once.
When they did, the voice was folded too. Corin heard it. Not the words. The words came through, clear enough, one by one. But the voice that carried them was creased and flattened, so that he could not tell if it was a man's voice or a woman's, young or old, high or low. As if every word had been put through a mangle before it reached him.
"My lord," said the folded voice. "You remembered."
"The garden door. And the words." Lord Thorne did not get up. Corin felt why. His knees had gone weak. "She told me. Before she went. If K ever comes to the garden door and says the words, Aurelian, you let them in, and you listen, and you do not ask how." A pause. "Nine years. I had almost stopped listening for the knock."
"I had almost stopped meaning to knock."
The visitor came across the rug to the fire. Stood in front of it. Held out two hands to it, in wet gloves, black. And the steam came up off the cloak in the heat. And Corin, looking through Lord Thorne's eyes, saw the visitor's shoulders. Shaking. Very slightly. With cold. Or with something else.
"Sit down," said Lord Thorne. "Please. You'll catch your death."
And something in the folded figure flinched. Corin saw it. A small, sharp movement of the shoulders. As if the words had touched an old burn.
Isolde, you'll catch your death. It was in the Ledger. Lyra's mother's memory. Rain on Crown Walk. Her father's voice. Isolde, you'll catch your death.
The visitor did not sit.
"I haven't long," said the folded voice. "They'll be here at the ninth bell."
Lord Thorne did not move.
But Corin felt it go through him. The cold water, coming up through the cellar floor. Rising. All at once now. To the chest. To the throat.
"Who?"
"Linus Hale. In a black carriage from the Ministry. With the papers already signed. For the protection of a Councillor in poor health. The slow way. Whole." The visitor's gloved hands did not move from the fire. "And somebody else. Somebody who'll stand behind your chair while he does it. To make sure it's done."
"Who?"
The visitor did not answer.
"K. Who?"
"You'll see her," said the folded voice. Very quietly. "I'm sorry, Aurelian. You'll see her. And you'll know her. And you won't be able to stop it."
Her.
Lord Thorne sat very still in his chair by the fire with his hands on the arms of it.
Corin felt him think. Fast. The way a good chess player thinks, all the moves at once, branching. Her. Who stands behind my chair. Who would want my evening, whole. Who would want to be sure. And Corin felt him come to the end of the branching, and find a name there, and not want it. And push it away. And come back to it. And not be able to push it away again.
He did not say it. Not aloud. Corin did not hear it.
But the man in the chair went cold, all through. From the inside out. Like a house when the fire goes out.
"Why tonight?" he said. His voice was quite steady still. Corin did not know how. "Why me? I've done nothing. I sit on the Council and I vote with the Duke and I go home and read by the fire. I've done nothing for nine years."
"You asked a question," said the folded voice.
"I ask a great many questions."
"You asked this one at Council. In the spring. In the Treasurer's report, when he read out the Fourth Desk figures. You asked why there was a sum, every quarter, going out of the Bank to a place called the vault, under Sundries, the North, and nobody could tell you what it was for." The gloved hands turned, slowly, in front of the fire. "Nobody else heard it. The Treasurer smiled and said he would look into it. And the Archon wrote something in his book. A small mark. Beside your name."
Lord Thorne said nothing.
"A circle," said the visitor.
The tall clock in the corner ticked.
Corin, behind Lord Thorne's eyes, felt the word go into him like a nail into wood. A circle. He knew it. He knew it from somewhere outside this room, outside this evening, outside this man. From a list. Three names. A circle beside the first.
Hale, L.
"So they're coming," said Lord Thorne. "For my evening. Because I asked about a vault."
"Because you asked about the vault. And because of what they think you know." The visitor turned from the fire at last. And looked at him. With the folded face. "And because of what you're about to know. Because I'm going to tell you. Now. Before they come."
"Why?"
"Because she asked me to. Nine years ago. If it ever came to this." The folded voice was very low. "She said: If they ever come for him, K, tell him everything first. Everything. So that whatever they take, they take it all, and he isn't left half-knowing. Aurelian hates to be half-knowing."
And for the first time that evening, Corin felt Lord Aurelian Thorne smile.
It was a small smile. Very tired. Very sad. It did not reach his mouth. It stayed somewhere inside him, the way a candle stays inside a lantern.
"She was right," he said. "I do."
He got up out of his chair.
It was hard. His knees were still weak. He put his long pale hands on the arms of the chair and pushed, and stood, and stood there a moment by the fire, a tall thin man of fifty-two in a dark coat, with gray coming into his hair at the sides. And then he crossed the room to his desk by the window, and the rain on the black glass, and the dark green curtains. And sat down at it. And moved the brass lamp with the green shade a little nearer. And took a clean sheet of paper from the drawer. And a pen.
And laid them on the desk in front of him, side by side. And did not write anything.
"Tell me," he said. "I'm listening."
The visitor came across the room. Stood beside the desk. In the light of the brass lamp. With the rain on the window behind, and the wet cloak dripping on the floor.
And reached inside the cloak. And took out a book.
Corin could see the book. The fold did not cover it. A small flat book, very old, bound in boards covered with faded blue cloth. Swollen with damp. With a strap round it and a brass buckle. The visitor laid it on the desk, beside the clean sheet of paper. And unbuckled the strap. And opened it.
"Do you know what this is?" said the folded voice.
Lord Thorne looked at it.
Corin looked through his eyes.
It was a register. A parish register. The kind of book a priest keeps in a village church, of births and marriages and deaths, year by year, in a careful hand. The pages were brown with damp and the ink had run in places. And at the top of the open page, in the old careful hand, a name.
The name of a village.
OAKHAVEN.
Corin's breath stopped.
Not Lord Thorne's. His own. Far away. Behind him. Below him. On a stone floor, by a fire, with a warm stone on his forehead and two hands holding his. He felt his own body, far away, go rigid. He felt the hands round his own tighten. Hard. And somewhere, very far off, a voice he knew, quick and a little cross, said something sharp. He could not hear what.
Oakhaven.
It was on the first page of the Ledger. In his own square capitals. Near the top. You were born in a place called Oakhaven. You do not remember it.
He did not remember it.
But he knew the word. The way you know your own name. The way you know a tune.
Steady, boy, said Malakor. Behind his left ear. Very far away. Very steady. Steady. Hold the rope. It's his evening. Not yours. Watch. Just watch.
Corin held the rope.
And watched.
"I know the name," said Lord Thorne slowly. "A village. In the north. In the hills above the Glass. It burned." He frowned. "Sixteen years ago? In the war. The fever came, and they burned it to stop the fever. The Council voted the money for it. I remember. I was new on the Council. I voted for it." His voice went quiet. "Everybody did."
"There was no fever," said the visitor.
The rain ran down the black window.
"There was no fever," said the folded voice again. "There was never any fever. I was there, my lord. I was there the night it burned. I was—" The voice stopped. Started again. "I was there. And there was no fever. There were men in gray with lamps, and a man with a gentle voice who walked through the village from door to door, and at every door he stopped, and went in, and when he came out there was nobody left inside who remembered anything. And then the men in gray took the children. Some of the children. Not all. And then they burned it."
Lord Thorne sat at his desk and did not move.
"Why?" he said.
The visitor put one gloved finger on the open page of the register. On a line, halfway down. A family. A name, in the old careful hand, that ran down the page in a column, again and again, year after year, births and marriages and deaths, for a hundred years.
"Because of them," said the visitor.
And Corin, looking through Lord Thorne's eyes, read the name.
Bright.
And outside, in the dark, in the rain, at the far end of Crown Walk, the clock on the steeple of Saint Clement's began to strike the half hour.
Half past eight.
Bright
"Bright," said Lord Thorne.
He said it the way you say a word in a language you learned as a child and have not spoken since. Slowly. Trying the shape of it.
"A common name," he said. "In the north. There are Brights in the Lower Docks. There's a woman called Bright who sells eels on the Fish Stairs."
"There are," said the visitor. "There are Brights everywhere. That's why they chose it."
"Who chose it?"
The folded face looked down at the open register. At the column of names, running down the page, year after year. The gloved finger moved down it, slowly. Not touching. Just moving. Over the names.
"My lord," said the folded voice. "How many Emperors called Cassian have there been?"
Lord Thorne did not answer at once.
Corin felt him reach for it. The way you reach for a thing you have always known, without looking, the way you reach for the door handle in your own house in the dark. And find it. And frown.
"Seven," said Lord Thorne.
"Are you sure?"
"Of course I'm sure. Every child in Veridia knows the seven Cassians. Cassian the Builder. Cassian the Fair. Cassian of the Long Peace—" He stopped. "Seven. Cassian the Seventh died without a son, and the crown went to his cousin, the first of the Valerian line, and the Council was made to keep the peace between them, two hundred years ago. It's in every schoolbook." He looked up at the folded face. "Seven."
"There were eight," said the visitor.
The rain ran down the window.
"There were eight," said the folded voice. "Cassian the Seventh had a son. Late. When he was old. A boy, by his second wife, who was nobody, a miller's daughter from the north. And when the old Emperor died, the boy was crowned. Cassian the Eighth. He was nineteen. He reigned for four years." The gloved finger stopped on the page. "And then he was taken. In the night. By men in gray, with lamps. And emptied. And everybody who had ever known him was emptied of him too. Every lord. Every servant. Every clerk who had ever written his name. Every book that had his name in it was burned, or had the page cut out. Every coin with his face on it was melted down. Every painting was painted over." The folded voice was very steady. "It took them three years. And at the end of it, there had never been a Cassian the Eighth. There had only ever been seven. And the crown had gone to the cousin. And the Council had been made. And every child in Veridia learned it in school."
Lord Thorne did not say anything.
Corin, inside him, felt him not say anything. Felt the man's mind go round and round it, the way a dog goes round and round a thing it does not understand. Felt him try to remember. An eighth Cassian. A boy of nineteen. A coin. A painting. Anything.
There was nothing. Not a crack. Not a gap. Nothing to show that anything had ever been there. A smooth wall, where a door had been bricked up so long ago and so well that the bricks had grown moss.
"That's not possible," he said.
"It's possible," said the visitor. "It's what they do. It's all they've ever done. You've seen the Bank, my lord. You've seen the Bourse. You've seen the Health, and the sweets, and the Friday cart. They didn't learn that last year. They learned it two hundred years ago, on a boy of nineteen. They were very good at it even then." The gloved hand lay flat on the register. "But they missed one thing."
"What?"
"His wife."
The tall clock ticked.
"He had a wife," said the visitor. "Cassian the Eighth. A girl from the north, like his mother. They'd only been married a year. Nobody at court liked her. Nobody at court knew her. She'd never been painted. She'd never been on a coin. When the men in gray came, in the night, she was not in the palace. She was in the north, at her father's house, in a village in the hills above the Glass. Having a child."
The gloved finger moved. On the page. To the top of the column of names.
"They forgot to look for her," said the folded voice. "Because they had already made everybody forget she was there."
Lord Thorne looked at the top of the column.
Corin looked through his eyes.
The first name in the column was written in a different ink from the rest. Older. Browner. In a hand that was not the careful priest's hand. A rougher hand. A hand that had been in a hurry, or afraid, or both.
Bright. Born here. A boy. His mother calls him only by that.
And beside it, very small, in the margin, in the same brown hurried ink, a drawing.
A tree. With a crown in its branches.
Corin knew it. He knew it from the man's own hand. From the signet ring on the man's own little finger, resting now on the edge of the desk beside the register. A tree, with a crown in its branches. The Thorne crest.
Lord Thorne looked down at his own ring for a long time.
"That's our crest," he said.
"It was his," said the visitor. "Before it was yours. The Thornes were his mother's people. The miller's daughter. Your family were millers once, my lord, two hundred years ago, before they were lords. Did you know that?" A pause. "No. Of course not. Nobody does. When the Eighth was emptied out of the world, they gave your family a title and a house on the Hill, to keep them quiet. And then they emptied the family of why."
Lord Aurelian Thorne sat at his desk and looked at the tree with the crown in its branches, on his own ring, and on the page.
Corin felt something move in him. Very deep. Not a memory. Older than a memory. The way the ground moves, a long way down, before a building falls.
"So the Brights," he said slowly. "Of Oakhaven."
"Are the Eighth's line. His son's son's son. Two hundred years of them. Hiding in a village in the hills under a common name, being nobody. Millers and carters and shepherds. Marrying other nobodies. Keeping a register. Keeping the mark. Telling each child, when it was old enough, one thing, and only one. You are a Bright. Remember it. Don't ask why." The folded voice went quiet. "And keeping one more thing. Hidden. In the village. In something the men in gray would never think to look in."
"What?"
"A song," said the visitor.
The rain ran down the black glass.
Can a memory be hidden in a song?
Corin heard it. Far away. Not in this room. In another place, another time, another voice. A woman's voice, in a dark blue cloak, at the door of the Lamplighter's Rest, asking an old harvester a question. He had read it in the Ledger. Isolde asked him: Can a memory be hidden in a song?
"And then sixteen years ago," said the visitor, "somebody found the register."
Lord Thorne did not move.
"I don't know how. I've never known. A clerk, perhaps, at the Bank. A priest who talked. A Bright who married out and said too much. It doesn't matter. Somebody found it. And the man with the gentle voice came to Oakhaven in the night, with the men in gray, and the lamps. And he went from door to door. And at every door he went in. And when he came out, there was nobody inside who remembered anything. And he did not only take the Brights. He took everybody. Every family. Every child. Every grandmother. Every dog. Because he could not be sure who had married whom, or who had been told what, or who might have heard a song." The voice was very low. "And then they took the children. The Bright children. All of them they could find. Seven. And put them in a crate. And sent them south."
"Where?"
"To the vault," said the visitor.
The vault.
Sundries, the North.
Lord Thorne put his hand flat on the desk. Hard.
"That's the sum," he said. "The quarterly sum. In the Fourth Desk figures. The vault. That's—"
"That's what keeps them," said the visitor. "Yes. Every quarter. For sixteen years. Food, and a nurse, and a lamp, and a guard. And once a year, a man with a gentle voice who comes and sits with each of them and makes sure they still don't remember." The folded face turned toward him. "They don't kill them, my lord. They never kill them. That's the thing you have to understand about the people who did this. They never kill anything they might need. They keep it. Cold. In the dark. On a shelf. In case."
"In case of what?"
The visitor did not answer.
"K. In case of what?"
"In case," said the folded voice, "they ever need an Emperor."
The tall clock in the corner of the study struck the three-quarters.
Nobody moved.
Corin lay behind Lord Thorne's eyes in the warm study in the rain, two autumns gone, and did not breathe. Far away, below him, behind him, on a stone floor, a pair of hands was holding his so hard that even through the gray he could feel it. And a voice was saying something. Over and over. He could not hear it. Not yet.
Steady, said Malakor. Steady. Not yet. Hold on.
"Seven," said Lord Thorne at last. "Seven children."
"Seven went south in the crate. Five are in the vault still. One died, the first winter." The visitor paused. "And one got out."
Lord Thorne looked up.
"Got out?"
"On the road. The first night. Somebody opened the crate." The folded voice did not change. "I won't tell you who. I won't tell you which child. Not even you. Not even tonight. Because in a quarter of an hour, Linus Hale is going to sit down in that chair by your fire and take this whole evening out of you and put it in a box. And then it won't be yours any more. It'll be theirs." The gloved hand closed on the register. "So I won't tell you. But you should know. One got out. One is alive, and grown, and walking about in the world, and doesn't know what it is. And they don't know where."
Lord Thorne sat very still.
"Why are you telling me this?" he said quietly. "If they're going to take it."
And the visitor answered with the words that had come to the garden door.
"Because the dark is the day with its eyes shut, my lord," said K. "Hers. Isolde's. She used to say it to the children when they were afraid of the night. Don't be frightened. The dark is only the day with its eyes shut. It's all still there. It's just waiting to be looked at again."
The folded face turned toward the window. Toward the rain.
"They can take your evening," said the folded voice. "They can put it in a box. They can keep it cold. But it's still there. Everything I've told you. It's all still there, in the box, in the dark, waiting. And one day somebody is going to open the box." A pause. "And look."
And Lord Aurelian Thorne, sitting at his desk with his signet ring on his finger and a register from a burned village open in front of him, turned his head and looked at the clean sheet of paper lying beside it. The one he had taken out of the drawer. And not written on.
And Corin, inside him, felt him decide.
