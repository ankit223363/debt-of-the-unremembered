Chapter 58: A Lord's Last Evening, Part 1 of 5: Saint Bride's
Saint Bride's
The church of Saint Bride stood at the top of a short steep lane above the river, on the far side of Lantern Bridge, and it had not had a priest for nine years.
Corin knew that. It was in the Ledger. He had read it on the bridge, hanging head-down over Big Gideon's shoulder, with the Ledger open in his hands against the leather apron and the lamps of the bridge going past above him, blue, one after another, with the snow on their glass. Saint Bride's. No priest nine years. Doctor Silas. The crypt. Safe. He had read it three times. He did not remember the church. But he remembered the word safe, the way you remember the taste of something you have not eaten for a long time.
He saw the church at last from upside down. A low square tower, black against the gray beginning of the sky. A porch. A heavy door studded with iron. And a lamp burning in the porch. An oil lamp, yellow, in a glass case, the kind you hang out for somebody who is late.
Somebody had left it burning for them.
The girl in the gray gown went ahead and knocked. Three times, then twice. A pattern. He did not know it. And the door opened at once, as if somebody had been standing just inside it with a hand on the latch, all night.
It was a tall man. Very tall, and very thin, like a heron standing in a reed bed. In a long black coat with the sleeves rolled up, and a gray waistcoat, and no collar, and spectacles on the end of his nose, crooked, as if he had pushed them up a great many times with the back of his wrist and they had given up. He had a candle in one hand. His face was long and lined and very tired.
He looked at the girl. And at Juno. And at the huge gray barefoot man with two people over his shoulders, filling the porch.
"Saints," said Doctor Silas, very quietly.
Then he stood back from the door and held it wide.
"Bring them in," he said. "Bring them down. Mind the steps. There's a fire."
There was a fire.
Corin saw it from Big Gideon's shoulder as they went down. A long flight of stone steps, worn into dips in the middle, going down from the back of the church into the dark. And at the bottom, a crypt. Low and wide, with a roof of round stone arches like the inside of a loaf, and squat stone pillars holding them up, and the floor of old stone slabs, some of them carved with names and worn almost smooth. And in the middle of the floor, in an iron brazier on three legs, a fire. Coal, glowing red. With a black kettle on a hook over it, steaming.
It was the warmest place Corin had ever seen.
He did not remember it. He knew that he must have been here. The Ledger said so. The crypt. Safe. He had slept here, perhaps, on these stones. He had sat by this fire. But he looked at it from Big Gideon's shoulder and it was a crypt, and a fire, and a kettle, and nothing more.
And then he saw the floor.
On the stone slabs, near the fire, in a clear space where nobody had swept, there was a word. Written in chalk. Blue chalk, the kind porters use, in big uneven capitals, the way a child writes when it has only just learned what letters are.
LYRA.
He read it.
He could read. He had not lost that. He read it the way you read any word. Four letters. A name.
He looked at the girl in the gray gown.
She was standing by the brazier, holding her black hands out to the fire. And she had seen him look at the word on the floor. And she had seen him look at her. And she did not say anything. She turned her face to the fire.
LYRA THORNE. PARTNER.
Somebody had written her name on the floor of this crypt in blue chalk. Somebody who had only just learned what letters were. He did not know who. He did not know why. It was a word on a floor.
He looked away from it.
"Down," said the girl, to Big Gideon. "Put them down. By the fire. Gently."
Big Gideon put them down.
He did it very slowly. He bent his great knees, and leaned, and let Gideon slide off his left shoulder onto a heap of blankets by the brazier, as softly as a mother puts down a sleeping baby. And then he bent the other way, and let Corin down off his right shoulder onto the stone floor, not as softly, and Corin's bad leg hit the slab and he made a sound he did not mean to make. And then Big Gideon stood up straight again, all the way, until his head almost touched the stone of the arches. And stood there, beside the fire, with his great gray hands hanging at his sides. And waited.
Nobody told him anything. So he waited.
"Who—" said Doctor Silas. And stopped.
He was looking up at Big Gideon. At the hook-shaped scar. At the empty pale eyes.
Then he looked down at Gideon, on the blankets. At the thin gray face, eyes shut, turned to the fire. At the coat buttoned to the neck over something at the chest.
"His father," said the girl in the gray gown. "The other one's dead. His uncle. Saul." Her voice was quite flat. "He went back."
Silas did not say anything. He knelt down on the stone by Gideon, and put his long fingers on the side of the boy's neck, and held them there. And then he put his ear to Gideon's chest, over the coat, and listened. A long time.
"Smoke," he said at last. "Only smoke. He'll cough for a week. He'll live."
He got up. And turned to Corin.
And stopped.
Corin saw his face change. Saw the tired lined face go still, and the eyes behind the crooked spectacles go to Corin's hands, and stay there.
"Later," said Corin. "Is it here?"
Silas did not answer.
But somebody else did.
"It's here."
The voice came from the dark at the far end of the crypt. Beyond the fire, beyond the pillars, where the light did not reach. A deep voice. Rough. Very tired. And then the scrape of a boot on stone. And a man came out of the dark into the red light of the brazier.
A big man. Gray-bearded. With one arm in a sling of black shawl, and a sword on his hip, and his coat white at the shoulders with snow that had not melted. He had been sitting in the dark against the far wall, Corin thought. For hours. With his sword across his knees.
And under his good arm, held tight against his side, a black wooden case. Silver corners. A silver clasp shaped like a closed hand.
Captain Oren.
Corin knew him. He did not have to read it. He knew him the way he knew Juno, and Gideon, and Silas, and his own name. Captain Oren. Forty-six. Commonwealth Foot at sixteen. Thorne house guard. Hates being thanked. He knew him.
Oren came across the crypt, past the fire, and stopped in front of Corin, where he lay on the stone.
He did not look at Corin.
He looked at the girl in the gray gown.
He looked at her for a long time. At her black hands. At her burned hair. At the hem of her mother's gown, black and ragged. At her face. And Corin saw something go out of the old soldier's shoulders. All at once. The way the air goes out of a man when he puts down a load he has been carrying for a very long way.
"You're out," said Oren.
"I'm out."
"He brought you out."
The girl looked at Corin.
"Yes," she said. "He brought me out."
Oren nodded, slowly. And then he turned, at last, and looked down at Corin. And held out the black case. Not to give it to him. Just to show him. That it was here. That it was safe. That he had done what he was told.
"Her father first," said Oren. "Like you said."
"Thank you, Captain," said Corin.
And Oren's face went dark red under the gray beard, and he said something under his breath that Corin did not catch, and turned away.
Hates being thanked.
Corin almost smiled.
Then Oren turned back. And looked at the girl again, and at Corin, and at the girl. And Corin saw him see it. Saw the old soldier's eyes go from one face to the other, and back, and narrow. The way a sergeant's eyes narrow when he is counting men coming back from a fight, and the number is wrong.
"What's the matter with him?" said Oren.
Nobody answered.
"Lyra." Oren's voice had gone very quiet. "What's the matter with him? He looked at you like—" He stopped. "He looked at you like you were a stranger."
The girl in the gray gown stood by the fire with her back very straight.
"I am," she said. "To him. Now."
The crypt was very quiet. The kettle on its hook over the brazier had begun to sing, a thin high note, on and on. Nobody moved to take it off.
"He held up the dome," said the girl. "At the Bourse. When it came down. He held it up on his hands, from his knees, for long enough for us to get them out from under. The porters. Everybody. I saw it. Juno saw it. Half the city saw the light after." She did not look at Corin. "And to hold it, he had to burn something big enough. So he did."
Oren looked at her.
He did not ask what. He was a soldier. He had been a soldier for thirty years. He knew what it cost, to hold a thing that big. Corin saw him know it. Saw him look at the girl, and then at Corin, and then at the word in blue chalk on the floor by the fire. LYRA. And understand.
The old soldier's face did not change.
He turned round, without a word, and went back across the crypt into the dark at the far end, where he had been sitting. And sat down there again, against the wall, with the black case on his knees. And put his good hand over his eyes.
And stayed like that.
Doctor Silas took the kettle off the fire.
He did it quietly. He poured the hot water into a tin basin, and put a cloth in it, and brought it to where Corin lay on the stone. And knelt down. And did not say anything for a long time.
Then he said, "Your hands."
"Yes."
"Let me see them."
Corin held them out.
And beside him, the girl in the gray gown knelt down on the stone too, on his other side. Without being asked. And took his left wrist, very lightly, in her black fingers, to hold it steady. The way you hold the wrist of somebody who has been hurt, so that the doctor can work.
He did not know her.
But he let her hold it.
Slate
Silas washed them first.
His face first, in fact. Fenton's face. Vespera's binding had been meant to last a week, and it had lasted two nights and a dome. The heat of the gallery had loosened it. When Silas put the warm cloth to Corin's cheeks, the heavy brow and the wax nose and the cotton cheeks came away under it in soft gray flakes, like ash off a log, and fell into the basin, and sank. Only the small tattooed mole under the left eye stayed. Vespera had said it always would. What was left round it was his own face. Thinner than the Ledger said. With a burn along one cheekbone from the dome. Silas looked at it for a moment, and said nothing, and went on to the hands.
He did it very slowly, with the cloth from the tin basin, warm, in long soft strokes from the elbow down. The soot came off. The ash came off. The gray did not. It was under the skin, the gray, not on it. It was the skin. And as the soot came away, and the water ran off his arms into the basin and turned it black, Corin saw his hands properly for the first time since the dome.
He had known they were gray. They had been gray for a long time. Since the autumn, the Ledger said. Gray at the fingertips first, then the knuckles, then the backs of the hands, then the wrists, a little further every time he burned. That was what happened to harvesters. Everybody knew that. The Ash you took in from other people, and the things you burned of your own, they left their mark on you, and the mark was gray. It came up your arms like a tide coming up a beach. Slowly. A little more every year. And when it reached your shoulders, you stopped. Or you were stopped.
Yesterday morning, in Vespera's arch, his hands had been gray from the fingertips to a little past the elbows. A pale gray. Ash-gray. The color of a winter sky. He had looked at them while Lyra held them, and Vespera's needles went into his face.
They were not that color now.
They were dark. Gray like slate. Gray like the stone of a roof in the rain. All the way up past the elbows, and further, he could see it now, as Silas pushed the sleeve of Fenton's coat back, dark gray to halfway up the upper arm. A hand's width from the shoulder.
And the fingertips.
All ten of them. To the first joint. Black.
Not burned black. Not the black of a burn, with blisters and broken skin. Smooth. Hard. Black like jet, or like the black of a bruise that has gone on for a year. As if the gray had gone so deep there, so far down into him, that it had gone past gray altogether and out the other side.
Silas turned the left hand over in his long fingers. Very gently. And pressed the tip of the first finger with his thumb.
"Can you feel that?"
Corin looked at it. At Silas's thumb pressing on the black fingertip.
"No."
Silas pressed the second finger.
"That?"
"No."
The third. The fourth. The thumb. The other hand. One after another. Corin watched Silas press each black fingertip, and felt nothing. Nothing at all. As if they were not his. As if somebody had sewn ten small black stones onto the ends of his fingers while he slept.
Silas pressed the first joint of the first finger, just above the black. Where the slate-gray began.
"That?"
"Yes. A little. Like through a glove."
Silas let go of his hand. He sat back on his heels on the stone floor, and took his crooked spectacles off, and rubbed his eyes with the back of his wrist, and put them back on. Still crooked.
"I've seen that before," he said. "Once."
"Where?"
"Highmoor. The field hospitals. Twenty years ago. In the war." He did not look at Corin. He looked at the fire. "There was a war mage they brought in from the line. A young one. He'd held a bridge. Alone. For an hour, they said, with his hands, against a whole company, while the wounded went over it behind him. He'd burned everything. His whole life. To hold it." Silas was quiet. "His hands looked like that when they brought him in. Black at the ends. Gray to here." He touched his own arm, a hand's width below the shoulder.
"What happened to him?"
Silas did not answer for a moment.
"He lived," he said. "For a while. He didn't know his name, or the war, or the bridge. But he lived. He used to sit in the sun by the tents, and the nurses fed him. He was very gentle." Silas looked down at Corin's hands. "And then one day he tried to light a candle. Just a candle. Out of habit. The way a man who's smoked all his life reaches for a pipe. He held up his hand and tried to light a candle with it." He stopped.
"And?"
"And it went up his arm," said Silas. "The gray. All of it. In a breath. Up past the shoulder. Into his neck. Into his face." He did not look away. "And he sat there by the tent in the sun and smiled at the nurses and didn't know them. Didn't know anything. Like a Slate. For the rest of his life. Which wasn't long."
The fire crackled in the brazier.
On the other side of Corin, the girl in the gray gown still held his left wrist in her black fingers. He felt her hand tighten. Just a little.
"So," said Silas. "No more."
Corin looked at him.
"No more burning," said Silas. "Not a candle. Not a breath of wind. Not a spark. Nothing. Not for a long time. Months. Perhaps longer. Perhaps never." His tired voice was very steady. "You've a hand's width left. A hand's width of gray between you and the rest of your life. And you can't feel your fingers to know when you've used it."
Corin did not say anything.
Silas dried his hands, gently, with a clean cloth. And then he took a pot of something from his bag, something that smelled of honey and of green leaves, and rubbed it into the slate-gray skin with his long fingers, all the way up, from the black tips to the edge of the gray near the shoulder. It did not hurt. Corin could not feel most of it. And then he wrapped each black fingertip in a strip of clean white linen, one by one, very neatly, the way you wrap a cut. Not because it would help, Corin thought. Because it was something to do.
When it was done, Corin's hands lay in his lap like two strangers' hands, with white linen on the ends of the fingers. Like a pair of gloves that somebody else had worn and left behind.
"Sleep," said Silas. "All of you. It's nearly the fifth bell. Sleep till noon. I'll keep the fire."
He got up and went to see to Gideon.
The girl in the gray gown let go of Corin's wrist.
She did not say anything. She got up, stiffly, the way you get up when you have been on your knees on cold stone a long time. And went to the far side of the brazier, where there was a heap of old blankets against a pillar, and lay down on them in her burned gown. With her back to the fire. With her back to him.
And was still.
Corin did not sleep.
He sat against the pillar by the fire, with the Ledger in his lap, and his strangers' hands on the cover, and read it.
All of it. From the beginning. READ THIS FIRST. Page after page, by the red light of the brazier, turning the pages slowly with the sides of his hands because his fingers would not do it. He had always read the first page every morning. He did not remember ever reading all of it. He read all of it now.
And she was everywhere.
That was what he had not understood, on the roof. On the roof he had read the first page and the line near the bottom. LYRA THORNE. PARTNER. And her letter, in the small quick hand. He had thought that was all. A line, and a letter.
It was not all.
She was on every page. Every page since the autumn. Not always her name. Sometimes just L. Sometimes she. Sometimes a line in the small quick hand, squeezed in at the edge of his square capitals, as if she had taken the pencil out of his fingers to add something he had got wrong. Page after page after page.
TUESDAY. THE INKWELL. L. CAME WITH A LETTER. SHE THINKS I CAN FIND HER FATHER. SHE IS PROBABLY WRONG.
THURSDAY. THE BARGE. L. TAPPED ON HER FATHER'S HAND: 4-2-9-3. HE TAPPED BACK ONCE. SHE DID NOT CRY UNTIL HE WAS ASLEEP.
FRIDAY. SHE SAYS I WALK LIKE A HERON. I SAID SHE TALKS LIKE A KETTLE. And under it, in the small quick hand: A kettle is useful. A heron just stands about.
SUNDAY. THE ARCHES. SHE CUT HER HAIR OFF WITH SILAS'S KNIFE AND SOLD IT FOR TWO CROWNS FOUR SHILLINGS FOR MY LEG. SHE SAID IT WILL GROW. I DID NOT SAY ANYTHING. I SHOULD HAVE.
MONDAY. I AM MORE AFRAID OF HER KNOWING THAN OF HER NOT KNOWING. RULE ONE. I TOLD HER.
He read them all.
He read them the way you read a book about somebody else. Somebody who lived a long time ago, in another country. He could see the man in the pages. A tall lame man with gray hands who walked like a heron and talked to a girl who talked like a kettle. Who was afraid, and told her anyway. Who wrote I should have and meant it. He could see him quite clearly. He liked him, a little.
He did not know him.
And the girl. He could see her too. In the pages. Quick and cross and brave and sharp-tongued, with her chin up. Cutting off her hair. Tapping on her father's hand. Taking the pencil out of a man's fingers to tell him a kettle is useful.
He did not know her either.
He read it all, and shut the Ledger, and sat with his hands on the cover. And looked across the brazier at the heap of blankets by the pillar, and the thin back in the burned gray gown, turned to the fire. Quite still.
She was not asleep. He could tell. Nobody lies that still when they are asleep.
It's still there, she had written. Under this. I promise.
He looked inside himself.
At the empty room. Where she had been. He went into it, the way he had gone into it on the roof, slowly, a strip at a time. It was not quite empty now. That was the strange thing. On the roof it had been empty. Swept and bare. Now there were things in it.
Not hers.
A fish market. Ice on the stones. A girl with a knife too big for her, gutting. A boy called Alistair behind a stack of crates. A ball at Ashcombe, and an old lord crossing the room to bow. A great house of glass, full of orange trees, warm in the winter. Shelves going into a cellar, one by one. A wicker basket on a doorstep, at night.
The Lady.
She had moved in. Eighty-one years of her. Into the room where the girl had been. She had come in through the door like a river into an empty valley, and found the room bare, and spread herself out in it. The way water spreads. Filling every corner. Not pushing anything out. There had been nothing to push.
He had made room.
He sat by the fire and looked at the Lady's memories in the empty room, and felt sick.
Boy.
The dry voice. Behind his left ear. Very quiet.
Boy. She's not in there. Don't look for her in there. You won't find her.
Where, then?
Malakor was quiet for a moment.
Nowhere, he said. Not in you. Not any more. I'm sorry. And then, after a moment, more quietly still: But she's in the book. And she's over there, on the blankets, pretending to sleep. That's two places. That's more than most people get.
Corin looked at the thin back in the gray gown.
Tell me one thing, he said. Inside. About her. Before. One thing I don't have.
Malakor did not answer for a long time.
Then he said: She argued with you. All the time. About everything. Which way to go. What to eat. Whether you were a fool. You were usually a fool. She was usually right. A pause. And you liked it. You liked being argued with. Nobody had ever bothered to argue with you before. They'd only ever been afraid of you, or sorry for you. She was never either. She just thought you were wrong, and said so.
Corin sat very still.
Was I happy? he said.
And the old dead harvester behind his left ear, who had never once in six years said a kind thing to him without taking it back in the next breath, said:
Sometimes. More than I ever was.
And did not take it back.
Corin opened the Ledger again. To the last page with writing on it. Her letter, from the roof. And under it, with the stub of pencil held clumsily between the white-wrapped ends of his black fingers, in square capitals that came out crooked because he could not feel the pencil, he wrote:
TUESDAY. SAINT BRIDE'S. MALAKOR SAYS SHE ARGUED WITH ME ABOUT EVERYTHING AND I LIKED IT. MALAKOR SAYS I WAS SOMETIMES HAPPY.
He looked at it.
Then he added, smaller:
I WANT TO KNOW WHAT THAT WAS LIKE.
And shut the book. And put his head back against the cold stone of the pillar. And looked at the fire.
Across the brazier, on the blankets, the thin back in the gray gown moved. Just once. As if somebody had let out a breath they had been holding a long time.
And then, at last, it was still in the way that sleeping people are still.
