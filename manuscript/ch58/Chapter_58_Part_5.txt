Folded Away
He picked up the clean sheet of paper.
Corin felt his hands do it. Long and pale, with the swollen knuckles and the two rings. They took the sheet off the desk and held it, flat, in the light of the brass lamp. Good paper. Thick and white and smooth, the Council's own, with a faint watermark of the city's arms in one corner if you held it to the light.
"You should go," said Lord Thorne. "Now. By the garden. Before they come."
"Yes."
"And take that." He nodded at the register. "Take it far away. Somewhere they've never heard of. And never come back to this house."
The visitor buckled the strap round the faded blue book, and put it back inside the wet cloak. And stood by the desk, in the lamplight, with the folded face turned toward him.
"What are you going to do?" said K.
Lord Thorne did not answer at once.
He was holding the sheet of paper. And Corin felt him think. Not fast, now. Slowly. Carefully. The way a man thinks who has made up his mind and is checking, one last time, that the knot is tied.
They'll take the evening. Whole. All of it. Everything K said. The register. Oakhaven. The Eighth. The vault. They'll take it out of me and put it in a box and keep it, and then they'll know I knew, and they'll know what I knew, and they'll know who told me.
Who told me.
That's the one thing they mustn't have.
Not K.
"Isolde taught me a thing," said Lord Thorne. "When we were first married. Before Lyra. A thing her people did, she said, where she came from, over the water. When there was something they couldn't let anybody take. A face. A name. A place. A secret." He turned the sheet of paper over in his hands. "They'd fold it."
The visitor did not move.
"You think of the thing," said Lord Thorne. "As hard as you can. All of it. And while you think of it, you fold. And with every fold, the thing goes into the paper, a little more, and out of you, a little more. Until it's all in the paper. And the paper's so small that nobody would look at it twice." He almost smiled. "Fold it small, she used to say. Fold it small, Aurelian, and let it go under."
"It burns," said K. "Folding. It burns as much as anything else."
"It burns," said Lord Thorne. "Yes."
He looked up at the folded face.
"But they're going to take the evening anyway," he said. "Tonight. Whole. So it doesn't matter what it costs me, does it? Not tonight. Whatever I burn tonight, they were going to take."
The visitor was very still.

Corin felt it.
He would not, afterward, be able to say what it was like. He tried, to the girl, in the crypt, and could not. He had burned things. He had burned more than most men alive. He had burned the word for his mother and his first tongue and three winters and the sky over the Bourse. He knew what burning was. It was a fire in you. It was something going up in your hand like paper, and the power coming, and the place where it had been going cold.
This was not that.
This was slower. This was a man sitting very still at a desk, thinking of one thing, one face, one name, one voice, as hard as he could, the way you hold a burning coal in your bare hand and do not let go. And folding. Once. The paper in half. And the thing he was thinking of, a little of it, going into the paper. Leaving him. He felt it go. A corner of a face. The sound of a laugh, years ago, in another house, by another fire.
Folding again.
Another corner. A voice, saying my lord. A hand on a garden door.
Again.
A name. The first letter of it. And then the rest.
Again.
And again.
And with every fold, it hurt more. Not in his hands. In his head. Behind his eyes. As if the paper were not being folded but his own mind, creased and pressed and creased again, along lines it had never been meant to bend along. And Corin felt it hurt, inside the man, and the man did not stop. He sat at his desk with the sweat running down his face in the lamplight and folded and folded, with his long pale hands steady, and his teeth shut, and did not make a sound.
And the folds got smaller. And smaller. Until the paper was no bigger than a thumbnail. Hard. Tight. White. So small that you could close your hand over it and nobody would know it was there.
And the face of the visitor was gone.
Corin felt it go. The last of it. Out of the man in the chair. Into the paper. And the man sat at his desk, breathing hard, with the sweat on his face, and tried to remember who had come to the garden door. And there was a fold there. A crease. A pale flat place where a face had been.
He knew somebody had come. He knew what they had told him. Oakhaven. The register. The Eighth. The vault. Five in the vault and one dead and one got out. He knew all of it.
