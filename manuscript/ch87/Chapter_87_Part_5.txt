Chapter 87: The Man Who Forgot His Sword, Part 5 of 5: The Iron Horse
The Iron Horse
Later, when the ring had broken up and the Ashborn had lain down where they were, one by one, with their packs under their heads, Oren found Lyra.
She was sitting a little way out on the glass, by herself, in the dark. Not far. Ten paces from the dome. Sitting with her knees drawn up and her bound hand in her lap and her face turned up to the stars. He could see her lips moving. Counting, he thought.
He went and sat down by her.
Slowly. It hurt his back. The sword on it knocked against the glass as he sat, and rang, a small ring that went out into the dark and did not come back. He sat beside her, not too close. The way the lad sat. A hand's width.
She stopped counting.
They sat a while.
"Lass," he said at last.
"Yes."
"The lad's book. What you read. Lyra has the cut." He said it carefully. He wanted to get it right. "What's the cut?"
She did not answer at once.
He did not hurry her. He had learned that, today, from her. You did not have to hurry. You could sit, and let a thing be, and it would come when it was ready.
"It's a stroke," she said at last. "With a sword. A family stroke. Your family's. It comes up from low down, by the heel, and round, like this." He felt her move, beside him in the dark. Her good hand, sweeping round and up, low to high, in a long curve. "It's called the Vance cut. Your father taught it to you. With the stick. For twelve years, till you got it right. And then you did it ten thousand times more."
Oren sat with that.
"The stick," he said.
"Yes."
"That's what the stick was for."
"Yes."
He looked down at his hands in the dark. He could not see them. Only feel them, lying on his knees. Big and rough. With the white lines across the knuckles that he could not see either, but could feel, a little, if he ran his thumb over them.
Twelve years. Ten thousand times.
And all of it gone, in one breath, through a pale hand on his wrist.
"And you have it," he said.
"Yes."
"How?"
She turned her head. He could feel her look at him, even in the dark.
"You showed me," she said. "In the shadow of the iron horse. Before the Ridges. You took me round behind it, where the others couldn't see, and you showed me. Slowly. Over and over. Until the sun went down. You put your hands on my shoulders and turned me, and you said Again, and No, and Again." She was quiet a moment. "So that if you lost it, somebody would still have it. That's what you said. Those were your words."
Oren did not say anything.
He sat on the black glass in the dark and tried to see it. The iron horse. He could see that, a little. A great black shape of iron, standing on the glass with its head up and one hoof lifted, like a statue in a square. He knew there was an iron horse. He had slept under it. He remembered the shade of it, and the smell of hot iron, and its great still belly over his head. And he tried to see himself behind it, with the girl, in the red light, turning her by the shoulders. Saying Again.
He could not.
There was nothing there. It had gone with the rest of it. With the stick and the twelve years and the ten thousand times. He could see the iron horse, standing, and he could see the shade behind it. And the shade was empty.
But she said he had done it.
And he believed her.
He believed her the way he believed the stars were there, now, above them, even though he was looking down at his hands. Because he knew her. Because she did not say things that were not so. Because she had stopped on the stair this afternoon rather than say a word in front of him that he had lost.
"So that if I lost it," said Oren slowly, "somebody would still have it."
"Yes."
"Then I knew."
She did not answer that.
"I must have," said Oren. "Mustn't I? Back then. Under the horse. I must have known it was coming. That somebody would take it. Or that I'd have to burn it. Or something." He shook his head slowly in the dark. "I don't know how I knew. But I knew. And I gave it to you, before it went."
"Yes," said Lyra. Very quietly.
Oren sat.
And he felt something come up in him that he did not have a word for. It was not the sickness of the afternoon, the thing he had kept down. It was something else. Warmer. Stranger. It was the feeling of finding out that a man you did not know had done you a kindness. And then finding out that the man was you.
"Lass," he said.
"Yes."
"Will you do something for me?"
"Yes."
"You don't know what it is yet."
"Yes," said Lyra.
And he laughed. A little. Quietly, so as not to wake the others.
"Do it every morning," he said. "The cut. Every morning, when we stop. Do it where I can see. Slow, the way I showed you. With your hands empty, if you have to, till that one's healed." He nodded at the pale shape of the bound hand in her lap. "I can't keep it in my hands anymore. But I can keep it in my eyes, maybe. If I see it every day. I'll know it's still there. I'll know where it is."
She did not answer for a long time.
Then she said: "Like the sword on your back."
Oren thought about that.
"Aye," he said. "Like the sword on my back. So I know where it is."
And beside him, in the dark, he felt her nod.
"Every morning," said Lyra.
"Every morning."
"And you watch. And if I do it wrong, you tell me."
Oren opened his mouth to say I won't know if it's wrong, lass. And shut it again. Because he did not know that. He did not know anything about it anymore. Maybe he would know. Maybe his eyes would know a thing his hands did not. Maybe, if she did it wrong, something in him would pull, the way a fish pulls at a line, and he would feel it.
He did not know.
"Aye," he said instead. "I'll tell you."
And they sat on the glass, the two of them, a hand's width apart, under the great wheel of the stars.
After a while Lyra began to count again. Under her breath. He heard her. And he did not ask her what she was counting. He thought he knew. He thought she was counting them. All of them, asleep by the dome. One by one. To be sure they were all still there.
He counted with her, in his head.
And when she got to the end, she said a number he did not catch, and stopped, and was quiet. And then she got up, and touched his shoulder once with her good hand, lightly, through his coat. And went back to the dome to sleep.
And Oren got up too, slowly, with his back hurting and the sword knocking on the glass. And went to the way in, and sat down on the sill.
To keep the watch.
The Watch
He took the watch anyway.
Nobody asked him to. Kestrel had said, when the dark came down, that Sef would sit the first part of the night and Oda the second. Oren had listened, and nodded, and then gone and sat down on the iron sill of the way in with his back against the edge of it and his legs stuck out in front of him on the glass. And nobody had told him to move. Sef had looked at him for a while, and then sat down a little way off, against the curve of the dome, and after a time Oren heard him start to snore.
So he had the watch.
He took the sword off his back. He untied the strip of hide at his chest and unwound it and let the sword come down into his hands, and laid it across his knees, the way it had lain all afternoon on the stair. It was a habit. He knew that much. He did not know where the habit came from or what it had been for. But it was his, and he let it be.
And he sat, and watched.
The Eye at night was not like the Eye by day.
By day it was a mirror. A great flat black mirror laid out under a white sky, throwing back the heat and the light so that you could hardly look at it. By night it was a hole. That was the only way he could think of it. The sky came down over it, black and clear, crowded with stars, more stars than he had ever seen anywhere, so many that they ran together in places into long pale smears like spilt flour. And the glass took the stars and laid them out underneath, so that the stars went down as well as up. Down and down. There was no edge to the glass at night. No shore. You could not see where the black glass stopped and the black sky began. It was all one thing, stars above and stars below, and the dome sitting in the middle of it, and him sitting in the way in of the dome, like a man sitting on the sill of a house at the bottom of the sea.
And it was so quiet.
He had known it was quiet. It had been quiet all day. But by day there had been the Ashborn moving about, and the voices, and the knock of spears, and the fight. At night there was nothing. No wind. No insects. No birds. No water moving anywhere. No hum under it, the way there was a hum under the rest of the Glass, the low sound you felt in your teeth all the time out there and only noticed when it stopped. Here it had stopped. The Eye was the only place where it stopped. And the quiet was so big and so deep that when Sef snored, a little way off, the sound seemed to go out across the glass and never come back.
Oren sat in it.
And after a while his hand went into his pocket.
The right-hand pocket, at the front, low down. The one with the patch. It went in and found the stone and brought it out, the same as before, without asking him. And he let it. He laid the blade along his thigh and set the stone on the edge, at the slant, and drew it down.
Sssssss.
The sound went out into the dark.
He did not need to sharpen it. He had sharpened it that afternoon, on the stair, as sharp as it would go. He knew that. It did not matter. He drew the stone down again, slowly, from the guard to the point. Sssssss. And again. Not pressing. Hardly touching. Just letting the stone go along the steel the way you let your hand go along the neck of a horse, in the dark, in a stable, to let it know you are there.
Sssssss.
And the sound, and the slow stroke of his hands, and the weight of the sword on his knees, and the quiet, all went together. And he found he was thinking.
Not the slow thinking of the afternoon. Not laying things out like tools on a cloth. Something easier. The way thoughts come when you are walking a long road by yourself and there is nothing to look at.
He thought about his father.
He could see him very clearly tonight. The big man at the anvil, with no hair on his arms. The hazel stick. The voice. He could not hear what the voice said. But he could hear the sound of it. Low. Rough. Not often kind. And yet there was something in it, when he listened, that he had never noticed before. Under the roughness. Something that might have been, if you squinted at it, a kind of fear. As if his father had been afraid for him. As if all those years with the stick had been a man trying, as hard as he knew how, to give his son something that would keep him alive.
Sssssss.
And it had, thought Oren. Whatever it was. It had kept him alive. Through the war. Through the mud and the rain. Through the morning at the way in, today, at dawn.
And now it was gone, and he was still alive anyway.
That was a strange thing. He turned it over in the dark. He had lost the thing his father gave him to keep him alive, and he had not died. He was sitting here. Breathing. Sharpening a sword. Thinking about it.
And the girl had it.
Lyra has the cut. That was what the lad had written. He knew what it meant now. Lyra had told him, out on the glass. It meant that the thing was not gone. Not altogether. It had gone out of him, and some of it had gone into the man in gray, but some of it had gone into her too, before. He had given it to her, under the iron horse. You showed me. In the shadow of the iron horse. So that if you lost it, somebody would still have it.
He had not remembered. He did not remember now. But he believed her.
And he had known. That was the thing he kept coming back to, in the dark.
Then, back then, under the iron horse, some part of him had known this was coming. Had looked ahead down the road and seen it. And had done the one thing a man can do, when he sees a thing coming that he cannot stop. He had put what he had into somebody else's hands, so that it would not be lost.
Sssssss.
He was glad.
He sat in the way in, under the stars, and was glad, and the gladness surprised him even more than the laughing had on the stair. It was not a big gladness. It did not fill him up. It was small, and quiet, and it sat in him like a coal in a pot, warm. But it was there.
He did not know how long he sat.
The stars went round. He watched them go. Slowly. The long pale smear of them, the spilt flour, swung across the sky from one side to the other, and the stars under the glass swung with it, the other way, so that it was like sitting in the middle of a great slow wheel. Once a star fell. A thin white line, very quick, from high up down toward the shore. And its twin under the glass fell up, to meet it, and they went out together at the edge.
He did not make a wish. He did not know if he believed in wishes. But he watched the place where it had been for a long time.
Behind him, inside the dome, somebody turned over in their sleep and muttered. Brin, he thought, or the child. Then quiet again.
He thought about the morning.
Tomorrow there would be the girl, doing the cut, slow, with her hands empty, on the glass in front of him. He would watch. He did not know yet what he would see. Maybe nothing. Maybe only a thin girl turning round in a strange way, like a scythe in long grass, the way it had looked to him on the glass this afternoon. Maybe that was all it would ever look like, now.
Or maybe not.
Maybe one morning, a long way from here, she would do it, and something in him would sit up. The way a dog sits up when it hears a step it knows on the road. Not because it remembers. Because something older than remembering knows.
He did not know which it would be.
But he would watch. Every morning. That much he could do. That much was still in his hands, or in his eyes, or wherever it was a man kept the things he had decided to do.
He drew the stone down the blade again, slowly, and listened to the sound go out across the glass.
It was a good sound. He had always liked it. He knew that now, sitting here. Of all the sounds a sword made, the ring of it, the hiss of it in the air, the knock of it on a shield, this was the one he had liked best. The quiet one. The one that came at the end of the day, by a fire, when the fighting was over, and a man sat and made his blade ready for whatever came next.
He had not known that about himself. Or he had known it, and forgotten.
He was glad to know it again.
The silent man was out there.
Oren could see him, a little. Not far off. Twenty paces, maybe, out on the glass, south of the dome. Sitting. The way he always sat, facing south, with his back to the Eye and his face to the far shore, toward the Glass and the way they had come. A dark shape against the stars. Not moving. He had been there since the dark came down. And by him, a smaller dark shape. Tansy. Lying curled on the glass, with her head against his leg, asleep.
Oren watched them.
He did not know what the man was looking at. Out there, to the south. There was nothing to see, at night. Only stars, and stars under the glass. But the man looked at it, every night, the same way. As if he were waiting for something to come up over the edge. Or for somebody.
Sssssss.
And then, behind him, down the stair, Oren heard the steps ring.
He stopped the stone.
Step. And the crutch. And step. Coming up out of the dark under the dome. Slow. Careful. The sound of a man who has not got all his body working the way it should.
The lad.
Oren did not turn round. He sat with the stone stopped on the blade, and listened to the lad come up. Ring, and knock, and ring. All the way from the bottom. A long way. He had not known the lad was down there. He had thought he was asleep with the others, round the front of the dome. But the steps came up and up out of the bunker, from the room at the bottom with the big map on the table, and they did not hurry.
And then they stopped, just behind him, at the top.
Oren looked round.
The lad was standing in the way in, a step back from the sill, in the shadow of it. Leaning on his crutch. And under his other arm, the living arm, he had a book. Not his own book. Not the brown one he wrote in every night. A different one. Bigger. Bound in black leather, with faded red edges, and swollen pages that stuck out a little, uneven, as if some of them had been folded and folded again.
The old man's book. The one from the iron box under the map table. Oren knew it. He had seen it lying on the table, at the bottom of the stair. The book nobody had taken.
The starlight came in at the way in and lay on the lad's face.
On half of it.
The right side was in the light, pale, almost silver, with the dark of the stubble on it and the eye open and bright and wet. Very wet. As if he had been crying, down there in the dark, or was about to. And the left side was gray, and the starlight lay on the gray and did nothing to it at all. It stayed gray. It stayed still. It looked, in that light, like the glass itself. Like the Eye. Like a piece of the Eye had come up and settled over half his face and gone cold there.
The lad looked at him.
And then past him, out at the stars, and the stars under the glass, and the silent man sitting with his back to them all, facing south.
Then back at Oren.
"He's talking," said the lad. "Malakor. He's been talking all night. Down there, by the table." He held the old book a little tighter under his arm. "He says it's time."
Oren did not say anything.
The lad took a breath. Oren saw it go in, and stop, and go out again. And then the lad said it, quietly, so quietly that it hardly went past the sill of the way in, out into the great dark quiet of the Eye.
"Malakor wants to open it," said the lad. "Tonight."
