Chapter 87: The Man Who Forgot His Sword, Part 4 of 5: Two Old Soldiers
Two Old Soldiers
After that, nobody said anything for a long time.
And then the lad came down.
One step. Step, and the crutch, and step. From where he had been, beside Lyra, down to the step Oren was on. And he sat. On Oren's other side. The right side. Not the side where the silent man was below. Close. So close that when he laid the crutch across his knees, the end of it rested against Oren's leg.
Oren looked at him.
The red light from the way in was coming in low now, almost level, so that it lay along the stair and up the curved wall behind them, and it caught the lad's face side-on. The right side. The living side. It made it look warm. Brown, and young, and tired, with the dark stubble coming through on the jaw, and the eye on that side bright and moving, looking back at Oren.
And the other side was in shadow.
The gray side. Oren could just see it. The dark smooth gray of it, coming up out of the lad's collar and over his jaw. In the red light it did not look like a face at all. It looked like a carving. A face that someone had begun to cut out of slate and stopped, half-done, and left to go cold.
The lad's left arm lay along his thigh. The hand at the end of it was gray. All of it. Gray to the wrist, and the fingertips black, the way they had been black for weeks. It lay on his leg, palm down, and did not move. It had not moved, Oren thought, all afternoon. Not once.
And the lad's right leg, the lame one, was stuck out stiff in front of him down the stair, because it would not bend the way the other one did.
Oren looked at all of it.
And then he looked at himself. Down at his own body, on the iron step, as if he were somebody else looking at it. A big man gone soft round the middle from too many years of not marching. A brown beard gone gray at the chin. A cut left hand, bound in a dirty rag. A back so bruised from being thrown against stone two days ago that he could not lean against the wall without it hurting. Seven holes in him where words used to be. And a sword on his knees that he could sharpen and could not use.
He looked at the lad again.
And the lad was looking at him. Doing the same thing, Oren thought. Looking at all of Oren. The rag, and the beard, and the sword, and the way he sat. The right side of the lad's mouth had gone a little crooked.
"Saints," said Oren. "Look at the pair of us."
The lad did not laugh. But the right side of his mouth went more crooked. And the eye on that side crinkled.
"Two old soldiers," said the lad.
Oren looked at him.
He looked at the young brown half of his face, in the red light. The dark stubble. The eye, bright.
"You're twenty-eight," said Oren.
The lad looked back at him.
"Not today," said the lad.
And Oren laughed.
It came up out of him before he could stop it. Not the ugly bark from before, when he cut his thumb. A real laugh. Deep down, from the belly, the way he had not laughed in a long time. It came up, and up, and he could not stop it. He sat on the iron step with the sword on his knees and laughed until his bruised back hurt, and he had to put his hand flat on the stair to hold himself up, and his eyes ran.
And the lad laughed too.
Only with half his face. The left side did not move at all. The gray corner of his mouth stayed where it was, still, straight, while the right side went up and up. It looked strange. It should have looked terrible. But it did not. It looked like the lad. It looked exactly like the lad, laughing.
They laughed together on the iron stair in the red light.
And behind them, on the step above, Lyra made a sound too, a small helpless snort, as if she had been trying very hard not to, and failed. And that set Oren off again.
Only the silent man, on the step below, did not laugh.
But he looked up. He looked up at the three of them, from his step, with his pale gray eyes. And watched them. For a long time. As if laughing were a thing he had heard of once, a long time ago, in a story, and had not thought to see.
It ran down in the end. Laughs do. Oren wiped his eyes on the back of his wrist and sat back, carefully, against the wall, and felt his back tell him about it. And breathed.
"Saints," he said. "Saints. I needed that, lad."
"So did I," said the lad.
They sat. The red light had gone darker. It was hardly a bar of light now. More a glow, coming in at the way in and lying along the top step, deep red, almost brown.
"You've lost more than me," said Oren after a while.
The lad did not answer.
"Don't tell me you haven't," said Oren. "I can see it. Your arm. Your face. And all the things inside you can't see. The things you put in that book every night." He nodded at it, on the lad's knees, under the crutch. "You've a page for me in there. Two pages, maybe, now. But you must have a hundred for yourself."
"More," said the lad quietly.
"Aye. More." Oren looked at him. "And you go on."
The lad was quiet.
"How?" said Oren.
It was not a thing he had meant to ask. It came out. And once it was out he found that he wanted the answer very much. More than he had wanted anything in a long time.
The lad thought about it. Oren could see him thinking. The right side of his face went still, the way it went when he was listening to something inside himself.
"I read the book," he said at last. "Every morning. Before I do anything else. I read it, and it tells me who I am. What I've lost. Who I've got." He paused. "And then I get up, and I do the next thing. Whatever it is. I don't think about the rest. I just do the next thing."
"The next thing," said Oren.
"Yes."
"And if you don't know what the next thing is?"
The lad looked at him with his one bright eye.
"Then I ask somebody," he said. "Lyra, mostly."
Behind them, Lyra made another small sound.
"Aye," said Oren. "Aye. That's sense." He nodded slowly. "That's good sense, lad."
And they sat a while longer.
And Oren noticed, after a time, that the lad had gone quiet in a different way. His head had tipped a little, to the left. The gray side. As if he were listening to something on that side, something very far off, or very small. His living hand had gone still on the brown book. His eye was looking at nothing.
Oren had seen him do it before. On the glass. In the Ridges. In the bunker, last night. A look as if somebody inside him had started to talk, and he had stopped to hear.
"Lad?" said Oren.
The lad blinked. His head came straight.
"Sorry," he said. "It's nothing."
But it was not nothing. Oren could see that. And he saw the lad's living hand close on the brown book, hard, the knuckles going white, and stay closed.
Carrying It
When the light was nearly gone, Oren went up.
He went up the steps slowly, one at a time, with his free hand on the warm iron rail, and the sword in the other. The lad came behind him, step and crutch and step. Lyra behind the lad. The silent man last, making no sound at all. They came up out of the dim of the stair into the way in, and out through it, and onto the glass.
And Oren stopped, and looked.
The Eye was going dark.
The sun had gone down behind the far rim of the world, out past the shore, and left the sky behind it the color of a bruise. Purple at the top, and a dull red lower down, and at the very bottom, along the edge, a thin line of orange, like the last of a fire under ash. And the black glass took all of it and laid it out flat, under their feet, so that it was like standing on a second sky. Purple and red and a line of orange, and their own shapes hanging upside down in it, black.
And under that, far down, deep in the glass, there was Applegarth.
Oren had not looked at it, at noon. He had not looked at anything but the man in gray. Now he looked down past his own boots, through the dark glass, and there it was. Very small, very far, the way a thing looks at the bottom of a deep well. The square, with the well in the middle of it, and the cart. The roofs. The little lane going off between the houses. All of it gray and still and drowned, and lit, faintly, from somewhere, by the last of the sky above.
He could not see the woman with the basket. It was too dark now. But he knew she was there. The lad had said so.
The Ashborn were round the front of the dome, in the long shadow of it, where the glass was cooler. They had made a camp of sorts. There was nothing to burn, so there was no fire, but somebody had laid out the packs in a ring, and the spears were stuck point-up in a heap of glass rubble, and they were sitting with their backs against the packs. Brin was lying flat out, snoring. Marrit had the child on her lap, asleep. Oda was rubbing her shoulder. Sef was singing, very low. And Kestrel was standing, the way she always stood, a little apart, with her spear, looking out across the glass toward the south, where the man in gray had gone.
She turned when Oren came out.
She did not say anything. She looked at him. Her lined gray face, in the last light, was hard to read. It always was. But Oren thought he saw something in it, and he thought he knew what it was. She had seen him stand up on the stair. Like a scarecrow. She had seen him sit down.
He went past her, to where his pack was. He had left it by the wall of the dome, that morning, before the siege. It was still there. He set the sword down on the glass beside it and knelt, and opened the pack, and felt about inside it.
He was looking for a strip of hide.
He did not know how he knew it was in there. His hands knew. They went into the pack and down past the rolled blanket and the empty water skin and the bag of hard bread, and found it at the bottom. A long strip of dark hide, soft and greasy, as long as his arm twice over and as wide as two fingers. He pulled it out. He did not know what it had been for. Something. Tying something.
It would do.
He stood up with it. He picked up the sword. He looked at the sword for a moment, and at the strip of hide.
And then he put the sword across his back.
Corner to corner. The grip up over his right shoulder, the guard by his ear. The blade going down slantwise across his back, so the point came out low by his left hip. He held it there with one hand behind him, against his coat, and with the other hand he passed the strip of hide round his chest. Under his left arm, over his right shoulder. Round the blade, twice, just below the guard. Back round his chest. Round the blade again, low down, near the point. And tied it off in front, at his breastbone, in a knot his hands knew how to make even if he did not.
He let go.
The sword stayed. He felt the weight of it settle against his back. On the bruises. It hurt. He did not care. He rolled his shoulders, and it moved with him, and stayed.
When he turned round, Kestrel was watching him.
She had come over. She stood a few paces off with her spear upright beside her, and looked at him, at the grip of the sword sticking up over his shoulder, and at the knot of hide on his chest.
"Why?" she said.
The Ashborn did not waste words. Kestrel least of all. Oren had got used to that. He liked it, mostly.
"Why carry it," said Kestrel, "if you can't use it?"
Oren thought about it.
It was a fair question. It was the question he would have asked, in her place. A sword was a heavy thing. They were a long way from anywhere. They had a long way still to go. Every pound a man carried on the Glass was a pound he had to carry all the way, in the heat, by night, with no water but what was on his back. A sword you could not use was only a weight.
"So I know where it is," he said.
Kestrel waited.
"I've had it a long time," said Oren. "I can feel that. If I put it down, here, on the glass, and walked away, I'd feel it. Like a hole. The same as the other holes. I've enough holes." He reached up and touched the grip, over his shoulder, with his fingertips. "This way, I know where it is. Even if I don't know what it's for."
Kestrel said nothing.
And Oren looked past her. To where Lyra stood by the way in, in the last of the light, with her bound hand held against her chest. Thin. Small. In a coat too big for her. With her bent sword in her other hand, her poor bent sword, notched in three places, with a bend in it above the guard.
"And because she might need it," he said.
He nodded at Lyra.
Kestrel turned her head and looked.
She looked at Lyra for a long moment. At the bent sword. At the bound hand. And then back at Oren.
And something in her face changed. Not much. A little. The hard lines round her mouth went a little less hard.
"Yes," said Kestrel. "She might."
And she turned, and went back to her place a little apart, and stood looking south again.
Oren stood where he was. He felt the sword on his back. He felt the weight of it, and the hurt of it on the bruises, and he found he did not mind. It was a good weight. It was a weight he knew.
And then somebody pulled at his sleeve.
He looked down.
It was the girl. Tansy. The small one, the one who talked to nobody, with her thin brown face and her eyes shut. Her eyes were shut a good deal of the time. She walked with them shut, and sang with them shut, and somehow she never fell over anything. She had come from somewhere. From the shore, he supposed. With the silent man. She was always where he was.
She was holding a cup.
A tin cup. A small one, dented, with a handle bent out of true. She held it in both hands, out in front of her, up toward him, very carefully, the way a child holds a thing she has been told not to spill. And it was full. Right to the brim. He could see the water in it, black in the dusk, trembling a little at the edge with the trembling of her hands.
"From the cistern," said Tansy. "Under the floor. I let the bucket down myself. Nobody helped." She held it up higher. "It's for you."
Oren looked at her.
He looked at the cup. At the small brown hands round it, holding it so carefully. At her shut eyes, and her serious face, turned up to his.
He was dry. He had been dry all afternoon. He had not known how dry until he saw the water.
He bent down, slowly, because of his back, and the sword on it. And took the cup. Carefully, with both hands, so that he would not spill it either. His big hands round her small ones, for a moment. And then round the cup.
And he drank.
It was cold. Colder than he had thought anything could be, out here. It tasted of stone, and of iron, and of old rain, and a little of the white ferns that grew down the walls of the cistern. It went down his throat like a blade going into its sheath. He drank it all. Slowly. Every drop. And when it was gone he held the cup and looked into it, at the wet bottom, and breathed.
Then he gave it back to her.
She took it with both hands.
"I can still carry water," Oren told her. He did not know why he said it. It was a foolish thing to say. But he wanted to tell her something, and that was what came. "I can't do much else, now, lass. But I can carry water. And I can stand. And I can sharpen a blade."
Tansy thought about this. Very seriously. With her eyes shut.
"Good," said Tansy. "Somebody has to."
And she went away with the cup, back toward the way in and the cistern, holding it out in front of her in both hands as if it were still full.
Supper
They ate in the dark.
There was not much. There was never much. The hard bread the Ashborn baked on hot stones, flat and gray and as tough as a boot sole, broken into pieces and passed round the ring. A little dried meat, gone black and stringy, that you had to chew a long time before it gave up anything. And water. That was the new thing. Water from the cistern, as much as anyone wanted, passed round in Tansy's dented tin cup and in two clay jars from the store room under the dome, and everybody drank and drank again, and nobody said anything about it, because there was nothing to say. It was water. It was enough.
Oren sat in the ring with the sword across his back.
He had not taken it off. He sat with his back to a pack, carefully, because of the bruises and the sword both, and chewed his bread, and looked round at them.
At Brin, who had woken up for supper and was eating lying down, on one elbow, groaning between mouthfuls. At Oda, beside him, tearing his bread into small pieces for him so he would not have to sit up. At Sef, who ate the way the Ashborn ate, slowly, every crumb, with his eyes half shut. At Marrit, feeding the child from her own piece, a bit at a time, soaked in water first to make it soft. At Kestrel, who sat a little apart even to eat. At the old one with the inked arms, Vespera, who sat nearest the way in, with her back to the dome and her knees drawn up, and did not eat much at all.
At the lad and Lyra, side by side. Not touching. A hand's width apart, the way they always were, now, out here on the Eye. Lyra was holding her bread in her left hand, because of the bound right, and eating it badly, and the lad was watching her do it with the living half of his face, and pretending not to.
And at the empty places.
Oren did not mean to count them. But he found he was doing it. The way he had counted Lyra's breaths on the stair. There was a gap in the ring, on the far side, between Sef and Marrit, where nobody sat. There had been nobody there last night, either. Or the night before. But on the nights before that, there had been somebody. A tall young one, with a laugh like a fox barking. Arran. He had a name for him, still. Arran. Under a heap of glass at the start of the short way.
And before that, an old man, with a hum.
And the old woman. Sola. His aunt.
He stopped chewing.
He sat with the bread in his mouth and thought about her. It was hard to think about. She had been the old woman in the dome, the one who threw him against the wall, who had been dead for longer than he had been alive, and who had been inside the lad's head, and come out through the lad's hands this morning and saved them all. And she had been his father's sister. He knew that. He had known it, somehow, at the front of the dome, when he cut his palm. Aunt. I'm here. He had said that. He could remember saying it.
And now she was gone. The lad had burned her. All of her. To save the girl, Tansy. And there was nothing left of her anywhere.
He had not known her. Not really. He had met her, if you could call it meeting, for a hand of days. She had thrown him at a wall.
But she had been his.
He swallowed the bread. It went down hard.
"Captain."
He looked up.
Vespera was looking at him across the ring. Her old face was a pale shape in the starlight, with the dark lines of it like cracks in a plate. She did not call him Captain often. Nobody did, out here.
"Come here," she said. "I want to look at you."
He got up. It took a while. He went round the ring, past Brin's legs and Oda's back, and sat down again beside her, against the curve of the dome. The stone was still warm from the day. He felt it through his coat, and through the sword, on the bruises.
Vespera looked at him.
She looked at him for a long time, the way she looked at the lad. Reading him. He let her. He did not know what she was looking for. But he had seen her look at the lad like that, every night and every morning, and he had seen the lad sit still for it, and he thought he could sit still for it too.
"Say your words," said Vespera.
He did not understand.
"The ones you've still got," said Vespera. "Not the holes. I know the holes. The lad's got them written down, and I've read them. Say me the ones next to the holes. The words that are still there." She paused. "I want to know they haven't gone thin."
Oren thought about it.
It was a strange thing to ask. But he thought he saw what she meant. When a word went, it did not go clean. He had found that. It took a little of the words beside it with it, sometimes. Pulled at them. Like a stone pulled out of a wall, that loosens the stones on either side.
"Rope," he said slowly. "Bread. Morning. Horse. Lad. Lass."
Those were the ones that had crowded into him, after the first ones went. He did not know why those. They had come, and stayed, and he held on to them.
"Good," said Vespera. "More."
"Step," said Oren. "Wall. Water. Stone. Cup. Sword." He stopped. "Sword's still there. I can say it. I just can't do it."
"I know," said Vespera. "Go on."
"Way in," said Oren. "That's two words. But it's what I've got." He thought. "Sit. The thing you sit on. That's another one with a hole in it. The hard cold. The white flowers. The round things the boys beat. The little one for soup. The shut." He stopped again. "They're all holes, those. With words round them, like a fence round a pond."
"Yes," said Vespera. "That's how it's done. You build a fence round the hole, and then you don't fall in." She nodded. "You've built good fences, Captain. Most men don't. Most men fall in, the first one, and keep falling."
She put out her hand.
She put her old thin hand on his, on his knee. On the back of it. On the old white lines across the knuckles.
"And these," she said. "Do you know what these are?"
He looked down at them.
"No," he said. "I wondered. On the stair. This afternoon. I never wondered before."
"A stick," said Vespera. "Your father's stick. Every time your hands went wrong on the grip. For years." She traced one of the white lines with the tip of her finger. Slowly. From one side of his hand to the other. "You told the girl so yourself, under the iron horse, and she told me, the night after. You said you hated him for it, for a long time, and then you didn't."
Oren looked at the white line under her finger.
"Why didn't I?" he said.
"Because it kept you alive," said Vespera. "That's what you said. You said it kept you alive, and you'd come to think that was what he meant it to do. And then you said you'd give it to the girl, so she'd have it too. And you did."
Oren said nothing.
He sat with the old woman's hand on his, and looked at the white lines, and did not say anything for a long time. And the ring of people round the dark camp ate their bread, and drank their water, and the child fell asleep again, and Brin groaned, and above them all the stars came out and out until the sky was white with them.
"I don't remember saying that," said Oren at last.
"No," said Vespera. "I know. That's why I'm telling you."
She took her hand away.
"Somebody has to remember it for you," she said. "That's all the lad's book is. That's all any of it is. Somebody holding on to the thing, so it isn't lost, until the man it belongs to can come and get it back." She looked at him with her old pale eyes. "Or until he can't, and it has to go to somebody else."
Oren nodded slowly.
"The girl," he said.
"The girl," said Vespera.
And she leaned her head back against the warm stone of the dome, and shut her eyes, and that was all she said.
