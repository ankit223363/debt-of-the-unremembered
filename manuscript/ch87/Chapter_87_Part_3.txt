Chapter 87: The Man Who Forgot His Sword, Part 3 of 5: The Page
The Page
The lad came down later.
Oren heard him before he saw him, because the lad was not like the silent man. The lad made a noise on the iron. Step, and the crutch, and step. Step, and the crutch, and step. A ring and a knock and a ring, coming down from the bright square of the way in. Slow. Careful. The sound of a man who has not got all his body working the way it should and has learned to go carefully because of it.
Oren looked up.
The lad was a black shape against the light at first, the same as all of them. Thin. Leaning on the crutch, with his left arm hanging. Then he came down out of the light and into the dim, past Lyra, and Oren could see him.
And he could see the face.
He had seen it before. He had seen it this morning, at the front of the dome, after the old woman went. He had seen it on the glass at noon. But he had not looked at it properly. There had been too much else. Now he looked at it, in the dim cool light on the iron stair, and something in his chest went tight and stayed tight.
The left side of the lad's face was gray.
Not pale. Not white. Gray. The gray of the glass at night, Tansy had said, and she was right. A dark, smooth, dead gray, like slate, like the skin of a fish that has been lying on the bank a long time. It came up from under his collar, up the left side of his neck, under his left ear, over the jaw. Across the cheek. All the way to the corner of his mouth, where it stopped, in a line. And it did not move. When the lad's face moved, when he frowned, or opened his mouth, only the right side moved. The left side stayed still. Smooth. Like a mask that had been put on half his face and could not be got off.
"Oren," said the lad.
"Lad," said Oren.
The lad looked down at the two of them, Oren and the silent man, on their two steps with their forearms on their knees. Something went across the right side of his face. Oren did not know what. It was there and gone.
"Can I sit?" said the lad.
"It's not my stair," said Oren.
The lad sat. On the step above Oren, by Lyra. He did it slowly, the way a man sits who is all bones and aches. He laid the crutch along the step beside him. He had a book under his arm. Oren had not seen it until he sat. A thick book with a brown cover, cracked and soft and dark at the corners from being held. The lad's book. The one he wrote in.
The lad held it on his knees.
"Lyra says you've been sitting here a long time," he said.
"Aye," said Oren. "It's cool."
"Yes."
They sat. The four of them now. On the iron stair, in the dim light, with the bar of gold coming down from the way in and lying across the top steps, lower now, and redder. The afternoon was getting on.
And Oren said, after a while: "Your book, lad."
The lad looked down at it.
"The one you write in," said Oren. "The things you've lost. You put them in there, don't you? So you'll know. In the morning. When you wake."
"Yes."
"You've a page in there for me." Oren did not look round. "I remember that. You told me once. When the words started going. You said you'd keep a page. For the ones I lost. So somebody would know."
The lad was quiet a moment.
"Yes," he said. "I keep it."
"What's on it?"
The lad opened the book. Oren heard the pages go. Soft and dry, like leaves. Then they stopped.
"It says SPENT, at the top," said the lad. "OREN VANCE. SPENT. In big letters. And then the words. One under the other." He paused. Oren heard him breathe. "Do you want me to read them?"
Oren thought about it.
"No," he said. "No. I know them. I can feel where they were." He turned the sword a little on his knees. "I want you to write some more."
The lad did not say anything.
"Two more," said Oren. "Two more things. Write them down for me. Will you?"
He heard the lad move. A small sound of cloth. And then a scratching. The lad had a stub of pencil; Oren had seen him use it. Gray. Short. He held it in his right hand, his living hand, because the left was no good for it now. Oren heard it scratch on the page.
"Go on," said the lad.
Oren looked at the bright square of the way in.
"The first one," he said. "The iron thing. At the front of the dome. The thing you open, to get in. The thing I opened with my blood. And held, with my back, when they came at us." He stopped. He could feel the word. He could feel exactly where it was, the hole where it should be. It was right there, like a stone in his boot. And it would not come. "The word for that," he said. "I can't say it. I can't think it. I burned it, didn't I? Yesterday. Or the day before."
"This morning," said the lad quietly. "At dawn. At the siege. You burned it to hold it shut."
"Aye." Oren nodded slowly. "This morning. Saints. It feels a week. Write that."
The pencil scratched.
"And the other," said Oren.
He looked down at the sword.
"The sword," he said. "Not the sword. I've got the sword. Write the other thing. What I did with it. What the man in gray took. On the glass. At noon." He was quiet for a moment. "I don't know what to call it, lad. That's the trouble. I don't know what it was. You'll have to find the word for it. You'll know."
The pencil did not scratch for a long moment.
Then it did. Slowly. A long time. Longer than for the first thing. Oren listened to it go, and stop, and go again, and stop. As if the lad were thinking hard about every letter.
Then it stopped for good.
"Read it," said Oren. "Not you, lad. Lass. You read it. You've a better voice for it."
He heard Lyra move. He heard the book go from one pair of hands to another.
And Lyra read it.
She read it slowly. Her voice was low and clear and steady, and it did not shake. She read it the way you read a thing out in a church. Each word laid down on its own.
"THE WAY IN," Lyra read. "HE CANNOT SAY THE WORD."
A pause.
"AND THE SWORD. KROSS TOOK IT IN THE EYE. LYRA HAS THE CUT."
The stair was very quiet.
Oren sat with it. He let it go into him, the way you let a cold drink go down, slowly. The way in. He cannot say the word. And the sword. Kross took it in the Eye. Lyra has the cut.
Kross. That was the man in gray. He had not known the name. Or he had known it, and it had gone. Now he had it again. Kross.
And: Lyra has the cut.
He did not know what that meant. Not all of it. But he knew it was good. He could hear that it was good, from the way she read it. Her voice had gone different on those four words. Lower. As if she were saying them to herself. As if they were a thing she was holding, carefully, in both hands.
He nodded.
"That's right," he said. "That's all of it."
Nobody said anything.
"That's all of it," said Oren again. "Thank you, lad. Thank you, lass."
And he heard the book close. A soft, flat sound. And the lad take it back, and hold it on his knees again.
And on the step below him the silent man had not moved at all. But his head had turned, a little, while Lyra read. Toward her voice. And stayed turned, and listened, until she stopped.
What Am I
The bar of light had gone red.
Oren watched it. It lay across the top two steps now, not three, and it was not gold anymore. It was the red of a coal at the back of a fire, when the flames have gone down and only the heat is left. The dust in it had stopped turning. Or he could not see it turn. The light was too low and too thick.
Outside, the Ashborn had gone quiet again. Not the bad quiet of before, when they had all stopped to watch him stand up like a scarecrow. A tired quiet. The quiet of people at the end of a long day, sitting down, with their backs against something, too tired to talk. He could hear somebody snoring. Brin, he thought. It sounded like Brin. A long slow rattle, and a whistle, and a rattle.
He sat with the sword on his knees.
And a thing came up in him that he did not want. He had been keeping it down all afternoon. Since noon. Since the man in gray stepped back from him on the glass, and he looked down and saw his own hands on the grip, in the wrong place. He had felt it there, under everything, the whole time, the way you feel a sickness coming on before you know you are sick. He had kept it down with sitting, and with Lyra's quiet, and with the stone, and with the silent man on the step below.
Now it came up anyway.
"Lass," he said.
She was behind him, on the step above. He heard her move.
"What am I?" said Oren.
He did not turn round. He said it to the red light. To the rust on the curved wall. To the dark below.
"Without it, I mean," he said. "Without the thing he took. I've been sitting here trying to think. All afternoon. What I am." He turned the sword over on his knees, slowly. "I was a soldier. I know that. I was a captain. Men called me Captain. But a soldier is a man who can fight, isn't he? And a captain is a man who can show other men how. That's all a soldier is. That's what the word means." He looked at the bright edge. "And I can't. I can't stand in the first guard. I can't think which end to hold. So I'm not that. Not anymore."
Nobody said anything.
"I'm not a smith," said Oren. "I never was. My father wanted me to be. I know that. I can remember him wanting it. But I went for a soldier instead. I can remember that, too. Him standing in the yard, and me walking away up the road, and not looking back." He was quiet a moment. "And now I can't be a soldier. And I'm too old to learn to be a smith."
He heard the lad shift, on the step behind. The crutch knocked on the iron.
"I'm a man who can sharpen a blade," said Oren. "That's what I am now. I've worked it out. A man who can sharpen a blade, and carry things, and say lad and lass, and not much else." He laughed, short. "Saints. You could get a boy to do all that for a penny a day."
The red light lay on the steps.
And behind him, Lyra said: "You're the one who stands in the—"
And stopped.
Oren heard her stop. He heard the word she had been about to say. He did not hear it, exactly. He could not hear it; it was gone out of him, and when it went past his ears now it was only a sound, like a word in a language he did not know. But he heard the shape of the place where it would have gone. He heard her mouth start to make it, and close on it, and stop, the way your hand stops when you reach for a cup and see in time that it is too hot to touch.
She had been going to say the word he could not say.
And she had stopped, because it was his, and he had lost it, and she would not say it in front of him.
He sat very still.
And he found that his eyes had gone hot. Not his face, this time. His eyes. Hot and stinging, the way they had not gone since he was a boy and his mother died. He blinked. He looked at the red light on the iron steps until it stopped swimming.
She had stopped. For him.
"In the way in," said Oren.
He said it for her. Quietly. Into the dim.
"That's what you were going to say, lass. Isn't it? The one who stands in the way in."
Lyra did not answer. But he heard her breathe out. A long breath. Shaking a little at the end.
"Aye," said Oren.
He thought about it.
He thought about it the slow way. Laying it out. He thought about the front of the dome, this morning, at dawn, when they came at him. Men in gray. And iron men, with springs. Coming at the way in, out of the half-dark, across the glass. And him in it. In the way in. With his back against the iron thing that he had opened with his own blood, and his feet set, and his sword up. Not letting them by. He could remember that. He could remember it very clearly, more clearly than he could remember almost anything else from the morning. The weight of the iron thing against his back, and the cold of it, through his coat. And the men coming. And the word, the word for the iron thing, going out of him like a breath, burning, so that he could hold it shut for one more moment with the last of himself.
He had held it.
He had stood in the way in and held it, and they had not got by.
And he thought: that wasn't the sword.
That was the strange thing. He turned it over, and over, and looked at it from every side, and it was true. The sword had been in his hand, this morning, at the way in. He had used it, he supposed. He did not know how, now. But the sword was not what had held the way in. He had held the way in. His back. His feet. His weight. Him, standing there. Not moving. Not letting them by.
The man in gray had taken the sword.
He had not taken that.
"Aye," said Oren again, slowly. "That's right. I'm the one who stands in the way in."
He looked down at his hands on the sword. Big and brown and scarred. With the old white lines across the knuckles, from the hazel stick, from the years and years of whatever it was he had done with them, that his father had hit them for.
And he lifted them off the blade, and put them flat on his knees, on either side of it, and pressed down. And stood up.
Not the way he had stood up before, to try the guard. Not quickly, to prove something. Slowly. The way a man stands up at the end of a long day, because there is still a thing to be done. He put his weight on his feet. He felt the iron step under his boots, hard and warm, with its little raised squares. He felt his knees take it. His back. His shoulders. All of him, going up, from the feet.
And he stood there on the iron stair, with the sword hanging from one hand, point down, and the red light on the steps above him, and he did not try to do anything with the sword at all.
He just stood.
And he could do that. He found that he could do that very well. His body knew it. It had not known the guard, but it knew this. Feet apart, a little. Knees soft. Weight low, down in the heels, where nobody could move it. Shoulders square to the way in. It was not a guard. It was not a stroke. It did not have a name. It was only a big man, standing in a narrow place, so that nothing could get past him.
His body knew it the way his hands knew the stone.
"I can still stand," said Oren.
He said it to the way in. To the red square of light at the top of the steps. To whatever was out there.
"That's left, too," he said. "I can still stand."
And behind him, on the step above, he heard Lyra make a small sound. Not a word. He did not turn round. He thought she would not want him to.
And on the step below him, the silent man looked up.
Oren saw it out of the side of his eye. The pale gray eyes came up from the iron between the man's boots and went to Oren, standing. To his feet. His knees. The set of his shoulders. They went over him slowly, from the boots up, the way a man looks at a wall to see if it is straight.
And then, very slowly, the man stood up too.
He stood on his own step, below Oren, with his wrapped hands hanging. His feet went apart a little. His knees went soft. His weight went down. His shoulders came square to the way in.
Two big men, standing on an iron stair, one above the other, in a narrow place, in the red light.
Not doing anything.
Just standing.
The Man in Gray
After a while they sat down again. You could not stand on a stair all evening. Oren sat first, slowly, with the sword across his knees, and the silent man a moment after him, on the step below, forearm on knee. And when they had sat, Oren thought about the man in gray.
Kross. He had the name now. The lad had written it, and Lyra had read it, and he had it. He turned it over. Kross. A short hard name, like a stone. He thought it suited the man.
He could see him, if he tried. That was strange too. He could not see the fight. He could not see the thing he had done with the sword, at noon, out on the glass, or the thing the man had done back. That was all inside the hole. But he could see the man's face, afterward. Very clearly. Crouched in front of him on the black glass, a pace away, so their eyes were level. A thin face. Tired. Wet with sweat, gray at the mouth. Short hair going gray at the roots. And the eyes. Gray, like the coat. Careful eyes. Eyes that did not look away.
I'm sorry, Captain.
Oren heard it again. Low. Not the voice a man uses when he wants you to hear him being sorry. The other voice. The one he uses when he is sorry whether you hear it or not.
It had to be seen.
He had not understood it, at the time. He did not understand it now. He sat on the iron step and tried.
Seen by who?
Not by him. He had seen nothing. He had been on his knee on the glass looking at his own hands, and none of it had meant anything. Not by the girl, either, or the lad, or the Ashborn. They had all seen it. But they did not need it to be seen. Nobody on the Eye needed it.
The pale one, then. The one with the book, who had fallen on the steps. Oren had heard the Ashborn talking about him, while he sat in the shade, after. How he wrote everything down. How he had stood on the glass all morning, writing. How he had gone down the steps for the old man's book, and not come up, and the man in gray had come up instead, wiping something on his glove.
Oren looked down the stair. Into the dark below the seventh step. Where the light gave out.
The pale one had fallen down there. In the dark. A long way.
A great pity, the man in gray had said. Lyra had told him that. She had told it in a flat voice, with nothing in it, and then she had looked at him, and he had seen in her face that there was more to it than the words. A good deal more. And that she was not going to tell him the rest, because she did not know it either, and she did not want to guess.
Oren thought about it.
He thought he could guess.
He had been a soldier. He had seen men fall in the dark, in the war, who had not fallen. Officers, mostly. The kind who walked about behind the lines with little books, writing down the names of men who did not salute fast enough, or said the wrong thing at the fire. Some of those had fallen. Into ditches. Into rivers. Off the backs of carts. And nobody asked very hard how. And the next morning there was a little less writing down of names.
He did not know if that was what had happened here. But it had the same shape. He could feel the shape of it, the way he could feel the shape of the holes in himself.
So the man in gray had done that. Down there in the dark. To one of his own.
And before that, out on the glass, he had stood in front of the girl, with his hand bare, and could have taken the thing out of her too. Lyra had told him that part as well. He could have taken it. He looked at me. And he didn't. He shut his hand. She had said it in a puzzled voice, as if it were a sum she could not get to come out even.
And before that, he had taken it out of Oren.
Oren sat with all of it, laid out. The way he laid things out now.
A man who took the thing from him. And said sorry, and meant it.
A man who could have taken it from the girl. And did not.
A man who went down a dark stair after one of his own, and came up alone.
It did not come out even. Lyra was right. It was like a cart with three wheels that all turned different ways. You looked at it, and you could not see how it went along the road at all. And yet it did. It had gone along the road all the way across the Glass, behind them, for a week. And now it had turned round and gone back.
He found that he did not hate the man.
He looked for it. He thought he ought to. He thought a man ought to hate the one who took twenty years of his life out of his hands in one breath. He looked inside himself for the hate, the way he had looked for the guard, and it was not there either. There was a hole where the thing had been taken. And next to the hole, where the hate should be, there was only the face. Crouched in front of him. Tired. Wet. Gray at the mouth.
And the voice. I'm sorry, Captain.
He had heard men say sorry in the war. A great many of them. Men who had done things they had been told to do, and were sorry after. Most of them had said it the way you say a thing to get it off your hands, like wiping mud on the grass.
The man in gray had not said it like that.
He had said it like a man picking something up. Something heavy. To carry.
"Lad," said Oren.
The lad, on the step behind him, moved.
"The man in gray," said Oren. "Kross. Do you know him?"
The lad was quiet a long time.
"A little," he said at last. "Not as much as he knows me."
"Is he a bad man?"
The lad did not answer that for even longer.
"I don't know," he said. "I used to think so. Now I don't know." A pause. "He told me to go where I'm needed. And be quick."
Oren thought about that.
"That's what a captain says," he said slowly. "To a man he's sending off. On his own. Somewhere bad." He frowned. "That's not what you say to a man you're hunting."
"No," said the lad.
And nobody said anything after that, for a long time. And on the step below, the silent man sat with his head down. And did not seem to be listening. But Oren had begun to think, today, that he was always listening. That he heard everything. And that it all went into him, down and down, the way the gray thread had gone into him out on the glass, and found no floor.
