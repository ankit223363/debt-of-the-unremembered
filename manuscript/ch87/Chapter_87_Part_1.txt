Chapter 87: The Man Who Forgot His Sword, Part 1 of 5: Which End
Which End
Oren knew it was his sword, and he knew it was a sword, and he had to look down to find out which end his hand should go on.
That was the first thing.
He was sitting on the iron steps inside the dome, a little way down from the top, where the light from the way in still reached. It was cooler there. That was why he had come. Out on the glass the afternoon had turned into a furnace, the white sky pressing down and the black glass throwing it all back up again, so that a man walking across it felt cooked from both sides like a loaf in an oven. In here it was dim, and the air smelled of old iron and old stone and dust, and the steps under him were warm but not hot.
The steps went down below him into the dark. Iron, every one of them. Each one was a plate of black metal as wide as a man's two arms held out, with a pattern of little raised squares on it so your boots would not slip. They were bolted at each end into the curved stone wall, big round bolt-heads as wide as his thumb, rusted orange. The rust had run down from the bolts in long streaks, the way it ran down the outside of the dome, so that the whole stair looked as if it had been weeping for a long time. Below the seventh step or so the light gave out. After that there was only the sound of the steps, if you went down them. A ring, and a ring, and a ring, going away into the black.
He did not want to go down them.
There was a man at the bottom of them. Or there had been. They had carried him up. Oren had watched them carry him up, from where he sat in the shade outside: two men in gray and a third, with a long thing in a canvas sheet between them, and a gray boot showing at one end. He did not know who the man was. He had not asked. Lyra had told him, a little, in a low voice, and he had nodded, and then it had slid away from him, the way water slides off a slate.
He sat with the sword across his knees.
It was a long sword. Longer than his arm. The blade was a hand wide at the top, near the guard, and went down narrower and narrower to the point, and down the middle of it ran a groove, a straight shallow channel the width of his little finger. The steel was gray, darker near the guard and lighter near the point, and there were scratches in it, hundreds of fine scratches going every way, the way there are scratches in the bottom of an old pan. Near the point, on one edge, there was a notch. A little bite out of the steel, the size of a pea. He had put his thumb in it, a while ago, and it fitted.
The guard was a plain bar of black iron, straight across, with the ends turned down a little. The grip was wood, bound with leather. The leather was dark brown, almost black, and worn so smooth and shiny in two places that you could see where the hands went. A place high up, just under the guard. And a place low down, near the round iron knob at the end.
He looked at those two shiny places for a long time.
He knew they were where the hands went. You could see that. Anybody could see that. Twenty years of hands had worn them shiny, the way the step in front of a farmhouse gets worn in the middle where everybody treads. They were his hands. He knew that too. He knew it the way you know the smell of your own coat. He could feel it, almost, if he held his palm a little way above the leather without touching: a kind of warmth, a kind of fit, as if the grip were waiting for him.
But which hand went where?
He did not know.
That was the thing. He sat on the warm iron step in the dim light with the sword across his knees and he did not know. The right one high, under the guard? Or the left one? Or did they go the other way round, the right low and the left high? Or did they go together, both in the middle, the way you hold a spade?
He tried the spade way. That was the way his hands went, if he let them. Both together. In the middle. Not on either of the shiny places. On the plain dull leather between them, where nobody's hand had been for twenty years. His hands went there, and closed, and held, and it felt wrong. He knew it felt wrong. The way a boot on the wrong foot feels wrong. It held, but it did not fit.
He let go.
He sat and looked at the sword some more.
It was not that he had forgotten the sword. He wanted to be clear about that, in his own head. He sat there and went over it, slowly, the way he went over things now, since the words had begun to go. Laying each thing down in front of himself, one at a time, like a man laying out tools on a cloth.
He knew it was his.
He knew where it had come from. His father's forge, in the yard behind the smithy at Haverstock, where he was born. He could see the forge. The black stone of it, and the bellows with the cracked leather, and the anvil standing on its block of oak. He could see his father, a big man with no hair on his arms because the sparks had burned it all off, hammering. He could hear the hammer. He could smell the hot iron and the coal smoke. He could see the blade coming out of the fire, orange, and going into the trough, and the steam going up with a scream.
He knew all that.
He knew he had carried it in the war. He could see the war, too. Some of it. The mud, mostly. The mud and the rain, and the long brown lines of men walking, and the tents in the fields at night with the fires in front of them. The bad bread. The men he was with, their faces, their names: Penner and Tolley and the two Breck brothers and little Dace, who could not have been more than sixteen. He could see the sword in his hand, in all of it. He could see it hanging at his side when he walked. Lying across his knees by the fire. Being wiped with an oily rag at night.
But in his hand?
Doing what?
There was a hole there.
He sat and looked at the hole. He could feel the edges of it, the way you can feel with your tongue the place where a tooth used to be. It was a big hole. Bigger than the holes where the words had gone. The words had left holes too, small ones, six of them, and then a seventh this morning, or yesterday, he was not sure, when he had burned the word for the iron thing at the front of the dome. The way in. He knew those holes. He had got used to them. He could walk round them. When he came to one, he found another way to say a thing, and he went on, and nobody much noticed.
This one he could not walk round.
This one was in the middle of everything. It was not a word. It was twenty years. He could feel the shape of them, all round the edges of the hole: the forge at one end, his father's voice, and the war at the other end, the mud and the rain. And between them, where there should have been something, there was nothing.
He knew there had been a stick.
That was strange. He knew that. A stick. A long thin hazel stick, peeled white. His father had held it. And it had come down, sometimes, across his knuckles. Hard. He could remember the sting of it. He could remember looking down at his knuckles afterward, with a red line across them, and blowing on them. Over and over. For years.
But he could not remember why.
What had he been doing, with his hands, that his father hit them with a stick?
He did not know.
He sat on the iron step and looked at the two shiny places on the grip, and then at his own two hands, lying on his knees on either side of the blade. Big hands. Brown, and scarred, and rough. With a cut across the left palm, under the dirty strip of cloth, where he had opened it with his own knife at the front of the dome to make the iron thing let them in. The knuckles were big and knotted. There were old white lines across the backs of them. Lots of old white lines. He had never wondered what they were from.
He wondered now.
And from somewhere above him, outside, out in the white heat on the glass, he heard a voice say, quietly, "Oren?"
It was Lyra.
He looked up. She was standing in the bright square of the way in, at the top of the steps, with the light behind her, so that he could only see the shape of her. Thin. Small. A girl's shape, in a coat too big for her, with her right hand held against her chest, wrapped in white.
"Aye," he said. "Here, lass."
She did not come down. She stood at the top and looked at him.
"Are you all right?"
He thought about it. He did not want to say yes too quickly. She would know. She always knew. She had a way of looking at a man that went right through his face to the back of his head.
"I'm sitting," he said at last. "It's cool, in here. That's all."
She was quiet.
"I'm looking at my sword," he said.
And then, because she was still quiet, and because she was Lyra, and because there did not seem to be any point in not saying it, he said: "I can't think which end you hold, lass. Isn't that a thing? Twenty years. I can't think which end."
The First Guard
Lyra came down three steps and sat on the fourth from the top, above him, so that her boots were by his shoulder.
She did not say anything.
Oren waited for her to say something. He waited for her to say It'll come back, or Don't think about it, or You're tired, that's all. The things people say. He would not have minded. He would have nodded and said Aye, and they would both have known it was only a thing people say, and it would have been all right.
She did not say any of them.
She sat on the step above him with her bound hand in her lap, and looked down past him at the sword across his knees, and said nothing at all.
After a while he found that he was glad.
He sat with the sword. She sat with him. Out on the glass, through the bright square of the way in, he could hear the Ashborn moving about. Kestrel's voice, low. A spear butt knocking on the glass. Brin, groaning as he turned over. Somebody singing a few notes and stopping. It was all very far off. In here it was dim and cool and quiet, and the rust on the walls was the color of dried blood in the gray light, and the steps went down into the dark below them, and he could hear Lyra breathing.
And then he thought: I'll try.
He did not know where the thought came from. It came up in him the way a bubble comes up in a pond, from somewhere down in the mud. I'll try it. Just once. Just to see. It was a stubborn thought. It was the kind of thought his father used to have. He could almost hear it in his father's voice.
There was a guard. He knew that much. There was a first guard, the one a boy learned before he learned anything else. The one you stood in before you did anything. He had taught it. He knew he had taught it. He could see the boys he had taught it to. A long line of them, in a muddy yard, in the rain, in their new gray coats with the buttons still bright, holding swords that were too heavy for them. A hundred of them. More. Farm boys, and baker's boys, and boys from the city with soft hands. All of them standing in a line, holding their swords out, and him walking up and down the line in front of them.
He could see himself doing it.
He could see himself stopping in front of a boy, a thin boy with red ears, and taking hold of the boy's elbow, and moving it. And the boy's foot. Kicking it, gently, with the side of his own boot, so it went where it should. And saying something. He could not hear what. His own mouth moving, in the picture in his head, and no sound coming out.
The first guard.
He stood up.
He did it before he could think better of it. He put one hand on the warm iron rail at the edge of the steps, the one that ran down along the curve of the wall, and pulled himself up. His knees cracked. His back hurt, low down, where the old woman had thrown him against the stone two days ago. He stood on the iron step with the sword in his other hand, hanging, the point down by his boot.
And he tried.
He tried to stand the way the boys had stood, in the muddy yard, in the picture in his head. He moved his feet. One forward, one back. Was that right? He thought it was right. He thought the left one went forward. Or was it the right? He looked down at them. They looked wrong. They looked like the feet of a man standing on a ladder, not sure which rung he was on.
He moved them again. The right one forward. That looked wrong too.
He lifted the sword.
He got his hands on it. Both together, at first, in the middle, the spade way, and then he made himself move them. He made the right hand go up, under the guard, to the high shiny place. It went there slowly, like a hand going into cold water. And the left down, to the low shiny place, near the knob. And he held it out in front of him. Out and up, a little, the point toward the bright square of the way in, the way he thought the boys had held it.
And he stood there.
And his body did not know it.
That was the only way he could think of to say it, afterward. His hands were on the shiny places. His feet were apart. The sword was out in front of him. Every part of him was more or less where he thought it should be. And none of it knew. It was like standing in another man's clothes. Like standing in a coat that had been made for somebody else, the same size as you, the same cloth, and still all wrong, pulling at the shoulders, tight under the arms.
His elbows were wrong. He could feel it. They were sticking out. His wrists were stiff. The weight of the sword was all in the wrong place; it pulled forward and down, so that the point began to sink, slowly, toward the step below, and he had to fight to keep it up, and his arms began to shake. His knees were stiff and straight, like two posts. He knew that was wrong too. He did not know how he knew. He bent them. Now they were bent too far. He was crouching. He looked like a man about to sit down on something that was not there.
The point sank.
He pulled it up. It sank again.
And his feet. His feet would not stay. He could feel them wanting to move, to slide, to go somewhere else, and he did not know where. They were like dogs straining on a rope, pulling toward a smell he could not smell.
He held it.
He made himself hold it. He stood there on the iron step in the dim light with his arms shaking and his elbows out and his knees bent like an old man's, and the sword wavering in front of him like a stick in a stream. He held it, and held it, and waited for it to come.
It did not come.
Nothing came. No picture. No feeling. No voice, his own or his father's, saying Here or There or No, lad, again. Just the weight of the sword pulling at his arms, and the wrong feeling, all over him, everywhere.
He let the point drop.
It went down and rang on the iron step below him, a small flat ring, and the sound went away down the stair into the dark and came back up again, faint, a long time later, from the bottom.
And he sat down.
Quickly. Before anybody could see. He sat back down on the step where he had been, hard, so that it jarred his back, and laid the sword across his knees again, and put his hands flat on it, one on each side of the blade, and looked at it. As if he had only stood up for a moment to stretch his legs. As if nothing had happened.
His face was hot.
He could feel it. Hot all over, from his collar to his hair, the way it had not been hot since he was a boy. Since he was a boy in the yard behind the smithy, and had done something wrong in front of the other boys, and his father had laughed.
He did not look up.
He did not look up at Lyra, on the step above him. And he did not look up at the bright square of the way in, either. But he knew. He did not have to look. He could feel it, the way you can feel the sun on the back of your neck.
They had all seen.
The Ashborn, out on the glass. They had gone quiet. He had not noticed when. The voices had stopped. The spear butt had stopped knocking. Brin had stopped groaning. Out there, in the white heat, it was as quiet as it was in here. And he knew that if he looked up, he would see them. Kestrel, standing a little back from the bright square, with her spear upright. Sef, beside her. Oda. The small old one with the blue hands. And the lad, the harvester, on his crutch, with his gray face.
All of them, standing outside the way in, looking in at him.
At the big man on the iron step who had stood up, and lifted his sword, and stood there like a scarecrow in a field with his elbows out and his knees bent and his feet not knowing where to go, and then sat down again as fast as he could.
He sat and looked at the sword and did not look up.
And after a while, outside, he heard Kestrel say something, low, in the Ashborn way, and the spear butt began to knock again on the glass, and somebody laughed at something else, a little too loud, and Brin groaned, and they were all doing things again. Busy things. Things that were not looking.
He was grateful to them for that.
He sat with his hot face and his hands flat on the blade, and on the step above him, Lyra still said nothing at all.
