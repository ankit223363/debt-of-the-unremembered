Chapter 87: The Man Who Forgot His Sword, Part 2 of 5: Silence
Silence
She went on saying nothing for a long time.
Oren had known a lot of quiet people. In the war, there had been men who did not talk. Men who sat by the fire in the evening and looked into it and did not say a word from dark to the time they lay down. He had liked those men, mostly. You knew where you were with them. And there had been his father, who did not talk much either, except to the iron, and to the horses, and sometimes, late at night, to Oren's mother's empty place at the table.
But Lyra's quiet was not like any of them.
Those men had been quiet because they had nothing to say. Or because they had too much, and it was all stuck in them, like a cart in a ditch. You could feel it, with some of them, sitting by them. All the stuck things, pushing.
Lyra's quiet was not stuck. It was not empty, either. It was the quiet of a person who has thought about all the things she could say and has decided, one by one, to put them all down. On purpose. And to just sit.
It was the kindest thing anybody had done for him in years.
He thought that, sitting there on the warm iron step, and it surprised him. He turned it over and looked at it, to see if it was true. And it was. Nobody had done a kinder thing. Not in all the years since the war. People had done kind things, of course. The lad had. The old one with the inked arms had. People had given him food and patted his shoulder and said they were sorry. But they had always said something. They had always needed him to say something back. Aye. Thank you. It's all right. So that they could feel the kind thing had been done, and go away.
Lyra did not need anything back.
She was just there.
On the step above him, with her bound hand in her lap. He could feel her there, the way you can feel a fire in a room even with your back to it. Not touching him. Not looking at him, even, most of the time. Looking out at the bright square of the way in, or down at the dark stair, or at the rust on the wall. But there.
And he found, after a while, that he was counting her breaths.
He did not mean to. He noticed he was doing it, and he did not know when he had begun. In. Out. One. In. Out. Two. Slow. Even. Not loud. He could only just hear them, under the sounds from outside. But once he had started, he could not stop. He sat there with his hands on his sword and counted the breaths of the girl on the step above him, the way you count sheep going through a gate.
Three. Four. Five.
It was her thing, counting. He knew that about her. She counted everything. Steps, and stairs, and paces, and people, and the strokes in a fight. He had seen her do it on the glass, her lips moving. He had seen her do it at night, lying on her back, looking up at the stars. Counting them, he supposed. He had never asked her why. He had thought it was a girl's thing, a nervous habit, like biting your nails.
Now he was doing it.
Twelve. Thirteen.
And he understood, a little, why she did it.
It was something to hold on to. That was all. When everything else was going, when things were slipping and sliding and you did not know what was solid anymore, a number was solid. A number did not change. Thirteen was thirteen. It came after twelve and before fourteen and it would always come after twelve and before fourteen, whatever else happened to you. You could count, and know that the next number would come. It always came.
Twenty. Twenty-one.
He counted her breaths and his own breathing slowed to match them. He felt it happen. His chest had been tight, since he stood up and tried the guard. Tight and hot, and his heart going too fast, the way it went after a run. Now it began to loosen. In. Out. Her breath and his, going together, slow.
Thirty.
His face was not hot anymore.
Forty.
Outside, the Ashborn had gone back to being busy, and the sounds of them came and went. Through the bright square of the way in he could see a strip of the white sky and a strip of the black glass under it, and the shadow of the dome lying across the glass, long and curved, longer than it had been. The afternoon was going on. The sun had moved round. A little thin light came slanting in at the way in now, at an angle, and lay across the top three steps in a long bright bar, and the dust in it hung and turned, very slowly, gold.
Fifty.
He watched the dust.
It was a thing he had not done since he was small. Watched dust in a bar of light. He had done it in the smithy, on summer afternoons, when his father was at the anvil and the two big wooden leaves at the front of the smithy were swung back and the sun came in sideways over the yard. Sat on an upturned bucket by the coal heap and watched the dust hang in the light, gold, turning, and the sparks going up through it when the hammer came down. He had forgotten that. He had not thought of it since he was a boy.
Now he thought of it, and it was there. Clear and whole and warm. The upturned bucket, and the coal heap, and the smell of hot iron, and the dust.
And he thought: that's still here, then.
That's still mine.
Sixty.
He did not say anything. He did not want to break it. He sat and watched the dust, and counted, and on the step above him Lyra breathed, and the afternoon went on outside, and the shadow of the dome grew longer on the glass.
And somewhere in the eighties he lost count.
He did not mind. He let the numbers go, the way you let go of a rope when you are already on the bank. He did not need them now. He sat, loose, his hands on the sword, his back against the warm stone of the wall, and his eyes half shut, and for a while he did not think about anything at all.
It was the first time since noon.
And when at last Lyra moved, on the step above him, he opened his eyes and looked up at her, and she was looking down at him.
She did not smile. She did not say anything. She only looked at him, with that look of hers that went right through to the back of his head. And then she nodded. Once. Very small. As if he had asked her a question and she was saying yes.
And he nodded back.
He did not know what they had agreed. But he knew it was something good.
The Stone
His hand found the stone by itself.
He did not send it. He was sitting with his back to the wall and his eyes half shut, not thinking, and his right hand came off the blade and went into the pocket of his coat. The right-hand pocket, at the front, low down. The deep one with the patch on the corner where the cloth had worn through. And it went in, and found what was there, and closed on it, and came out again.
He looked down to see what he had.
A stone.
A flat gray stone, as long as his hand, and as wide as two fingers, and as thick as one. It was smooth on both sides, smoother than any river stone, so smooth it was almost soft under his thumb. One side was a little darker than the other. Both sides were a little hollow in the middle, worn down into a long shallow dip, the way the stone at the front of a house—
The way the step into a house is worn, in the middle, where everybody treads. He had the word for that, still. Step. He held on to it.
He held it up and looked at it.
He knew it.
He knew it the way he knew the sword. The way he knew his own coat. It was his, and it had been his for a long time, and his hand knew the weight of it so well that it had gone and got it out of his pocket without asking him. He turned it over. On the darker side there was a small mark scratched near one end. A letter, he thought. He could not read letters anymore. He had not been able to since the Ash took them. But he knew the shape of this one. Two straight lines meeting at the bottom, like a bird's foot. It had been scratched there a long time ago with the point of a nail.
His father had scratched it.
He knew that suddenly, the way you know a thing in a dream. His father, at the anvil, with the stone in his big black hand and a nail in the other, scratching. And then holding it out. There. That's yours now. Don't lose it.
He looked at the stone for a long time.
And then, because his hands seemed to want to, he let them.
He did not think about it. That was the strange part. If he had thought about it, it would not have happened. He would have looked at the stone and the sword and tried to work out what went where, the way he had tried with the guard, and it would all have gone wrong in the same way. But he did not think. He was too tired to think. He sat on the warm iron step with his back to the stone wall, with the long bar of gold light lying across the steps above him, and Lyra quiet on the step behind, and he let his hands do whatever they were going to do.
His left hand turned the sword over on his knees.
It turned it so the edge was up. The edge with the notch in it, near the point. It laid the blade along his left thigh, flat, with the guard against his knee and the point out past his hip, and held it there with the heel of the hand, firm, pressing down.
His right hand came up with the stone.
And laid it on the blade.
Not flat. At a slant. A small slant. The stone tilted on the steel so that only one long edge of it touched, and it touched the very edge of the blade, the thin bright line where the two sides came together. His hand found the slant without looking for it. It went there the way your foot finds the next step in the dark. There. Just there. Not a hair more, not a hair less.
And his hand drew the stone down.
From the guard toward the point. One long stroke. Even. Not fast, not slow. Pressing down, but not hard. The way you stroke a dog that might bite. And as the stone went down the steel it made a sound, a long soft hiss, like a breath let out through the teeth. Sssssss.
And lifted at the point. And came back to the guard through the air. And went down again. Sssssss.
He shut his eyes.
He did not mean to. They shut by themselves, the way they shut when you are lying in the sun and someone starts to sing. He sat on the iron step with his eyes shut and his hands going, and he did not watch them. He did not need to watch them. They knew.
They knew.
Sssssss.
He could feel it in them. In the fingers and the palms and the backs of the wrists. The slant of the stone. The weight. How hard to press. How long to make the stroke, and how to keep it even, all the way down, so the edge came out straight and not wavy. When to turn the blade over and do the other side. How many strokes on one side before you turned it. Eleven. It was always eleven. He did not know why. His father had done eleven. Eleven on one side, turn, eleven on the other, turn. And then fewer. Seven. Five. Three. And then the last one, a single stroke on each side, very light, hardly touching, just to take off the tiny curl of steel the stone left behind.
His hands knew it all.
He sat with his eyes shut and they did it, and the hiss of the stone went up into the dim air of the dome and came back off the curved walls, soft. Sssssss. Sssssss. He heard Lyra move, on the step above him. He heard her stop breathing, for a moment, and then start again.
He turned the blade. He felt his hands turn it. Eleven more.
And at the notch near the point, the little bite out of the edge the size of a pea, his hands slowed. All by themselves. He felt them slow. They went round the notch, the way you go round a hole in the road. Light, and careful, and then a little harder just past it, to bring the edge up level on both sides of the bite, so it would cut clean even there.
They knew about the notch. They had known about it for years.
Seven. Five. Three.
The last strokes. One on each side. So light that the stone only kissed the steel. Ss. Ss.
And his hands stopped.
He opened his eyes.
The edge was bright.
It had been dull before. He had not noticed how dull, until now. Gray, and a little rough, with small nicks in it from the iron men and from the fight on the glass. Now it was bright. A thin line of silver light ran all the way down it from the guard to the point, clean and straight, without a break, even past the notch. It caught the bar of gold light from the way in and threw it back so sharply that it hurt his eyes.
He held up his thumb and drew it, very gently, across the edge. Not along. Across. The way you test it.
It bit.
Not deep. He was careful. But he felt it bite, at once, the skin of his thumb opening under it as if it were paper. He looked at his thumb. A thin red line. A bead of blood coming up, round and dark and bright.
It was sharp. It was as sharp as he had ever made it.
And he laughed.
Once. Out loud. Not a happy laugh. A short, hard sound, like a dog barking once at a noise in the night. It went up into the dome and rang back off the iron and the stone, and he heard how ugly it was, and stopped.
Lyra had not moved.
He sat and looked at the bright edge of the sword, and the blood on his thumb, and the gray stone in his other hand with the bird's-foot letter scratched on it.
"That's left, then," he said.
His voice was rough. He cleared his throat.
"That's left. The hands know that. They know how to make it sharp." He turned the sword a little, so the bright edge caught the light again. "Saints. They know it better than they know my own name. Look at it, lass. You could shave with that."
He was quiet for a moment.
"I can make it sharp," he said. "I just can't use it."
He put the stone back in his pocket. Slowly. The right-hand pocket, at the front, low down. The one with the patch. His hand went in and laid it in the bottom, and came out, and he looked at the hand. Big and brown and scarred. With the old white lines across the knuckles. With blood on the thumb.
"I can make it sharp for somebody else," said Oren.
Company
The man came while he was still looking at his hand.
Oren did not hear him. Nobody ever heard him. He was a big man, as big as Oren nearly, and he wore heavy boots, and the iron steps rang under anybody who walked on them. But they did not ring under him. Oren looked up from the blood on his thumb, and the bright square of the way in had a shape in it that had not been there before. Tall. Wide in the shoulders. With long arms hanging, and hands wrapped in strips of brown-stained cloth.
The silent man.
He stood at the top of the steps with the light behind him, and Oren could not see his face. Only the shape of him, and the line of his jaw with the gray stubble on it, catching a little of the gold.
Oren waited for him to say something.
That was foolish. He knew it was foolish as soon as he thought it. The man did not say things. Not to anybody. Not to Tansy, even, who talked to him all day long. He had never said one word in all the days since they first came on him out on the Glass. Four times, two times, nine times, three. Oren had heard him knock. That was the only thing he had ever heard him do.
The man came down the steps.
Past Lyra. Lyra moved her knees to let him by, and he went past her without looking at her, without a sound, and came down to the step where Oren sat, and then one more. And sat down. On the step below Oren's. With his back to the wall, the same as Oren. A little way along, so that their shoulders were not quite touching but were close.
And there he stayed.
Oren looked at him sideways.
In here, out of the light, he could see the man's face a little better. It was a long face, and a hard one, and older than it ought to be. Oren had never been able to tell how old. Some days he thought forty, and some days sixty. The skin was brown and dry and cracked at the corners of the eyes, like old leather. The eyes themselves were gray. Pale gray, the color of the sky before rain. And they did not look at anything. They looked straight ahead, at the curved wall across the stair, at the rust running down it, and did not move.
Oren looked away again.
He sat with his sword across his knees and his bleeding thumb and did not know what to do. He did not know what the man wanted. He did not know if he wanted anything. He was not sure, with this one, that wanting came into it.
So he did what he always did, when he did not know what to do. He sat.
He put his right forearm on his right knee. It was a way he sat. He had always sat like that, on a step, or a log, or a low wall. Forearm on knee, wrist hanging, and the other hand on the sword across his lap. And his head down a little, looking at the floor between his boots. It was how he sat when he was tired. It was how he had sat by a hundred fires in the war, at the end of a long day's walking, waiting for the pot to boil.
And after a while he became aware that the man was sitting the same way.
He did not see it happen. He did not see the man move. But he looked sideways again, a little later, and there it was. The man's right forearm on his right knee. The wrapped hand hanging loose off the end of it, at the wrist. The other hand resting on his thigh, palm down, where Oren's rested on the sword. And the man's head down. Just a little. Looking at the iron step between his boots.
Exactly the same.
Oren looked at it for a long time.
And he felt, first, a small hot prick of something. Like the prick you feel when somebody at the back of a room laughs just after you have said a thing. Is he making fun of me? It came up in him quick and sharp, the way it would have come up in him when he was young. Because that was what it looked like. A man copying another man. The way boys copy a teacher behind his back, the way he himself had copied the drill sergeant behind his back, a hundred years ago, to make the others laugh.
The man did not laugh.
He did not do anything. He sat with his forearm on his knee and his head down, and he was not looking at Oren, and there was nothing in his face at all. No laughing. No meaning. The same as always.
Oren thought about it.
He thought about it the slow way, the way he thought about things now. Laying it out. He's sitting like me. Why? Because he's making fun. No. He doesn't make fun. He doesn't do anything. He just is. Then why? Because he doesn't know how to sit. No. He knows how to sit. He sits on the shore every day facing south. Then why?
He did not know.
He looked at the man's wrapped hand, hanging. The strips of cloth round it were brown with old blood, from the Ridges, from the iron men. The man had torn the springs out of two of them with his bare hands. Oren had seen it. He had seen the man's hands afterward, cut to the bone in the palms, and the man looking down at them as if they belonged to somebody else. And some days ago, in the Ridges, Oren had heard, in among all the other things, that the man had done something. A stroke. A cut. Better than Oren. Somebody had said it. He could not remember who, or what the stroke was. Only that somebody had said better than Oren, and he had felt proud, somehow, and not known why.
He looked at the man's face again.
And he thought: it doesn't matter.
It did not matter why. Whether it was mockery. Or a man who did not know how to be a man anymore and was copying the nearest one, the way a child copies. Or something else. It did not matter. The man had come down the steps, where nobody had asked him to come. He had sat down. Close. Not touching. And he was sitting the way Oren sat, on the step below, with his head down, in the dim cool light, while out on the glass the afternoon went on.
That was company. Whatever else it was.
Oren decided it was company.
He decided it the way he had decided things in the war, when there was no time to think them through. You looked at it, and you picked, and then that was what it was. He picked company. And as soon as he had picked it, he felt it settle in him. The small hot prick went out. He let his shoulders go down. He let his head go down a little more, too, so that he was looking at the step between his boots, and the man was looking at the step between his, and they sat there.
Two big men on an iron stair, with their heads down.
After a while Oren said, without looking up, "You don't know me, do you."
The man did not answer.
"No," said Oren. "Nor I you. Not really." He turned the sword a little on his knees. "But we've both had things taken. I can see that much. You've had more than me. A great deal more, I think. You've had near everything."
The man did not answer.
"Well," said Oren. "I've had something now, too. Not as much. But something." He looked at the bright edge of the blade. "It's a strange thing, isn't it? Not knowing what you've lost. You can feel the hole. You can't see what was in it."
The man's head moved.
Not much. A little. It turned, slowly, on his neck, until his pale gray eyes were looking not at the step between his boots but at the sword on Oren's knees. At the bright edge, with the gold light on it.
And stayed there.
Oren did not move. He did not want to break it. He sat, and the man looked at the sword, and the bar of light lay across the steps above them, and the dust turned in it.
And then the man's head went down again, to the step between his boots.
And that was all.
But Oren found that his thumb had stopped bleeding. And that he did not feel so much like a scarecrow in a field anymore. And that it was easier, a great deal easier, to sit on an iron stair with a sword you could not use if there was somebody else on the step below you, sitting the same way, who could not use his either.
