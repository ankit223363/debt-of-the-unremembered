Chapter 38: Under the Glass Dome, Part 2 of 5: Two Nines and Four
Two Nines and Four
The passage went down in pieces, the way Gideon had drawn it.
A stretch of flat stone floor, perhaps twenty paces long, with the walls close on either side and the ceiling low enough that Corin had to stoop. Then steps. Five of them, worn hollow in the middle by a hundred years of feet. Then flat again. Then eight. Then flat. Corin counted each one, with the lantern held low in his right hand and his left hand flat on the wall, and Gideon going ahead of him, quick and silent in the edge of the light.
He could not use the crutch. The crutch was lying across the peep hatch, up in Cinder Lane, under the frost. He used the wall instead. The wall and his good leg and the lantern and his teeth.
The bad leg would not bend at the knee now. Doctor Silas's linen had gone heavy and warm down the back of his thigh, and when he put weight on the leg he felt it give, a little, every time, like a step that is going to break. He did not put weight on it. He hopped. On the flat, he hopped. Hand on the wall, hop, drag the bad leg after, hop. On the steps, he sat. He sat down on the top step with the bad leg out straight in front of him, and lowered himself onto the next with his hands, and the next, and the next, like a child going down a staircase on its bottom.
Gideon waited for him at the foot of each flight. He did not offer to help. He did not seem impatient. He just stood there in the edge of the lantern light with his hands in his coat pockets, watching, until Corin reached him. And then went on.
At the bottom of the eight, the passage turned.
It turned to the right, round a corner of rough stone, and when Corin came round it with the lantern held out in front of him, he stopped.
The steps went down. Not a little flight, like the others. A long one. Steep and narrow and straight, going down and down into the dark beyond the reach of the lantern, between two walls so close together that a big man would have had to go sideways. The stone of the steps was smoother here, and paler, and somebody had cut a handrail into the right-hand wall. A groove in the stone, at the height of a hand, worn smooth and dark and greasy by a great many hands.
And from the bottom, far below, very faint, there came a light.
Not yellow. Not lantern light. A soft, cold, dim gray. Like the light of a winter morning coming under a door.
"Two nines and four," said Gideon quietly.
Corin looked down at the gray light.
"And at the bottom," he said.
"At the bottom, the lamp." Gideon looked at him. "It's on the wall. On the left. Right at the bottom. Where the steps come out into the ring passage. You'll go right under it." He paused. "Or right past it. It's very close. You can't go further away than the other wall."
Corin sat down on the top step.
He put the lantern down beside him. He put his hand into the groove of the handrail. It was cold and smooth and a little sticky, like the handle of a cup that many people have drunk from.
He began to go down.
One.
He went down on his bottom, one step at a time, the way he had gone down the others, with the bad leg straight out in front of him and the lantern in his lap. Gideon went ahead, a few steps below, walking, so that his fair head bobbed in and out of the edge of the light.
Two. Three. Four.
The walls were close. So close that Corin's shoulders brushed both of them at once. Cold stone, damp, beaded with water that ran down in little threads and dripped from the edge of the groove.
Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.
One nine.
He stopped, for a breath. The leg was screaming at him now. Not loud. A steady, high, shrill note, like a kettle left on the stove. He waited for it to drop. It did not drop. He went on.
One. Two. Three.
The gray light was getting closer. It came up the narrow stair toward him, soft and cold, and lay on the steps below him like frost.
Four. Five. Six.
He could see Gideon at the bottom now. Standing at the foot of the last step, in the gray light, with his hands in his pockets, looking up. The gray light lay on his face, and it made the face look even paler, even emptier, like a face carved out of candle wax.
Nothing happened to Gideon's hands. Nothing happened to his skin. He stood in the gray light as if it were ordinary light.
Seven. Eight. Nine.
Two nines.
Corin stopped again. He was four steps from the bottom.
And now he could see the lamp.
It hung on the left-hand wall, on an iron hook, at the height of a tall man's head. Right at the foot of the stair, where the narrow passage opened out. A brass lamp, old and dark, shaped like a little house with a pointed roof, with glass windows in its four sides. There was no flame in it. Inside the glass, on a little brass stand, there was a stone. Gray. Dull. About the size of a hen's egg. And out of the stone, very slowly, like smoke out of a dying fire, came the light.
It did not shine. It seeped. It came out of the stone and through the glass and spread out into the air of the passage, gray and soft and cold, and lay on everything.
Ash, Vespera had said. A burned gem. Ash set in glass. What it does is find other Ash.
Do not stop, said Malakor. Very quietly. Very close. If you stop under it, it has longer to look. Go. Go fast. Go now.
Corin went.
He did not go down the last four steps on his bottom. He stood up. He did not know how. He put his hand in the groove of the handrail and pulled, and got his good leg under him, and stood, and went down the last four steps in two hops, one, two, with the bad leg swinging and the lantern swinging and the pain going through him like a bell. And at the bottom, he did not stop. He went straight on, past Gideon, past the foot of the stair, out of the narrow passage, under the lamp.
And the lamp saw him.
He felt it. He did not know how else to say it. It was like walking into a room where somebody is sitting very still in the dark, and knowing, before your eyes can find them, that they are there and that they are looking at you. The gray light did not change. It did not get brighter. But it changed what it lay on.
His hands began to smoke.
That was what it looked like. Out of the coal-black skin of his hands, through Vespera's paint, through Hessa's gray neckcloth wound round the left one, a faint gray haze rose up. Thin. Wavering. Like the breath off a horse in the cold. Like smoke off a wet fire. It came up off his fingers and his knuckles and his wrists, and up his forearms under his sleeves, he could feel it there, cold, crawling, like the touch of a cobweb. And it hung in the air round his arms in the gray light, so that he walked inside a little cloud of his own Ash, glowing faintly, the color of a winter sky.
And something else.
Behind his left ear.
He felt it there. Stronger than on his hands. Much stronger. A cold, prickling, crawling heat, just behind and below his left ear, at the top of his neck, under the hair. As if the lamp had found something there that it wanted very badly to see. As if it were leaning toward it.
He did not stop.
He went on. One hop. Two. Three. Out of the reach of the gray light, into the dark of the ring passage beyond, and leaned on the wall, and breathed.
The smoke went away from his hands. Slowly. Thinning. Gone.
The cold behind his ear went away too. Last of all.
He turned his head.
Gideon was standing at the foot of the stair, under the lamp, in the gray light, looking at him with mild interest.
"Your hands smoked," he said.
"Yes."
"Mister Gant's do that. But only a bit. Yours did a lot." He tilted his head. "And your ear."
Corin did not move.
"Behind your ear," said Gideon. "On the left. That smoked most of all. Like a chimney." He considered it. "Mister Gant's ear doesn't do that. Nobody's does. I've never seen anybody's ear do that before."
The ring passage was very quiet.
Behind Corin's left ear, there was nothing. No voice. No breath. Only silence. The same silence there had been in the hay, at the mouth of the tunnel, by Lord Thorne's bed.
Malakor, thought Corin.
Nothing.
Malakor.
And then, very faint, very far away, as if from the other side of a thick wall:
Walk on, harvester. We are wasting the night.
That was all.
The Ring Passage
It was wider than Corin had imagined.
Gideon had said you could drive a cart down it, nearly, and he had thought that was the way a boy says things. It was not. The ring passage was as wide as Cinder Lane, and much higher, and it curved away from him on both sides, gently, the way the wall of the Bourse curved above, so that he could see only a little way in either direction before the curve took it. The floor was stone, great square flags of pale gray stone, laid close and smooth and swept so clean that it shone faintly in the lamplight. The roof was brick, in arches, like Mother Grue's tunnel. But dry. No dye. No drips. Clean, dry, old red brick, going up in a long row of curves overhead, like the inside of the ribs of some enormous animal.
There were lamps. Oil ones, as Gideon had said, small and yellow, on iron hooks on the outer wall, every twenty paces or so. Most of them were out. Sunday. Only every third or fourth one was lit, turned very low, so that the passage was a long curving row of small yellow lights with deep pools of dark between them.
It was quiet.
Not silent. Corin had thought it would be silent. But there was a sound, very faint, so low that he felt it in his teeth more than heard it. A hum. Steady. Never stopping. Coming from somewhere to his left. From the inside of the ring. From the middle.
Mmmmmmmmm.
Like bees in a wall.
And under the hum, lower still, so low that he was not sure it was a sound at all, something else. A slow, deep, heavy pressure, coming and going, through the stone under his feet. In. And out. In. And out.
Like something very big breathing, Gideon had said.
Corin stood with his back to the wall at the foot of the stair, outside the reach of the gray lamp, and felt it come up through the soles of his boots, and did not like it.
And the smell.
Straw. Dry straw, a great deal of it, from somewhere close. And under the straw, very strong, the smell Corin knew better than any smell in the world. Better than bread. Better than his own skin. The smell of the Archive. Of the Third Basement. Of twelve years of shelves, and the cool dark, and the jars.
It was like walking into his own house.
Do not get fond of it, said Malakor, still very far away. It is not your house. It is a butcher's cold room.
Corin did not answer.
He was looking at the floor.
The clean, pale, swept flags of the ring passage. And on them, behind him, coming out of the narrow mouth of the stair and following him the three hops he had made to the wall, there were marks. Dark. Round. Each one about the size of a coin. One, and then another, and then another, a hand's width apart, shining faintly in the yellow light from the nearest lamp.
Blood.
He looked down at his leg. At the back of his left trouser leg, where it had gone dark and heavy. At the place just above his boot, where a drop was gathering, slowly, swelling, trembling. And falling.
Tap.
On the clean stone.
"Gideon," he said very quietly.
Gideon came. He looked at the floor. He looked at Corin's leg. He looked at the floor again, and the line of dark coins on the pale stone, going back to the foot of the stair, and under the gray lamp, and up.
"They sweep it," said Gideon. "Every morning. At the sixth bell. Mistress Fane's girl does it." He considered the marks. "She'll see."
"Yes."
"And they go up the stairs. All the way up. To the coal. To Coker's stool." Gideon looked at the stair, and up into the dark of it. "Coker will see. When he comes back. If he has his lantern."
"Yes."
Gideon thought about it.
"Spit," he said.
Corin looked at him.
"Spit on it," said Gideon. "And rub it. With your sleeve. Mam used to. On the floor at home, when I cut my knee." He was already crouching down by the nearest mark. He spat on it, neatly, the way he did everything, and rubbed it with the cuff of his too-big coat until it was gone. Then he moved to the next. "I'll do these. You do something about your leg. Or there'll just be more."
Corin did something about his leg.
He sat down on the clean floor with his back against the outer wall of the ring, and he took off his coat. He took off his shirt, under it. The cold of the passage came onto his bare chest and arms like water, and he shivered, and the shiver went into the leg and made it worse. He tore the shirt. Down the back, along the seam, and across. Into strips. It was an old shirt, worn thin, and it tore easily. He wound the strips round his thigh, over Doctor Silas's soaked linen, above the wound and over it, round and round, as tight as he could pull them. Tighter. He tied them off with his teeth, the way he had tied the neckcloth round his hand.
It hurt so much that for a moment the ring passage went away altogether, and he was in the grass again, and there was a wagon, and a drum.
Then it came back.
He put his coat on again, over his bare skin. It was cold and stiff and smelled of coal. He buttoned it to the throat.
Gideon had finished the marks. He had done all of them, from the wall back to the foot of the stair, and then he had gone up the stair, into the dark, and Corin had heard him spitting and rubbing his way up, step by step, two nines and four. And then he had come back down, through the gray light of the lamp, as if it were nothing, and was standing in front of Corin now, wiping his mouth on the back of his hand.
"That's all of them," he said. "Unless you do more."
"I won't." Corin did not know if that was true. "Thank you."
Gideon nodded.
He did not say you're welcome. Corin did not think he knew that people said that. He just stood there, with his hands back in his pockets and his face quite calm, and waited.
Corin got up. Slowly. With his hand on the wall.
The new bandage was tight. Very tight. The leg was numb below it, almost. A heavy, dull, far-away numbness, like a leg that has gone to sleep. That was bad, he knew. That was very bad. You could not leave a leg bound that tight for long. But it was better than a line of red coins on a swept floor leading straight to the coal chute.
"Which way?" he said.
Gideon turned and looked along the curve of the ring passage. To the left. Toward the middle. Toward the hum.
"The selling room's that way," he said. "First. Then the counting room. Then the store rooms. All along on the outside of the ring." He paused. "There's a gray lamp outside the counting room door. On the outer wall. We'll go along the inner wall, there. Close. In the dark between the yellow lamps. It doesn't reach that far." He considered. "Mostly."
"Mostly."
"I've never tried it," said Gideon. "I never needed to. Nothing happens to me."
Corin picked up Coker's lantern from where he had put it on the floor, and turned it down, until the yellow bead of flame was very small. Small enough to see the floor in front of his feet, and no more.
The hum went on, from the middle, steady and low.
The floor breathed under his boots.
"Then we'll find out together," he said, "how far mostly is."
And they went along the ring passage, side by side, in the dark between the yellow lamps, the boy who felt nothing and the man who could not stop feeling, toward the smell of straw.
Mistress Fane's Book
The selling room had no door.
It was an archway, opening off the outer side of the ring passage, with a curtain hung across it on brass rings. Heavy plum velvet, gone brown at the hem where it brushed the floor. Gideon pulled it aside, and the rings slid along their rail with a soft metal whisper, and they went in.
Corin turned the lantern up a little.
It was a small room. Square. Whitewashed walls, very clean. A floor of the same pale swept stone as the passage. There was a table against the far wall, plain scrubbed wood, with a lamp on it, not lit, and a wooden box with a sliding lid. There was a stool behind the table. And in the middle of the room, facing the table, there was a chair.
It was a good chair. Better than anything in the arches. Oak, dark and heavy, with a high straight back and wide flat arms. And on the arms, buckled loosely, hanging down, there were straps. Leather ones. Wide. With brass buckles. One on each arm, at the wrist. And two more, lower down, on the front legs, at the height of a man's ankle.
Gideon looked at the chair.
"That's it," he said. "That's where you sit."
He did not go any closer to it. Corin noticed that. He stood just inside the curtain, with his hands in his pockets, and looked at the chair from where he was, quite calmly. But he did not go any closer.
"You don't need the straps," he said. "Nobody fights. You can't fight. Once she puts the glass on you, you can't move. It's like being asleep with your eyes open." He considered. "But Mistress Fane does them up anyway. Every time. She says it's the rules."
Corin went to the table.
He slid back the lid of the wooden box. Inside, in rows, in a bed of straw, there were glasses. Not jars. Glasses. Small round flat ones, like the lenses in the Duke's cases, each one about the size of a coin, with a little brass rim. Harvesting glasses. The kind that sit on the temple and draw. The kind Doctor Silas used on the fever children.
They were all clear. Empty. Waiting for Monday.
He closed the box.
There was nothing else in the selling room. Nothing on the walls. Nothing under the table. It was the cleanest room Corin had ever been in. It looked, he thought, the way a room looks that is washed every night because of what happens in it in the day.
They went through a low door in the side wall into the counting room.
This one was longer. A long narrow room with a long narrow table down the middle of it, under two lamps that hung from the ceiling on chains. The table was covered with green baize, worn to the threads in places, with ink stains all over it like the map of a country. There were two stools at it. There were inkpots, and pens in a jar, and stacks of little paper labels, cut square, with holes punched in their corners for string. There was a pot of paste. There was a ball of string.
And on the walls, all the way round, from the floor to the ceiling, there were racks.
Wooden racks, like the racks in a wine cellar, with round holes in them. And in almost every hole, a jar.
Corin stood still in the doorway with the lantern held up and looked at them.
Hundreds. In rows. Each one in its own round hole, with its neck sticking out and a paper label tied round the neck with string. Not glowing, like the Duke's. These were ordinary jars. Gray glass, cheap, the kind the Ministry bought by the thousand. The kind he had filled every day of his working life. They did not glow because there was nothing in them worth the trouble of glowing. Cold. Fright. Sad. A copper each.
They sat in their racks in the lamplight, in rows, like the eggs of something.
It is not your house, Malakor had said. It is a butcher's cold room.
Corin put the lantern down on the green baize.
There was a book at the end of the table.
It was big and black and heavy, bound in canvas, with a strap round it and a brass clasp. A ledger. Not like his. A clerk's ledger, a counting-house ledger, the kind with ruled columns on every page. It lay closed at the end of the long table, under the second lamp, beside a pen laid neatly across a blotter.
Corin opened it.
The columns were ruled in red. Six of them. And at the head of each, in a small hard upright hand, a word.
Date. No. Kind. From. Paid. To.
And under the words, page after page after page of entries, in the same small hard hand. Corin turned them. Slowly. The way he had turned the pages of a thousand ledgers at the Archive. Reading the way he read. With his whole face.
Mon. 4,211. Blue. Lower Docks (girl). 1c. Shelf 9.
Mon. 4,212. Black. Lower Docks (boy). 1c. Shelf 9.
Mon. 4,213. Gray. Saltgate (woman). 2c. Shelf 11.
On and on. Hundreds of lines. Thousands. Monday, and Tuesday, and Wednesday. Every day but Sunday. Blue, black, gray, red, yellow, green. Lower Docks. Saltgate. The Steps. Wharf Street. Fuller's Yard. Girl. Boy. Woman. Man. Child. No names. Never names. Only places, and a number, and a color, and a price. One copper. Two. Sometimes three, for blue, in winter. Very cold. Winter stock.
And every so often, in among them, a different kind of line.
Fri. H. series. From the Bell. 40 jars. Blue/black/gray. Gant. Shelf 2.
Fri. H. series. From the Bell. 31 jars. Black chiefly. Gant. Shelf 2.
Corin's finger stopped on the page.
H.
From the Bell.
He turned back. Further back. A month. Two. A year. Always the same. Every Friday, or nearly every Friday, a line in the small hard hand. H. series. From the Bell. Twenty jars. Forty. Sixty, once, in a hard winter. Gant. Shelf 2.
The counting room under the Bell. The glass, every morning and every night. They took what I'd got.
He did not look round at Gideon. He kept turning.
And then, among the Friday lines, a new one. One that he had not seen before. That began, he saw, turning back to find the first of it, only in the autumn.
Fri. Health. St. Bride's. 30 glasses. Slag, drawn. Cart. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P.
He read it twice.
Then he turned forward. A week. Another.
Fri. Health. St. Bride's. 34 glasses. Slag, drawn. Cart. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P.
Fri. Health. St. Bride's. 41 glasses. Slag, drawn, fast. Cart. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P.
Fri. Health. St. Bride's. 52 glasses. Slag, drawn, very fast. Good yield. Cart. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P.
The last one was this Friday. Two days ago.
Good yield.
Corin stood at the end of the long table with his finger on the line and did not move for a long time.
Doctor Silas's glasses. The fever children's glasses. The slag he drew off them, so carefully, with so much patience, from the children on the floor of the crypt under Saint Bride's. A cart comes. Every Friday. From the Ministry. To take the slag away. To the proper place, they say. For disposal.
A cart with no crest on its door, and a driver in a plum-colored scarf.
Here. It came here. Every Friday. To Shelf 40.
And somebody called A. P. was paid for it.
Drawn very fast, said the line. Good yield.
The more the children drained, the faster they drained, the more slag there was in the glasses. And the more slag there was, the more there was to sell. To blend. To put in crates with a white mark and send north by the wet door.
Something that loosens it, Doctor Silas had said. Something that makes it come away.
White sweets. Like peppermints. On the Fish Stairs. Every Friday morning.
A gentleman from the Health.
Corin took his finger off the page.
His hand was shaking so badly that he had to put it flat on the green baize to stop it. The coal-black hand, with Hessa's gray neckcloth wound round the knuckles. He looked at it, lying there on the stained green cloth beside the black book.
"What does it say?" said Gideon.
He had come to stand beside Corin at the end of the table. Corin had not heard him come. He was looking at the ledger with mild interest. He could read a little, Corin knew. Mother Grue had taught him his letters. But not well. Not this small hard hand.
"It says where everything comes from," said Corin. "And where it goes. And who gets paid."
"Does it say H?"
"Yes."
Gideon looked at the page for a while.
"That's us," he said. "The hollow ones." He did not seem to mind. "Shelf 2."
"Yes."
"Is that where my cold is?"
Corin looked at him.
"Maybe," he said. "If it's still here. If it hasn't been sold." He closed the ledger. Gently. The way Lyra had closed the catalogue, as if it were asleep. "Shelf 2."
"I know where Shelf 2 is," said Gideon. "It's the second store room. Along the ring. Past the gray lamp."
He turned and went to the door.
Corin did not follow him at once. He stood at the end of the long table with his hand flat on the black book, and he thought about taking it. Tucking it under his coat. Carrying it out of here, up two nines and four, up the coal chute, to Doctor Silas. To Lyra. To anybody. Here. Look. It's all written down. Every child. Every Friday. Every copper. A. P.
And on Monday morning, said Malakor, very quietly, from very far away, Mistress Fane comes down to the counting room, and the book is gone. And by Monday noon, Vivienne knows somebody has been in her cellars. And by Monday night, there is no Shelf 40, and no Friday cart, and no A. P. And every glass from Saint Bride's has gone out of the wet door in a crate. And the man with the peppermints has gone to the country. To a sister.
Corin took his hand off the book.
He took out a stub of pencil instead. Not his own. His own was in the Ledger, and the Ledger was against Lyra's heart in a doorway in Cinder Lane. This was Coker's. He had found it on the floor by the stool, beside the folded newspaper. And he tore a strip from the margin of Coker's newspaper, which he had also taken, and wrote on it, very small, against the green baize, everything he could remember from the page. The dates. The numbers. St. Bride's. Slag, drawn. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P. And Good yield.
He folded it small.
Smaller.
Into a hard tight square, the size of a fingernail.
And put it in the pocket inside his coat, where the Ledger lived. Against his heart. In the empty place.
Then he picked up the lantern and followed Gideon out into the ring.
