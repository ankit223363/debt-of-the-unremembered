Chapter 38: Under the Glass Dome, Part 3 of 5: Blue Chalk, and H
Blue Chalk, and H
They went past the gray lamp outside the counting room door the way Gideon had said. Along the inner wall of the ring, close, in the dark between the yellow lamps, with Corin's shoulder brushing the brick and the lantern turned down to a bead.
The gray light lay on the outer wall, across the passage, like moonlight on water. It reached out toward them. It did not reach far enough.
Mostly.
At the very edge of it, as Corin went past, he felt the cold crawling heat come up behind his left ear. Just for a breath. Faint. Like a draft from a door that is nearly shut. Then it was gone, and they were past, and the gray light was behind them.
Gideon did not ask about his ear this time.
The store rooms began a little way past the counting room. Gideon had drawn them as small squares coming off the ring, like teeth. They were not small. Each one was an archway, like the selling room, with no curtain, opening off the outer wall into a long low brick vault that went back and back into the dark. And each vault was full.
Crates.
Corin held the lantern up in the mouth of the first one, and looked, and did not say anything.
They were stacked from the floor to the curve of the roof. Square wooden crates, rough pine, nailed shut, with rope handles at the ends. In rows. With narrow paths between the rows, just wide enough for a big man to walk down sideways. Hundreds of them, in the first vault alone, going back into the dark beyond the reach of the lantern. And on the end of every one, a smear of colored chalk.
Blue. Black. Gray. Red. Yellow. Green.
In the lantern light, the colors were faint and dusty, like the colors in an old painting. But they were there. Every crate. Every row.
The smell was very strong in here. Straw. Pine. And the jars. The church smell, the Archive smell, the smell of his own life. So strong that for a moment Corin could not breathe.
"That's Shelf 1," said Gideon. "Lower Docks, mostly. Summers. Red." He pointed. "Shelf 2 is the next."
They went on.
The mouth of the second vault was the same. Crates to the roof. Chalk on the ends. But the chalk here was nearly all the same color. Blue, and black, and gray. Cold, and fright, and sad. Over and over. Row after row. And on every crate, under the colored smear, a single letter, in white chalk, very neat.
H.
Corin stood in the mouth of Shelf 2 and looked at the letters.
There were so many. More than he could count. Far more than nine. Row upon row upon row of H, going back into the dark, every one of them a crate, and every crate full of jars, and every jar—
"Are these all hollow ones?" he said. His voice came out very strange.
Gideon looked at the crates.
"Yes," he said. "From the Bell. From the glasses. All the hollow ones. Not just me." He considered. "There's lots of us. Mister Gant said there's always more. He said the Lower Docks grow them like mushrooms." A pause. "These are all of us. All our cold and our frightened and our sad. For years."
Corin put his hand on the nearest crate.
It was rough and cold and splintery under his coal-black palm. Blue chalk. H. He did not know what he had thought he would do. Read it, perhaps. The way he read Ash, through the glass of a jar. Hold it, and know. This one's a girl from Fuller's Yard. This one's a boy from the Steps. This one's Gideon.
He felt nothing. It was a box of jars. It was pine and nails and straw and glass, and inside the glass, things that had been taken from children too small to know they were worth a copper each.
"How do I find yours?" he said.
"Numbers," said Gideon.
Corin turned his head.
"On the jars," said Gideon. "I told you. They write H and a number. On the label." He was looking at the crates, quite calmly. "I watched them write mine. Every time. In the counting room under the Bell, after the glass. Mister Gant's clerk. He had a squint and a cold. He wrote the number on the label and tied it on and said it out loud, so the other clerk could put it in the book." He paused. "I remember them."
"All of them?"
"All of them," said Gideon. "There's nothing much else in my head, Mother Grue says. So the numbers stayed." He closed his eyes, lightly. "My cold was first. When I was eight. It was H, two hundred and ninety-one. And then two hundred and ninety-two. And ninety-three. Up to three hundred and four. That was all the cold. Fourteen jars. Then it was frightened. That was more. Then sad." He opened his eyes. "Blue chalk. H, two-nine-one, to H, three-oh-four."
Corin stared at him.
He could not feel those numbers. Two hundred and ninety-one. He could say them. He could see them, if he shut his eyes, written on a label in a clerk's squinting hand. But they had no size. They had no weight. They were a word in a language he could read and not speak.
But Gideon could.
"Then we look for those," he said. "Two-nine-one to three-oh-four. Blue."
They looked.
It took a long time. The crates were nailed shut. They could not open them all. Corin found that there was a gap at the top of each one, under the lid, where the planks did not quite meet, and that if he held the lantern up to it and put his eye close, he could see in. Straw. The necks of jars. And the labels, tied on with string, with H and a number. He read them aloud, in a whisper. Gideon listened, and shook his head, and they went on to the next.
H, four thousand and twelve. No. H, six hundred and nine. No. H, one thousand, one hundred and—
No. No. No.
Row after row. The leg was numb now, almost all the way to the knee, under the tight shirt-strips. Corin dragged it. He did not care. He held the lantern up to the gap in the top of the crate and put his eye to it, and read, and Gideon said no, and they went on.
They had done three rows, and half of a fourth, when Gideon stopped.
He had gone ahead, to the end of the row, where the vault met the back wall. He was standing there, in the dark at the edge of the lantern light, looking at the floor.
"There's a space," he said.
Corin came.
There was a space. At the end of the fourth row, against the back wall. A gap in the stack, the size of perhaps a dozen crates, where the floor was bare. Swept stone. And in the dust at the edges of the bare place, where the broom had not quite reached, the marks of where crates had stood. Square marks. And drag marks, going away. Toward the mouth of the vault. Toward the ring passage. And left. West.
There was a piece of chalk on the floor. Blue. A stub of it, worn down, dropped and forgotten.
Corin bent, slowly, with his hand on the wall. And picked it up.
"They've gone," said Gideon.
He was looking at the bare place. His face was quite calm.
"Those were the old ones," he said. "From when I was eight. They were at the back. At the bottom. The oldest ones are always at the back." He paused. "They've gone."
"Where?"
Gideon turned and looked at the drag marks in the dust. Going out of the vault. Going left. West.
"The wet door," he said.
He did not say anything else. He went. Out of Shelf 2, into the ring, along the inner wall in the dark, following the drag marks, quick and silent in his new brown boots. And Corin went after him, as fast as he could, which was not fast. With the lantern. And the stub of blue chalk in his fist.
The ring passage curved.
The hum from the middle got louder as they went west. And the breathing in the floor got stronger. Corin could feel it now in his knees, in his hips, in the bones of his good leg. In. And out. Slow and deep and heavy, like a great beast asleep under the stone.
And then the outer wall of the ring opened.
Not into an archway, like the store rooms. Into a room. A great square room, much bigger than any of the others, set into the west side of the ring like a stone set in a ring. Its floor went down in three wide shallow steps from the level of the passage. And at the far side of it, in the far wall, there was another opening. A low black arch. And through it, faint and cold, came the smell of the river. And a sound. Very soft. Water lapping on stone.
And on the far wall, beside the low black arch, at the height of a tall man's head, there was a brass lamp shaped like a little house. With a gray stone in it. And gray light seeping out.
And in the middle of the great square room, on the floor, under the yellow light of a single oil lamp hanging from the roof on a chain, there were crates. A dozen of them. Stacked in two neat piles. Nailed. Roped. Waiting.
Blue chalk on the ends.
And under the blue, in white, H.
And beside the H, very small, in white chalk too, a little mark. Like a key. Or a bird on a string.
Frost Reach
The gray lamp by the low black arch did not reach the crates.
Corin saw that at once. He had learned to see it, in the last hour, the way you learn to see the edge of the ice on a pond. The gray light seeped out of the brass house on the far wall and lay on the stone round the black arch, and on the first of the three shallow steps going down to the water, and on the floor for perhaps ten paces out into the room. And then it stopped. Thinned. Gave up.
The crates were in the middle of the room. Twenty paces from the arch. In the yellow light of the hanging oil lamp, not the gray.
"Stay on this side," said Gideon, very low. "Don't go past the crates. Toward the arch. That's where it starts."
Corin did not go past the crates.
He went down the three shallow steps from the ring passage into the great square room, one at a time, sitting, the way he had gone down two nines and four. And across the floor to the crates. And stood in front of them, with his hand on the nearest, breathing.
The room was very cold. Colder than the ring passage. The cold came in through the low black arch, off the river, wet and raw, and lay along the floor like water. Corin could hear the river now quite clearly. A soft, steady lapping, very close, on the other side of the arch. And beyond the lapping, far away and muffled, the sounds of the city on the water. A boat's bell. A man calling. The creak of a mooring rope.
The hum from the middle was very loud in here. And the breathing in the floor was so strong that he could feel the crates shiver under his hand with it. In. And out.
He looked at the crates.
They were the same as the ones in Shelf 2. Rough pine. Rope handles. Nailed shut. Blue chalk on the ends, and a white H, and beside the H the small white mark like a key or a bird on a string.
But they were different, too. Something had been done to them.
There was a label on each one. Not chalk. Paper. A square of stiff brown card, tacked to the side of each crate with four small nails, one at each corner. And on the card, in black ink, in a large plain hand that was not Mistress Fane's small hard one, there were words.
Corin held the lantern close and read.
TO: THE FROST REACH OF NYM.
BY THE NORTH WATER, VIA THE LOCKS.
COLD STOCK. FOR THE WINTER MARKETS.
CARRY UPRIGHT. KEEP FROM HEAT. DO NOT OPEN BEFORE DELIVERY.
And under that, smaller, a line of letters and numbers that meant nothing to him. A code. A tally. A shipping mark.
He read it three times.
The Frost Reach of Nym.
He did not know it. He did not know any of it. Nym was not a town on any map he had ever seen in the Archive. It was not a street, or a district, or a family on the Hill. The Frost Reach. It sounded like a place at the very top of the world. A place where the snow never melted and the rivers ran under the ice all year.
A place, he thought, where they would want to buy cold.
He thought of the tannery on the Tanners' Ditch, and the stack of crates on its landing stage. NYM & SONS. FINE LEATHER. NOT TO BE OPENED BEFORE DELIVERY. With the same small white mark.
By the north water. Via the locks.
Out of the wet door at high tide, in a low boat with no lights. Up the river. Into the canal. Through the locks. To the Long Pond, and the Tanners' Ditch. And there, at the last tannery, under the stink of the hides, where no gray coat would ever think to look, the jars were packed again. Into leather crates. Fine leather. And sent on. North. Up the canal past Brindle and Withy Reach and further, to wherever the canal ended and the road began, and then on by road, or by sledge, or by whatever they used up there, to the Frost Reach of Nym.
Past Brindle.
Past the hay wharf, where seven barges lay in the ice, and one of them had a small room in the middle of its hay where an old man lay tapping.
Corin's skin went cold under the coal dust.
Every boat that goes north passes the Patient Grace, he thought. Every one. Every week.
And somebody on every one of those boats knows the river.
Most of the river owes Hessa.
Not all of it.
"Corin."
Gideon was on the other side of the stack, crouching, with his eye to the gap under the lid of a crate. He did not look up.
"Here," he said. "Two-nine-seven."
Corin went round.
Gideon moved aside. Corin held the lantern to the gap and put his eye to it. Straw. The neck of a jar, gray glass. A paper label tied round it with string, turned a little toward him, so that by the yellow light he could just read the number, written in a squinting clerk's hand, in ink gone brown with years.
H. 297.
And beside it, another. H. 298. And beyond that, half buried in the straw, a third. He could not read its number. But it was the same blue-gray glass, and the same brown ink.
"That's mine," said Gideon.
He said it quite calmly. The way he might have said that's my coat, or that's my stool.
"That's my cold," he said. "Some of it. From when I was eight. The first winter."
Corin straightened up.
He looked round the great square room. There was a rack on the wall by the steps, where they had come down from the ring. Tools. A broom. A coil of rope. A mallet. A crowbar, long and black, with a flat bent end.
He went and got the crowbar.
Gideon watched him come back with it. He did not say anything. He did not ask what Corin was going to do. He just stood beside the crate, with his hands in his pockets, and watched.
If you open that crate, said Malakor, from very far away, they will know. On Monday. When the boat comes. When they count. One jar short. A nail bent. A lid that does not sit right. A pause. And then they will start asking who has been in the cellars on a Sunday night.
I know.
And they will ask Coker.
I know.
And Coker will remember the peep hatch, and the crutch lying across it in the frost, that he will find when he comes back from the Bell at the eleventh bell.
Corin stopped.
He had forgotten the crutch.
It was still there. Up in Cinder Lane. Lying across the little iron square in the corner of the coal flap. Where anybody coming along the lane with a lantern would see it.
How long? he thought. How long since the ninth bell?
He did not know. He could not feel it. An hour. More than an hour. He had been sitting on stairs, and crawling through doors, and reading labels in the dark, and he had lost it.
Then be quick, said Malakor.
Corin put the flat end of the crowbar under the lid of the crate. At the corner. Where the nails were.
He leaned on it.
The nails came up with a long thin shriek, like a cat. It went round the great square room and out through the low black arch onto the water. Corin froze. Gideon froze. They stood there, the two of them, with the crowbar half lifted, and listened.
The river lapped. The hum went on. The floor breathed.
Nothing else.
Corin lifted the corner of the lid. Just the corner. Just far enough. He put his coal-black hand in, through the gap, into the straw. It was cold. Very cold. Colder than the room. The jars in there were cold, the way snow is cold. He felt them under his fingers, smooth and round, packed close. He felt for the one with the label turned toward the gap. He found the string. He followed it to the neck.
He drew it out.
It came up out of the straw slowly, with bits of straw clinging to it. A jar. Gray glass. About as big as his two fists. A ground-glass stopper sealed with a blob of black wax. And a paper label, tied round the neck with string, brown with age.
H. 297.
He held it up in the lantern light.
It was cold. Bitterly cold. Colder than anything he had ever held. His hand, under the coal and the paint and the gray, began to ache with it, the way your hand aches when you hold a snowball too long. He could feel the cold coming out of the glass and into his palm and up his wrist.
A child's cold. A boy of eight, sitting on a glass in a counting room under a tavern, every morning and every night, while a clerk with a squint wrote down the number.
He pushed the lid of the crate back down. He laid the crowbar on top of it, and leaned his weight on the bar, slowly, until the nails went back into their holes. Not all the way. Not quite. But nearly.
Then he turned to Gideon, and held out the jar.
Gideon looked at it.
He did not take it. He looked at it for a long time, in the yellow light, with his pale calm eyes. At the gray glass, and the black wax, and the brown label with his number on it.
"Is that it?" he said.
"One of them," said Corin. "One of fourteen. The others are in the crate." His voice was not steady. "I can't take them all. They'd know. I can only take one."
Gideon nodded slowly.
He put out his thin hand, and took the jar.
He held it in both hands, the way Mother Grue had held the loaf. He looked at it. Corin watched his face. Waited.
Nothing happened in it.
"It's cold," said Gideon.
He said it the way you would say it's heavy, or it's round. A thing about the jar. Not a thing about himself.
"I can feel it's cold," he said. "On my hands. Like the river. Like snow." He turned the jar a little. "But I don't feel cold. I just know it is." He considered. "That's the same as always."
"It's still in the jar," said Corin. "It hasn't gone back. I don't know how to put it back. Not yet." He swallowed. "But it's yours. You can keep it. Till I find out."
Gideon looked at him.
Then he unbuttoned the front of his too-big coat, and put the jar inside it, against his thin chest, in the place where Corin kept the Ledger. And buttoned the coat up again over it.
"I'll keep it here," he said. "So I'll know where it is."
And from the far side of the great square room, from the ring passage, from the dark beyond the shallow steps where the curve of the passage went on round toward the east and the lift and the kitchen stair, there came a sound.
A long, deep, wooden groan.
The sound of a very heavy weight getting up off a bench.
