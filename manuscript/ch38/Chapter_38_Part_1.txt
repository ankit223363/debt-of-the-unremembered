Chapter 38: Under the Glass Dome, Part 1 of 5: Cinder Lane
Cinder Lane
Cinder Lane was so narrow that Corin could have touched both walls at once, if he had let go of the crutch.
He did not let go of the crutch.
It ran along the back of the Bourse like a crack in a plate. On one side, the great curved wall of the building itself, pale stone going up and up into the dark, so high that he could not see the top of it, only the faint green glow of the copper dome far above against a sky with no stars. On the other side, the backs of old warehouses and counting houses, black brick, with no windows at all at street level and only small barred ones higher up, every one of them dark. Between them, the lane. Cobbles, wet and black and broken, with a gutter down the middle full of something that did not freeze even on a night like this. It smelled of coal, and old beer, and the river.
It was very dark.
There was one lamp. At the far end, on the corner where the lane met the foot of Tanner's Steps, on a bracket over a low black door. The Bell. Corin could see the sign hanging beside the lamp. A wooden bell, painted gold once, gone brown. Under the lamp, the door of the tavern stood half open, and light came out of it, and noise. Voices. A fiddle. A man singing something about a girl from Saltgate, very loudly and very badly.
They were standing in a doorway about halfway along. A deep one, set back into the warehouse wall, with a door at the back of it that had been nailed shut so long ago that the nails had rusted into the wood. It was full of old leaves and broken glass and the smell of cats. There was room in it for three people, if they stood close. There were four.
Corin, on his crutch. Gideon, beside him, in his new brown boots, with his hands in the pockets of his too-big coat. Lyra, at the back, in the brown arches dress and Ellis's flat cap and Hessa's gray coat, with the Ledger inside her dress and one hand over it. And Nell, squeezed in at Lyra's elbow, with a shuttered lantern held against her chest, not lit.
Nobody had said anything for a long time.
They were waiting for the ninth bell.
Corin's hands were stiff. Vespera's paint had dried tight over them, and over his wrists and his forearms, and every time he moved his fingers he could feel it pull, like the skin of a healing burn. He had not put the gloves back on. Vespera had said not to. Gloves rub. Gloves make you sweat. Go bare, and keep your hands out of water, and pray.
He was not sure who he was supposed to pray to.
His leg throbbed under Doctor Silas's linen. Not the hot living throb of yesterday. A dull, steady ache, like a bruise the size of a dinner plate. He kept his weight off it. He leaned on the crutch and on the cold brick of the doorway and on his good leg, and waited, and tried not to think about the coal chute.
You will open it, said Malakor quietly. Going down. You know that.
Maybe.
There is no maybe. The doctor said so. You heard him.
I heard him.
The voice did not say anything else. But Corin felt it there. Close behind his left ear. Watching, the way it had watched in the reeds, and in the sluice, and in the hay.
"There," breathed Gideon.
At the far end of the lane, a figure had come out of the dark.
It came from the other end of Cinder Lane, from the corner they could not see, round the long curve of the Bourse wall. A big, round, slow figure, carrying a lantern. It walked the way a man walks who has done the same walk every night for twenty years and could do it with his eyes shut. Heavy. Rolling a little from side to side. And as it came along under the curving wall toward them, Corin heard it whistling.
Not a tune. Just a whistle. Tuneless and breathy and pleased with itself, the whistle of a man on his way to a pie and four pints.
"Coker," whispered Gideon.
They pressed back into the doorway.
The figure came along the lane. It passed the coal chute. Corin saw the lantern light swing across the iron flap in the pavement, big as a table, with its ring, and pass on. It came closer. Closer. Ten yards. Five. The whistling. The smell. Beer and sweat and coal dust and, under it, something sweet and sickly, like old pipe smoke.
It went past the doorway.
Corin saw it, for a moment, in the swing of its own lantern. A fat man, short and wide, in a coat that had been black once and was gray now with coal, and a woolen cap pulled down over his ears. A great red face, wet at the lips. Small eyes, half shut. A thick stick under his arm, hooked at the end, like a shepherd's crook. The kind of stick you could pull a boy back up a coal chute with.
He did not look into the doorway. He did not look at anything. He went past, whistling, rolling from side to side, toward the light at the end of the lane.
At the door of the Bell he stopped. Somebody inside shouted his name. Coker! Coker, you old devil! He lifted the lantern in greeting, and laughed, a big wet wheezing laugh, and went in.
The door swung to behind him. Not quite shut. The light and the noise came out of the gap.
And far away across the city, from the great tower of the Chancery, a bell began to ring the ninth hour.
Corin counted it. He could not help it.
One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight.
Nine.
The edge.
"Two hours," said Gideon. "Till the eleventh. Then he comes back. Then he's asleep by the half." He looked up at Corin with his calm pale eyes. "We should be out by the eleventh. Before he comes back. Not by the half. By the eleventh."
"Yes."
Lyra had not moved. She was standing at the back of the doorway with her hand over the Ledger inside her dress, and her face, under Ellis's cap, was very white in the dark.
"Half past eleven," she said. Very low. "You said half past."
"I said if I'm not out by half past." Corin did not look at her. "Then you go. With the book. You don't come in."
"I know what you said."
"Nell," said Corin. "If anybody comes down the lane. Anybody at all. Not Coker. Somebody else. You open the shutter on the lantern, just a crack, and close it again. Three times. Like a knock."
"Like a knock," whispered Nell. "Three times." She was holding the lantern so tight against her chest that her knuckles were white. "I won't forget, mister. I won't."
"I know you won't."
He turned to look at Lyra, at last.
She was looking at him. Her eyes in the dark were very dark and very steady. She did not say anything. She did not need to. He could see everything she was not saying, the way you can see the shapes of things in a room with the curtains drawn.
"Half past," he said.
"Half past," said Lyra.
Then Gideon was moving. Out of the doorway, into the lane, quick and light and silent in his new boots, toward the iron flap in the pavement. And Corin got the crutch under his arm, and his good leg under him, and swung the bad one, and followed.
Down the Coal Chute
The iron flap was bigger than Corin had thought.
It lay in the pavement against the foot of the Bourse wall like a great black door laid down flat, square, with raised bumps all over it so that horses would not slip, and a ring in the middle as thick as his wrist. It was cold. He could feel the cold coming up off it through the soles of his boots. There was frost in the bumps, white, like salt.
There was a chain through the ring. A heavy one, rusted, going down to an iron staple in the stone of the wall, with a padlock on it the size of a fist.
"It's always locked," said Gideon. "At night. In case." He did not seem troubled. He crouched down beside the flap, at the corner furthest from the wall, and felt along the edge of it with his thin fingers. "That's not the way."
"Then what is?"
"This."
There was a smaller square, set into the corner of the big one. Corin had not seen it. It was no bigger than a book, flush with the rest of the iron, with a little sunken handle like a drawer pull. A peep hatch, he thought. For the coal men to look down and see how full the cellar was without lifting the whole flap.
Gideon put his fingers into the sunken handle and pulled. Nothing happened. He put his other hand flat on the iron beside it and pressed down, hard, with all his weight, and pulled again.
The little hatch came up with a soft iron squeak.
Under it was black. A square of pure black, cold, breathing out a smell of coal and damp and something else. Old stone. And, very faintly, so faintly that Corin was not sure he was not imagining it, the church smell. The smell of jars.
"It's small," said Corin.
"It's big enough." Gideon looked at him. Then at the hatch. Then at him again, at his shoulders, measuring. "For me. And for you. If you go in sideways. Arms first. Then your head. Then turn." He considered. "Your leg will hurt."
"Yes."
"There's a slide under it. Iron. Very smooth. Very steep. You'll go fast. You can't stop." He paused. "At the bottom there's the coal. It's soft, mostly. Mind your face."
He did not wait to see if Corin would follow. He sat down on the edge of the hatch with his legs in the black square. He put his hands flat on the frost either side. And he went. Just like that. Slid forward and down and was gone, with a soft hissing rush, like a sack going down a chute, and then, a long way below, a thump, and a rattle of coal, and silence.
Corin stood on his crutch over the black square.
Arms first, he thought. Then your head. Then turn.
He looked back once, down the lane, toward the doorway. He could not see them. It was too dark. Only the deeper dark of the doorway itself, set back into the warehouse wall. But he knew they were there. Lyra, with the Ledger against her heart. Nell, with the lantern.
He got down.
It was the worst part, getting down. He had to let go of the crutch, and lower himself onto the frozen iron on his good knee, and then sit, with the bad leg straight out in front of him like a stick of wood. The cold of the flap came up through his trousers at once. He felt the leg, under Doctor Silas's linen, start to throb harder, as if it knew what was coming.
He laid the crutch down across the hatch. He could not take it. It would not go. He would have to come back up without it, or not come back up at all.
He put his arms into the black square.
Then his head.
The smell came up into his face, coal and damp and jars. The cold. The black. He could see nothing at all. He felt with his painted hands for the edge of the slide, and found it. Iron, as Gideon had said. Smooth as glass and cold as ice and steep, very steep, going down into nothing at an angle like the roof of a house.
He turned. Twisted his shoulders sideways, the way Gideon had said, so that they would go through the square. The iron edge of the hatch scraped his back through his coat. It caught. He pushed. It gave.
And then he was through to the waist, hanging head-first down the slide in the black, with his painted hands flat on the iron, and his legs still up in the lane, and his weight going, going—
He went.
He did not decide to. The slide decided. His hands lost the iron and his body followed his head, and his legs came through the hatch after him, the good one and the bad one, the bad one scraping on the edge so that the pain went up through him white and huge. And then he was going down, fast, faster, on his front, head-first, in the black, with the cold iron under his chest and the air rushing past his face and the coal smell filling his mouth, and he could not stop, and he could not see, and he could not breathe.
It went on for a long time.
It went on for no time at all.
And then the slide ended, and he hit the coal.
He hit it with his face and his shoulders and his hands, all at once, a great soft crunching black heap that gave under him and slid and rattled and came up round him in a cloud. Coal in his mouth. Coal in his eyes. Coal down the neck of his coat. He rolled. He could not help it. He rolled sideways down the slope of the heap, over and over, and the bad leg hit something, a lump, a stone, he did not know, and he heard himself make a sound.
Not a scream. He did not scream. But very close.
He stopped rolling.
He lay on his back at the bottom of the coal heap, in the black, with the dust settling on his face, and breathed.
He could feel the leg. He could feel the place where Doctor Silas had opened it. Hot. Wet. Something running, slowly, down the back of his thigh under the linen, warm.
Open, said Malakor quietly. I told you.
I know.
How far will you get on it, harvester?
As far as I have to.
Something touched his face.
A hand. Thin and cold and dry. It felt over his face in the dark, quite gently, the way a blind man feels a stranger's face. His forehead. His eyes. His mouth. Then it stopped.
"You're alive," said Gideon's voice. Close. Calm. Interested. "Good. I thought you might be dead. You were very loud."
"Was I?"
"Not very. But a bit." A pause. "Nobody heard. There's nobody. Coker's at the Bell." The hand moved down, and found Corin's hand, and took hold of it. "Can you get up?"
Corin got up.
He did not know how. He did it by inches, in the black, with Gideon's thin hand pulling and his own good leg pushing against the shifting coal, and the bad leg dragging behind him like a log. He got to his knees. He got to his good foot. He stood, swaying, in a darkness so complete that it seemed to have weight, to press on his eyes like two thumbs.
Gideon let go of his hand.
And then he heard a small scratching, very close, and a hiss, and there was light.
A match. A single match, in Gideon's thin fingers, held up between their faces. The flame was tiny and yellow and shook a little in the cold. It lit Gideon's face from below, white and thin and smeared black with coal. It lit Corin's own hands, held up in front of him.
He looked at them.
They were black. Black to the wrist with coal dust. And under the coal, where it had been rubbed away on the slide, there was Vespera's paint. Gray-brown, the color of a clerk's hands in winter.
And in one place, on the back of his left hand, along the ridge of the knuckles, where he had hit the coal hardest, the paint had come away.
And under it, pale and dry and rough, like old frost on a window, the gray.
Five minutes if you're not lucky, Vespera had said.
The match went out.
Coker's Stool
In the dark, Gideon was already moving.
Corin heard him. The soft crunch of new boots on loose coal. The rustle of the too-big coat. Going away from him, to the left, along the foot of the heap. Then stopping. Then a sound of fingers on stone. Feeling. Patient. The way the boy had felt along the edge of the iron flap in the lane.
"Here," said Gideon's voice. "Come here. Follow my voice. There's coal on the floor but it's flat after the heap. Mind your leg."
Corin went toward the voice.
He went with one hand out in front of him, in the black, and the other on the coal heap at his side, to steady himself. Hop. Drag. Hop. The bad leg was wet now all down the back, under the linen, and warm, and every time he put it down the heat went up through him in a slow red wave. He did not stop. He went toward the voice.
His outstretched hand touched a wall.
Stone. Rough and cold and gritty with coal dust. He felt along it, the way Gideon had. And found Gideon's shoulder, thin and bony under the coat. And beside it, set into the stone, something that was not stone.
Iron. Flat and cold. A door. A small one, its top no higher than his chest. He felt the shape of it under his palm. The rivets. The hinge. And at the height of his waist, a slot. A narrow oblong hole, about as long as his hand and as wide as two fingers, with something on the other side of it, sliding, that his fingertips could just touch.
"That's the slot," whispered Gideon. "Coker looks through it. When you knock." His thin hand closed over Corin's and moved it, down, to the right of the slot. "And that's the bar. On the other side. It goes across. Like on a barn." He took Corin's hand away. "He doesn't lock it. Not on Sundays. He just drops the bar. So nobody can come in from the coal side while he's out."
"Then how do we get in?"
"Like this."
Corin heard the boy's coat rustle. Felt him shift. And then heard something he did not understand at first. A soft scrape of cloth on iron, and a small grunt, and a breath let out very slowly.
Gideon had put his arm through the slot.
The whole of it. His thin bony arm, in the sleeve of the too-big coat, up to the shoulder, through a hole as wide as two fingers. Corin could not see it. He could hear it. He could hear the boy's breathing change, go short and quick, as the iron edges of the slot pressed into the flesh.
"It hurts," said Corin. "Gideon, it must—"
"It doesn't," said Gideon. "Not to me." His voice was a little strained, but quite calm. "I did it lots of times. When Mister Gant locked me in the coal for being slow. The bar's just—there."
A scrape. A clank. A long iron sliding.
"Got it," said Gideon.
He drew his arm back through the slot. Slowly. Corin heard the coat sleeve tear, very softly, on the iron. Then a creak, and a breath of air that was warmer than the coal cellar, and smelled different. Oil. Tobacco. Beer.
And a faint, faint yellow light.
The little iron door had swung open.
Corin bent. His leg screamed at him. He went through the low doorway on one knee and one hand, the way he had gone through the tunnel into the hay, and came out the other side, and straightened up, slowly, holding on to the wall.
He was in a small room. No. Not a room. A widening of a passage. A place where the stone corridor that ran away ahead of him into the dark had been dug out a little, on one side, into a sort of nook. Just big enough for a man to sit in.
And in the nook, there was the stool.
It was a three-legged one, low and wide, with a seat polished black by twenty years of Coker. Beside it, on the floor, a stone jug with a cork in it. A clay pipe laid across the top of the jug. A newspaper, folded small, with a ring on it from the bottom of a mug. A pair of old slippers. On a hook in the wall above the stool, a second coat, even blacker than the one Coker had worn out. And on another hook, a lantern.
It was turned down very low. Almost out. The flame was a small blue bead at the bottom of the glass. But it was light. After the coal cellar, it was more light than Corin had ever seen.
There was a string hanging down the wall beside the hook. A long cord, going up through a hole in the stone ceiling. And on the end of it, a wooden handle, like the handle of a bell pull in a great house.
Gideon saw him looking at it.
"That rings upstairs," he said. "In the kitchen. For Mister Gant, when somebody comes down the chute that Coker doesn't know. Or when there's trouble." He looked at it. "Don't touch it."
Corin did not touch it.
He took the lantern down off its hook instead, and turned the little brass wheel on its side, very carefully, until the blue bead of flame grew into a yellow one. Not much. Just enough. And held it up.
And looked at his hands.
They were worse in the light. Coal-black to the wrists, the paint smeared and streaked under it. And on the back of the left hand, along the ridge of the knuckles, a long bare patch. A strip of skin as long as his finger, where the paint had scraped right off on the iron of the slide. And there, under the coal dust, unmistakable, the gray. Dry and pale and rough. Like old ash.
He looked at it for a long moment.
Then he put the lantern down on Coker's stool, and bent, and scooped up a handful of coal dust from the floor of the passage, where it had trodden in from the cellar over twenty years, black and fine as soot. And he rubbed it into the bare patch. Hard. Into the gray. Until the whole back of the hand was as black as the rest.
It will not help, said Malakor. Not under the lamps. The lamps do not care what color your skin is. They see what is under it.
I know.
Then why—
Because it's the only thing I can do, thought Corin. So I'm doing it.
He took off his neckcloth. It was an old one, Hessa's, gray wool, frayed at the ends. He wound it round his left hand, over the knuckles, twice, and tied it off with his teeth. A bandage. A man who had hurt his hand. Nobody looked twice at a bandage.
Gideon had watched all this without a word.
"The lamps," he said now. "There's one at the bottom. Of the two nines and four. Where the passage comes out into the ring." He tilted his head. "You have to go under that one. There's no other way into the ring from here."
Corin looked at him.
"You didn't say that," he said. "Last night. On the map. You said three, by the doors that matter. And one by the wet door."
"I know." Gideon did not seem troubled. "I forgot that one. Because it doesn't matter to me. I walk under it every time and nothing happens." He considered. "I only remembered it when I saw your hand."
The passage stretched away ahead of them, past Coker's nook, into the dark. Stone walls. Stone floor. Going down, very slightly, already. And somewhere at the end of it, past five steps and eight steps and two nines and four, a brass lamp with a gray light in it, hanging on a hook, waiting.
Corin picked up the lantern.
"Then we'll find out," he said, "how lucky I am."
