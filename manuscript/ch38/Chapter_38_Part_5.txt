Chapter 38: Under the Glass Dome, Part 5 of 5: Footsteps on the Kitchen Stair
Footsteps on the Kitchen Stair
It was not a loud sound. A latch, lifted. A hinge that wanted oil. And then the soft thud of a heavy door swinging back against a wall.
And then voices.
They came round the curve of the ring passage the way the humming had gone, but the other way. Thin with distance. Bouncing off the stone. Two voices, perhaps three. A man's, low and quick. A woman's, small and hard. And light with them. Not the gray of the Ash lamps. A warm, swinging, yellow light, the light of a lantern carried by someone walking, that came sliding along the curved wall of the passage ahead of the voices, and grew.
Corin's hand closed on Gideon's shoulder.
"Fane," breathed Gideon. "That's her. The small voice."
On a Sunday, Corin thought. She is never here on a Sunday. You said. Coker said. The Bourse is shut on a Sunday.
But she was here.
The light grew on the wall. The voices grew with it. They were coming. Not quickly. Not like people who have heard a noise and come to look. Like people walking somewhere they walk every week, talking as they go.
West. Toward the great square room. Toward the counting room. Toward the brass lamp at the foot of two nines and four, and the only way out.
Corin looked round wildly.
The crates. The bare stone. The black arch to the river. The rack of tools. The three shallow steps up to the ring passage, where the light was coming.
The arch, said Malakor, very faint. The water.
The lamp's there. The gray. You said that's where it starts.
Yes.
My hands are bare. They'll smoke. The paint's half gone, and the coal won't hold it, and you— He stopped. You'll go further away.
Yes.
The light grew. The voices were very near now. He could almost hear the words. —not before the ninth, he said. And he wants it counted, Fane. Every one—
There was no time.
Corin took Gideon by the collar of his too-big coat, and half pushed, half fell with him, out from behind the crates and across the bare stone floor toward the low black arch. He went on his good leg and his hands and the knee of his bad leg, dragging the stiff foot behind him in its wet boot, scraping on the stone. He did not care about the sound now. The voices were louder than the sound. Ten paces. Gideon was ahead of him, moving low and fast and quiet like a cat, without being told. Five.
The gray light took him.
He felt it come over him like cold water over a dam. The skin on the backs of his hands went hot, and then hotter, and then he smelled the burning and saw the thin smoke come up from his knuckles, out through the coal, through Hessa's neckcloth, in little gray threads. And behind his left ear, under his hair, the patch there went hot too, sudden and sharp, as if someone had laid a coin fresh from the fire against his skull.
Malakor's voice went out like a candle.
Not faint. Not far. Out.
Corin reached the first of the three shallow steps that went down from the arch to the water. He half rolled down them. The second. The third. And there was black water, an arm's length below the last step, lapping against the stone, cold and oily and smelling of the river. And beside the step, an iron ring set in the wall, and from the ring a chain going down into the water. And along the wall, under the arch, just above the water, a narrow ledge of wet stone, no wider than a shelf, that went along the inside of the arch and round its corner, out of the gray, into the dark.
Gideon was already on the ledge. Flat against the wall. His face was a white smudge in the dark.
Corin went after him.
He went sideways, with his back to the wet stone and his heels on the ledge and his bad leg trailing, one hand on the wall, one hand on the iron chain. The river lapped at his boots. The stone was slimy with weed. Two shuffles. Three. Round the corner of the arch. Out of the gray.
The burning stopped. The smoke from his hands thinned and went out. The patch behind his ear stopped burning and began to throb, slow and dull, like a bruise.
Inside his head there was nothing. No voice. No old dry sigh. No Corin. Nothing at all but the lapping of the water and the thud of his own heart.
Malakor, he thought.
Nothing.
Malakor.
Nothing.
He pressed his back against the wet stone and closed his eyes and held his breath and held on.
The voices came down the shallow steps into the great square room.
He could hear them quite well now. Round the corner of the arch, with his ear against the stone, he could hear everything. The scrape of boots on the steps. The swing and creak of a lantern. And then Mistress Fane's voice. The small hard one. Very close.
"There. The H crates. Twelve. Counted Friday."
A man's voice. Quick and low and rough. A voice Corin did not know. "And the lids?"
"Nailed. Look for yourself."
A pause. Boots crossing the stone. The creak of the stack as a hand leaned on it. The same creak the big gray hand had made.
"Fine." The rough voice. "The Nym boat's Monday. High water's at the half past one. They'll want it all aboard by the first bell, so the porters'll want waking by midnight. Tell Gorse."
"Tell him yourself," said Mistress Fane. "I'm not his girl."
"Then whose are you?"
No answer. A small dry sound that might have been a laugh. Then Fane's voice again, lower. "Is he coming down? He said he might."
"He's on the stair. He's slow on stairs. Bad knee."
"In the cold, too. At his age. I don't know why he doesn't send."
"Because he likes to see," said the rough voice. "He always likes to see."
And then, from the top of the shallow steps, from the ring passage, there came a third voice.
It was a man's voice. An old man's, or nearly. Soft, and a little breathless, as if from a long stair. A gentle voice. A kind voice. It had a smile in it. It was the kind of voice that tells a child the medicine will not taste so very bad, and that the nice doctor only wants to look at his chest, and that afterwards there will be a sweet.
"There we are," it said. "There we are. All down safely. No, no, Fane, don't fuss, my dear, I'm quite all right. Just give me a moment."
A slow step. Another. Down the shallow steps, into the room. A small tap with each step, as of a stick on the stone.
And round the corner of the low black arch, through the cold wet air off the river, through the stink of weed and oil and old rope, something came drifting to where Corin stood flat against the wall with the black water lapping at his boots.
A smell.
Sharp. Clean. Sweet. Cold in the nose, like frost.
Peppermint.
"Now then," said the kind voice, in the great square room, a few paces away. "Before we see the cold. Where are my glasses from Friday? Shelf forty, wasn't it? Fifty-two of them." A soft sigh, almost fond. "Such a good yield. Such very good children."
On the narrow ledge beside Corin, in the dark, Gideon's thin hand found his, and held on.
Outside, far above, in the cold, a bell began to ring over the sleeping city.
Corin counted the strokes, the way he had always counted them. With his body. With the slow beat in his blood that knew how many things there were.
One. Two. Three.
Four. Five. Six.
Seven. Eight. Nine.
Ten.
He felt the tenth go past him like a step in the dark that is not there. And then another, and he did not know it, he could not feel it, he could only hear it, a sound with no number.
The eleventh bell.
Up in Cinder Lane, in the frost, Coker would be coming back from the Bell. Walking home along the lane with his lantern swinging, to his stool and his newspaper and his little iron peep hatch, where a crutch lay across the corner of the coal flap.
And somewhere out in the dark, in a doorway, Nell was watching the lane.
Corin held Gideon's hand on the wet ledge, with the river at his feet and the peppermint in the air and the silence in his head where Malakor had been, and waited for three flashes of a lantern that he could not see.
The Doorway
Lyra had counted every bell since the ninth.
She had counted them standing up, in the deep doorway of the warehouse across Cinder Lane, with her back against the cold boards of the door and the Ledger inside her dress, against her heart. She had counted the quarter bells too, the small thin ones from the chapel by the river, that rang between the big ones. She had counted until her feet had gone numb in her boots, and then until she could not feel them at all, and then she had stopped thinking about her feet.
Nell stood at her elbow. The girl had not moved in two hours. She held the shuttered lantern against her chest with both hands, the way you would hold a baby, and kept her eyes on the lane, and every now and then she whispered something under her breath. Lyra had leaned close the first time, to hear it. Three times. Like a knock. Three times. Like a knock.
The lane was empty. It had been empty all night.
The frost had come down hard. It lay white on the cobbles, and on the roofs, and on the iron railings of the steps across the way. It made the dark seem lighter. Lyra could see the whole length of Cinder Lane from the doorway, from the corner by the river, where a single lamp burned in a bracket on the wall, to the far corner by the Bourse, where the great green dome rose up black against the stars. She could see the coal flap in the wall below the kitchen windows. She could see Coker's empty stool beside it, with frost on the seat.
And she could see the crutch.
She had seen it since the ninth bell. Since the moment Corin had laid it down across the little iron hatch in the corner of the flap, and slid down into the black, and the flap had closed over him. The ash crutch Doctor Silas had given him. Pale wood. It lay in the frost like a bone. She had watched it go white with frost, a little at a time, and she had told herself that nobody would see it. That nobody came down Cinder Lane on a Sunday night. That Coker would not be back till the eleventh bell, and Corin would be out long before.
The tenth bell had rung. She had counted it.
The quarter. The half. The three quarters.
Corin had not come out.
"Miss," whispered Nell. "It's nearly."
"I know."
"He said Coker comes at the eleventh. From the Bell." Nell's voice shook. "He comes that way, miss. From the river end. Past the lamp."
"I know."
Lyra's mouth was dry. She looked at the crutch.
The plan, said a voice in her head. Her own voice, but tired. Don't go in. Don't go near. You're the one who holds the book. If he doesn't come out, you take it home to Hessa.
She had promised him that. On the barge, before they left. With her hand on the Ledger.
She had not promised about the crutch.
The first stroke of the eleventh bell came over the roofs, heavy and slow, from the Bourse tower itself. So close and so loud that she felt it in the boards at her back.
And at the river end of Cinder Lane, under the lamp, a man came round the corner.
He was a big, slow man, wrapped in a long coat, with a muffler round his neck and a cap pulled low. He had a lantern in one hand, swinging. He walked the way a man walks who has had three pints and a hot pie and a sit by a good fire, and is not in any hurry at all to leave them for a cold stool.
Coker.
Nell made a small sound.
"Not him," said Lyra quickly. "Not for him. He said not Coker. Don't flash."
"But miss—"
"Don't."
The man came up the lane. Slow. His boots crunched on the frost. His lantern swung, and the yellow light swung with it, across the cobbles, across the walls, across the iron railings. He was humming something. A drinking song. Out of tune.
Forty paces from the coal flap. Thirty.
In a moment the light from his lantern would swing across the corner of the flap, and fall on a pale ash crutch lying in the frost where no crutch should be.
Lyra took the Ledger out of her dress.
She put it into Nell's hands, on top of the lantern. The girl looked at it, and then at her, with her eyes very wide.
"Hold it," said Lyra. "Don't let go of it. Whatever happens. If I don't come back, you take it to the fifth arch, and give it to Mother Grue, and tell her it's Hessa Doon's, and she'll know."
"Miss—"
Lyra was already out of the doorway.
She did not run. Running was wrong. A girl running in the dark is a thief. She walked. She came out into the lane with her hands in the pockets of Hessa's gray coat and Ellis's cap pulled down over her short hacked hair, and she walked fast and a little crooked, the way the girls on Comb Street walked when they had been out too late and had nowhere to go.
She reached the coal flap at the same moment as the light from Coker's lantern.
She bent down. She picked up the crutch. It was so cold that it burned her fingers. She tucked it under her arm, and leaned on it, hard, and bent her knee, and turned round into the yellow light.
"Oi," said Coker.
He had stopped. Ten paces off. He held up the lantern. His face under the cap was red and heavy and not at all unkind. Just surprised.
"What you doing there?" he said. "That's my wall."
"Sorry, mister." Lyra let her voice go thin and high and a little bit Comb Street. It was not hard. She had heard Nell talk for two days. "Sorry. I was just resting. Me leg." She leaned on the crutch and lifted her foot off the frost, as if it hurt. "It's the cold. Gets in it."
Coker looked at her.
He looked at the crutch, and at her foot, and at her face under the flat cap. Lyra kept her eyes down. She thought of the gray coat, and whether it looked too good. She thought of her hands, which had never done a day of work until a week ago, and she pushed them deeper into the pockets.
"You can't rest there," said Coker. "That's the Bourse. They don't like folk on the wall. They'll set the watch on you."
"Sorry, mister."
"Where you from?"
"The arches." It was true. "Mother Grue's."
Something changed a little in his heavy face. He breathed out through his nose, a long white cloud in the lamplight.
"Grue," he said. "She still going, is she? The old bird."
"Yes, mister."
He stood there. He looked at her a while longer. Then he put his hand in his coat pocket, and rummaged, and held something out.
A penny.
"Go on," he said. "Get yourself something hot. And don't let me catch you on my wall again, or I'll have to do something, and I don't want to."
Lyra took the penny. Her fingers touched his. They were warm. Thick and warm, from the fire at the Bell.
"Thank you, mister," she said.
She went. Down the lane, toward the Bourse end, away from the doorway. Leaning on the crutch. Limping. Slow. She did not look back. She heard him behind her, the scrape of the stool on the cobbles, the grunt as he sat down, the rustle of his newspaper.
At the corner by the Bourse she stopped, in the deep shadow of the great building, and pressed herself against the wall, and shook.
She shook so hard that the crutch rattled on the stones. She held it tighter. She held the penny in her other hand, in her pocket, so tight that the edge of it bit into her palm. She thought she was going to be sick. She was not sick.
He was kind, she thought. He gave me a penny.
He sits on that stool and keeps the door for them. For Fane. For the counting room. For the children on the glass.
And he gave me a penny, and told me to get something hot.
She did not know what to do with that. She put it away, to think about later. It went into the place where she had put the strap chair she had never seen, and the fifty-two children, and her mother's voice going keep them safe as it left her.
Then she looked up.
She was at the corner of the Bourse. The back corner, where Cinder Lane met the wider street that ran round behind the great building to the river. And there in that street, drawn up close to the wall, under a high arched gate with a lamp burning over it, there was a carriage.
It had not been there at the ninth bell. She was sure of it. She had watched this corner too.
It was a plain carriage. Black. Good. Not grand, like the Duke's, but well kept, with two gray horses in blankets standing quiet in the cold, and a driver up on the box wrapped to the eyes, asleep or nearly. The lamp over the gate fell on the carriage door. And on the door there was a painted crest.
Lyra knew crests. She had grown up on the Hill. She had learned the great houses before she learned her letters. This was not a house.
It was a round seal, the size of a plate. Blue, with a white rim. In the middle, a white hand, open, palm out, and over the hand a small gold lamp. And round the white rim, in neat gold letters, words she could just read in the lamplight.
THE MINISTRY. OFFICE OF PUBLIC HEALTH.
And under that, smaller: FOR THE CARE OF THE CITY.
Lyra stood very still in the shadow, with the crutch under her arm, and looked at it.
The Health.
A gentleman from the Health, Bet had said. Gives out sweets on the Fish Stairs. Friday mornings.
The gate under the lamp was the kitchen gate. She knew it from Corin's map in the Ledger. The kitchen gate, and the kitchen door beyond it, and the kitchen stair beyond that, going down.
The carriage door was not quite shut. And from the dark inside it, faint and cold on the frosty air, so faint she might have dreamed it, there drifted a smell.
Peppermint.
Behind her, far down Cinder Lane, in the deep doorway of the warehouse, a shuttered lantern opened, and closed, and opened, and closed, and opened.
Three times.
Like a knock.
