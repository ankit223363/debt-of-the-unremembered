Chapter 38: Under the Glass Dome, Part 4 of 5: The Bench by the Lift
The Bench by the Lift
Corin put his hand over the lantern.
He did it without thinking. The way you would put your hand over a baby's mouth. The yellow light went out between his fingers, and the great square room went dim, lit now only by the hanging oil lamp over the crates, turned low, and by the gray seep from the brass house on the far wall.
Gideon did not move. He stood beside the crates with his coat buttoned over the jar and his hands at his sides, quite still, and looked toward the shallow steps and the ring passage beyond them. His face was calm. He was listening the way a dog listens. All of him pointed one way.
The groan came again. Wood, bending. And then a sound Corin had never heard before. A slow heavy scrape, like a sack of coal being dragged across stone. And then another. And another. Coming closer, round the curve of the ring passage, from the east.
Footsteps.
But not like any footsteps Corin knew. Too slow. Too heavy. Too far apart. As if whoever was walking had to stop and think between each step and the next, and lift each foot like a stone, and put it down like a stone. Scrape. A long wait. Scrape. A long wait.
"The bench," Gideon whispered. "By the lift."
Corin had read it in his own Ledger that morning. In Lyra's hand. The porters' bench by the lift. Two of them. Big. They don't talk.
"You said they sleep," he breathed. "On Sundays."
"They do." Gideon did not look at him. "Mostly."
Scrape. A long wait. Scrape.
Corin looked round the great square room for somewhere to go. There was nowhere. The crates, in the middle, in the lamplight. The low black arch to the river, in the gray. The rack of tools by the steps. The three shallow steps up to the ring passage, which was where the footsteps were coming from. Bare stone walls. Bare stone floor. A room made to be seen across.
The crates, then. Behind the crates.
He took Gideon by the sleeve and pulled him down, and they crouched together behind the stack, on the side away from the steps, with their backs to the rough pine. Corin's bad leg would not bend. He had to put it out straight in front of him along the floor, stiff as a plank, with the bound rag on it black and wet in the low light. He drew the lantern in against his chest and kept his hand over it. The heat of the glass burned his palm through the coal and the paint. He did not move it.
Between two crates there was a crack, about as wide as a finger. He put his eye to it.
He could see the three shallow steps. And the dark mouth of the ring passage beyond them, where the Ash lamps on their brackets threw their thin gray light on the curved wall.
Scrape.
Something came into the gray light.
At first Corin did not understand what he was looking at. It was too big. It filled the passage. It was a shape like a door that had learned to walk, wide and dark and slow, with a head that had to bend to clear the arch of the ceiling. It came round the curve of the wall and stopped at the top of the shallow steps, and stood there, swaying a little, with the Ash lamp behind it.
A man.
The biggest man Corin had ever seen. Bigger than Big Abel the tanner. Bigger than the docker who had carried Wat down the barge steps at Brindle. He was taller than the doorway of No. 14, and broad as a cart, with shoulders that were round and thick from carrying, and long arms that hung down nearly to his knees. He wore a porter's leather apron, black with grease, over a shirt with no collar. His trousers were held up with rope. His feet were bare. Huge bare feet, gray with cold, on the stone.
His hands were the worst. They hung at his sides, open, the fingers a little curled, and they were each as big as a spade. And they were gray. Not Ash gray. Not like Corin's. The gray of dead skin. Of a fish on a slab. The nails were thick and yellow and split.
Corin looked up at the face.
It was a wide face. A square face, with a heavy jaw, and a thick neck. A face that should have belonged to a man who laughed loud in taverns and lifted barrels for a bet. The hair on the big head was cut very short, close to the skull, gray-brown, like a field after harvest. There was a scar on the cheek, old and white. The nose had been broken, long ago, and set crooked.
And the eyes were empty.
Not shut. Not sleeping. Open, and pale, and looking straight ahead, down the steps and across the great square room toward the crates. Toward the crack where Corin's eye was. And there was nothing in them at all. No thought. No question. No knowing. They were like two windows in a house where nobody has lived for a long, long time.
Corin had seen eyes like that before.
In the Archive. In the lowest room, where they kept the Slates. The ones who had burned too much. Who had paid and paid until there was nothing left to pay with, and then had paid that too. The Archive kept them for a while, in white beds in a long white room, and fed them broth with a spoon, and turned them twice a day so they would not get sores. And then they were moved somewhere else, and their beds were made up fresh.
A Slate, said Malakor, very quietly, very far away. A deep one.
The big man stood at the top of the steps. He did not come down. He swayed. His empty eyes looked at the crates.
And then, behind him, the second one came.
He came round the curve of the ring passage the same way. Scrape. A long wait. Scrape. He was a little shorter than the first, but not much. A little broader, if that was possible. The same leather apron. The same rope. The same bare gray feet. The same spade hands hanging open.
The same face.
Not like. The same. The same square jaw, and the same heavy neck, and the same short gray-brown hair. Brothers, Corin thought. Twins, almost. One a few years older. The second had no scar on the cheek, and his nose was straight. But the face under it was the face of the first, and the eyes were the same empty windows.
He came and stood beside his brother at the top of the shallow steps. Their shoulders nearly touched. They stood there together, two great gray shapes in the gray light, swaying a little, and looked down into the room.
And the second one was humming.
Corin heard it now, under the great hum of the middle and the breathing of the floor. A thin, small sound. It came out of that huge chest like a thread pulled out of a sack. A tune. Low and slow, going up a little and then down, and round again. Over and over. The same few notes. With no words. With no breath in it, almost. The way a man hums who does not know he is humming.
Beside Corin, behind the crates, Gideon had gone very still.
Not the stillness of hiding. Another kind. Corin felt it through the sleeve of the too-big coat where his hand still held it. The boy's arm had gone stiff. Not trembling. Just stiff. Like a stick.
Corin took his eye from the crack and turned his head.
Gideon was not looking through the crack. He did not need to. He was sitting with his back to the crates and his eyes wide open, looking at nothing, at the bare stone wall across from them, and his lips were moving.
No sound came out. But Corin could see the shape of it. Could see the lips go round and flat and round again, in time with the thin tune from the top of the steps. Up a little, and down. And round again.
Gideon was humming it too. Without a sound. Without knowing.
The tune he hummed in his sleep. The tune Mother Grue had heard in her arches, and said she knew. The tune a big man had hummed, long ago, carrying a small boy on his shoulders down Comb Street to the docks, when the boy was four and the man was whole.
Big Gideon Hollow, Corin thought. And Saul.
Dockers. Who went to the war together, and came back, and went carrying for the Bourse.
Carrying.
He put his eye back to the crack.
The two great gray men stood at the top of the shallow steps. The humming one, the younger one, with no scar. And the older one, with the scar and the broken nose, who did not hum. Saul, then. Or Gideon. Corin did not know which was which. He did not think it mattered any more. He did not think either of them knew.
The older one, the scarred one, lifted his great head.
Slowly. Like a horse lifting its head from a trough. And his nose moved. The wide crooked nostrils went wider. He was smelling the air.
Corin's heart stopped.
The blood, he thought. My leg. The rag.
The big head turned. A little to the left. A little to the right. The empty eyes did not change. But the nostrils moved, and moved, the way a dog's do when it has found the edge of something.
Then he came down the steps.
One. Scrape. A long wait. Two. Scrape. Three.
He was in the room. He was on the bare stone floor of the great square room, in the lamplight, and he was coming across it, toward the crates. Scrape. Slow. So slow. Each step like a stone dropped in a well. His spade hands hung open at his sides.
Behind him the younger one stood at the top of the steps and hummed.
Corin did not breathe. He did not move. He could not move. His bad leg was out in front of him, straight, along the floor, and the end of it, the foot in its wet boot, stuck out past the edge of the crates. Out into the open. Where anybody coming round the stack would see it.
He could not pull it in. It would not bend. If he tried, it would scrape on the stone. And the big man would hear.
Scrape.
The big man was at the crates. On the other side of the stack. Corin could hear him breathing now. A slow, deep, heavy breath, in and out, like the floor. He could smell him. Grease and sweat and cold stone and something else, something sour and old, like a cellar where apples have been left to rot.
The stack of crates moved.
Just a little. Just a creak. The big man had put one of his great gray hands on top of it, the way you would put your hand on a table. And leaned.
And stood there. Breathing. Smelling.
Corin looked up.
Above him, over the top of the crates, against the yellow light of the hanging lamp, he could see the hand. The great gray fingers, curled over the edge of the top crate. A thumb as thick as a candle. A thick split yellow nail. Not two feet above his head.
The fingers moved. Slowly. Felt along the edge of the crate. Found the lid. The corner of the lid, where Corin had pushed the nails back in with the crowbar. Not all the way. Not quite.
The big gray thumb pressed on it.
The nails went down into their holes with a small soft creak. All the way.
The thumb pressed again, on the next corner. And the next. As if this was a thing it had done a thousand times. As if the hand knew, all on its own, that lids should be shut, and nails should be home. The hand finished its work. It lay flat on the lid for a moment. Then it lifted away.
Scrape.
The big man had turned.
Scrape.
He was going back. Across the floor. Toward the steps. Slow. So slow.
Scrape.
Corin let his breath out. A little. A thread of it. Through his teeth.
And the big man stopped.
In the middle of the floor. In the lamplight. With his back to the crates. He stood there, with his great head a little bowed, and his spade hands hanging.
And then, very slowly, he turned his head. Not his body. Just his head. Over his great round shoulder. Back toward the crates.
Toward the end of the stack. Where a wet black boot stuck out on the stone.
The empty eyes looked at it.
Corin did not breathe.
The eyes did not change. Nothing came into them. Nothing at all. They looked at the boot the way they had looked at the crates, and at the room, and at the wall. A thing in the world. A shape. Nothing more.
The big head turned away again.
Scrape.
The big man went up the three shallow steps, one at a time, and stood beside his brother at the top. The humming one did not stop humming. They stood there together, side by side, for a long moment. Then the older one turned, and went back along the ring passage, toward the east, toward the lift. Scrape. A long wait. Scrape.
The younger one stayed.
He stood at the top of the steps, alone now, in the gray light, swaying. Humming. His empty eyes looked down into the great square room.
At the crates.
At the gap between the crates.
And beside Corin, behind the stack, Gideon got up.
Corin Feels It for Him
Corin grabbed for him and missed.
His hand closed on the hem of the too-big coat, and the cloth slid through his coal-black fingers, and was gone. He could not follow. His leg was out straight along the floor like a log, and to get up he would have had to roll onto his good knee and push with both hands against the crates, and the crates would creak, and the floor would scrape.
He stayed where he was. He put his eye back to the crack. And watched.
Gideon walked out from behind the stack.
He did not hurry. He did not creep. He walked the way he walked everywhere, in his new boots that were too big for him, with his thin shoulders a little hunched under the coat, and his hands at his sides. Out into the open floor of the great square room. Into the yellow lamplight. Across the bare stone toward the shallow steps, where the younger of the two great gray men stood alone at the top, swaying, humming his thin tune.
The empty eyes watched him come. They did not change.
Gideon stopped at the foot of the steps.
He looked up. He had to look a long way up. The top of his head did not reach the big man's belt. He stood there, very small, at the bottom of the three shallow steps, with his white thin face turned up into the gray light, and his pale calm eyes on the huge gray face above him.
The humming went on.
Gideon listened to it. His head was on one side. The way a bird holds its head, listening for a worm. He listened for a long time. Corin counted his own heartbeats, behind the crates, and lost count. He could not feel them past nine.
Then Gideon opened his mouth. And hummed.
Out loud this time. Small and thin and a little off the note, in a boy's voice that had not broken yet. The same tune. Up a little, and down, and round again. He hummed it with the big man, under him, beside him. Not quite in time. Then in time. The two sounds went up together in the great square room, the huge thin thread from the chest at the top of the steps and the small thin thread from the boy at the bottom, and wound round each other, and came down together, and went round again.
The big man did not look at him.
His empty eyes stayed on the crates. His spade hands stayed open at his sides. Nothing moved in his face. He swayed a little. He hummed.
Gideon stopped humming.
He stood there for a moment longer, with his head on one side. Then he put up his hand. His thin white hand, with the dirt under the nails and the knuckles red and cracked with cold. He reached up, as high as he could reach, and he took hold of the big man's apron. Of the edge of the greasy black leather, where it hung down over the rope. And held it. The way a small child holds the edge of its father's coat in a crowd, so as not to be lost.
The humming did not stop.
The big man did not look down.
They stood like that. The big gray man at the top of the steps, swaying and humming and looking at nothing. The small white boy at the bottom, holding his apron, looking up at him. For a long, long time.
Then Gideon let go.
He let go carefully. He opened his fingers one at a time and let the leather fall back against the rope. He took his hand down and put it in his coat pocket.
He turned round. And walked back across the bare stone floor, in the yellow light, in his too-big boots, to the crates. And came round the end of the stack, past the wet black boot sticking out on the floor, and sat down again beside Corin with his back to the rough pine.
At the top of the steps, the big man swayed. He hummed. After a while, slowly, he turned, the way his brother had turned. Scrape. A long wait. Scrape. He went away along the ring passage, toward the east, toward the bench by the lift. The humming went with him, thinner and thinner, round the curve of the wall, until Corin could not tell it from the hum of the middle any more.
And then it was gone.
Gideon sat beside him. He had his hands in his pockets and his knees drawn up, and he was looking at the stone wall across from them. His face was quite calm.
"That was my uncle," he said. "Saul. The one that hums."
He said it in an ordinary voice. Not a whisper. Corin did not tell him to whisper. He did not think he could have said anything at all.
"The other one's my father," said Gideon. "Big Gideon. I thought it was. I wasn't sure. When I was little, before, he used to have a scar there." He touched his own cheek. "From a hook. On the docks. I remember Mam saying he was lucky it wasn't his eye." He thought about it. "He's got it still. So that's him."
Corin said nothing.
"I've seen them before," said Gideon. "When I was carrying. Before they put me out. I used to see them on the bench, and sometimes on the stair, with crates. I thought maybe. But I couldn't feel if it was. You know. You'd think you'd feel it. If it was your own father." He moved one shoulder. "I didn't feel anything. So I thought maybe it wasn't."
He looked at the wall.
"I still don't," he said. "Feel anything. I know it's him. I know it's my father, and that's Saul, and they used to carry me. Down Comb Street. On their shoulders. Saul used to sing that, and Da used to tell him to shut up, he couldn't hold a note in a bucket." A small pause. "I know that. I know it like I know the number on my jar. But it's just a thing I know." He turned his pale calm eyes on Corin. "Is that wrong? Is it meant to be different?"
Corin opened his mouth.
Nothing came out of it.
He was looking at the boy's face. The thin white face, with the pale calm eyes, in the low yellow light behind the crates. Thirteen years old, and small for it. Sitting on a cold floor under a tavern with his father's cold in a jar inside his coat, and his own cold in a jar inside his coat, and asking, as calmly as you would ask the way to the market, whether it was wrong not to feel anything when you held your father's apron and he did not know you.
And Corin felt it.
Not for himself. For the boy. It came up out of somewhere very deep. Out of the place behind his breastbone where the Ledger sat, where the names were. Where Hessa was, and Wat, and Old Jory and Bramble, and Marta and her bread, and the girl with the plait whose name he did not know any more. It came up through him like water through a cracked floor. Hot. Heavy. Too big for him to hold.
The boy on the bottom step, holding the apron. The big man at the top, swaying, looking at nothing. The thin tune going up and coming down.
The small hand pulling away. Corin, where are they going?
He felt the tears come before he knew they were coming. They ran out of his eyes and down his face, hot through the coal dust, and dripped off his chin onto the front of his shirt. He did not wipe them. He did not make a sound. He sat there behind the crates with his leg out stiff along the floor and the lantern against his chest and wept, quite silently, like a pipe that has burst.
Gideon watched him.
He watched for a long time, with his head a little on one side. The way he had listened to the humming. Then he reached out with one thin finger, and touched one of the tears on Corin's cheek, where it had cut a clean white line down through the black of the coal. He looked at the wet on his fingertip.
"Oh," he said.
And then, after a while: "Is that for me?"
Corin nodded. He could not speak.
Gideon looked at his fingertip a little longer. Then he wiped it carefully on the front of his coat, over the place where the jar was.
"Thank you," he said. Quite seriously. As if Corin had given him a loaf. "I'll keep that too."
Corin, said Malakor.
Very far away. Very faint. Like a voice calling from the other end of a long field, in the wind.
Corin. Do not burn anything. Not now. Not here. You are too open. If you reach for anything now, you will not stop.
Corin knew. He could feel it. He could feel the whole of himself leaning, the way a man leans over the edge of a well. All he would have to do was reach. Reach for any of it. For the warm sluice wheel on the Long Pond, or Marta's kitchen, or Hessa's needle going in and out. Reach for it and light it and make something with it. A ward. A key. A small hard light to carry. Anything. And it would come up out of him with all the rest of this, all the hot heavy water, and it would not stop coming.
I'm not, he said. I won't.
Good. A long pause. The voice was very thin. That is good, Corin.
He wiped his face at last. With the back of his hand, which made it worse. He felt the coal smear across his cheek, and the paint flake off his knuckles. He did not care.
"We have to go," he whispered. His voice came out cracked and strange. "The crutch. Coker. The eleventh bell."
Gideon nodded. He stood up. He put down his hand to Corin, and Corin took it, and the boy leaned back with all his small weight while Corin rolled onto his good knee and pushed against the crates and came up, slowly, onto his good leg, with the bad one hanging. The crates creaked. Nobody came.
They stood there in the low light, the two of them, swaying a little. Corin with his hand on the boy's thin shoulder for balance. Gideon with his coat buttoned up over his jar.
Corin looked once more at the crates. At the brown card labels. THE FROST REACH OF NYM.
Then he looked across the room at the low black arch, where the river lapped in the gray, and the cold came in.
Monday night, he thought. High tide. A low boat with no lights.
I'll be here.
He did not know how. He did not know with what. He did not know who would come with him, or whether any of them would come back. But he made the promise, there in the great square room under the Bourse, the way he had made it on the bridge over the Tanners' Ditch with the tannery stink in his nose. Without words. Into the place behind his breastbone where the names were.
Gideon Hollow. Big Gideon. Saul.
They turned toward the steps. Toward the ring passage, and the long way round to the west, and the brass lamp at the foot of two nines and four, and the counting room, and the dark iron door at the top of the stairs, and the coal chute, and Cinder Lane, and the crutch on the peep hatch.
And from far round the curve of the ring passage, from the east, from the kitchen stair, they heard a door open.
