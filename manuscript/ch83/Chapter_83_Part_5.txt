Chapter 83: The Last Fold, Part 5 of 5: Her Brown Book
Her Brown Book
After a long time she sat up and took out her book.
She did not decide to. Her hand went inside her coat, on the left, and found it, the way it had found the stone in her other pocket. The little brown book from Quill Row. Brown card covers gone soft at the corners from being carried. A dozen sheets, folded and sewn down the middle with brown thread. Fourpence. She had bought it in the autumn, on her way home from the Long Office, on a day when she had taken something out of a boy on the Fish Stairs and had not been able to stop thinking about it.
She opened it on her knee and tilted it toward the last of the lantern light.
She turned the pages slowly. She knew what was on them. She read them anyway.
Boy, about seven. Fish Stairs. Health cart. His first fish. Given back to nobody.
Woman, old. The river stairs. Her husband saying goodnight. Given back to nobody.
Man, about forty. Ministry cells. The name of the street he was born on. Given back to nobody.
Every line ended the same way. She had written them all in the same small careful hand, the hand they had taught her at the Collegium, so that her reports would be easy to copy. Given back to nobody. She had not known why she wrote that. It had seemed like the only true thing she could say about it. The things went into jars and the jars went down to the Bank, and nobody got them back. Not the boy. Not the old woman. Nobody.
She turned the page.
Sergeant Moll.
That was all. She had started that one and stopped. Because she had not taken anything from Sergeant Moll. She had only sat with him in Room Nine every morning and tapped heard on the back of his hand. And he had tapped back. And she had not known what to write.
She turned the page.
Corwen Lusk.
Farmer.
He stood in his own gate.
In big shaky letters, not her careful hand at all. Pressed so hard into the paper that the pencil had gone through in one place, on the G. Nothing after it. No given back. She had not known how to finish it. She still did not.
She looked at it for a long time.
Then she took out her pencil. The stub of it, from the inside of the cover, where she kept it tucked behind the thread. She turned to the next clean page. And she began to write.
Not a line. More than a line. She had never written more than a line in the brown book before. She wrote slowly, in the careful hand at first and then not, as the letters began to go their own way. She wrote what she had seen. Exactly. The way he had told her to.
Monday night. The Ridges. A yard with three walls. Two iron men broken on the glass. One on its face, one on its side. The spring of the second one lying beside it. Blood. A great deal of it. Strips of gray cloth. A broken spear. The Inquisitor took off his hat. Master Ferrand took off his glove and laid his hand on the iron and said, Somebody has been very clever.
A heap of glass at the foot of a fallen wall. Built by hand. With a tiny piece on top. Somebody's grave. The Inquisitor took off his hat again. Pike took off his cap.
Tuesday. The bowl. The hum climbing. The last cask. The glass going clear.
The beach. Gray stones with white rings. I picked one up. I did not know why. It is the same as the one I gave away.
The Eye. The hum stopped. My hands came back.
The dome. A man and a girl by the door. Two with spears. Master Ferrand said his word and the iron turned toward the girl. The Inquisitor said, Wait. Master Ferrand said the Archon's word outranks his. The Inquisitor said, Not today. Today it waits till morning.
The iron waited.
She stopped. Her hand was aching. She flexed the fingers inside her glove and looked at what she had written.
It did not look like a report. Reports did not have I in them. Reports said the cadet observed and the subject was seen to. They did not say I picked one up. I did not know why.
But it was true. Every word of it. She had seen it, and she had written it, and a stranger could read it and see it.
She thought about the Inquisitor's grandfather, and the chandler's shop by the river, and the name taken off something. She did not write that. It was not hers to write.
She thought about Harrow's sister-in-law, Ada, with her apron burned through. She did not write that either.
She thought about Pike. She had a red thread. She did not write that.
And she thought about the page in the Inquisitor's black book with her name on it, torn out and folded eleven times and put away over his heart. She held her pencil over the paper for a long time.
She did not write that.
Instead she turned back. Back to the page with the farmer on it. Corwen Lusk. Farmer. He stood in his own gate. She looked at the big shaky letters and the hole the pencil had made in the G.
And under it, slowly, in her own small careful hand, she wrote one more line.
Remembered.
She looked at it. One word. It was not given back. She could not give him back. Nobody could. He was under a sack in a lane in the valleys with his boots sticking out, and his wife had every coin the Inquisitor owned in her floury hand, and his boy had watched her through the window. Nothing could give him back.
But she could remember him. She could keep him. In the book. Over her heart. And not give him to anybody, and not let anybody take him.
Remembered.
She closed the book.
She put it back inside her coat, on the left, and buttoned the coat over it, and put her hand flat on the place for a moment, the way the Inquisitor had put his hand on his waistcoat pocket out on the black glass.
Then she lay down again. On her side, with her knees drawn up, and her right hand in her pocket round the cold smooth stone.
Across the camp Master Ferrand's pencil had stopped.
She did not know when. She did not look to see if he was watching her. She lay and looked at the dome instead, at the dim red line of firelight down the side of its iron door, and the two still shapes on guard, and the man and the child sitting against its curve. And the stars came down round all of it, falling, falling, into the black.
The Song across the Glass
The stars turned.
Wren lay on the black glass with her blanket over her and watched them do it. She had never watched stars turn before. In the city you could not see enough of them, for the lamps and the smoke. On the Glass she had been too tired, and too cold, and the stars there had lain on the top of the glass like frost on a window, so that she had felt as if she were walking on them. Here they were different. Here they were above her and below her both, and they turned together, slowly, the whole great double bowl of them wheeling round some point she could not find, and she lay in the middle of it and could not sleep.
The camp slept round her.
Or most of it. Pike had stopped saying the girl's name. Harrow had stopped snoring. The other two Enforcers lay like logs. The lanterns had burned down low, and the little pool of yellow light they threw into the glass had shrunk, so that the drowned farm far below was only a dark shape now, a roof and a yard and a well, with no bucket that she could see.
Master Ferrand's slit of light had gone out. She did not know when. She could see him, a pale shape on his square of blanket, sitting up. Still sitting up. With his head bowed. She did not know if he was asleep. She did not think he ever was.
And the Inquisitor sat where he had sat all night, with his knees up and his hands on them, looking at the dome.
Wren looked at the dome too.
The orange line of firelight down the side of the iron door had gone dim. Red. Somebody inside had let the fire burn down. The two spear-carriers had changed. There were two others now, standing in the same places, as still as posts. And on the glass beside them, against the curve of the dome, the man and the child.
The child was talking.
Wren could hear her. She had been able to hear her, a little, for some time. That was the Eye. On the Eye, sound went a long way. There was nothing to stop it. No hum, no wind, no walls. A voice went out across the flat black glass in the dead silence of the night and did not fade, or hardly, so that from three hundred paces Wren could hear the child the way you hear somebody talking in the next room through an open door. Not every word. But the shape of it. The rise and fall.
It went on and on.
It was a high quick small voice, like a sparrow's. It did not seem to need anybody to answer it. It went up at the ends of things, as if it were asking questions, and then went straight on without waiting. It laughed, once, at something. It went low, and serious, and slow, for a while. Then it went quick again.
The man said nothing. Wren could see him. A long dark shape against the dome, sitting, with the smaller shape of the child against his side. He did not move. He did not answer. But the child talked to him as if he did. As if he were the best listener in the world.
Wren lay and listened to the shape of it and did not know why her eyes were stinging.
And then the child stopped talking.
There was a silence. A long one.
And then the child began to sing.
Wren knew the tune before the first note was finished.
It went into her like cold water. She lay on the glass and did not move. She did not breathe. She had heard it before. Not this. Not all of it. But the start of it. Three notes. Low, high, a little lower. She had hummed them herself in the Long Office, one Friday, without meaning to, and every head in the room had come up. She had hummed them in Room Nine for Sergeant Moll, and Moll had hummed them back, and then a fourth, going up, and hung there. She had hummed them in the dark on the Glass, three nights ago, alone, and from his blankets the Inquisitor had hummed the fourth.
She had never heard what came after.
She heard it now.
The child sang the three notes. And then the fourth, going up, and hanging, high and thin and sweet, like a question. And then she did not stop. She went on. Down from the high note, in a long slow curve, and up again, and round. And words came with it. Wren could not catch them. They were too far away. But she could hear that there were words, and that they were the kind of words that go with an old tune that everybody's grandmother knows. Simple ones. Ones that rhymed.
The child's voice was thin and a little flat. It wobbled on the high notes. It was the voice of a child singing to herself in the dark to keep herself brave, and it went out across the black glass of the Eye under the turning stars, small and clear and alone, and nothing in all that great silence came to stop it.
Verse after verse.
Wren lay and listened. She could not have moved if the iron men had walked over her.
It was the whole song. She knew that. She did not know how she knew. She had only ever had the start of it, three notes, a scrap, the way you have a scrap of a dream when you wake. And here was the rest of it. All of it. Going on and on, out of the dark, from a child she did not know, sitting beside a silent man at the door of a dead woman's house.
And then the last verse.
She knew it was the last. You always know. The tune came round for the last time, and slowed, and came down. Down from the high hanging note, slowly, all the way, lower and lower, softer and softer, the way you set something down at the end of a long day when you have carried it far enough. And at the very end there were two or three words that Wren did catch. They came across the glass on the last low note, clear, in the child's thin small voice.
—came home.
And then nothing.
The silence came back over the Eye like water closing over a stone.
Wren found that she was humming.
She did not know when she had started. She was lying on her side on the cold black glass with her blanket pulled up to her chin, and her lips were closed, and in her throat, very softly, she was humming. Three notes. Low, high, a little lower.
She stopped.
She stopped so suddenly that she bit her tongue. She lay rigid on the glass with her heart slamming. She had not meant to. She had not meant to at all. It had come up out of her by itself, the way your hand goes to a stone in your pocket, the way your feet go down a stair you know in the dark.
And on the Eye, where sound went such a long way.
She did not move. She lay and listened. For a voice. For a step. For Master Ferrand's soft careful cough.
Nothing.
She turned her head, very slowly, a finger's width at a time, and looked across the camp.
Master Ferrand was still sitting on his square of blanket with his head bowed. He had not moved.
She looked the other way. At the Inquisitor.
He had not moved either. He was sitting with his knees up and his hands on them, as he had sat all night. Looking at the dome. His face, in the dying lantern light, was turned away from her. She could only see the line of his cheek, and his jaw.
But his bare right hand had moved.
It was not on his knee anymore. It was on his chest. Flat. On the left side of his waistcoat, over the pocket. Over his heart. And it was pressed there, hard, the fingers spread, the way you press your hand over a wound.
Wren looked at it for a long time.
Then she turned her head back, slowly, and shut her eyes, and lay still. And did not hum again.
Winding
She must have slept, in the end. A little. Because she woke.
It was the gray hour before the dawn, when the stars have gone pale and thin and the sky has not yet decided what color it will be. The cold had got worse. Her feet were numb inside her boots. Her right hand had gone to sleep in her pocket, still curled round the stone.
She did not know what had woken her. Then she did.
A sound. From the dome. The scrape of iron on stone, long and low. The door.
She lifted her head.
The iron door in the side of the dome had moved. The narrow black gap down its side had grown wider. And in the gap there was somebody. A man. Very big. So big that he had to turn sideways to come out, and bend his head. He came out onto the glass in the gray light, slowly, stiffly, like a man whose back hurts, and stood for a moment looking across the Eye. At the camp. At the iron men standing in their row. At the Inquisitor.
Then he turned away and went to the place beside the door where the child was.
She was asleep. Wren could see that, even from here. A small hump under a heap of blankets against the man's side. The big man bent down. Wren saw him put his hands under the blankets. And lift. The whole hump of them, child and blankets and all, came up off the glass in his arms, and he settled it over his shoulder, the way you carry a sleeping child home from a fair. Gently. With one big hand on the back of the blankets where the child's head would be.
The silent man did not move.
The big man stood over him for a moment with the child on his shoulder. He seemed to be saying something. Wren could not hear it. Then he bent again, awkwardly, with the child held against him, and set something down on the glass beside the silent man. Something gray and folded. A blanket.
The silent man did not look at it. He went on looking south. At the camp. At the iron men. At them.
The big man straightened, slowly, with a hand on his back. He looked across the glass once more. And then he turned and went sideways in through the gap in the door with the child over his shoulder, and was gone.
The door did not close. The gap stayed as it was.
And the silent man sat on beside it. Alone now. With his bound hands palm up in his lap and the folded blanket on the glass beside him where he would not use it. Looking at them across the gray morning.
The Inquisitor had seen it too. Wren saw his head turn, very slightly, and follow the big man in through the door, and come back to the silent man, and stay there.
And then there was another sound.
A small one. A tiny dry clicking, like a key turning in a well-oiled lock. Then another. Then another.
Wren turned her head.
Master Ferrand was standing in front of the iron men.
He had got up from his blanket while she was watching the dome. She had not heard him. She never did. He was standing by the first iron man in his near-white coat, neat and pale in the gray light, without his hat, with his fine fair hair a little ruffled from the night. And in his gloved right hand he had something small and bright.
A key.
She had seen it before. In the cellar under the Records yard, in Old Rook's shaking hands. A brass key, longer than a finger, with a bow at the end of it shaped like a bird. A small bird, with its wings folded, and its beak open. Old Rook had wound the iron men with it, one by one, on the trestles, with his hands steady only while he was working.
Master Ferrand's hands were steady all the time.
He had fitted the key into the hole in the square brass plate in the small of the first iron man's back. And he was turning it. Slowly. Click. Click. Click. Each turn the same. Each click the same. He was not hurrying. He held the iron man's shoulder with his other hand, lightly, the way you would hold the shoulder of a child you were buttoning into a coat. And he was smiling at it.
Not at anybody. At the iron man. At its blank oval head with the single slit in it. As if it were a dog he was fond of.
Click. Click. Click.
The iron man began to tick.
Quietly at first. Then louder. The sound came up out of its chest like a heart starting. And Master Ferrand nodded to it, pleased, and patted its shoulder twice, and took the key out, and moved on to the next.
Click. Click. Click.
The Enforcers were waking. Wren heard them behind her, the shuffle and groan of men getting up off cold glass, a cough, Pike's thin voice asking something and Harrow's low one telling him to hush. She did not look round. She was watching Master Ferrand.
The second iron man began to tick. Master Ferrand patted its shoulder. Moved on.
Click. Click. Click.
The third.
Wren got up. She did not know why. She got up off the glass with her blanket falling off her shoulders and her numb feet stinging as the blood came back, and she found that she had taken a step. Toward them. Toward the iron men. As if to see better. As if to stop something. She did not know which.
The third iron man began to tick.
And all three of them ticked together. In one beat. The sound went out across the gray morning, across the silent Eye, loud, steady, like a roomful of clocks all striking the same hour. Toward the dome. Toward the man sitting alone by the door.
Master Ferrand took the bird key out of the third iron man's back. He wiped it carefully on a fold of his near-white coat, and put it away inside his coat, on the left, over his heart, next to his notebook. And then he turned round.
And saw her.
She was standing three paces from him. Closer than she had ever stood to him of her own choosing. With her blanket on the glass behind her, and her hand in her pocket, and her face, she supposed, white.
Master Ferrand smiled.
Kindly. The kindest smile in the world. The smile of a man who has never once been unhappy.
"If you would stand back, Cadet," he said softly. He lifted one gloved hand, a little, and moved it in the air between them, gently, the way you would shoo a cat off a chair. "They do get confused about who is in the way."
