Chapter 83: The Last Fold, Part 2 of 5: Wait
Wait
They walked out across the Eye toward the dome.
The sun went down while they walked. Wren felt it go behind her right shoulder. The red light on the black glass went darker, wine-colored, and the long shadows of the walking men went longer still, stretching away ahead of them across the Eye, and then they faded. The drowned village under their feet went dark, roof by roof. The sky turned a strange clear green, like the glass of an old bottle. One star came out. Then more.
And the iron men walked ahead of them. Three of them, in a row. Ticking. On the Eye, with no hum, the ticking was loud. Far louder than it had been on the Glass. It went out ahead of them across the silence, tick, tick, tick, all three together, in the one beat Master Ferrand had given them, and nothing came back. Nothing echoed. The sound went out and out and was swallowed.
The dome came closer.
It was bigger than Wren had thought. Not big. A cottage. But stranger. It was made of rings of gray stone with bands of iron going over them, curved, like the ribs of a basket. And all of it, stone and iron together, had run. Melted. A long time ago. So that the stones had slumped into each other at the edges and the iron bands had gone soft and glassy, and down the sides of the whole thing there ran long red streaks. Rust. Like tears.
There was a door. On the side facing them. South. An iron door, man-high, set into the curve of the dome. Wren could see it in the last of the green light. It was open. Or nearly open. Slid back, a little, into the wall, so that there was a narrow black gap down one side of it.
And beside the door, there were people.
Two of them, standing. Thin and straight, with long sticks in their hands. Not sticks. Spears. Pale shafts with something white bound to the tips. Their heads were shaved. And down the middle of each face, from the top of the head to the tip of the nose, there ran a line. Gray.
Wren had seen those lines before. On the children at Last Thorn. Before the fire.
And beside them, on the glass, against the curve of the dome, two more people. Sitting.
A man and a girl.
The girl was small. A child. Wren could see a shock of short hair sticking up, and a gray shirt, and bare skinny arms. She was sitting with her knees pulled up, close against the man's side. She had seen them coming. She was on her feet now, standing, with one hand on the man's shoulder, staring.
The man did not get up.
He was thin. Very thin. A long thin body in clothes that hung on it like clothes on a scarecrow, folded up against the wall of the dome with his long legs crossed. He had a beard. Dark, untidy. His hands were bound up in strips of cloth, both of them, and lay in his lap, palm up, the way you would hold your hands out to catch rain.
And he was looking at them.
Wren felt it before she understood it. A kind of cold, at the back of her neck. He was looking straight at them. At the line of iron men, and the Enforcers with their lanterns, and Master Ferrand, and the Inquisitor, and her. He was not surprised. He did not move. He only looked. Steadily. Across the darkening glass. As if he had been looking that way, south, for a long time, at exactly that spot on the black, waiting for something to come over the edge of it.
As if he had known they would.
The iron men had stopped.
Wren had not heard Master Ferrand tell them to. But they had stopped, a long stone's throw from the dome, in their row, ticking. And Master Ferrand had stepped out from behind them. He stood a little ahead of the rest of them, on his own, in his near-white coat, very neat and pale in the last of the green light, with his hands folded in front of him.
He looked at the people by the door. At the child. At the man. At the two with spears.
Then he said his word.
Wren had never been able to catch it. She had heard him say it in the cellar under the Records yard, and at the farm gate in the valley, and on the Glass. It was a word, but a worn one. A word with the edges rubbed off it, like a coin that has been in too many pockets. She could hear that there were sounds in it. She could not hear what they were. It slid off her ears the way rain slides off a window.
The iron men turned.
All three. All at once. Their blank oval heads swung round on their brass necks, smoothly, with a little whirring click, and faced the dome. Faced the door. Faced the child standing by it with her hand on the man's shoulder, and the man sitting, and the two with their spears. And the ticking changed. It went faster. Louder. The way a clock sounds in a quiet room when you are waiting for somebody who is late.
The child's face went white. Wren saw it. Even in that light, at that distance, she saw it go. And the two with spears stepped forward, together, in front of the child and the man, and lowered their spears, and stood.
The man did not move. He went on looking.
And the first iron man lifted its foot.
"Wait," said the Inquisitor.
He did not say it loudly. He said it the way he said everything. Quietly. Without any weight on it at all. But on the Eye, in that silence, it went out across the glass like a stone dropped into a well, and the iron man's foot stopped in the air.
It hung there.
Then it came down again. In the same place. And the iron man stood. Ticking. Waiting.
Master Ferrand turned his head.
He looked at the Inquisitor for a long moment. He was smiling. He was always smiling. But it was a different smile now. Wren had seen it once before, when he came back across the Glass from the false trail with the goatskin in his hand and two red spots on his cheeks.
"Inquisitor," he said. Very politely. "I'm so sorry. Perhaps you didn't hear. The Archon's orders were quite clear." He lifted one gloved hand a little, palm up, toward the dome. "The harvester, and whatever he finds. The others need not come back." He let the hand fall. "The Archon's word, I think, outranks yours."
"Not today," said the Inquisitor.
Master Ferrand's eyebrows went up. Just a little.
The Inquisitor had not moved. He was standing where he had stopped, a few paces behind the iron men, with his bare right hand hanging at his side and his gloved left in his coat pocket. He was not looking at Master Ferrand. He was looking at the dome. At the door. At the man sitting by it.
"Today," said the Inquisitor, "it waits till morning."
Nobody moved.
"The sun's down," said the Inquisitor. Still quietly. Still looking at the dome. "In an hour it will be black dark. Your iron will slow, and then it will stop, Master Ferrand, wherever it happens to be standing. Master Rook told us so. If it is standing in that doorway when it stops, there are people inside who will walk out round it in the night and do to it what was done to the two in the Ridges. I saw those two. So did you." He paused. "And then you will have one iron man, or none, and the Archon will read that in your notebook, and not in mine."
Master Ferrand said nothing.
"At dawn," said the Inquisitor, "it will have the whole day. And so will we."
The silence went on. The ticking went on. The first stars stood in the green sky over the dome, and more came out, and began to fall into the Eye, down and down, under their feet.
Then Master Ferrand smiled. The ordinary smile. The kind one.
"How very sensible," he said. "Of course."
And he took out his black notebook, and his silver pencil, and wrote something in it. Quite a long something. Standing there on the Eye in the last of the light, with his pencil moving neatly, and his head a little on one side.
Then he put the notebook away, in the inside pocket of his near-white coat, on the left, over his heart. And he said his worn word again, softly.
And the iron men turned their heads back, all three together, with that little whirring click. And faced south. Away from the dome. And stood there in their row on the black glass, ticking, waiting.
The child by the door let out her breath. Wren heard it. It went across the glass like a sob.
And the man sitting against the dome went on looking at them. Steadily. Without moving. Not at Master Ferrand. Not at the iron men. Wren followed his eyes, slowly, along the line of them, across the dark glass.
At the Inquisitor.
The Inquisitor was looking back at him.
For a long moment they looked at each other across the Eye in the falling dark. The thin bearded man with his bound hands in his lap, and the slight gray man with his bare right hand at his side. Neither of them moved. Neither of them gave any sign. Wren could not see the Inquisitor's face. She could only see the side of it, and his jaw, which was set, and his throat, which moved once, as if he had swallowed something.
Then the Inquisitor turned his back on the dome.
"We'll camp here," he said. "Harrow. Three hundred paces back. Lanterns, but low."
"Sir."
And his bare right hand went, as he turned, to the pocket of his waistcoat. On the left. Over his heart. And rested there. And Wren, walking after him, one step behind his left shoulder, with the stone cold in her own pocket, did not ask him why.
The Drowned Farm
Harrow counted the paces out loud, under his breath, the way soldiers do. Three hundred. Then he stopped, and set down his lantern on the glass, and said, "Here, sir," and the Inquisitor said, "Here," and that was the camp.
There was nothing to make. That was the strange thing about the Eye. On the Glass, every night, there had been the canvas to put up against the wind that never came, and the heat stones to lay out, and the blankets to heap. Here there was no wind, and no heat, and nothing to put the canvas up on. There was only the black glass, flat in every direction, and the stars coming out in it, and the cold.
The cold was ordinary. Wren noticed that too. It was not the bitter, biting cold of the Glass at night, that came up out of the stars and seemed to go straight into your bones. It was plain cold. The cold of a winter night in the city, on the quays, when the river fog has gone and the sky is clear. The kind you could fight with a coat.
They sat down on the glass where they were. The Enforcers in a rough ring round the lanterns, with their carbines across their knees and their gray coats pulled close. Master Ferrand a little apart, on a blanket he folded very neatly into a square before he sat on it. The Inquisitor on his own, a little way off from the lanterns, toward the dome. And Wren, because she did not know where else to go, near the Inquisitor. Not beside him. A little behind. Where she always was.
The last cask went round.
The Inquisitor did it himself. He took the tin cup from Harrow and held it while Harrow tipped the cask, and gave it to each of them in turn, one swallow each, and watched them drink. Pike first, because his lips were worst. Then the other two. Then Harrow. Then Master Ferrand, who took his swallow very neatly and thanked him. Then Wren.
The water was warm and tasted of the cask, of old wood and tar. She drank her swallow and gave him back the cup. He held it a moment. Then he tipped the cask himself, very slowly, and looked into it, and set it down.
He did not drink.
"Sir," said Harrow.
"There's one swallow left," said the Inquisitor. "For the morning."
"Sir, you've not had—"
"I know." The Inquisitor put the cup down on the glass beside the cask. "There's water in there, Harrow." He nodded toward the dome, without looking at it. "Or they'd be dead. They came across the Glass ahead of us with less than we had, and they have children with them. There's water in there."
Harrow looked at the dome. At the small black gap in the side of it, where the iron door was slid back.
"Yes, sir," he said.
The Inquisitor did not say anything else.
He sat on the glass with his knees drawn up and his bare right hand resting on one of them, and his hat on the glass beside him, and he looked down.
Not at the dome. Down. Between his knees. At the Eye.
Wren looked too. She could not help it.
It was full dark now. Or nearly. The green had gone out of the sky, and the stars were thick, so thick that they made a kind of pale dust across the black. And the Eye had filled up with them. They were under her too. Under the glass. Falling. Down and down, so deep that she felt, for a moment, that she was not sitting on anything at all, that she was hanging in the middle of the night with stars above her and stars below her and nothing in between.
And through the stars, far down, faint, she could see the shapes.
The lanterns showed them. Harrow had turned them low, but they still threw a little yellow light down into the glass, a soft pool of it, round their camp. And in that pool, far below, there was a roof.
Not the village. The village was farther out, under the middle of the Eye, toward the dome. This was on its own. A single roof, long and low, thatched, with the thatch gone dark and smooth like a pelt. A farmhouse. Wren could see the chimney at one end. And a yard. A square of something flat and pale, with low walls round it, and a gate. And in the yard, a cart. Tipped on its side, with one wheel in the air. And a shape beside the cart that might have been a dog.
She could see a well. A round stone well in the corner of the yard, with a little roof over it on two posts. And a bucket. Standing on the rim of the well, as if somebody had just set it down to go and see what that light was, coming down the valley.
She looked at the bucket for a long time.
The Inquisitor was looking at it too. She knew because his head had not moved. He was sitting with his knees drawn up and looking down between them through the black glass at the drowned farm, at the cart and the well and the bucket, and his face in the low lantern light was perfectly still.
"My grandfather came from up these valleys," he said.
Wren went very still.
He had not looked up. He had not turned his head. He said it to the glass, quietly, the way you say a thing you have been thinking for so long that it comes out without your meaning it to.
"My father's father," he said. "From up here somewhere. The north. He came south after the war. Just after. With his boy, my father, who was ten. Nobody else. They had a little money, and they went to the city and kept a chandler's shop by the river, and they did well enough."
Wren did not say anything. She did not dare.
"He never told us his family name," said the Inquisitor. "We asked. We were children. You ask. Everybody else had a grandfather with a name. A family. Somewhere they came from. He would only say the north, and up the valleys, and change the subject." He was quiet for a moment. "Once I pushed. I was nine. I said, But what were you called, Grandad? Before the city? And he looked at me. And he said, It was taken off something, Silas. A long time ago. It's not ours anymore. Don't ask me again."
Silas.
Wren had never heard anybody call him that. She had seen it written. Once. On the corner of a form. S. Kross. She had wondered what the S was for. She had never thought she would know.
"Taken off what, sir?" she said. Very quietly.
The Inquisitor did not answer for a long time.
"I don't know," he said at last. "He died the winter after. I never asked him again. I didn't think there would be any hurry."
He looked down at the bucket on the rim of the drowned well.
Wren looked at it too. She thought about it. A name, taken off something. She did not know what that meant. Taken off a door, perhaps. A sign over a shop. A list. A stone.
She had never been to Haverstock. She did not know what was carved on the cross in the square there, in four rings, going round. She had never seen the place on the north side, in the second ring, where the stone was smooth.
She did not think of anything at all.
Harrow
Harrow found her a biscuit.
She did not know where he got it. They had eaten the last of the hard ship's biscuit the night before, in the Ridges, broken into pieces and passed round in the dark. But Harrow came across the camp in the low lantern light, slowly, with his big shoulders hunched in his gray coat, and sat down on the glass beside her with a grunt, and held it out. Half a biscuit. Gray and hard as a roof slate, with the mark of his thumb in it.
"Go on," he said. "I had the other half."
She did not believe him. She took it anyway.
It was so dry that it took all the spit she had to soften it. She sat with it in her mouth and let it go soft slowly, the way you suck a stone to keep your mouth wet on a long march, and did not chew. Harrow sat beside her and looked at the dome.
He was older than she had thought, at the start. She had thought he was forty. He was not. In the lantern light, with the dust of the Glass in the lines of his face and his broken nose throwing a crooked shadow, he looked fifty, or more. His hands were very big. They lay on his knees like two sleeping dogs. One of them had a scar across the back of it, white and old and puckered, as if somebody had once put a knife through it.
"Water in there," he said. Not to her. To the dome. "He's right. Must be."
"Yes," said Wren.
"Fire, too. Look at that." He nodded at the thin orange line down the side of the iron door. "Real fire. Not that cold blue muck the girl had, or the nothing we had on the Glass." He was quiet a moment. "I'd give a week's pay to put my hands to it."
Wren did not say anything. She thought of fire. She thought of the bread fires at Last Thorn, and the tents going up one after another in the wind, and the children against the bread racks. She thought Harrow was thinking of it too. She did not ask.
They sat.
Across the camp the other two Enforcers were talking in low voices. Pike was lying on his back with his eyes shut and his split mouth open, not asleep. Master Ferrand was writing.
"How's your boy?" said Wren.
She did not know why she said it. It came out. Harrow turned his head and looked at her, surprised, and then something in his face went softer.
"Seven," he said. "Eight in the spring. I told you."
"Yes."
"Ink Quarter. Above the baker's. Two rooms and the use of the yard." He looked down at his hands. "His mother's with him. Her sister comes in, days. He's got a cough every winter, regular as the bells. Comes on at Midwinter, goes off at the thaw." He was quiet a moment. "He'll have had it by now. I wasn't there. First time."
"I'm sorry."
"Not your doing." Harrow rubbed his thumb along the scar on the back of his hand. "He'll be all right. His mother does him a mustard plaster. He hates it. Screams the house down." Something moved in his beard that was nearly a smile. "I promised him I'd bring him something. From the north. When I went. He said, bring me a bear. I said there's no bears in the north, son, they're all in the south, in books. He said, then bring me a book with a bear in it." He shook his head. "I don't know where I'll find a book with a bear in it up here."
Wren swallowed the soft gray paste of the biscuit. It went down like wet sand.
She put her hand in her pocket, round the stone. Then she took it out again. With the stone in it.
"Here," she said.
Harrow looked at it. At the small gray stone on her gloved palm, with its clean white ring round the middle.
"What's that?"
"It's from the beach. Back there. There's thousands of them." She held it out. "It's from the north. From the middle. Where nobody goes. Tell him there's a whole beach of them at the bottom of the world, with a ring round every one, and you picked one up for him. It's not a bear. But it's from here."
Harrow did not take it at once. He looked at the stone. Then at her.
"That's yours," he said. "I saw you pick it up. You put it in your pocket like it was a gold piece."
"I can get another."
"Can you." He looked at her face. She did not know what he saw in it. "No. You keep that one, Cadet. I'll get my own. On the way back." He paused. "If there's a way back."
She closed her hand on the stone and put it back in her pocket. Her fingers were shaking a little. She did not know why.
Harrow looked at the dome again.
"Can I ask you something?" he said.
She had never heard Harrow ask anybody anything. In eleven years with the Inquisitor, he had told her once, on the Glass, he had never asked a question. He did what he was told and kept his own counsel and went home to the Ink Quarter at night.
"Yes," she said.
"The harvester." Harrow did not look at her. "The lame one. The one with the gray hands. Him in there." He nodded at the dome. "You knew him. Before. In the city. I heard you say so, to the Inquisitor, the night in the Trench."
"A little."
"Is he a bad man?"
Wren did not answer at once.
She thought about it. She thought about the man she had seen through the window of the Harvesting Section on the third floor, in the autumn, before any of this, bent over his bare desk with a jar in his hands and his eyes shut, very still, like somebody praying. She thought about him in the white room in the Ministry, looking at her and not knowing her. About the gray stone she had put into his hand.
She thought about the Bourse.
She had been there. In a box high up in the gallery, in her white mask, at the Inquisitor's back, when the dome began to come down. She had felt it. Everybody had felt it, a great groaning shudder in the stone under their feet, and people screaming, and the light, and the Inquisitor's hand on her arm, taking her out through the smoke without a word. And then it had stopped. The dome had stopped coming down. It had hung there, over all those people, and not fallen. For a long time. Long enough.
She had not seen who held it. But she knew. Everybody knew. They had put it on a poster in the chophouse window on Mercer Street, the next week. A MAN HELD THE ROOF UP.
"He held the roof up," said Wren. "At the Bourse. At Midwinter. When it came down."
Harrow was quiet.
"There were hundreds of people under it," said Wren. "Lords and ladies. Servants. Porters. Girls who carried trays. He held it up over all of them until they got out. With his hands. I think it's why they're gray." She stopped. "I don't think a bad man would do that."
Harrow did not say anything for a long time.
"My sister-in-law," he said at last. "The one who comes in days. Ada. She was a tray girl at the Bourse that winter. For the money. She was there that night." He looked down at his hands. "She got out."
Wren stared at him.
"She came home at two in the morning with her apron burned through and her hair singed off one side," said Harrow, "and she sat down in our kitchen in front of my boy, who'd woken up with his cough, and she said, A man held the roof up, Kit. A lame man. I saw his face." Harrow's voice did not change. "And my boy said, Was he a giant, Aunt Ada? And she said, No, love. He was just a man."
The lanterns hissed.
"I didn't know that was him," said Harrow. "Till now. In there."
He got up. Slowly. With a hand on his knee, the way old soldiers get up. He stood for a moment looking down at her in the low light, with his big scarred hands hanging at his sides.
"Thank you, Cadet," he said.
And he went back across the camp to the Enforcers and lay down on the glass among them with his back to the dome, and pulled his coat over his head, and did not move again.
Wren sat where she was with the stone in her pocket and the taste of the biscuit in her mouth, and did not know what she had done. She thought, perhaps, nothing. She thought, perhaps, something.
She thought that tomorrow, when it began, Harrow would have his carbine in his hands, and his orders, and the Inquisitor in front of him, and Master Ferrand writing.
And a sister-in-law called Ada who had got out.
