Chapter 83: The Last Fold, Part 4 of 5: Orders
Orders
The Inquisitor stood up.
He did it slowly. Not because he was stiff. Wren had never seen him stiff, not even after the long nights on the Glass. He did it slowly the way he did things he had decided to do. He picked up his hat from the glass and put it on, and settled it, and looked once across the camp at Master Ferrand's slit of yellow light. Then he walked away from the lanterns. Toward the dome. Out onto the black.
Not far. Ten paces. Twenty. Out of the light. He stopped there, with his back to the camp, and stood looking at the dome.
Wren sat where she was.
After a moment, without turning round, he lifted his bare right hand a little from his side. Two fingers. A small movement. The kind you would not see unless you were watching for it.
She got up and went to him.
She stood one step behind his left shoulder. Where she always stood. And for a while neither of them said anything. They stood on the black glass in the starlight and looked at the dome, and the thin orange line of firelight down the side of its iron door, and the dark shapes keeping guard beside it.
It was very cold out here, away from the lanterns. Her breath smoked.
"When it begins," said the Inquisitor, "tomorrow."
He did not turn round. He spoke to the dome. Low and level, in the voice he used in his glass office at the end of the Long Office, when the door was shut and he was giving her the day's work.
"Stay behind me. Wherever I go. One step. As you are now. Do not go forward. Do not go round. Do not go in." He paused. "Whatever Master Ferrand tells you to do, you are under my orders, not his, and my orders are to stay behind me."
"Yes, sir."
"Whatever you see," said the Inquisitor, "write it down. Afterward. Exactly. Not what you think happened. Not what you think ought to have happened. What you saw. Who stood where. Who said what. Who struck first. What it cost." He was quiet for a moment. "Write it the way you write in your brown book. Plainly. So that a stranger could read it and see it."
"Yes, sir."
"And then," said the Inquisitor, "decide whose report you sign."
Wren did not understand.
She stood behind him in the cold and waited for him to go on. He did not go on. He stood looking at the dome with his hands at his sides, one bare, one gloved.
"Sir," she said, at last. "I don't— Sir, there's only one report."
The Inquisitor turned his head.
Not all the way. Just enough that she could see the side of his face, in the starlight. The deep lines from his nose to his mouth. The tiredness. And something at the corner of his mouth that she had seen only once before, on the Glass, in the dark, at the bottom of the Trench, when he read a chalk arrow on a wall that said THIS WAY IF YOURE NICE.
"There is always more than one report, Halloway," he said.
And turned back to the dome.
Wren stood very still.
She thought about the Long Office. Forty desks under green lamps. The black numbered cupboards. Sedley at the next desk, cold and correct, copying out reports in his perfect hand. She had copied out reports herself, for two years. Hundreds of them. On the night of the ninth, the subject was taken at his lodging. On the morning of the tenth, the subject was examined in Room Nine. She had never once thought about who had written them. Or whether there had been another one. One that said something else. One that nobody copied.
She thought about Master Ferrand's notebook, open on his knee, with his silver pencil moving.
She thought about her own brown book, inside her coat, on the left, over her heart. With the farmer in it.
Decide whose report you sign.
"Yes, sir," she said.
Her voice came out very small.
The Inquisitor nodded, once, without looking at her. As if she had said something much bigger than yes, sir. As if she had said something he had been waiting to hear for a long time, and was not sure she had said it yet, and would wait a little longer to be sure.
Why
She did not go back to the lanterns. Neither did he.
They stood out on the black glass in the cold, a little way from the camp, with the dome in front of them and the stars falling into the Eye under their feet. The Inquisitor with his hands at his sides. Wren one step behind his left shoulder, with her blanket pulled round her and her right hand in her pocket on the stone.
She had a question.
She had had it for a long time. Since the autumn. Since before the autumn, perhaps, though she had not known it was a question then. It had sat in her like a stone in a shoe, small and hard, and every time she put her weight on it, it hurt. She had carried it through the city, and up the north road, and across the Glass. She had not asked it. She was a cadet. Cadets did not ask the Inquisitor why.
But they were on the Eye. Where nothing was taken from anybody unless somebody chose. And tomorrow there would be iron men, and Enforcers with stones, and Master Ferrand with his notebook. And she did not know what would be left of any of them, afterward. Of her. Of him.
She thought, if I do not ask it now, I never will.
"Sir," she said.
He did not turn round. "Halloway."
"May I ask you something else?"
A pause. "You may ask."
She took a breath. The cold went into her chest like a knife.
"Why did you let him go?"
The Inquisitor did not move.
"The harvester," said Wren. "Corin Vane." She had not said his name out loud since the city. It felt strange in her mouth. "On the Saltmarsh, in the autumn. You could have had him. I was there. And at the Bourse. And on the Thursday lists. You left his name off. More than once. I copied the lists." She stopped. Her voice was shaking. She made it stop shaking. "And on the night of the dyke road. You told the girl which road to take. You told her about the Glass. You told her it was the only place a man with gray hands could sleep without being afraid."
The Inquisitor said nothing.
"And now we've followed him here," said Wren. "All the way. With iron men. And tomorrow—" She stopped again.
She did not know how to finish it.
"Why, sir?" she said. "Every time. Why did you let him go?"
The silence went on.
It went on so long that Wren thought he was not going to answer. That he would turn round and look at her with his tired gray eyes and say Go to sleep, Halloway, the way he had before, and she would go, and she would never ask again. She stood behind him in the cold and waited for it.
Across the dark glass, at the dome, one of the spear-carriers moved. Shifted their weight. Wren heard the soft scrape of a foot. Then stillness again.
The firelight down the side of the iron door flickered.
"Because someone asked me," said the Inquisitor.
He said it very quietly. Not to her. To the dome. To the dark. The way he had said My grandfather came from up these valleys. As if it had come out on its own, after being kept in for so long that it had worn smooth.
"Someone asked me to keep counting," said the Inquisitor. "A long time ago."
He was quiet a moment.
"And I have."
Wren stood very still.
She did not understand it. She thought she should understand it, and she did not. Keep counting. Counting what? She thought of the Thursday lists. The names on them, going down the page. She thought of the girl, Lyra Thorne, who counted stairs. Rowan Thorne, in the Long Office, telling her, She counts them. She's counted them every day since she was nine. She thought of Sergeant Moll, in Room Nine, tapping on the back of her hand. Four, two, nine, three. The new pattern she did not know.
She did not know what any of it meant.
"Who, sir?" she said.
The Inquisitor did not answer.
"Who asked you?"
He did not turn round. He did not move at all. He stood looking at the dome, and the starlight lay on his shoulders and on the brim of his hat, and his bare right hand hung at his side. And then, slowly, it moved. It went up. To his waistcoat. To the pocket on the left side, over his heart. And rested there. Flat. The way you rest your hand on a door you are not going to open.
Wren knew what was in that pocket.
She had seen it on the Glass. In the small hours, three nights ago, when he thought everybody was asleep. A tiny vial, corked and sealed with wax, no bigger than her little finger, with a faded pink ribbon tied round its neck in a bow. He had held it to his ear. In the dark. With his eyes shut. And his face had been open in a way she had never seen any face be open, the face of a man who has come home after a long time away and hears somebody laughing in the kitchen.
Who is it? she had asked him then.
Somebody, he had said. Somebody I would not like to see on another form.
She did not ask again. She knew she would not get an answer. She knew it the way you know, when you put your hand on a door, that it is not going to open.
The Inquisitor took his hand away from the pocket.
"Go and sleep, Halloway," he said.
But he said it differently this time. More gently. And he did not wait to see if she went.
The Fifth Step
She went back toward the lanterns slowly. She did not lie down at once. She stood at the edge of the light with her blanket round her and thought about a staircase.
The staircase in the Inquisitor's house.
She had lived in that house for two years and five months. In a small room at the back, on the second floor, with a window that looked out over the yard and the roofs of the old town to the Spire. She had gone up and down that staircase a thousand times. Up in the morning with the tray for Room Nine. Down at night with the empty cups. It was a plain staircase, dark wood, with a worn red runner held down by brass rods, and a rail polished smooth by hands.
And on the fifth step from the bottom, there was a ring.
She had noticed it in her first week. A pale ring in the dark wood, at one end of the step, near the wall. Worn. Not a stain. A place where the polish had been rubbed away, slowly, over a long time, by something that sat there. Or somebody.
She had asked Mrs. Oddie about it once. Mrs. Oddie, the cook, short and wide, with arms like hams, who never said a word if she could help it and left a dish of peppermints on every table in the house. Mrs. Oddie had been in the kitchen, rolling pastry. She had not looked up.
That's him, she had said. Sitting.
That was all, the first time.
But on the night before the carts went north, Wren had come down the back stair late and found the Inquisitor sitting there. On the fifth step. In the dark. With a cup of tea beside him on the step below, gone cold, with a skin on it. He had not drunk it. He had not moved. He had sat there all night, Mrs. Oddie told her in the morning, the way he had sat there the night they brought word of Lord Thorne, and other nights. And then Mrs. Oddie had said more than Wren had ever heard her say at once.
He went north once, Mrs. Oddie had said. Rolling pastry. Not looking up. Long time ago. Eighteen years. With the men in gray. In the first frost, that was. The winter of the fever. He was gone near a month. And when he came back he sat on that step all night, and he's sat on it since, whenever there's something. Eighteen years. That's what wore the ring.
Wren had not thought about it since. Not properly. There had been the cellar, and the iron men, and the carts, and the farm in the valley, and the Glass.
She thought about it now.
She stood at the edge of the lantern light on the black glass of the Eye, and looked at the Inquisitor sitting a little way off with his knees drawn up and his hat beside him, the way he had sat on the fifth step. And she thought: he has been north before.
Not here, perhaps. Not to the Eye. But north. Eighteen years ago. In the first frost. With the men in gray.
She thought of the way he had walked onto the Glass. The first morning. Without stopping at the Edge to stare, the way the Enforcers had. Without asking anybody what it would be like. He had looked at the clean white line where the snow stopped and the black began, and he had stepped over it as if he were stepping over a puddle in a street he knew.
She thought of the way he had said, on the fifth step, on the evening the carts went north: On the Glass nobody can take anything, Wren. He had called her Wren. The only time. Nobody can take from you. Nobody can take from me.
How had he known that? Nobody in the Long Office knew that. It was not on any form. She had never heard it said until he said it.
And she thought of the winter of the fever. Mrs. Oddie's words. She did not know what fever. There were fevers every winter in the city, along the river, in the old town. But Mrs. Oddie had said it as if it were one fever. The fever. As if everybody knew which one.
She did not know which one.
She stood there with her blanket round her and the stone in her pocket and the cold coming up through her boots, and she felt something turn over, very slowly, far down, the way a fish turns over in deep water. A shape she could not see. Something to do with the north, and the first frost, and a fever, and a man who sat on a step all night and wore a ring into the wood.
And a vial with a pink ribbon. Somebody I would not like to see on another form.
And someone asked me to keep counting.
She did not know how any of it fitted together. She did not know if it did. She only knew that it was there, all of it, in a heap, like the pieces of a broken jar on a cellar floor, and that if she knelt down and looked at them long enough she might begin to see the shape they had been.
She did not kneel down. Not tonight.
She went and lay down on the glass by the lanterns, where she had lain before, with her knees drawn up and her right hand in her pocket on the stone. And she kept her eyes open, half, and watched him.
The Page
She did not sleep.
She went back to the lanterns and lay down on the glass near the place where he had sat, and pulled her blanket over her, and lay on her side with her knees drawn up and her right hand in her pocket on the stone. And she did not sleep. She lay with her eyes half open and watched him.
He came back after a while. Slowly, across the black glass, out of the dark into the edge of the lantern light. He sat down where he had sat before. Knees up. Hat beside him. He did not lie down.
The Enforcers slept round the lanterns, in a heap of gray coats. Pike muttered in his sleep, a girl's name, the same one, over and over. Harrow snored a little, softly, like a big dog by a fire. Across the camp, Master Ferrand's slit of yellow light still burned, and his pencil still moved. He did not look up.
The Inquisitor sat for a long time without doing anything at all.
Then he put his hand inside his coat.
Wren watched him through her eyelashes. She saw him take out a book. Not Master Ferrand's book. His own. A small black one, the size of a hand, with a worn cover and a strap round it. She had seen it on the corner of his desk in his glass office in the Long Office, every day for two years, squared exactly to the corner of the desk. She had never seen it open.
He undid the strap. He opened the book on his knee. He took a pencil out of his waistcoat pocket, a plain one, short, worn down to a stub.
And he wrote.
Not much. A word or two. She could not see what. The lantern light did not reach the page properly, and he was holding it tilted toward himself, away from her. She saw only his hand moving, slowly. Not neatly, the way Master Ferrand wrote. Heavily. Each letter pressed down.
Then he stopped.
And he sat and looked at what he had written.
He looked at it for a long time. Longer than it would take to read a word or two. Wren lay on the cold glass and watched him look at it, and she did not know why, but her heart had begun to go hard and slow in her chest, the way it went when she was standing outside his office door waiting to be called in.
Then he turned the book a little. Toward the light. To see it better, perhaps. Or because his arm was tired. She did not know.
And she saw what he had written.
Her name.
Wren Halloway.
In his heavy pressed-down hand. On a page by itself. Nothing else on it. No date. No place. Nothing beside it. Just her name, in the middle of the page, in pencil, and under it a line, drawn straight across.
Wren did not breathe.
She thought of the Thursday lists. Names going down the page. VANE, CORIN. Harvester, formerly of this Ministry. She thought of the list she had seen once, under the black book on his desk, when he had been called away and left it there. Three names. Hale, L. Thorne, C. E. Auditor. She thought of Master Ferrand's notebook. Kross, S. With a small closed circle drawn in the margin beside it.
She thought: that is how it starts. A name on a page, by itself.
She lay very still and watched him.
The Inquisitor looked at her name for a long time.
Then he did a thing she did not expect.
He took hold of the page, at the top, between his finger and thumb. And he tore it out. Slowly. Carefully. Along the stitching, so that it came away clean, with no ragged edge left in the book. The tearing made a small dry sound in the silence of the Eye. Across the camp, Master Ferrand's pencil paused.
Then it went on.
The Inquisitor held the torn page in his hand and looked at it once more.
And then he folded it.
In half. Then in half again. Then again. Wren counted without meaning to. The way the Thorne girl counted. Four. Five. Each fold pressed flat with his thumb, slowly, along its edge, the way he had folded the Archon's card in the Long Office, and the peppermint papers on his desk, and the napkin at dinner, every time she had ever seen him fold anything. Six. Seven. The page grew small. Thick. Hard. Eight. Nine. It was the size of his thumbnail now. Ten.
Eleven.
He stopped.
He held the tiny hard square of paper between his finger and thumb and looked at it. His face did not change.
Then he put it in his waistcoat pocket. The one on the left. Over his heart. Where the other thing was. The little vial with the faded pink ribbon.
And he put his hand flat over the pocket, for a moment, the way he had out on the black glass. As if he were holding something in. Or holding something still.
Then he closed the black book, and buckled the strap round it, and put it back inside his coat. And sat with his knees drawn up and his hands on them and looked at the dome.
Wren lay on the glass with her eyes half shut and her heart going.
She did not know what it meant. She did not know if it was a good thing or a bad thing. A name written on a page and torn out and folded small and put away, over his heart, next to the somebody he would not like to see on another form.
She did not know if she had just been put on a list. Or taken off one.
She lay there and held the stone in her pocket until her hand ached, and did not know, and could not ask.
And across the camp, very softly, Master Ferrand turned a page in his notebook. And smoothed it flat. And wrote.
