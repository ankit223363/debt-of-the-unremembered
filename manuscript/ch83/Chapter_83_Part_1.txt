Chapter 83: The Last Fold, Part 1 of 5: Down into the Bowl
Down into the Bowl
The beach of the Eye was made of small gray stones, more than anybody could ever count, each one with a white ring round it, and Wren picked one up before she knew she had bent down.
But that was at the end of the day. The day was long before that.
It had begun in the Ridges, in the dark, with Harrow's lantern swinging ahead of her down a passage of glass so narrow that she could have touched both walls at once, and the wind singing through the gaps above them in that strange shut-mouthed way, like a choir in another room. They had walked the Ridges all night. Round and round. In and out. The walls were so high that you could not see over them, and they leaned in at the top so that the strip of stars between them was thin as a ribbon, and every gap looked like every other gap, and twice Wren had been sure they were going in a circle.
The Inquisitor had not been sure of anything. He had walked at the front, beside Harrow and the lantern, and every so often he had stopped and bent and looked at the glass under his feet. There were no tracks. The storm had scrubbed the whole Glass clean the day before, like the inside of a shell. But there were other things. A smear of something dark on the corner of a wall. A thread of gray wool caught on a sharp edge. A single crumb.
He had followed those. All night.
And in the small hours they had come into a yard.
That was what Wren called it, afterward, in her head. A yard. Three walls of glass round an open space, like the three sides of a cattle pen, with gaps at the corners. And in the middle of it, lying on the glass, there had been two iron men.
Broken.
She had known them at once. She had watched Old Rook wind them in the cellar under the Records yard, on a Friday that felt now as if it had happened to somebody else, in another life. Man-high iron plates and brass joints, three-fingered hands like shovels, blank oval heads with a single slit in them. She had walked behind them for days across the Glass. She had heard them tick in her sleep.
These two did not tick.
One lay on its face by the west wall, with the brass plate in the small of its back torn half off and bent up like the lid of a tin, and a hole under it, and wheels spilling out of the hole. The other lay on its side near the east gap. Its plate was gone altogether. Its hole was empty. And beside it, on the glass, coiled and still, there was a long flat strip of dark steel, as long as Wren was tall. A spring.
There was blood on the glass. A great deal of it. Dried black in the lantern light, in smears and spatters and one long dark dragged line, going away toward the north gap.
And there were scraps. Strips of gray cloth, torn and bloody, lying here and there on the glass. And a broken spear. And, by the north wall, a place where the glass was scraped clean in a long patch, the size of a man lying down.
The Inquisitor had stood in the middle of that yard for a long time.
He had taken off his hat.
Wren had watched him do it. She had seen him do it before. On the Lip above Last Thorn, looking down at the black ashes of the camp. At the farm gate in the valley, over the sack with the boots sticking out. At the Trench, by the glass soldiers. He took off his hat the way other men touch their brows to the saints. Slowly. Without saying anything. And held it against his chest.
Master Ferrand had not taken off his hat.
Master Ferrand had walked round the yard with his lantern held up, neatly, picking his way round the blood the way he had picked his way round the dead goats at Last Thorn. He had looked at each of the iron men. He had bent and looked at the empty hole in the second one's back, and at the spring on the glass, for a long time. Then he had taken off his gray kid glove, his right one, and laid his bare hand flat on the iron back of the first one. The way you would lay your hand on the back of a horse that had fallen.
"Oh, dear," he had said. Quite softly. "Oh, dear. Somebody has been very clever."
And then he had put his glove back on, and taken out his black notebook and his silver pencil, and written for a long time.
They had gone on.
And a little before dawn, in a narrow passage at the foot of a fallen wall, they had passed a heap of glass.
Wren would not have noticed it. It was only broken glass, dark green, nearly black, in a low heap against the foot of the wall where a whole Ridge had come down long ago. There was broken glass everywhere in the Ridges. But Harrow's lantern had swung over it, and the Inquisitor had stopped.
It was not a heap. It was a shape. The big flat pieces leaned together at the top, like the roof of a tent. The small pieces were piled round them. Carefully. And on the very top, balanced, there was one tiny sliver of green, no longer than a finger.
Somebody had built it. By hand.
The Inquisitor had taken off his hat again.
They had stood there, all of them, in the cold, with the lanterns, round the little heap of glass. The four Enforcers in their gray coats. Wren. Master Ferrand, a little apart. The Inquisitor with his hat held against his chest. Nobody had said anything. Not even Master Ferrand. Pike, the young one, had looked at Harrow, and Harrow had shaken his head, and Pike had taken his cap off too.
Then they had gone round. The passage went on under the fallen wall, through a crack too low for anybody to walk through. They could not follow. They had gone round, the long way, for two hours, and come out of the last of the Ridges at sunrise onto open glass.
And the land had begun to go down.
Wren felt it in her knees before she saw it. A slope. Very gentle, so gentle that if you had put a marble down on it, it would have taken all day to roll. But it was there. Everything sloped. All the glass, in every direction, was tipped very slightly toward the north, toward the middle, like the inside of a huge shallow bowl. And the hum, which had been in her teeth for days, began to climb.
It climbed all morning. From a hum to a buzz. From a buzz to a whine. By noon it was not in her teeth anymore. It was in the bones behind her ears, and in the backs of her eyes, and in the ends of her fingers, so that when she held her hand up in front of her face she half expected to see it shaking. It did not shake. It only felt as if it did.
They had stopped at noon under the gray canvas. Not long. There was no shade worth having, and no time. The water was nearly gone. Two of the four casks the Enforcers carried on their wooden back frames were empty, and the third was low, and the fourth was the last. The Inquisitor had measured it out himself. One swallow each. Pike's lips had split down the middle and bled every time he spoke, so he had stopped speaking. Harrow's face had gone the color of old brick.
And the iron men had come up behind them while they sat.
The Inquisitor's two, ticking, walking in their own footprints from where they had frozen on the open glass at dusk. And Master Ferrand's last one, the only one of his that was left, behind them. Three iron men in a row, man-high, walking with their slow steady stride across the glare, their shadows short under them. Wren heard them before she saw them. Tick, tick, tick. Like a roomful of clocks coming to find you.
They had come up, and stopped beside the canvas, and stood there. Waiting. Ticking. Master Ferrand had got up and gone along the row of them, and laid his gloved hand on each one's chest, and said something to each one that Wren could not hear. And their ticking had changed. Fallen in together. Into one beat.
Then they had walked on, all of them, down the long slope, through the afternoon, into the glare.
And the glass had begun to change.
It went paler. Wren noticed it first under her own feet, an hour after noon. The green-black she had walked on for days had gone a little green-gray. And then gray. And then, as the afternoon went on and the slope went on, it went lighter still. The color of rainwater in a bucket. And then she could see into it.
Not far. A hand's depth. Then an arm's. There were things in it. Bubbles, small and round and silver, caught in the glass like fish eggs. Streaks of color, red and gold and blue, running down into the depths like weed in a river. And far down, too far to see properly, shapes. Dark shapes. Long ones. She did not look at them for long.
And by the end of the afternoon, when the sun had gone down the western sky and turned orange and huge, and the shadows of the walking men had grown long and thin and stretched out ahead of them down the slope like the shadows of trees, Wren came over a last low swell in the glass and saw the bottom of the bowl.
A Stone
It was a beach.
She did not understand it at first. She had walked so long on glass that she had stopped thinking there could be anything else. And here, at the bottom of the long slope, the glass stopped. In a line. A curving line, going away to the left and to the right as far as she could see, round and round, like the rim of a plate. And on the other side of the line there were stones.
Small ones. Gray. Thousands and thousands of them, lying in a wide band all round the bottom of the bowl, the way the shingle lies along the edge of the sea at the bottom of the Fish Stairs. A beach, with no sea. They were each about the size of the tip of her thumb. Round and smooth. And as she came closer, walking down the last of the slope behind the Inquisitor's back, she saw that every one of them had a line round it.
A white line. Thin and clean, round the middle of each stone, the way a ribbon goes round a parcel.
All of them. Every single one.
Wren stopped walking.
She did not mean to. Her feet stopped, at the very edge, where the glass ended and the stones began. The Enforcers went on past her, crunching down onto the stones in their heavy boots. The iron men went on past her, ticking, and their iron feet came down on the beach and the stones shifted and rattled under them like dice in a cup. Master Ferrand went on past her. He glanced at her as he went. He smiled.
The Inquisitor went on past her too. He did not look at her.
And Wren bent down.
She did not know why. She did not decide to. She bent down, at the edge of the glass, and put out her hand in its glove, and picked up a stone. One of the gray ones with the white ring round it. It was cool through her glove. Smooth. A little bigger than the end of her thumb.
She held it in her palm and looked at it.
She had had one like it.
She had found it on the beach below the Fish Stairs, a long time ago, when she was small and the river was low and she had gone down with her father to look for things in the mud. A gray stone with a white ring round it. It had not looked like any of the other stones on that beach. They were brown and black and broken, and this one was round and smooth and gray, with its clean white ring. Her father had turned it over in his fingers and said, That's not from round here. And she had kept it. For years. In the pocket of every coat she had ever had. Through the Collegium and into the Ministry and into the white coat with the twelve silver buttons. She used to hold it when she was frightened. She did not know where it came from. She had thought, once, that she might find out one day.
And then she had given it away.
To a man with gray hands, who had looked at her and not known her, and whom she had lied for. She had put it into his hand and closed his gray fingers over it. I think it came from there, she had said. From the Glass. Take it back. She did not know why she had said that either. It had seemed the right thing to say.
And he had taken it. And gone north.
She stood at the edge of the beach with the stone in her palm and looked down at the beach. At the thousands and thousands of gray stones with their white rings, going away round the bottom of the bowl on either side of her.
That's not from round here.
No, she thought. It was from here.
She did not know what that meant. She did not know what any of it meant. She only knew that her throat had gone tight, the way it went tight when she was going to cry, and she was not going to cry. Not here. Not in front of Master Ferrand.
She put the stone in her pocket.
In the right-hand pocket of her coat. The one where the other one used to be. Where her fingers had gone, every day for years, to find it, and had found nothing since the autumn, and had gone on going there anyway, out of habit, the way your tongue goes to the place where a tooth used to be.
They found it now.
Her hand stayed on it. Inside the pocket. She held it, and it was cool and smooth and round, and it fitted into the hollow of her palm exactly the way the other one had, as if they had been made in the same place. As if they had been made by the same hand.
She did not take her hand out.
"Halloway."
She looked up. The Inquisitor had stopped a little way down the beach. He was standing among the stones with his back to her, looking out at something. He had not turned round.
"Sir."
"Keep up."
"Yes, sir."
She went down onto the beach. The stones moved under her boots. They made a sound, a soft dry rattle, like rain on a slate roof. She walked across them with her right hand in her pocket, holding on, and did not look down at them, and came up behind the Inquisitor, one step behind his left shoulder, where she always walked.
And saw what he was looking at.
Into the Eye
On the far side of the beach, there was the Eye.
Wren had heard the word. She had heard Master Ferrand say it, in his soft careful voice, reading from the Archon's notes. The middle. The Eye. She had pictured something. She did not know what. A pit. A crater. A mountain of glass, perhaps, black and jagged, with fire at the top.
It was not any of those.
It was flat. Perfectly flat. It began where the beach ended, in another curving line, and went out from there across the whole bottom of the bowl, round, wider than the river at the Fish Stairs, wider than the whole of the old town, so wide that the beach on the far side was only a faint gray thread at the very edge of seeing. And it was clear.
Not like the glass they had walked on for days, green-black and thick, so that you could see nothing in it but your own blurred face. Not like the pale glass of the last afternoon, rainwater-gray, with bubbles. Clear. Clear the way water is clear in a deep well, on a still day, when you lean over the edge and look down and down and cannot see the bottom.
It was black. But it was black the way the sky is black at night. Not because there was nothing there. Because there was so much there, so far down, that the light could not reach it.
The sun was very low now. Huge and orange, sitting on the western rim of the bowl like a coin on the edge of a table. And its light came in across the Eye sideways, long and red, and went down into it. Wren saw it go. Down into the clear black. And far below, where the light went, there were shapes.
Roofs.
She stopped breathing. She did not mean to. She stood at the edge of the beach with her hand in her pocket on the cold stone and looked down through the clear black glass at roofs. Slate ones. Thatched ones. Far, far down, so small they looked like the roofs of a toy village on a nursery floor. A square. A well. A church tower, square, with something on the top that might once have been a weathervane. And people.
She did not look at the people.
"Saints," said Pike. His lips split when he said it. He put his hand up to his mouth and it came away red. "Saints keep us."
Nobody else said anything.
And in the middle of the Eye, a long way out, there was a dome.
Small, from here. Gray. Round. Half of it stood up out of the glass and half of it, Wren saw, as her eyes got used to it, went on down under the glass, sunk, the way a stone sits in a pond with its top showing above the water. It was the only thing in all that great flat clear black that stood up. It threw a long shadow across the Eye toward them, in the red evening light, like the shadow of a sundial.
The Inquisitor looked at it for a long time.
Then he stepped off the beach.
Wren watched him do it. He did not hesitate. He walked down the last of the gray stones to the place where they stopped and the clear black glass began, and he put his boot out over the edge, and set it down on the Eye. And then the other. And then he stood there, a pace out onto the black, with the sun on one side of his face and his long shadow going away from him across the glass.
And Wren saw his face change.
She was looking at him from behind, from the side. She saw it in the line of his cheek. In his jaw. On the Glass, these last days, something had happened to the Inquisitor's face. She had seen it the first night he slept, under the gray canvas, with his hat over his eyes. When the hat slipped. His face had been smooth. Young. As if somebody had taken all the lines out of it in the night. As if, on the Glass, where nothing could be taken from anybody, he had been able to put something down that he had carried for a long time, and sleep.
She had seen it every morning since. Fading a little as he woke. But there.
It was gone now.
She saw it go. The moment his second foot came down on the Eye. The lines came back into his face all at once, the deep ones from the nose to the mouth, the ones at the corners of the eyes. His shoulders came up, very slightly. His jaw set. He looked older, suddenly, standing there in the red light. Older than she had ever seen him in the city. As tired as a man who has been walking for twenty years and has just been told he has twenty more to go.
He did not say anything.
He only took off his right glove. Slowly. A finger at a time. And put it in his coat pocket. And stood there on the black glass with his bare right hand hanging at his side.
Then he said, without turning round, "Halloway. Come here."
She went.
She went down the last of the stones and put her foot out over the edge and set it down on the Eye.
And the hum stopped.
It stopped all at once. Like a door shutting on a loud room. One moment it was there, in the bones behind her ears and the backs of her eyes and the ends of her fingers, the great thin whining hum that she had carried with her across the Glass for six days. And the next moment it was not. And the silence where it had been was so huge that she swayed.
She heard her own breath. Loud. Far too loud. She heard her heart.
And then she felt her hands.
It came into them like a glove being pulled on. A cold glove, wet, too tight, drawn on over her fingers one by one by somebody else. It started at the fingertips. A prickling. Then a cold. Then a kind of ache, a waking, the way your foot aches when it has gone to sleep under you and the blood comes back. It went up from the fingertips into her palms and her wrists, and stopped there, and waited.
Her Extraction.
She had not felt it since the morning they stepped onto the Glass. On the Glass, it had been there, but stuck. She had tried it once, the first day, alone, behind the canvas, reaching out toward Pike's sleeping back the way you reach for a cup on a high shelf, only to see. And it had gone out a little way and stopped. Stuck. Like walking into a wall. Like air gone thick as honey.
She had been glad. That was the thing she had not told anybody. When she found that she could not do it, she had been glad. And then ashamed of being glad. And then she had stopped thinking about it.
Now it was back. In her hands. Cold and wet and too tight. Waiting.
She could feel the Enforcers behind her.
Not see them. Feel them. The way you feel a fire in a room with your eyes shut. Pike, to her left, a few paces back, frightened and thirsty and full of a girl's face that he kept going back to in his head, over and over, a girl with a gap in her teeth. Harrow, behind her, steady and tired and full of a small boy in a nightshirt asking for a story. The other two. Master Ferrand.
She could not feel Master Ferrand.
She noticed that. She noticed it the way you notice a cold place in a warm room. Everybody else was there, a warmth, a weight, a hum of their own. Where Master Ferrand was, there was nothing. A smoothness. As if he were made of glass.
She turned her head and looked at him. He was stepping down off the beach onto the Eye, neatly, in his near-white coat, lifting it a little at the hem as if the black glass might be dusty. He saw her look. He smiled.
"Ah," he said. Mildly. "Can you feel it, Cadet? Isn't it a relief? I find the Glass very tiring. All that not being able to do anything." He stepped up beside her and stood looking out at the dome, pulling his gloves tight at the wrists. "Here we are ourselves again."
Wren did not say anything.
She put her hands in her coat pockets. Both of them. And held on to the stone in the right one.
