Chapter 83: The Last Fold, Part 3 of 5: What Ferrand Is For
What Ferrand Is For
Later, much later, when the Enforcers had rolled themselves in their blankets on the glass round the lanterns and Pike had gone to sleep with his split mouth open, and the stars had wheeled a little way round, the Inquisitor spoke again.
Wren was not asleep. She did not think she would ever sleep on the Eye. She was lying on her side on the glass with her blanket over her and her right hand in her coat pocket, holding the stone, and her eyes open, looking at the dome.
There was a light in it. Or at the gap in the door. Very faint. Orange. Not lantern light. Fire. Somebody had a fire in there. She could see it, a thin warm line down the side of the black iron door, and every so often it flickered, as if somebody had walked between it and the gap.
The Inquisitor had not lain down. He was sitting where he had sat all evening, with his knees up, with his hat beside him. He had not moved for a long time.
"Halloway," he said.
She sat up.
He did not turn his head. He was looking at the dome too.
"Come here."
She got up, and went, and sat down on the glass beside him. Not close. Her blanket round her shoulders.
For a while he did not say anything.
Then he said, very low, so low that she had to lean a little toward him to hear it: "Do you know why Master Ferrand is here?"
Wren looked across the camp. At the neat square of folded blanket, a little apart from the lanterns. Master Ferrand was sitting on it. Not asleep. He never seemed to sleep. He was sitting very straight with his notebook open on his knee and a small lantern of his own beside him, shuttered down to a slit, and he was writing.
"To see, sir," said Wren. "And to write. That's what the Archon's note said. Master Ferrand goes with him, to see and to write."
"Yes." The Inquisitor was quiet for a moment. "To see what?"
Wren did not answer.
"He is here to watch me," said the Inquisitor.
He said it quite plainly. As if he were telling her what time it was.
"Not the harvester. Not the girl. Not the Captain. Me." He did not look at Master Ferrand. He did not look at anything but the dome. "If I go back to the city without the harvester, he writes that down. That I failed. If I go back with the harvester, he writes down how. What I did. What I said. What I let go and what I did not. Either way, he writes."
"And the Archon reads it," said Wren.
"The Archon reads everything." The Inquisitor's voice did not change at all. "Once. He reads everything once, Halloway, and he never forgets any of it. Every word. Every form. Every Thursday list. He has been reading what I write for twenty years. Now he wants to read what somebody else writes about me." He paused. "That is what Master Ferrand is for."
Wren thought about the page in Master Ferrand's notebook. The one she had seen, three days ago, when he left it lying open on a folded hide in the noon glare and went to drink. Kross, S. Underlined. Dates. Places. And in the margin beside it, small, drawn with the silver pencil, a closed circle.
She had not told the Inquisitor about that. She did not know if she should.
She looked at him sideways. At his face in the low light. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the dome. And she thought, quite suddenly and clearly, that he knew. About the page. About the circle. That he had known for longer than she had.
She did not say anything.
"Sir," she said instead. "May I ask you something?"
"You may ask."
"When we stepped onto the Eye. I could feel them all. Harrow. Pike. The others. The way you can feel people, when your hands are awake." She stopped. "I can't feel him. Master Ferrand. There's nothing there. It's like he's made of glass."
The Inquisitor was silent.
"Why can't I feel him, sir?"
He was silent for so long that she thought he would not answer. Across the camp, Master Ferrand turned a page in his notebook, neatly, and smoothed it flat with the side of his gloved hand, and went on writing.
"Do you remember," said the Inquisitor, "what he said to you? In the cart. The first night out of the city."
Wren remembered. She would always remember. The cart swaying, and the shuttered lantern, and his pale kind face across from her in the dark, and his soft voice. His Excellency is the kindest man. He only takes away what hurts.
And then: I have never once been unhappy.
"He said he'd never been unhappy," said Wren slowly.
"Yes."
"Never once."
"Yes," said the Inquisitor. "That is why you cannot feel him, Halloway. There is nothing in him that hurts. Somebody took it all out. A long time ago. Very gently." He was quiet a moment. "And when you take out everything in a man that hurts, you find that there is not very much left."
Wren sat very still beside him on the black glass.
She looked across at Master Ferrand. At the neat pale shape of him on his folded blanket, writing by his little slit of light. Smiling a little as he wrote. He was always smiling.
She thought of the farmer at the gate in the valley, with his pitchfork. Nobody comes through my yard. And the iron man's hand coming down. And Master Ferrand writing it down.
She thought of the woman in the blue floury apron, screaming. And Master Ferrand walking past her, close enough that his coat brushed her shoulder. Shall we? They'll be getting ahead.
Nothing in him that hurts.
She found that her right hand had closed on the stone in her pocket so hard that it ached.
"Sir," she said. "Is that what—" She stopped.
The Inquisitor waited.
"Is that what happens?" said Wren. "To people? When they're— when somebody—" She could not finish it.
The Inquisitor did not answer that.
He only reached out, without looking, and picked up his hat from the glass beside him, and turned it round once in his bare right hand, and set it down again in the same place.
"Go to sleep, Halloway," he said. "You'll want your wits in the morning."
She did not go. And he did not tell her again.
Pike
It was Pike who came to her next. She had not expected that.
She was sitting by herself again, a little way from the lanterns, with the Inquisitor's back a few paces off and the dome beyond it. She heard somebody coming across the glass behind her, slowly, trying to be quiet and not managing it, and turned her head and saw Pike.
He was the youngest of the four Enforcers. Younger than her, she thought. Nineteen, perhaps. Thin, with big ears that stuck out under his gray cap and a long neck, like a heron's. He had burned his palm on the Glass the first day, putting his hand down flat on it at noon without thinking, and it had blistered, and the blister had burst, and he had kept it wrapped in a dirty rag since. His lips were split down the middle. Every time he spoke they bled.
He sat down on the glass beside her, at a little distance. He did not say anything for a while. He held his wrapped hand in his other hand in his lap and looked at the dome.
"Cadet," he said at last. Very low. The split in his lip opened and a bead of blood came up in it. He licked it away.
"Pike."
"Can you do it now?" he said. "Here? The thing you do?"
She did not understand. Then she did.
"Take things," said Pike. "Out of people. Like in the cellars. Like you did with the old soldier, in Room Nine. They said you were good at it. They said you could take a thing out of a man so he never knew it was there." He was not looking at her. He was looking at his own wrapped hand. "You couldn't on the Glass. Nobody could. But you can here. I saw your face when we stepped off the beach. You can, can't you?"
Wren did not answer.
She could feel it. In her hands. The cold wet glove, too tight, waiting. She had kept her hands in her pockets since they stepped onto the Eye, because she could feel it there, and she did not want to feel it. But she could. And sitting this close to Pike she could feel him. Not his face, not his thoughts. Something under them. A heaviness. A thing he kept going back to, over and over, the way your tongue goes back to a broken tooth.
"Yes," she said.
Pike let his breath out.
"Then I want you to take something," he said. "Out of me. Please."
The cold came up Wren's arms.
"What?" she said.
Pike did not answer for a long time. He sat with his wrapped hand in his lap, and his split mouth shut, and stared at the dome.
"At the camp," he said at last. "At the edge. The first one. Where the tents were."
Last Thorn.
Wren had not been at Last Thorn when it burned. She had come the morning after, with the Inquisitor, at dawn, and seen the black hoops of the tent poles in their rows like the ribs of something dead, and the singed goats, and the broken iron man by the fire ring. She had not been there in the night.
Pike had. Pike had gone ahead with Master Ferrand and four of the iron men and two of the other Enforcers, on the second day. She had forgotten that. She had not thought about it.
"There was a girl," said Pike. "Little. With one of those gray lines down her face. Painted. By the bread place. The racks. The iron one was going at the racks and she was in front of them with two others and I was—" He stopped. "I was just there. Behind it. With a torch. Master Ferrand said to keep the light on it so it could see. And I did. I kept the light on it."
His voice was perfectly flat. Like somebody reading off a slate.
"And she looked at me," said Pike. "Over its arm. Not at it. At me. With the torch. Like I was the one." He swallowed. "And then somebody came. A girl with a sword. From the side. And there was a sort of— a sound, like wind, and the back came off the iron one, and the spring came out, and I dropped the torch and ran."
Wren sat very still.
"I see her every time I shut my eyes," said Pike. "Every single time. On the Glass I couldn't sleep for it. I thought it was the Glass. I thought it'd go when we got off it." He laughed. A small cracked sound. His lip bled. "It didn't go. It's worse here. It's louder. I can't—" He put his good hand over his eyes. "I can't stop seeing her look at me."
He took the hand away. He turned and looked at Wren for the first time. His eyes were red-rimmed and very young.
"Take it out," he said. "Please, Cadet. You can. I know you can. Just that one. Her face. I don't need it. Take it and put it in a jar or burn it or whatever you do, I don't care. I just want to be able to shut my eyes."
Wren looked at him.
She could do it. She knew she could. It would be easy. Easier than Room Nine. She would only have to take her right hand out of her pocket and take off her glove and lay her bare fingers on the back of his neck, under his cap, where the skin was warm, and reach. Slowly. The slow way. The way she had been taught. She would find the face. A little girl with a painted gray line, by the bread racks, looking over the iron man's arm. She would take hold of it gently and draw it out of him, the way you draw a splinter, and it would come, and he would sleep.
He would sleep tonight. And tomorrow, when it began, he would not remember why he had been afraid.
She took her right hand out of her pocket.
She looked at it. Gloved, in the starlight. The cold wet feeling in it, waiting. It wanted to. It wanted to so badly that she could feel it in the bones of her wrist, like a dog straining at a rope.
She put it back in her pocket.
"No," she said.
Pike stared at her.
"No," said Wren again. She did not know where the word was coming from. It came from somewhere low down, under her breastbone, where the stone of the question had been sitting all these weeks. "I'm sorry, Pike. I won't."
"Why not?" His voice cracked. "You do it all the time. It's your job. It's what they pay you for."
"I know."
"Then—"
"If I take it," said Wren, "you won't remember."
"That's the point."
"No." She was shaking now. She made herself stop. "If I take it, you won't remember that she looked at you. And then tomorrow, when Master Ferrand tells you to hold the light up so the iron can see, you'll hold it up. Because you won't know why you shouldn't."
Pike opened his mouth. And shut it again.
"That's what it's for," said Wren. Very quietly. "Her face. That's what it's for. It's there so you won't do it again."
She did not know if that was true. She had never thought it before. She had spent two years taking things out of people because they hurt, and she had never once asked whether some things were meant to hurt. Whether you needed them to.
He only takes away what hurts, Master Ferrand had said, in the cart, with his kind pale face.
There is nothing in him that hurts, the Inquisitor had said, an hour ago, on the black glass.
Pike sat beside her with his mouth open and his wrapped hand in his lap and his eyes wet.
"I can't sleep," he said. Very small. "I can't."
"I know." Wren looked at him. "I'll stay awake too. If you want. We can not sleep together."
Pike looked at her for a long moment.
Then, slowly, something in his face gave way. Not much. A little. The way ice gives on a pond at the very first of the thaw, a soft crack you can hardly hear. He looked down at the glass between his knees, at the stars in it.
"She had a red thread," he said. "In her hair. Tied round a little bit of it at the back. I keep seeing that. The red thread."
"Then remember the red thread," said Wren.
Pike did not answer.
But he did not get up and go away. He sat beside her on the glass, a little apart, with his knees drawn up. And after a long time his head went down onto his knees, and stayed there. And she did not know whether he was asleep or not, and she did not look to see.
She sat with her hands in her pockets and the cold glove-feeling in them, aching, wanting, and did not take them out.
Ferrand Writes
She watched Master Ferrand.
She did not mean to. She sat on the glass beside the Inquisitor with her blanket round her shoulders and her hand in her pocket on the stone, and she meant to watch the dome. The thin orange line of firelight down the side of the iron door. The two dark shapes of the spear-carriers, standing guard, and the smaller shapes on the glass beside them, the man and the child. She meant to watch that, because tomorrow it would matter.
But her eyes kept going back to Master Ferrand.
He was sitting very straight on his folded blanket, a little apart from the Enforcers. He had made his own small camp. The blanket folded into an exact square, its edges lined up with each other. His hat beside him, crown down, with his gloves laid in it, one on top of the other, palm to palm. His little lantern beside the hat, shuttered down so that only a thin slit of yellow light came out of it and fell across the open page of his notebook.
And on the glass by his right knee, laid down very carefully, there was his rod.
Wren had seen it at his hip every day since the cellar. A silver rod, as long as her forearm, as thick as two fingers. Fine and plain and polished, without a mark on it. It looked like the rods in the locked rack in the Harvesting Section, the ones that Toby polished on Friday afternoons and was not allowed to touch. But it was not like them. She had seen that the first day, in the cart. The spiral that ran round it, the fine bright groove cut into the silver from one end to the other like the thread on a screw, went the wrong way.
The rods in the rack all turned to the right. Every one. She had been taught that her first week. The rod turns to the right, Cadet, always, like the sun. So the thing goes in and does not come out.
Master Ferrand's turned to the left.
She did not know what that meant. Nobody had taught her what a left-turning rod was for. She had never seen another one.
It lay on the glass by his knee in the slit of yellow light. Shining. And he wrote beside it.
He wrote the way he did everything. Neatly. Without hurry. The silver pencil moving across the page in small even strokes, stopping at the end of each line, going back to the start of the next. Every so often he paused, and lifted the pencil, and looked up, out across the dark glass toward the dome, with his head a little on one side. As if listening. And then he looked down and wrote again.
What was he writing?
She knew some of it. The Inquisitor halted the Archon's iron at dusk, a stone's throw from the door, against my advice. That would be there. The Inquisitor removed his glove on stepping onto the Eye. The Inquisitor did not drink. The Inquisitor sat a long time looking down at a drowned farm and spoke to the Cadet of his grandfather.
Did he hear that? He could not have heard that. He had been across the camp. He had been writing.
Wren looked at him and thought that he had heard everything.
And then Master Ferrand looked up from his notebook. Not toward the dome. Toward her.
She could not look away in time.
His pale eyes found her in the dark, across the lantern-light and the sleeping Enforcers, and rested on her. And he smiled. Kindly. The way an uncle smiles at a child who has been caught looking at the sweets.
Then he lifted the silver pencil, a little, the way you might lift a glass to somebody across a room. And went back to writing.
Wren's face burned.
She looked down at the glass between her knees. At the stars falling into it. She held the stone in her pocket so hard that the white ring round its middle pressed a line into her palm.
After a while she heard him get up.
She heard him. That was the strange thing. On the Eye, with no hum, every sound was loud, and he made almost none. But she heard the soft creak of his knees, and the whisper of his near-white coat, and the tiny clink as he picked the rod up off the glass and slid it back into its loop at his left hip. And then his footsteps. Coming round the edge of the camp. Toward her.
She did not look up.
He stopped a little way off. Not close. A polite distance. Where a gentleman would stop, in a drawing room, before he was introduced.
"Cadet," he said softly. "I do hope I'm not disturbing you."
The Inquisitor did not turn his head. He was still looking at the dome. His bare right hand still rested on his knee.
"No, sir," said Wren.
"I couldn't help seeing that you weren't asleep. It's very difficult, isn't it, on the Eye? After the Glass. One gets used to the hum. And then it stops and one hears oneself think." He made a little sound that might have been a laugh. "I find that very tiring. Don't you?"
Wren did not answer.
"I wondered," said Master Ferrand, "whether you were still keeping your book. Your little brown one. From Quill Row." A pause. "Fourpence, I think you said."
She had not said. She had never said. He had known that in the cart, the first night, too.
"Yes, sir," she said.
"I'm so glad. It's a good habit. Writing things down." He smiled down at her. "I've always found that if one doesn't write things down, one finds afterward that one remembers them quite wrongly. People do, you know. They remember things the way they wish they had been. It's very human." He tilted his head. "Did you write about the farmer? At the gate?"
Wren's heart stopped.
She felt it stop. One beat missing, like a missed stair. And then it went on, too fast.
She had written about the farmer. In big shaky letters, behind the barn, on the third day out, with her mouth still sour. Corwen Lusk. Farmer. He stood in his own gate. She had not written given back to nobody. She had not known how to finish it.
"Yes, sir," she said.
"Ah." Master Ferrand looked pleased. "I'd so like to see it. One day. I'd like to compare. It's always interesting, I find, to see how a thing looks from somebody else's side." He waited. When she said nothing, he went on, gently, as if she had answered. "Not tonight, of course. You're tired. But one day."
"Yes, sir."
"Good girl."
He stood there a moment longer. Looking at her. And then, for the first time since he had come over, he looked at the Inquisitor.
The Inquisitor had not moved at all.
"Inquisitor," said Master Ferrand. "Good night."
"Good night, Master Ferrand."
That was all. Master Ferrand stood a moment more, smiling at the side of the Inquisitor's face. Then he turned and went back round the edge of the camp, neatly, without hurry, to his folded blanket and his little lantern and his hat with the gloves in it. And sat down. And opened his notebook.
And wrote.
Wren let her breath out. She had not known she was holding it.
"He wants to know," she said. Very low. "Which of us. Tomorrow. Which of us I'll be standing behind."
The Inquisitor did not answer for a while.
"Yes," he said at last. "So do I."
