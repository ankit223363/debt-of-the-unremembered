Chapter 32: Rent Due Friday, Part 1 of 5: Four in the Morning
Four in the Morning
Mrs. Marta had not slept in a bed that was not her own for thirty-one years, and she was not going to start now.
So she did not sleep.
She sat at the kitchen table in the back room of the Watch house on Rope Street, in Sergeant Moll's best chair, with her feet on a little wooden box to keep them off the cold floor, and her mother's old gray shawl round her shoulders, and a pot of tea in front of her that had gone cold an hour ago. And she wrote.
The back room of the Watch house was not a nice room. She had decided that at once, the moment Moll brought her in on Wednesday, and nothing since had changed her mind. It was long and low, with a stone floor that sloped toward a drain in one corner, and walls that had been whitewashed so long ago they had gone the color of old teeth. There was one small window, high up, with bars on it. There was a long scrubbed table down the middle, scarred with knife cuts and ring marks and the initials of forty years of bored night constables. There were benches along both sides. There was a rack of wooden truncheons on the wall, and a row of hooks with old oilskin capes hanging on them, stiff as boards. There was a cupboard with a broken door, full of tin mugs. There was a smell of wet wool and lamp oil and boot blacking and men.
And there was the oven.
That was the only thing in the room Mrs. Marta approved of. It took up most of the end wall. A great black iron range, older than the building, older than Moll, older than anybody, with two ovens side by side and a firebox between them and a rail along the front where somebody had hung a row of gray socks to dry. It ticked as it cooled. It gave off a deep, steady, comfortable heat, like an old dog asleep by a door. And it ran hot on the left side. Moll had told her that the first night, very seriously, as if it were a secret of state. Left side's hot. You have to turn things.
She had turned the pie. Twice. It had still caught a little at the edge.
She pushed that thought away and went back to her list.
It was a long list. It went down one side of the paper and up the other and round the edges, in her square, careful, heavy hand, the hand she had learned at the Saint Ida's school sixty years ago from a teacher with a ruler. Names. Streets. Doors. Some of them had a tick beside them. Some had a cross. Some had a small drawing she had made herself, so that she would know which was which without reading. A key, for Juno's grandfather's friends at the night doors. A bucket, for the step women. A little round loaf, for the bakers.
Corin's people.
She had not needed the boy's book to make it. She had been carrying his parcels up those ninety-one stairs for six years. His laundry. His letters. His coal. His bread, when he forgot to buy any, which was most weeks. His medicine, the winter he had the fever and lay in that cold room at the top of the house talking to himself in a language she did not know. She had answered his door when people knocked. She had taken in his notes. She had watched who came up the stairs and who went down them, and what they looked like, and whether they wiped their feet.
She knew his people. She knew them better than he did. He forgot them, half the time. She never did.
Toby, from the Ministry. Little nervous thing, always apologizing, wipes his feet twice. Tick.
The ink woman. Hook Lane. Freckles. Laughs like a crow. Smells of oil of cloves. Cross. And beside it: Watched. Gone to Grue's.
The big captain with the scar. A question mark.
The Thorne girl. She had drawn a little sword. Then she had crossed out the sword and drawn a little boat instead, very small. Then she had scratched that out too, so hard that the pen went through the paper. Some things you did not write down. Not even in your own kitchen. Not even at four in the morning.
She put the pen down and rubbed her eyes.
They hurt. Everything hurt. Her back hurt from the chair. Her feet hurt from the box. Her hands hurt from the pen, the knuckles swollen and red, the way they always were in winter. Her chest hurt, under the shawl, in a way that was not her back or her feet or her hands, and that she did not want to think about.
Across the table, on the bench by the oven, Sergeant Moll was asleep.
He was sitting up. He slept sitting up, he had told her, because of his leg. If he lay down it set stiff and he could not get up again in the morning. So he sat on the bench with his back against the warm side of the range and his bad leg stretched out in front of him on a stool, and his big arms folded across his big chest, and his chin on his collar. He snored. Not loudly. A low, slow, rumbling snore, like a cart going over cobbles a long way off.
His hat was on the table beside her. A new one. The old one had been lost at the Bell on Sunday night, in the business with the gin. He had bought this one on Monday from a stall on Coal Street and it did not fit him, it sat too high on his head, and he kept taking it off and looking at it as if it had insulted him.
There was a cut across the back of his left hand. Long and red and healing, under a strip of clean linen she had put on it herself on Wednesday night, because the bar towel he had wrapped it in was a disgrace.
She looked at him for a long time.
She had known Moll for twenty years. Not well. The way you know a man who walks the same streets as you, and nods to you at the pump, and helps you carry your coal when your husband is a Slate and your granddaughter is too small. He had been at Terrence's door the night Terrence came home. He had been the one who knocked, and stood there in the rain with his hat in his hands, and said, Mrs. Marta, I'm very sorry, he's here, but he's not right. And then he had carried Terrence up the stairs himself, because Terrence did not know which ones were his anymore.
She had never thanked him for that. She did not know how.
And now he had given her his chair, and his oven, and his back room, and put a mattress on the floor that she would not lie on. Without being asked. Without saying a word about what it might cost him to have the landlady of No. 14 Tallow Street, whose top-floor tenant was the most wanted man in Veridia, sitting in his Watch house drinking his tea.
She looked down at the table.
At the paper that lay there, apart from her list. Small and square. Folded and unfolded so many times that the creases had gone soft and gray. She had found it on the step on Wednesday night. She had read it a hundred times since.
HE LET HIM GO ON PURPOSE.
HE WANTS TO SEE WHO HE RUNS TO.
DO NOT GO TO THE BARGE. —W.
She did not know who W was. She had an idea. She did not like the idea. A girl in a white coat, with her hair pulled back tight and a face like a knife, who had come up the stairs at No. 14 on Tuesday night and sat in Mrs. Marta's own kitchen and talked to Corin in a low voice for an hour, while Mrs. Marta listened at the scullery door and pretended to be scrubbing a pot.
An Inquisitor. In her kitchen. Warning him.
The world, Mrs. Marta thought, had gone very strange.
She picked up the pen again.
At the bottom of the list, under everything, she wrote, in her square, heavy, ruler-taught hand:
He wants to see who he runs to. Then he must not see. Nobody goes to any door twice. Nobody goes who has been seen. Use people he does not look at.
She looked at that.
Then she wrote, underneath it, one word, and drew a small bucket beside it.
Steps.
And at that moment, far off, through the high barred window, she heard the bell of Saint Bride's strike the half hour after four. And under it, faint and cold, the first clank of a pail handle on a pump. Somebody, somewhere in the dark of the Lower City, was already up and going to work.
Mrs. Marta put down the pen, and stood up, and reached for her coat.
The Step Women
The pump on Coal Street stood in the middle of a little square of black cobbles where five lanes met, under a lamp that had been put up by the Ministry in the year Mrs. Marta was born and had not been cleaned since.
It was a big pump. Iron, painted green once, now mostly rust, with a long curved handle like the neck of a goose and a heavy stone trough under the spout. The trough was frozen. Not all the way through. There was a skin of ice on top, thick and gray, with a ragged hole broken in the middle where the first pail of the morning had gone in. Round the hole, the ice was white and cracked like the top of a pie.
There were women at the pump.
Six of them, when Mrs. Marta came round the corner. Then eight. Then eleven. They came out of the five dark lanes one by one, in the bitter blue dark before dawn, each with her pail. Some had two. Some had a pail and a brush and a bundle of rags tied up in a sack. They wore old coats and older shawls and men's boots laced with string, and their breath went up in white clouds under the dirty lamp, and they did not talk much. It was too cold to talk. They stood in a loose line, stamping their feet, waiting their turn at the handle.
The step women.
Mrs. Marta had been one of them for six years. After Terrence came home. When there was no more night-porter's pay coming in, and a granddaughter to feed, and a top-floor tenant who forgot to pay his rent one month in three. She had come to this pump every morning at half past four, in every weather, with her pail and her brush and her bundle of rags. And then she had walked up the Hill in the dark. To the High Quarter. To the big houses with their tall white steps going up to their tall black doors. And she had knelt down on those steps and scrubbed them. One after another. Until they shone. So that when the great people came out of their great doors in the morning, the first thing their feet touched would be clean.
Nobody ever looked at her.
That was the thing about the step women. Nobody looked. Not the masters. Not the mistresses. Not the footmen or the maids or the boys who ran messages or the Watch constables walking their beats. A woman on her knees on a step with a bucket was not a person. She was a part of the morning, like the milk cart and the lamplighter going round to put out the lamps. You stepped round her. You did not see her face.
Mrs. Marta had hated it for six years.
She was going to use it now.
She walked up to the line at the pump. She had no pail. She had her coat, buttoned to the throat, and her mother's gray shawl over her head, and her hands pushed into her sleeves. And she stood at the end of the line and waited, the way she had always waited, until the woman in front of her turned round.
It was Big Maud Croft.
Big Maud was the biggest woman Mrs. Marta had ever seen. Not fat. Big. Tall and wide and solid, like a door, with arms like a man's and red hands like hams and a broad red face under a man's flat cap. She had scrubbed the steps of the Archon's own Chancery for twenty-two years. She could carry four full pails at once, two in each hand, up the whole of the Ministry Steps without stopping. She had once knocked down a drunk Enforcer with one of them.
She looked down at Mrs. Marta for a long moment.
"Marta," she said. Her voice was surprisingly soft for such a big woman. "Thought you'd given up the steps."
"I have."
"Then what're you doing at the pump at half four on a Thursday with no bucket?"
Mrs. Marta looked up at her.
And then she looked past her, at the others. At the whole line of them, stamping and steaming under the dirty lamp. Win Barrow, small and thin and coughing, who did the Thornwick Row houses. The Pole sisters, Bess and Hattie, who looked so alike that nobody could tell them apart, and who did the Bank Street side between them. Young Bridie Kane, only sixteen, with her red hands wrapped in rags, who had started in the autumn when her mother's back went. Old Granny Sedge, who was seventy if she was a day and had been scrubbing steps since before the Ministry put up the lamp. And five more. Eleven women, at a pump, in the dark. Every one of them on her way up the Hill to kneel on the doorsteps of the most powerful people in Veridia.
Nobody ever looked at them.
"I need help," said Mrs. Marta.
The line went quiet.
Not all at once. It went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone drops a plate. First one woman turned round. Then the next. Then the whole line was looking at her, under the lamp, with their pails in their hands and their breath going up white.
Mrs. Marta had never asked anyone for help in her life. Not when Terrence came home. Not when her son died. Not when the rent on No. 14 went up, and up again, and the Ministry put a tax on coal. She did not know how to do it. She had always thought that if she ever had to, she would cry, or shout, or make a fool of herself.
She did none of those things. She found that her voice was very steady.
"You know my top floor," she said. "The Ministry man. Corin."
A few nods. Win Barrow coughed into her shawl.
"He's in trouble," said Mrs. Marta. "The worst kind. The Inquisition came for him yesterday with an Eraser. You'll have heard." More nods. Everybody had heard. The Eraser had come out through the front window of No. 14 in broad daylight. Half the Lower City had seen it walking down Tallow Street with glass in its coat. "He got away. He's alive. But there's a man in the Inquisition who let him go on purpose. So he can follow him. So he can see every door he knocks on, and every friend who opens it." She paused. "And then he'll take them all."
The square was very quiet. Somewhere in one of the lanes a dog barked once, and stopped.
"I've got a list," said Mrs. Marta. "Of his people. His friends. The ones who've helped him. They need to know. They need to be told to stay away from him, and from each other, and from their own doors if they can. But if I send Juno, they'll see Juno. If I send a runner, they'll see the runner. They've got the runners' names. They've got everybody's names." She looked round the circle of faces. "Everybody they ever look at."
Nobody said anything for a long moment.
Then Big Maud Croft put her pail down on the cobbles with a clank.
"And nobody ever looks at us," she said slowly.
"No," said Mrs. Marta. "Nobody ever does."
Maud looked at her. Then she looked round at the others. At Win, and Bess and Hattie, and young Bridie, and old Granny Sedge. Something went from face to face round the square, under the lamp. Mrs. Marta could not have said what it was. Not a smile. Something harder and older than a smile. The look of women who have been on their knees for a very long time, and have just been told that it might, for once, be good for something.
"Well," said Big Maud Croft. She picked up her pail. "Give it here, then. This list."
Doors
They worked it out at the pump, in the dark, in less time than it takes to boil an egg.
Mrs. Marta read the list aloud. Not all of it. Not the parts she had scratched out. She read it in a low voice, close, with the eleven women crowded round her in a tight ring under the dirty lamp, their pails at their feet and their shawls pulled up over their heads so that from across the square they would have looked like nothing at all. A knot of washerwomen, gossiping at a pump. The most ordinary sight in the Lower City.
And as she read each name, a hand went up.
"Bram," said Mrs. Marta. "The stationer. Quill Row. The bow window with the little square panes."
"That's me," said young Bridie Kane. "I do the Quill Row houses on Thursdays. The bookbinder's next door's got a step like a cliff."
"Don't go to his door. Go to the next one. Do the bookbinder's step, slow. When he comes out for his milk, say it to the step, not to him. Don't look up."
Bridie nodded. Her small red face was very serious.
"Say what?"
Mrs. Marta thought about it.
She had thought about it all night. What to say. You could not say the Inquisitor is following Corin Vane and wants to take everyone who helps him. Not out loud. Not on a doorstep. Not in a city where walls had ears and children could be paid a copper to remember things.
"Say: the landlady says the rent is late, and not to call round."
Bridie frowned. "That's all?"
"That's all. They'll know."
"How?"
"Because I've never once in six years told anybody the rent was late," said Mrs. Marta. "I always say it's due Friday. Always. Whatever day it is. Corin's people know that. They've all heard me say it." She looked round the ring of faces. "If I say it's late, they'll know something's wrong."
Big Maud Croft let out a short sound through her nose that might have been a laugh.
"And not to call round," she said. "Meaning stay away from you."
"Meaning stay away from him. And each other. And their own doors, if they've got anywhere else to be."
The women looked at one another.
"Next," said Mrs. Marta.
The next was Briggs. Warden of the Intake Hall on Lantern Quay. Granny Sedge put up her hand. She did the Bank's own steps, the great white marble ones under the columns, every morning at five, before the doors opened. She had done them for thirty years. She had seen Warden Briggs go in and out of the side door a thousand times. He always nodded to her. He was the only one who ever did.
"He'll listen," she said. Her voice was thin and cracked, like an old cup. "He's a sad man. Sad men listen."
The next was Toby, the little nervous clerk from the Ministry. Win Barrow took that one, coughing. Her sister-in-law scrubbed the Ministry's back stair. The next was Mother Grue's, under the Dye-Works. Bess and Hattie Pole took that one between them, because nobody went near the Dye-Works arches alone, not even step women. The next was the cooper on Saltings Lane who had lent Corin his cart. The next was Doctor Silas, in his crypt. The next was the lamplighter at the bottom of Wharf Street who had once let Corin sleep in his hut.
One after another. Hand after hand.
By the time Mrs. Marta reached the bottom of the list, every woman at the pump had two doors, or three. And every door was one that woman passed every morning anyway, on her ordinary round, with her ordinary pail. Nobody would have to go anywhere they did not always go. Nobody would have to be seen anywhere they were not always seen.
"And not twice," said Mrs. Marta. "Not to the same door twice. Not ever. If they don't hear you the first time, let it go. Don't go back."
"What if they don't understand?" said Bridie.
"Then they don't," said Mrs. Marta. "And we've done what we could."
She folded the list and put it inside her coat.
"And if anybody asks you," she said. "Anybody. A constable. A man in a gray cape. A lady in a nice coat with a giant behind her. Anybody at all. You were at the pump this morning and you talked about the price of coal."
"It's gone up again," said Win Barrow, coughing. "Ha'penny on the hundredweight."
"There you are, then," said Mrs. Marta.
They broke up.
Not all at once. That would have been noticed. They went the way they always went, one or two at a time, pails clanking, into the five dark lanes. Granny Sedge first, slow and bent, toward the river and the Bank. The Pole sisters, arm in arm, toward the Dye-Works. Win Barrow, coughing, toward the Hill. Young Bridie last, with her red hands wrapped in rags and her two pails swinging, almost running.
Big Maud Croft stayed.
She stood beside Mrs. Marta at the frozen trough under the dirty lamp, with her four pails at her feet, and looked down at her.
"You've not given me a door," she said.
"No."
"I do the Chancery. The Archon's own steps." Maud's broad red face was very still. "I'm up there every morning at five. On my knees. Right outside his door. Right under his windows. Twenty-two years." A pause. "If anybody on that list's got a door anywhere near the Hill—"
"Nobody does," said Mrs. Marta. "Not near the Chancery."
Maud looked at her for a long time.
"That's not why," she said.
Mrs. Marta did not answer.
"You didn't give me a door," said Maud slowly, "because I'm the one who'd be noticed if I didn't turn up. I'm the one they know by sight. Big Maud on the Chancery steps. Twenty-two years." She folded her great arms. "You're keeping me back. For something else."
The lamp hissed over their heads. Somewhere a cart was starting up, iron wheels on cobbles, very far off.
"I don't know yet," said Mrs. Marta at last. Very quietly. "I don't know what for. But somebody's going to need somebody on those steps, one of these mornings. Somebody who's always there. Somebody who's always been there." She looked up at Maud. "I'd like it to be someone I trust."
Maud did not say anything for a long moment.
Then she bent down, slowly, and picked up all four of her pails, two in each huge red hand, as if they weighed nothing.
"Then I'll be there," she said. "Same as always. You know where."
And she walked away up Coal Street toward the Hill, in the blue dark, with her pails clanking, and did not look back.
