Chapter 32: Rent Due Friday, Part 2 of 5: Moll
Moll
When she got back to the Watch house, Moll was awake and furious.
He was standing in the middle of the back room in his shirtsleeves, with his braces hanging down round his knees and his new hat in one hand, and his big boiled-ham face had gone the color of a brick. The door to the yard was standing open behind him, letting in the cold. One of the night constables, a thin young man called Hurley with a nose like a parsnip, was hovering by the oven with a tin mug in his hand, trying very hard to look like part of the wall.
"Where," said Moll, "have you been?"
"Coal Street," said Mrs. Marta. She came in and shut the yard door behind her and took off her shawl and shook the frost off it. "At the pump."
"At the—" Moll stopped. He looked at the hat in his hand as if it had done something. "At the pump. At half past four in the morning. In the dark. Alone. With half the Inquisition looking for anybody who ever said good morning to your top floor."
"Yes."
"I woke up and you were gone. Your chair was empty. Your coat was gone. I thought—" He stopped again. He put the hat down on the table, very carefully, as if he were afraid he might throw it. "I had Hurley halfway out the door to go and look for you."
Mrs. Marta looked at Hurley.
Hurley looked at the floor.
"Well, he can come back in," said Mrs. Marta. "I'm here. Put the kettle on, Constable. The big one. And see if there's any of that bread left. And not the end. The end's for the birds."
Hurley put the kettle on with great speed.
Moll sat down. Slowly. On the bench by the oven, with his bad leg stuck out in front of him. He rubbed his face with both hands, hard, the way a man does when he has been frightened and is trying to rub the fright off like dirt.
"Marta," he said, through his fingers.
"I know."
"You don't know. You don't know what they're like. I've seen what they're like. I've seen what comes back." He took his hands down. His eyes were red-rimmed and very tired. "I carried Terrence up those stairs."
"I know you did," said Mrs. Marta. "I was there."
They looked at each other across the scarred table.
And then, because there was nothing else to do, and because she had never in her life been able to sit in a room where a man was upset without doing something with her hands, Mrs. Marta took the bread from Hurley, and the knife, and began to cut it.
She told Moll about the pump while she cut. About the step women, and the list, and the doors, and the message. The rent is late, and not to call round. She told him about Big Maud, and the Chancery steps. She did not tell him everything. She did not tell him about the boat she had scratched out on her list. But she told him enough.
Moll listened.
He listened the way she had seen him listen to drunks and thieves and lost children and furious wives for twenty years, at the front desk of the Watch house. Heavily. Without moving. With his big head a little on one side. And while he listened, his right hand lay on the table in front of him, and the fingers of it moved.
Tapping.
She had noticed it before. On Wednesday night, when she first came, and he sat across from her while she drank her tea and could not stop shaking. And again in the night, once, when she looked up from her list and he was awake, staring at the oven door, and did not know she was watching. His thick fingers on the wood, tapping. Not loudly. Softly, with the tips. A little run of taps, and then a pause, and then a different run.
Four, quick. Then two, slow. Then four again.
It was like rain on a window. Or a horse going by on the cobbles outside, a long way off. She did not think he knew he was doing it.
"Step women," said Moll, when she had finished.
"Yes."
"On the doorsteps of the Hill. Taking messages to Corin Vane's friends. Under the noses of the Inquisition." He shook his head slowly. "Saints help us. If Kross ever finds out—"
"He won't."
"He finds out everything, Marta."
"Then he'll find out that eleven women scrubbed eleven steps this morning, the same as every morning," said Mrs. Marta. "And talked about the price of coal." She put a slice of bread in front of him. Thick, the way he liked it. "Eat that."
He looked at it. He did not eat it.
His fingers went on tapping beside the plate. Four, two. Four, two.
"Where did you get that leg?" said Mrs. Marta suddenly.
Moll looked up, startled.
"What?"
"Your leg. Twenty years I've known you, and you've always had it, and I've never asked." She sat down across from him and folded her hands on the table. "So I'm asking."
Moll looked at her for a long moment.
Then, slowly, he looked down at the leg stretched out on the stool in front of him. At the knee, which was thick and stiff under his trouser leg, and the foot, which turned out a little at an angle a foot should not turn.
"The army," he said at last.
"I didn't know you were in the army."
"Twelve years. The Commonwealth Foot. Before the Watch." He was quiet for a moment. "A long time ago. I was very young. Younger than your Juno, when I went in."
"And the leg?"
"A wagon," said Moll. "On a march. In the rain. It went over in the mud, and I was underneath it." He moved his shoulders. "They sent me home. Gave me a paper saying I'd served well, and a bit of silver, and a stick. I came to Veridia because I'd never seen it. And I've been here since."
"Where was the march?"
Moll did not answer.
His fingers had stopped tapping. They lay flat on the table, all five of them, very still.
"North," he said at last. "Up the valleys. Long way north." He picked up the bread, suddenly, and bit into it, and chewed, and did not look at her. "Long time ago, Marta. I don't remember much of it."
And that was a lie. She knew it the way she knew when a tenant said the rent was coming tomorrow. She had been a landlady for thirty-one years. She knew every kind of lie there was.
She did not say anything. She poured him his tea.
They sat in the warm back room while the gray light came up in the barred window, and Hurley made himself small by the oven, and the old black range ticked and breathed. And after a while, without seeming to know it, Moll's fingers began to move on the table again.
Four, quick. Two, slow.
Four.
Mrs. Marta watched them, and drank her tea, and wondered what a boy younger than Juno had seen, a long time ago, up the valleys, a long way north, that a grown man would not tell an old woman in a warm kitchen thirty years later.
The Constable From Tallow Street
At eight o'clock, Terrence woke up.
He woke the way he always woke, all at once and very politely, as if somebody had knocked on a door inside him and he had gone to answer it. One moment he was a long thin shape under the gray Watch-house blanket on the mattress by the far wall, the mattress Moll had put down for Mrs. Marta and that she would not lie on. The next he was sitting up, with the blanket round his shoulders and his white hair standing up on one side of his head, looking round the long low room with his pale blue eyes. Taking it in. The scrubbed table. The rack of truncheons. The oilskin capes on their hooks. The old black range. Moll on his bench. Hurley by the oven. And Mrs. Marta, in Moll's best chair, with her list.
He did not know where he was. Mrs. Marta could see that. He never knew where he was, in the mornings. Not even at home. He would sit up in their own bed at No. 14 and look round their own room, at the wardrobe and the washstand and the picture of the sea over the mantel, with exactly that face, polite and puzzled and patient, the face of a guest who has woken in a house he does not remember being invited to.
Then his eyes came to her. And stopped.
And he smiled.
"Good morning," said Old Terrence.
"Good morning, love," said Mrs. Marta.
She got up. Her knees cracked. She went to him and knelt down on the cold stone by the mattress, the way she knelt every morning at home, and smoothed his hair flat with her hand. It would not stay. It never stayed. She wet her palm from the jug on the floor and smoothed it again, and this time it lay down, white and fine and thin as the fluff on a thistle, and she patted it.
"There," she said. "Now you're fit to be seen."
Terrence looked at her with his clean empty eyes. He did not know who she was. She knew that. He had not known for eight years. But he knew she was the one who smoothed his hair, and that was something. Some mornings it was everything.
His right hand had come out from under the blanket. It lay on his knee, and his thumb was moving. Rubbing. Slowly. Round and round, on the pad of his first finger, the way it had rubbed every day for eight years. As if there was something small and smooth and precious between his finger and his thumb, a coin or a key, and he was keeping it warm.
There was nothing there. There never was.
Mrs. Marta put her own hand over his for a moment. Then she got up, and went to the range, and started his porridge.
The constable came in at half past eight.
He came in from the front, through the door that led to the front office of the Watch house, where Moll's high wooden desk was and the bench for people waiting and the board with the notices on it. He was young. Very young. Twenty, perhaps, or not even that, with a round red face and big red ears that stuck out under his helmet, and a gray cape over his shoulders so wet that it dripped all the way across the stone floor. His boots squelched. His nose was running. He looked as if he had been standing in the rain all night, which, Mrs. Marta realized, looking at him, he had.
She knew him.
It took her a moment. Then she had it. He was the one from her own front step. The one the Inquisition had left outside No. 14 Tallow Street yesterday afternoon, after the Eraser had gone, standing by her broken window with his hands behind his back and his face going redder and redder with cold. She had seen him from the corner, when Moll brought her away. She had felt sorry for him even then.
"Pym," said Moll. He had woken at the sound of the door and was sitting up on his bench, rubbing his face. "What are you doing here? You're on Tallow Street till nine."
"Relieved, Sergeant," said the young constable. His teeth were chattering. "Early. The Inquisition sent their own man to take the door. Gray coat. Didn't say a word to me. Just stood there till I went." He looked at the range, and the kettle on it, and his whole face went soft with longing. "Sergeant, is there any tea?"
Mrs. Marta was already pouring it.
She gave him the mug, and a chair by the range, and a slice of bread with dripping on it, and a dry blanket for his shoulders, and she did not say anything at all until he had drunk half the tea and eaten all the bread and stopped shaking. Then she sat down across from him.
"That's my house," she said.
Pym looked up. He looked at her properly, for the first time. And his round red face went, if possible, even redder.
"Oh," he said. "Oh, missus. I'm sorry. I didn't know you were—" He stopped, and looked at Moll, and back at her. "I'm sorry about your window. And your floor. It wasn't me. I wasn't let in. I was only told to stand."
"I know it wasn't you," said Mrs. Marta. "Drink your tea."
He drank his tea.
"Who came?" said Mrs. Marta. "After I went. Who came to my house?"
Pym put down his mug. He wiped his nose on the back of his hand, and then looked guiltily at the hand, and at her, and wiped it on the blanket instead.
"A lot of people, missus," he said. "Inquisition men, mostly. In and out all afternoon. Carrying things down. Boxes. Papers. They took everything out of the top room. Every little thing." He swallowed. "They took the jars. All the empty jars off the shelf. Forty-one of them. I counted them going past. They wrapped each one in paper like it was glass from a church."
Mrs. Marta's mouth went thin.
"And a lady came," said Pym. "From the Bank."
"What lady?"
"A lady in a dark green coat, missus. With white gloves on. And a great big man with her holding an umbrella, bigger than you'd think a man could be. She showed me a card with the Bank's seal on it. Gold. She said the house was under audit." He looked at Moll. "I didn't know if I was allowed to stop her, Sergeant. I didn't think I was. Nobody stops the Bank."
"Nobody stops the Bank," agreed Moll heavily.
"She went right up," said Pym. "All the way. To the top room. She was up there nearly an hour. The big man stayed outside the door, on the landing. Couldn't fit in, I expect." He hesitated. "And when she came down, missus—"
"What?"
Pym looked down at his hands.
"She'd been crying," he said. "Proper crying. Not just a bit. Her face was all—and she had her hand on her chest, like this—" He pressed his big red hand flat against the front of his wet cape. "All the way down the street. And she stopped on your step, missus, and looked back at the house. Up at the top window. For a long time. Like she was trying to remember something."
The room was very quiet.
By the oven, Hurley had stopped pretending to be part of the wall and was listening with his mouth open. On the bench, Moll's fingers had begun to move again on his knee. Four. Two. On the mattress, Terrence was eating his porridge, very slowly and neatly, a little at a time, the way she had taught him.
Mrs. Marta sat very still.
She did not know who the lady in green was. She did not know why a lady from the Bank would go up ninety-one stairs to an empty room under the roof and come down crying. She did not understand it at all.
But she remembered something.
She remembered Tuesday night. The boy at her kitchen table, very late, with his Ledger open in front of him and a horseshoe nail in his hand that he had borrowed from the box under the sink. She had asked him what he wanted with a nail at that hour, and he had not answered, and she had not asked again. And in the morning, when she went up with his tea, she had seen the fresh white scratches in the plaster by his pillow, and she had not said anything about those either. Because some things in her house were not her business. Not even her plaster.
Three words. She had not read them. She had made a point of not reading them. She had put down his tea and looked at the window instead.
She wished now that she had read them.
"And then?" said Moll.
"And then, Sergeant, nothing. Nobody else came. Not all night. Just rain." Pym rubbed his eyes. "Except one thing."
"What thing?"
"Round about two in the morning," said Pym slowly, "a man came down the street. In a gray cape. Not Inquisition gray. Ordinary. He didn't stop. He didn't look at the house. He walked past me and said good night, Constable very polite, and went on down to the corner." He frowned. "And then he asked me something. On his way past. Just in passing. Like he didn't care about the answer."
"What did he ask?"
"He asked me where the old lady had gone," said Pym. "The landlady. He said he had a parcel for her."
Mrs. Marta felt the cold come into the room. It did not come through the door. It came from somewhere inside her own chest.
"And what did you say?"
Pym looked at her. His round red face was very tired, and very young, and quite honest.
"I said I didn't know, missus," he said. "Which was true. I didn't. Nobody told me." He looked at Moll. "Nobody tells me anything, Sergeant."
Moll let out a long breath.
"Good lad," he said. And then, after a moment, more quietly: "Keep it that way."
Juno at Noon
The step women came back one by one through the morning, and not one of them came to the Watch house.
That was the rule. Mrs. Marta had made it herself, at the pump, and she held to it, even though it nearly killed her. Nobody came to Rope Street. Nobody came near Moll. If a message had gone, it had gone, and if it had not, it had not, and either way she would know soon enough, the way you always know things in the Lower City, sideways, from somebody's cousin, over a wall.
She knew by nine that Bram had got his. Bridie Kane's little brother came running past the back window of the Watch house with a hoop and a stick, going nowhere in particular, and as he went past he sang at the top of his voice, to no tune at all, the stationer's shut his shutters, the stationer's shut his shutters, and then he was gone round the corner with the hoop bouncing on the cobbles.
She knew by ten that Briggs had got his. Granny Sedge's grandson came in at the front to report a lost dog. A brown one. With a white foot. It answered to the name of Fenn, he said, and it had been seen going into the Bank by the side door at five that morning and it had looked back over its shoulder at Granny Sedge on the steps and nodded, and then gone in. Moll wrote the lost dog down very seriously in the book on the front desk, and gave the boy a ha'penny, and the boy went away.
She knew by eleven about Toby. And Mother Grue's. And the cooper. And the lamplighter at the bottom of Wharf Street.
She did not know about the ink woman, because the ink woman had already gone to ground. She did not know about the big captain with the scar, because nobody had seen him since Tuesday night. She did not know about the Thorne girl, because she had not sent anybody to the Thorne girl, and was not going to, and had scratched her out of her own list so hard the pen went through.
And she did not know about Corin.
That was the one that sat in her chest under the shawl like a stone.
She made bread. It was the only thing she could think of to do with her hands. She found flour in Moll's cupboard, and a bit of old yeast in a cracked bowl that she did not trust, and made it go anyway, by sheer force of will, and set the dough to rise in a bowl on the warm right side of the range, not the left, because the left was hot and you had to turn things. And she kneaded it. Hard. Harder than it needed. With her swollen red winter knuckles, pushing and folding and pushing, as if the dough had done something to her.
Terrence sat in the chair by the range and watched her.
He liked to watch her make bread. He always had. Even now. Even when he did not know her name. He sat with his hands folded in his lap and his thumb rubbing, rubbing, and his pale blue eyes followed her hands on the dough, back and forth, back and forth, and sometimes he smiled at it. At the dough. As if it reminded him of something he could not quite reach.
She did not mind. She had stopped minding years ago.
At noon the bell of Saint Bride's rang, and she heard feet on the Ropewalk Stairs.
She knew those feet. She would have known them in her sleep. Fast, and light, and two steps at a time, and never once touching the wall, because the wall was slimy and Juno had decided when she was five that she was never going to touch it. Up and up from the river. Along the cobbles. To the back door of the Watch house, which did not lock properly and never had.
The door banged open.
And there she was. In her grandfather's huge brown coat, turned inside out so the gray lining showed. With mud on her face in streaks, on purpose. With reeds in her hair, not on purpose. With her chin stuck out and her eyes enormous and her whole body shaking, from the cold or from something else, so hard that her teeth rattled.
"He's alive," said Juno.
Mrs. Marta put down the dough.
She did not decide to. Her hands simply let go of it, and it sat there in the bowl on the table, half kneaded, with the marks of her knuckles in it. And she crossed the stone floor of the back room in four steps, she who had not crossed any floor in four steps for twenty years, and took her granddaughter in her floury arms, coat and mud and reeds and all, and held on.
She did not say anything. She could not have said anything. She held on, and felt the small hard shaking body against her, and the cold coming off the huge wet coat, and the smell of the river and the marsh and the mud, and under it all the smell of Juno, the one she would have known in the dark, warm and sharp and a little like bread. And she shut her eyes.
"Gran," said Juno, into her shoulder. "Gran, you're squashing me."
"Be quiet."
"Gran, you're getting flour on—"
"I said be quiet."
Juno was quiet.
After a while Mrs. Marta let go. She held her granddaughter at arm's length and looked at her. At all of her. At the cut on her cheek that had not been there yesterday, and the blisters on her palms, red and broken, and the blue round her lips. And the eyes. Which were Terrence's eyes, the way they had been before. Dark and quick and full of everything.
"Sit down," she said. "Eat. Then tell me."
Juno sat down. She ate. Bread and dripping, and the end of the cheese, and two cups of tea with three sugars in each, which Mrs. Marta would never have allowed on any ordinary day. Moll came in from the front office and stood in the doorway with his hat in his hand and watched her eat, and did not say anything either. Hurley had gone off duty. Pym was asleep on the bench under a blanket, snoring. Terrence watched the bread.
Then Juno told them.
She told it fast, the way she always did, with the words falling over each other. The sewer. The flood. The outfall, number three, and the long wait, and Wat saying five more minutes. The boat. The dead ship on the Saltmarsh, ten miles down, a great black burned thing called the Margery lying on its side in the mud like the bones of a whale. Corin wet and blue and shaking in the bottom of the boat. Corin eating the pie.
"All of it," said Juno. "The whole pie. He didn't even save the crust."
Mrs. Marta did not say anything. She looked at her hands on the table.
"He's all right, Gran," said Juno, more quietly. "He's not all right. But he's all right."
"What does that mean?"
Juno looked down at her empty cup.
"He burned something," she said. "In the cellar. To get away. Something big." She turned the cup round on the table. "He doesn't have his first words anymore. The ones from when he was little. From before." A pause. "He tried to say a word for me this morning. A word in the old language. He said it to the reeds. And nothing came out. He just stood there with his mouth open."
The back room was very quiet.
Mrs. Marta thought about a room at the top of her house, one winter six years ago. A young man with a fever, lying in the narrow iron bed under the slope of the roof, sweating and shaking, with his eyes half open and not seeing her. And talking. All night. In a language she did not know. Soft. Round. With the shape of hills in it. She had sat by him with a cold cloth and listened to it until morning, and she had not understood a single word, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard.
She had never told him that.
She would not be able to tell him now. There was nothing left in him to tell it to.
"The fool," she said. Her voice did not sound like her own. "The great soft stupid fool."
Juno looked at her.
"He did it for a boy," she said. "Gran. So the boy wouldn't have to cut him."
Mrs. Marta said nothing. She got up and went back to her dough, and put her hands in it, and found that they were shaking too badly to knead, and stood there with them in the dough anyway, with her back to the room.
Behind her, Juno told the rest. The man in the gray cape on the Lantern Bridge in the morning, that she had walked straight past. Wat, who was going north. Oren, who had gone north already. Mrs. Marta listened with her back turned and her hands in the cold dough.
And then Juno stood up and said: "I have to go back."
Mrs. Marta turned round.
"No."
"I have to, Gran. I said I would. I said I'd be back before the tide turned." Juno was already pulling the huge coat straight. "They've got no food. They've got no coat for him. His coat's torn right down the back like a dog's been at it. And Wat's—" She stopped. "Wat's got nobody. Not till the barge."
"You've done enough."
"I haven't done anything. I rowed a boat."
Mrs. Marta looked at her.
She thought of all the things she could say. That Juno was fourteen. That the river was ice and the marsh was ten miles of mud and there was a man in a gray cape who had already seen her once. That Juno's mother had gone out of a door one morning when Juno was two, to fetch milk, and had not come back, ever, and that Mrs. Marta had stood at the window that whole day and that whole night, and the next, and had sworn on the grave of her own mother that no child of hers would ever go out of a door again without her knowing where.
She did not say any of it.
She went to the cupboard and took down a sack. And she began to fill it. The new bread, not yet baked, would not do, so she took Moll's bread instead. The rest of the cheese. A string of sausages that Hurley had been saving. Four wrinkled apples. A clay bottle of cold tea, into which she poured, when Moll was not looking, a good deal of the brandy Moll kept on the top shelf for medical purposes. Two of the Watch house's gray blankets. A pair of Moll's wool stockings.
And from the hook by the front office door, where it had hung since the summer, a coat. A constable's coat, long and thick and gray, that a young man had left there in July and never come back for.
Moll watched her take it down. He did not say anything.
She tied the sack. She held it out to Juno.
"Before dark," she said.
"Gran—"
"Before dark, or I come after you myself. On the river wall. In my good shoes." She pushed the sack into Juno's arms. "And tell him rent's due Friday. Tell him I'm not having it late just because the Inquisition knocked my floor in."
Juno looked at her. Something went across her muddy face that was almost a laugh, and almost not.
"I'll tell him," she said.
She went to the back door. And stopped, with her hand on the latch. And turned round. And came back across the room, fast, and put her arms round Mrs. Marta's middle, sack and all, and held on very hard for one breath.
Then she was gone. Down the Ropewalk Stairs. Fast and light, two at a time, never touching the wall.
Mrs. Marta stood in the middle of the back room and listened to the feet go down until she could not hear them anymore.
Then she went back to her dough.
Behind her, very softly, by the range, Terrence said: "Good morning."
He said it to the door. To the place where Juno had gone.
And for one moment, one small terrible moment, Mrs. Marta thought she heard something else in it. Under the politeness. Very far down. Something that had once known a little girl who went out of doors and did not always come back.
