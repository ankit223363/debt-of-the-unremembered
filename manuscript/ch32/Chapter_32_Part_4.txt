Chapter 32: Rent Due Friday, Part 4 of 5: Ninety-One Steps
Ninety-One Steps
She took a candle from the dresser drawer, where she had always kept candles, and lit it from Pym's lantern, and went up.
She went slowly. She had always gone slowly on these stairs, ever since her knees went. But she had never gone up them like this. In the dark, in her own house, with the house empty round her and cold, and the front door below her with a stranger standing outside it, and her heart going in her chest the way it had not gone since she was a girl.
The first sixteen steps. She knew every one. The third creaked. The seventh had a dip in it where sixty years of feet had worn the wood. The eleventh was loose at the left end, and she had been going to have Abner Cole's nephew fix it for ten years, and had never got round to it. She put her foot on the right end of it without thinking.
The first landing. The Dunns' door, shut. No light under it. No noise. The Dunns had gone to the Dunns' sister in Gullwick on Wednesday afternoon, with all five children in a cart and the baby screaming, and the bed linen tied up in a sheet. Mrs. Dunn had stopped on the step with the baby on her hip and looked at the broken window and said, I'm sorry, Marta, I am, but I've got the children to think of, and Mrs. Marta had said, I know you have, Ida, go on, and meant it.
She went on.
The second sixteen. The second landing. The round window, with the street lamp outside it, throwing a pale cold circle of light onto the boards. And Terrence's chair.
It stood where it always stood, under the window. A plain wooden chair with a rush seat, worn smooth and dark by the shape of him. Eight years he had sat in it. Every morning. Every afternoon. Looking out at the street through the dirty glass, with his hands in his lap and his thumb going round and round. Saying good morning to everyone who went up or down. To the Dunns. To the coal boy. To Corin, every single day, who always stopped and stared at him as if he were a ghost, and then said good morning back.
The chair was empty now. There was a little dent in the rush seat. Somebody's boot had kicked it sideways, so that it stood at an angle to the window, looking at the wall.
Mrs. Marta put the candle down on the floor. She took hold of the chair with both hands and turned it back. Straight. Facing the window. The way it went.
Then she picked up the candle and went on.
The third sixteen. The third landing. She stopped there to get her breath, the way she always did. She leaned her shoulder against the wall, the way she always did. And the candle in her hand dipped and wavered, and its yellow light went down the wall, low, to the place near the floor where the old paint had worn through to the plaster.
She had never looked at that place. Not once in thirty-four years. There were scratches there. Names. Children's names, mostly, from long before her time. She knew that the way you know there are cracks in a ceiling. Somewhere in the back of her mind. Not worth looking at.
She looked at it now. She did not know why. Perhaps because the boy had. She had seen him, once or twice, coming up in the dark. Stopping on this landing. Putting out his hand to this place on the wall, low down, and touching it with his fingertips, the way you touch the head of a dog. And going on up.
She bent down, slowly, with the candle.
Names. DELLA. TOMMY B. KESTER WAS HERE. Cut into the plaster with a nail or a knife, old and gray and furred with dust. And under them, smaller, older, worn smooth by something. By a hand going past, night after night.
WE REMEMBER.
She did not know what it meant. She had never seen it before in her life. She looked at it for a long moment, with her knees aching and the candle dripping wax on her glove.
And then she saw the bird.
It was under the words. Just under. Very small. A round body, a round head, a little point for a beak. The kind of bird a child draws. Hanging from a single thin straight line that went up from its back to nowhere, like a bird on a string.
And it was new.
She knew it was new the way she knew when a step had been scrubbed. The old scratches were gray. Soft. Full of dust and years. This one was white. The cuts were sharp and clean, and the plaster in them was the pale bright color of new chalk, and there was a little fine white dust still lying on the boards below it, in a tiny heap, where it had fallen.
Somebody had been in her house.
Not the Inquisition. The Inquisition did not cut little birds on walls. Somebody else. Somebody had come up her stairs, in the last day or the last night, past her empty kitchen and her broken floor and the Inquisitor's man on the front step, all the way up to this landing. And knelt, here, where she was kneeling. And taken out a knife. And cut a little bird on a string under two words she had never noticed. And gone away again.
Mrs. Marta knelt very still on the cold boards of her own third-floor landing, with the candle in her hand.
She did not understand it. She did not try to. There were too many things this week she did not understand, and she had stopped trying with most of them. But she did not like it. She did not like it at all.
She got up. Slowly. Holding the wall.
And went on up.
The fourth sixteen. The fifth. And then the last flight, the eleven narrow steps that were more like a ladder than a stair, going up into the roof.
Ninety-one.
The door at the top stood open. The new brass lock hung off it, the wood round it split where someone had put a shoulder to it on Wednesday morning. She pushed the door wider and went in.
It was empty.
She had known it would be. Pym had told her. They took everything out of the top room. Every little thing. But she had not understood it until she stood in the doorway with the candle and looked.
They had taken the mattress off the bed. The bed was still there, the narrow iron frame, but it was only a frame now, with its bare criss-cross of springs showing, like the bones of something. They had taken the chair and the table. They had taken the washstand and the cracked blue jug. They had taken the kettle off the little black stove, and the stove itself was cold and open, and someone had raked the ashes out onto the floor and gone through them. They had taken the rug. They had taken the jars. All of them. Forty-one, Pym had said. The long shelf on the wall was bare, and in the candlelight she could see the round clean marks in the dust where every one had stood, in rows, close together, like the marks of fingertips.
They had even taken the curtain off the little window.
There was nothing left. Nothing at all. A bare room under a sloping roof, with the rain on the slates, and the cold coming in. As if nobody had ever lived in it. As if nobody had sat on that bed every morning for six years, with a black book on his knees, reading the first page out loud in a low voice, in the gray light, so that he would know who he was.
She had heard him. Every morning. Through the floor. She had never told him that either.
She went to the bed.
She knelt down beside it, on the bare boards, with the candle. And she held the candle up to the wall by the pillow end, where a man lying on his side would have his face.
There they were.
Three lines. Cut into the plaster with a horseshoe nail from the box under her own sink. Small, hard letters, sloping steeply to the right, pressed so deep that the plaster had cracked round each one. She had not read them on Wednesday morning, when she brought up his tea. She had looked at the window instead, because some things in her house were not her business.
She read them now.
CORIN VANE.
OAKHAVEN.
ELARA.
She read them twice.
She knew the first one. She had written it on the slip of paper she pinned to his door every month for six years, under the amount. She did not know the second one. She had heard him say it, sometimes, in his sleep, in the fever winter, in the middle of the soft round words she did not understand. She did not know the third one at all.
But she knew, kneeling there, who had stood in this room yesterday afternoon and come down the street afterward crying, with her hand on her chest.
And she knew one more thing. Because there was a smell in the room. Very faint, under the cold and the wet and the old ashes. Not the boy's smell, which was ink and tea and soap and the river. Something else. Something that did not belong in a room under a roof on Tallow Street at all. Sweet. Thick. Heavy as a hand on your face.
Lilies.
Mrs. Marta knelt beside the empty bed and smelled it, and went cold all over.
She had smelled lilies once before this week. On Monday. On the boy's coat, when he came home late from Lantern Quay with his face as white as paper and would not say where he had been. And she had smelled them on a note. A note pinned to his door one morning with a pin that had a white bead on the end of it, that said STOP LOOKING in a hand that was very nearly his and was not.
She put out her hand and touched the three words on the wall. Gently. With her bare fingers, the glove pulled off. The way the boy touched the words on the stairs.
"You daft great thing," she whispered. "You daft, soft, great thing."
Then she got up, and put on her glove, and picked up the candle, and went back down the ninety-one steps to her kitchen, where Pym was waiting with the lantern and the tin and his ears, and she did not stop on the third landing, and she did not look at the bird.
The Left Side Runs Hot
They got back to Rope Street at seven, the way they had come. Through the cellars. Through Abner Cole's coal, which was worse going back because her knees had stiffened. Out by the yard door, and along the dark lane behind the houses, and round by the ropewalk, keeping to the shadows, with Pym walking a little ahead and the lantern shuttered down to a slit.
Nobody stopped them.
The chestnut man was gone from under the bare tree. His brazier was gone. There was only the little brown heap of shells on the cobbles where he had stood, and a dog nosing at it.
Mrs. Marta did not know if that was better or worse.
The back room of the Watch house was warm and full of light and smelled of her bread. Moll had lit both lamps. He had built up the range until it roared, and set the big kettle on, and put Terrence's chair right up close to the warm, and wrapped a blanket round Terrence's knees. He had laid the table. Four tin plates. Four tin mugs. Knives and forks, of a sort. The heel of her loaf on a board, and a pot of dripping, and an onion, and a lump of cheese the size of a fist that she did not know where he had found.
He was standing by the range when they came in, with the poker in his hand, as if he had been standing there the whole two hours.
He looked at her. At her church shoes, gray with coal dust. At her good black skirt, torn at both knees. At her face. At the red and gold tin in her arms.
He did not say anything. He put the poker down and pulled out her chair.
She sat. Her knees made a sound like a door. She put the tin on the table in front of her and folded her hands on top of it.
"Well," said Moll, after a moment.
"Well," said Mrs. Marta.
"You found it, then."
"I found it."
He poured her tea. He put the mug in her hands, and closed her fingers round it with his own big ones, because her fingers were too cold and stiff to hold it on their own. Then he sat down across from her.
Pym took off his helmet and his cape and sat at the end of the bench, very quietly, and looked at the cheese with longing. Moll pushed the board toward him without looking. Pym took the smallest possible piece and ate it as slowly as a man can eat a piece of cheese.
They had supper.
It was the strangest supper Mrs. Marta had ever eaten. An old landlady and a lame sergeant and a boy constable with ears like handles and a man who did not know his own name, at a scarred table in the back room of a Watch house, with a dead geranium in the next room and a fugitive's rent money in a toffee tin, and the Inquisition outside somewhere in the dark, watching. She thought she would not be able to eat. She ate. She ate bread and dripping and onion and cheese, and drank three mugs of tea, and was ashamed of how hungry she was, and ate some more.
She fed Terrence first. She always fed Terrence first. She cut his bread into small squares and put dripping on each one and fed them to him one at a time, off the fork, the way you feed a child. He took each one politely. He chewed slowly, looking at the fire. When she gave him the tea he held the mug in both hands and blew on it, the way he had always blown on his tea, even before, even when he was Terrence, and she had to look away for a moment.
Afterward, when the plates were cleared and Pym had gone out to the front office to sit at Moll's desk and be the Watch for an hour, and the lamps were turned down low, Mrs. Marta opened the tin.
She tipped it out on the table.
Coins. Silver and copper, in a heap, sliding and chinking over the scarred wood. Shillings. Sixpences. Pennies, brown and worn. A few ha'pennies. One very small silver threepence that she remembered: he had paid her with it in the first summer, by mistake, thinking it was a sixpence, and she had kept it because she thought he might want it back, and he never had.
She began to count.
Moll watched her. He did not offer to help. He had known her twenty years and he knew better.
She counted the shillings into piles of four, and the sixpences into piles of eight, and the pennies into piles of twelve, the way she counted the rent. Slowly. Moving her lips. Her square red fingers going over the coins, pushing them into their piles, lining the piles up in a row along the edge of the table like soldiers.
Nineteen shillings and fivepence ha'penny.
And the threepence.
She sat back and looked at it.
"Six years," she said. "That's what six years comes to."
"It's a lot of money," said Moll.
"It's not a lot of money. It's nothing. It's a month's coal and a coat." She pushed the threepence a little apart from the rest with one finger. "It's all he's got in the world, now. Did you know that? They took everything out of that room. Everything. His jars, his blanket, his kettle. His razor. They raked out his stove." Her voice did not shake. She did not let it. "He's got the clothes he's standing up in and that book. And this."
Moll did not answer.
His fingers had begun, very softly, to tap on the table beside the piles of coins. Four. Two.
Terrence was watching them.
Mrs. Marta noticed it slowly. The way you notice that a clock has stopped. Terrence was in his chair by the range, wrapped in the blanket, and he had turned his head. Not toward the fire. Toward the table. Toward Moll's hand, tapping. His pale blue eyes were fixed on it. On the big thick fingers going up and down on the wood.
And his own thumb had stopped going round.
It was the first time she had seen it stop in eight years. Except in his sleep. His thumb lay still on the pad of his finger. Not rubbing. Holding. As if there were something between them that he had suddenly remembered was there.
Moll's fingers went on. Four, quick. Two, slow.
And Terrence's lips moved.
"Three," said Terrence.
The back room was absolutely still.
Moll's hand stopped. He turned his head and stared at the old man in the chair by the range.
Terrence was looking at Moll's hand. Only at the hand. His face had not changed. It was the same face it always was. Calm, and clean, and pleasant, and empty, the face of a man looking out of a window at a street where nothing is happening. But his lips had moved. And his thumb was holding something that was not there.
"Three," said Terrence again. Politely. Quietly. As if he were answering a question nobody had asked. "Three. At the fourth bell."
Mrs. Marta did not breathe.
She knew what Three was. Everybody in Tallow Street knew. Night Door Three. The little iron door in the long blank wall of Archive Lane, at the back of the Ministry, where Terrence had sat every night for thirty years, with the key on a string round his neck and a lamp and a flask of tea. Night Door Two had been old Wilkin, next to him. Night Door Four, a man called Barnaby who had died. They had knocked to each other through the wall, Wilkin always said. Twice at midnight. Once at the fourth bell. To say I'm here. All's well.
And one night, eight years ago, at the fourth bell, Wilkin had knocked, and nobody knocked back. And in the morning Terrence came home with his key gone and his face like a clean glass, and Moll was the one who knocked on her door in the rain, and said, Mrs. Marta, I'm very sorry, he's here, but he's not right.
"Terrence," she whispered.
Terrence did not look at her. He was looking at Moll's hand. Waiting. The way you wait for someone to knock back.
Moll looked at his own hand on the table. At his thick fingers, lying still on the wood beside the piles of coins.
Then, very slowly, as if he were afraid of breaking something, he lifted one finger. And tapped. Once.
Once. At the fourth bell. All's well.
Terrence watched the finger come down.
And his thumb began to move again. Round and round. Rubbing. Slowly. And he turned his head away, back toward the fire, and his face was the same as it had been, calm and clean and pleasant and empty, and he did not say anything else at all.
Mrs. Marta sat at the table with her hands flat on either side of the boy's rent money and did not move for a long time.
Moll did not move either. He sat looking at his own finger as if it belonged to somebody else.
"How did you know?" said Mrs. Marta at last. Her voice came out cracked. "Once. At the fourth bell. How did you know to do that?"
Moll did not answer at once.
"Wilkin told me," he said at last. "Years ago. When I brought Terrence home. He told me what they used to knock. Twice at midnight. Once at the fourth bell." He took his hand off the table and put it in his lap, under the edge, where she could not see it. "I'd forgotten I knew."
It was the second lie he had told her that day. She knew it the way she knew every lie there was.
She did not say anything. She got up, and swept the coins back into the toffee tin, all but the threepence, which she put into her own pocket. And she put the lid on the tin, and pressed it down hard with both thumbs. And she went and smoothed Terrence's hair, which did not need smoothing, and tucked the blanket tighter round his knees, which did not need tucking either.
And behind her, at the table, she heard Moll's fingers begin again, very softly, under the edge of the wood, where he thought she could not hear.
Four. Two.
And then, after a long pause, a third run. One she had not heard before.
Nine.
