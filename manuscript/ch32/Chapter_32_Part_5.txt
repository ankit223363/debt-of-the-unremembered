Chapter 32: Rent Due Friday, Part 5 of 5: A Cloth on a Desk
A Cloth on a Desk
At nine o'clock that night, on the fourth floor of the Ministry of Records, in a small warm room at the end of a long cold corridor, Mister Vell laid a folded square of cloth on the Inquisitor's desk.
He did not say anything. He never said anything when he laid things on the Inquisitor's desk. He put the little hard square down exactly in the middle of the green leather, between the inkstand and the lamp, and stepped back two paces, and folded his long pale hands in front of him, and waited.
The room was very tidy. It always was. A desk, a chair, a second chair for visitors that nobody ever sat in. A fire in a small iron grate, burning low and neat, with the coals arranged in a little pyramid. A window with the curtains drawn. Shelves of black books, all the same size, all the same black, with nothing written on their spines. On the corner of the desk, a paper bag, folded over at the top. Barley sugar.
Silas Kross looked at the square of cloth for a while without touching it.
He was in his shirtsleeves. His gray coat hung on a hook behind the door. He had his spectacles on, the little round ones he wore only for reading, and he took them off now, slowly, and folded them, and put them in his breast pocket. He looked tired, Mister Vell thought. Mild and kind and a little sad, the way he always looked. But tired too. Tired the way a man is tired when things are going well, and he knows they will not go on going well forever, and he is already counting what it will cost.
Then he reached out and picked up the cloth.
He unfolded it. Slowly. One fold at a time. He did it the way Mister Vell had folded it, in reverse, exactly, as if he could see each fold being made and was taking each one back. When it was open, he smoothed it flat on the green leather with the side of his hand. Blue and white checks. A small darn near one edge. And in the corner, in red thread, in tiny even stitches, the letter M.
Kross bent his head and smelled it.
"Mutton," he said. "Onion. Lard pastry." He straightened. "And thyme. She puts thyme in it. I noticed that on Wednesday, in her kitchen. There was a bunch hanging over the range, drying." He touched the darn with one fingertip. "A careful woman. A woman who mends."
"Yes, sir."
"Where?"
"A wreck on the Saltmarsh, sir. The Margery. Ten miles down. On a nail on the outer rib, drying. A fire, put out with water and smothered. Fish bones. The prints of three people on the deck. A man, a girl, and a very big man. The girl's the one from the bridge this morning, I think. The big one's a bargeman by his boots." Mister Vell paused. "Hagan had a pole in the water under the deck. He caught something. He thought it was a timber."
Kross looked up.
"Was it?"
"There was blood on the hook when he brought it up, sir. Not much. He didn't see it. I did."
Kross was quiet for a moment.
"And you didn't say."
"No, sir. You said you wanted to see who he ran to. I thought if I pulled him out from under the deck of a dead ship, you wouldn't see anybody."
Kross smiled. It was a small smile, and a real one, and it went all the way up to his eyes.
"Thank you, Mister Vell," he said. "That was exactly right."
Mister Vell inclined his head.
"And then?"
"And then I took the cloth to Rope Street, sir. To the Watch house. At four." Mister Vell's plain face did not change. "The old woman's there. In the back room. I could smell her bread through the door. Fresh. The sergeant said it was the night men's loaf. The sergeant lied very well, sir. Better than most. He stood like a soldier while he did it."
"Moll," said Kross softly.
"Sergeant Ebenezer Moll, sir. Rope Street Watch. Twenty years at the desk. Before that, nothing. No papers. Came to the city from the north with a bad leg and a stick and a paper from the Commonwealth Foot." Mister Vell paused. "The paper's a good one, sir. Very good. Too good, I'd say. I'd like to look at it closer."
Kross looked at him for a long moment.
Then he looked down at the cloth again. He began to fold it. Not the way it had been folded before. Differently. In half, and in half again, and then corner to corner, slowly, carefully, until it was a small neat triangle of blue and white, like a napkin at a good dinner. He laid it on the corner of the desk, beside the bag of barley sugar.
"Leave the old woman," he said.
"Sir?"
"Leave her. She's a landlady who made a pie. If we take her, the whole of the Lower City knows by noon, and every door on the boy's list shuts in our faces, and he hears of it from the first fishwife he passes." He picked up a pencil. "And she's no use to us. She'll never lead us anywhere. She'll sit in that Watch house in the sergeant's best chair until she dies, and dare us to come and get her."
Mister Vell said nothing.
"The sergeant, though." Kross turned the pencil in his fingers. "The sergeant came from the north with a bad leg and a paper that's too good. The sergeant spilled gin on a lady in white fur on Sunday night so that eleven people could walk out of Gant's cellar. The sergeant has a Watch house full of young constables who like him, and a desk at the front window that everybody on Rope Street walks past twice a day." He tapped the pencil on the green leather. Once. Twice. "Somebody will go to the sergeant. Sooner or later. Somebody the boy trusts. And when they do, I'd like to know who."
"Yes, sir. And the boy himself?"
Kross looked at the drawn curtains. At the window behind them, and the dark, and the river beyond it.
"North," he said. "He'll go north. To the girl. To the Thorne girl and her father, wherever Hessa Doon has put them. He can't help it. They're his." He smiled again, more faintly. "Put a man on every lock between here and Saltgate. Not to stop him. Just to watch. And find me Hessa Doon's barge."
"Yes, sir."
Kross reached into the inside pocket of his waistcoat and took out a little book. Small. Black. Bound in soft leather, with a silver pencil tucked into its spine. He opened it on the desk. Mister Vell did not look at the page. He never looked at the page. He looked at the fire.
Kross wrote one line. Slowly. In his small neat hand.
Then he blew on it to dry the ink, and closed the book, and put it away.
"That will be all, Mister Vell," he said. "Go home. Get some sleep. You've done very well."
Mister Vell bowed, very slightly, and went to the door.
He had his hand on the handle when the Inquisitor said, without looking up, in the same mild voice:
"Mister Vell."
"Sir?"
"The old woman's bread. Was it good?"
Mister Vell thought about it.
"It smelled very good, sir," he said.
"Yes," said Silas Kross. He opened the paper bag on the corner of the desk and took out a barley sugar, and unwrapped it, and put it in his mouth. And began, slowly, with his long careful fingers, to fold the wrapper. "I thought it would."
Mister Vell went out. He shut the door behind him without a sound, the way he did everything.
The long corridor outside was cold and dark, lit only by one lamp at the far end, near the stair. His footsteps made no noise on the old boards. He walked down it slowly, with his hands folded in front of him under the gray cape, past door after door, all shut, all dark, with small brass numbers on them that nobody ever read.
At the top of the stair he stopped.
He did not know why. He stood with one hand on the cold iron rail and looked down the well of the stair, four floors, into the dark. And he found that he was thinking about bread. About a smell coming through the crack of a thin door in a Watch house on Rope Street. Warm and brown and plain. The smell of a kitchen with somebody in it.
He had not smelled that smell in a long time. He lived alone, in two rooms over a chandler's on Wick Lane, and he ate his supper at a cookshop on the corner, standing up, and he had done so for twenty-two years. He did not mind. He had never minded. It was a good, quiet, tidy life.
He stood at the top of the stair a moment longer.
Then he took the little black book out of his cape, and opened it, and wrote one line, in pencil, under the day's other lines:
Rope Street. The bread was good.
And he looked at it, and did not know why he had written it, and tore the page out, and folded it, small, smaller, into a hard tight square. And put it in the fire grate on the landing as he went past, where it caught at once, and was gone.
Then he went down the stairs, and out into the dark, and home.
Moll Will Not Run
Juno came up the Ropewalk Stairs at ten.
Mrs. Marta heard her from a long way off, because she was listening for her. She had been listening since dark. Sitting at the table with the toffee tin in front of her and the threepence in her pocket and her ears turned toward the back door, the way you turn a lamp toward a window. Every cart on Rope Street had been Juno. Every footstep. Every cat on the yard wall.
This time it was.
Fast. Light. Two at a time. Never touching the wall. But not the way they had sounded at noon. Slower at the top. Heavy. As if whoever was climbing them had climbed a great many things that day and had very little left.
The back door opened.
Juno stood in it. She did not bang it this time. She stood on the step with the dark behind her and the yellow lamplight on her face, in her grandfather's huge coat, and she was not crying. Mrs. Marta saw that at once. She was past crying. She had the look of somebody who has cried a great deal earlier in the day, all of it, every drop, and has nothing left to do it with.
"Gran," she said. "Gran, I did something."
"Come in," said Mrs. Marta. "Shut the door. You're letting the cold in."
Juno came in. She shut the door. She stood with her back against it and her hands behind her, holding the latch.
"I left the cloth," she said. "Your cloth. The one from the pie. The blue one with your M on it. I hung it on a nail on the wreck last night, to dry. And I forgot it. I forgot it, Gran. And this morning, after I came here, a boat came. A black boat. With the Inquisition's eye on its flag. And they searched the ship. And Wat—" She stopped. "Wat got hurt. Hiding Corin. A hook. In his shoulder. He didn't make a sound." She swallowed. "And the man in the gray cape took the cloth."
Nobody said anything.
"I know," said Mrs. Marta.
Juno stared at her.
"He brought it here," said Mrs. Marta. "At four o'clock. He put it on Moll's desk and unfolded it and asked if anybody had lost it. And then he folded it up again and took it away."
The color went out of Juno's face all at once, the way water goes out of a basin when you pull the plug. She looked at Moll. Moll was sitting on the bench by the range with his bad leg out on the stool and his hands in his lap. He did not look away.
"He came here?"
"He came here."
"Then he knows. He knows where you are. He knows Gran's here. He knows—" Juno's voice went up and up and cracked at the top. "Corin said. Corin said to tell you. You have to go. Tonight. Both of you. Now. Anywhere. Somewhere Kross has never heard of. He made me promise to make you." Her hands came out from behind her back. They were fists. "He said make her."
Mrs. Marta looked at her granddaughter for a long moment.
Then she looked at the toffee tin on the table. And at Terrence, asleep now in the chair by the range, with his head on one side and his mouth a little open and his thumb still going round, slowly, even in his sleep. And at Pym, who had come in from the front office at the sound of the door and was standing in the doorway with his helmet in his hands and his ears gone red.
And at Moll.
"Well, Sergeant?" she said.
Moll did not answer for a long time.
He sat on his bench with his big hands in his lap and looked at the fire through the open firebox door. The light moved on his face. It was an old face, Mrs. Marta thought. Older than she had ever noticed. Heavy and red and tired, with deep lines going down from the corners of the mouth, and the eyes sunk in pouches of soft skin. A face that had sat behind a high wooden desk for twenty years and listened to drunks and thieves and lost children and furious wives, and written it all down in a book, and gone home.
"No," said Moll.
Juno made a sound.
"No," said Moll again. Not loudly. "I'm not going."
"You have to—"
"I don't have to do anything, girl." He turned his head and looked at her. And his eyes were not tired at all. They were small and hard and very clear. "Listen to me. Listen. You think he doesn't know where I live? You think he hasn't got my name on a list somewhere, and my father's name, and what I had for breakfast? He knows. He's known since Sunday. Since the Bell. Since the gin." He moved his shoulders. "If he wanted me, he'd have had me. He doesn't want me. Not yet. He wants to see who comes to me."
"Then go where he can't see!"
"And then what?" Moll leaned forward. "Then the Rope Street Watch is empty. No sergeant at the desk. No lamp in the window. And every man in the Inquisition knows why. And the first thing they do is take Hurley, and Pym, and old Dorrit on the late shift, and every constable who ever stood at that desk with me, and ask them where I've gone. And they don't know. And it doesn't matter that they don't know." He looked at Pym, in the doorway. Pym looked at the floor. "I'm not leaving them for that."
The back room was silent.
"If I stay," said Moll, "I'm a sergeant of the Watch, at his own desk, in his own Watch house, doing his job. Writing down stolen geese. And if Kross wants to take a sergeant of the Watch out from behind his own desk, he's going to have to do it in the daylight, with the whole of Rope Street watching. And he's going to have to say why." He sat back. "Kross doesn't like saying why. I don't think he's ever said why in his life."
Juno stared at him.
Her face worked. Her mouth opened and shut. And then, very suddenly, she turned to her grandmother.
"Gran. You go, then. You and Grandad. Tonight. To the Dunns' sister in Gullwick. Or to Mother Grue's. Or anywhere. Moll can stay if he wants. But you—"
"No," said Mrs. Marta.
"Gran—"
"I've lived in this city seventy-one years," said Mrs. Marta. "I've never run from anybody in it. Not from the Watch. Not from the bailiffs. Not from the Bank. Not from a man with a knife who thought your grandfather owed him money, which he didn't." She folded her hands on the toffee tin. "I'm not going to start for a man who folds tea cloths."
"He's not just a man who folds tea cloths!"
"No," said Mrs. Marta. "I know he isn't."
She said it very quietly. And Juno heard something in it, and stopped.
Mrs. Marta looked at her granddaughter. Fourteen. In a coat four times too big for her. With mud on her face and blisters on her hands and a cut on her cheek, standing with her back against a door in a Watch house at ten o'clock at night, having rowed twenty miles of river in one day for a man who did not always remember her name.
"Come here," she said.
Juno did not move.
"Come here, I said."
Juno came. Slowly. And Mrs. Marta took her two cold blistered hands in her own two warm swollen ones, and held them, on top of the toffee tin, on the table.
"Listen to me," she said. "Because I'm only going to say it once, and then I'm going to bed, and you're going to bed, and in the morning I'm going to say the rent's due tomorrow and you're going to roll your eyes at me like you always do. Are you listening?"
Juno nodded. She could not speak.
"That boy has got nobody," said Mrs. Marta. "Nobody in the world. He hasn't got a mother. He hasn't got a father. He's got a sister he can't remember, if she's even his sister. He hasn't got his own words anymore. He hasn't got a room, or a bed, or a kettle, or a coat that isn't torn. He's got that book, and that silver stick, and whatever friends he can keep." She held Juno's hands harder. "And every one of those friends is a door that man is watching. You know that. You told me. He told you."
"Yes."
"So if every one of his friends runs away and hides," said Mrs. Marta, "then he's got nobody at all. Then there's no door left anywhere in this city that he can knock on. Not one. And then the man who folds the tea cloths has won without even trying." She let go of one hand and pushed the toffee tin across the table, into the middle, between them. "I'm not hiding. I'm staying right here, in Moll's best chair, with the boy's money in a tin. And when he needs it, he'll know where it is. And so will you."
Juno looked at the tin.
She looked at it for a long time. And then she put her face down on her grandmother's hands, on the table, and her shoulders shook, but she did not make a sound.
Mrs. Marta left her there.
Across the room, Moll had turned his face back to the fire. Pym had gone back into the front office, very quietly, and shut the door, and Mrs. Marta could hear him sitting down at Moll's high desk, and the scratch of the pen, as if he had decided to write down the stolen goose himself.
And Terrence slept, with his thumb going round.
Later, much later, when Juno was asleep at last on the mattress by the far wall, wrapped in two gray blankets with her boots still on, and Moll was asleep sitting up on his bench, and the lamps were out and only the red glow of the firebox lit the room, Mrs. Marta got up.
She went into the front office, quietly, past Pym, who had fallen asleep with his head on Moll's desk and the pen still in his hand. She went to the notice board on the wall. To the little slate that hung there on a nail by a loop of string, where the constables wrote things for each other. DORRIT: SAW THE GOOSE. MOLL'S HAT IS ON THE PEG, NOT THE HOOK.
She wiped it with her sleeve.
And she took the stub of chalk from the shelf below it, and in her square, heavy, ruler-taught hand, she wrote:
RENT DUE FRIDAY.
She looked at it for a moment in the faint red light from the back room.
Then she went back to Moll's best chair, and sat down in it, and pulled her mother's gray shawl round her shoulders. And she did not sleep, because she had not slept in a bed that was not her own for thirty-one years, and she was not going to start now. But she closed her eyes.
And under the closed lids she saw a small round bird, cut new and white into old plaster, hanging from a string.
And across the room, in the red dark, very softly, on the bench beside the warm black range, a sleeping man's fingers moved on his knee.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
