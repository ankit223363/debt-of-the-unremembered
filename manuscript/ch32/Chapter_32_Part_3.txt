Chapter 32: Rent Due Friday, Part 3 of 5: The Chestnut Man and the Gray Cape
The Chestnut Man and the Gray Cape
She saw the chestnut man at two o'clock.
She would not have seen him at all if she had not gone into the front office to fetch Moll's ink. Moll had run out. He had a report to write about a stolen goose on Saltings Lane, and he could not find the bottle, and he was limping round the back room looking under things and swearing in a low steady voice that he thought she could not hear. So she went to look. She knew where men kept things. They kept them in the place they had last put them, and then forgot they had put them there.
The front office was a narrow room with a high wooden desk across it, like a counter in a shop, and a bench along the wall for people who were waiting, and a board with notices pinned on it. LOST. A GRAY MARE, LAME IN THE NEAR FORE. FOUND. A CHILD'S BOOT. BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY: ANY PERSON HARBORING THE FUGITIVE HARVESTER C. VANE. She did not read the rest of that one. She had already read it, on Wednesday night, three times.
There was a window in the front wall. Wide and low, with small square panes of old greenish glass, and the words ROPE STREET WATCH painted on it backwards in flaking gold, so that from inside you read them like a mirror. The glass was so old it had gone wavy. When you looked through it, the street outside seemed to ripple, as if you were seeing it through water.
She found the ink on the windowsill, behind the potted geranium that had died some years ago and that nobody had thrown away.
And as she reached for it, she looked out.
Rope Street was busy. It was always busy in the afternoons. Carts going down to the river, loaded with barrels. Carts coming up, loaded with coal. Women with baskets. Men with sacks. Children everywhere, running between the wheels. A dog. A boy with a tray of hot pies on his head, shouting. A man selling bootlaces from a pole. And on the corner opposite, by the pump at the end of the ropewalk, under the bare black tree that grew out of the cobbles there, a man selling roast chestnuts.
He had a little iron brazier on three legs, glowing red, with a pan of chestnuts on top. He had a brown coat, long and shabby, and a brown cap pulled low. He had a paper bag in one hand, and he was eating out of it. Shelling the chestnuts with his thumb and dropping the shells on the cobbles round his boots, where there was already a little brown heap of them.
He was not selling any.
Mrs. Marta stood very still at the window, with the ink bottle in her hand.
She watched. She watched for a long time, through the wavy green glass, with the backwards gold letters across her view. Three people stopped at the brazier while she watched. A woman with a baby. A carter. A small boy with a copper in his fist. The man in the brown coat sold to each of them. But he did it without looking at them. He took the money and gave the chestnuts and his eyes did not move from where they were.
Which was the front door of the Rope Street Watch.
A man in a brown coat, Juno had said, at noon, with her mouth full of bread. Across from Vespera's on Hook Lane. All morning. Eating chestnuts. Not selling any. Just eating.
Mrs. Marta put the ink bottle down on the sill, very gently, behind the dead geranium.
She went back into the back room. Moll was on his hands and knees by the cupboard with the broken door, looking under it for the ink.
"It's on the windowsill," she said. "Behind your dead flower."
He looked up at her. And saw her face. And got up off his knees, slowly, holding the edge of the table, and did not ask her anything at all. He went past her into the front office. She heard him stop by the window. She heard the boards creak as he stood there. A long time.
When he came back, his boiled-ham face had gone the color of wet paper.
"Brown coat," he said. "Under the tree."
"Yes."
"I've seen him before." Moll sat down heavily on the bench. "Not him. His kind. Years ago. When the Inquisition took the night porters off Archive Lane. There was a man selling hot potatoes on the corner by the Ministry gate for a week before. Nobody ever bought one." He rubbed his face. "Then one night the porters were gone, and the potato man was gone, and nobody ever saw either again."
Nobody said anything. Terrence's thumb went round and round on his finger.
Moll's fingers began to tap on his knee. Four. Two. Four.
The man in the gray cape came at four.
He came in at the front, through the street door, the way anybody would come into a Watch house. Mrs. Marta heard the door. She heard the little bell on its spring above the door give its tinny ring. She heard Moll get up from his stool behind the high desk, where he had gone back to his report about the goose, with a creak and a grunt. And she heard a voice.
She did not see the man. She was in the back room, at the table, with the bread. The door between the back room and the front office was shut, but it was an old door, and thin, and it had shrunk in its frame, so that there was a gap at the bottom you could put your fingers through. And a gap at the side, by the hinges, through which, if you stood in the right place, you could see a narrow strip of the front office. A slice of the high desk. A bit of Moll's back. A bit of the notice board.
She stood in the right place.
She saw a gray sleeve. The edge of a gray cape, long, beaded with wet. A hand on the high wooden desk. A long, pale, clean hand, with very short nails and no ring.
"Good afternoon, Sergeant," said the voice.
It was a pleasant voice. Quiet. Not deep, not high. The kind of voice you hear a hundred times a day in the city and never once listen to. A clerk's voice. A shopman's. The voice of a man who collects rents for a landlord and is sorry about it.
"Afternoon," said Moll. "What can I do for you?"
"Oh, nothing important. Nothing at all, really. I'm sorry to trouble you." A small apologetic laugh. "I found something on the river this morning. Lost property, I suppose you'd call it. And I thought, well, the Watch is the place for lost property. And this is the nearest Watch house to the river, isn't it? Rope Street?"
"It is."
"So I thought I'd bring it here."
The pale hand went into the gray cape and came out again. And laid something on the desk. Mrs. Marta could not see what it was. The hand was in the way. Then the hand moved, and unfolded it. Slowly. Fold by fold. A small hard square, opening out, getting larger, flattening, until it lay on the dark wood of the desk.
Blue and white checks.
She knew it before she saw the corner. She knew the color. She knew the weave. She knew the little darn near one edge where Juno had caught it on a nail two summers ago, and she had mended it by lamplight, grumbling.
And then the hand smoothed it flat, and she saw the corner. And the small red letter sewn there, in her own tiny even stitches.
M.
She put her hand over her mouth.
She stood behind the thin shrunk door with her hand over her mouth and did not breathe. Behind her, at the range, Terrence's thumb went round and round. Pym was still asleep on the bench, snoring softly. Nobody else moved.
"A tea cloth," said the pleasant voice. "I found it on a wreck. Out on the Saltmarsh. Can you imagine? Ten miles down. On a nail." A pause. "It's been round a pie. You can still smell it. Mutton, I think. And onion. A good home pie." Another pause. "Somebody will be missing it, I expect. A good cloth like that. Somebody who marks her linen."
Moll said nothing.
Mrs. Marta could see his back. The broad shoulders under his blue Watch coat, and the braces, and the thick red neck. He was standing very still behind the desk. Very straight. The way she had never once seen him stand in twenty years. Not like a sergeant of the Watch. Like a soldier.
"Doesn't ring a bell, I'm afraid," said Moll.
"No?"
"No." His voice was perfectly steady. "Lots of people mark their linen. Lots of M's in the Lower City. Mary, Margaret, Martha, Maud. Moll, even." A pause. "Could be anybody's."
"Of course," said the pleasant voice. "Of course it could." A little sound, like a man sniffing. Once. Twice. Mrs. Marta saw the edge of the gray cape move, as if its owner had turned his head. Toward the back. Toward the thin shrunk door. Toward the smell of her bread, which was in the oven now, on the right side, rising.
"You've got a baker in," said the voice. "How nice. You don't often smell real bread in a Watch house."
"The night men," said Moll. "They like a loaf."
"They're lucky men." The voice paused. "Your oven runs hot, I expect. They always do, those old ranges. On one side."
The front office was perfectly silent.
Mrs. Marta stood behind the door with her hand over her mouth and her heart going like a hammer. She could hear the clock on the wall. She could hear Pym snoring. She could hear, from very far off, through the front window, the man under the bare tree shelling chestnuts, the small dry crack of each one.
"Never noticed," said Moll.
"No. Well. Men don't, do they." The pale hand came back onto the desk. It folded the cloth. Slowly. Neatly. In half. In half again. Smaller. And smaller. Each fold pressed flat with a thumbnail. Until it was a hard tight square no bigger than the top of a man's thumb. And it went back inside the gray cape.
"I'll hang on to it, then," said the pleasant voice. "In case anyone asks. You never know."
"You never know," said Moll.
"Thank you, Sergeant. I'm sorry to have taken your time. Good afternoon."
The bell on its spring gave its tinny ring.
The street door shut.
For a long time nobody in the Rope Street Watch house moved at all.
Then Mrs. Marta took her hand away from her mouth. And she went to the range, and opened the right-hand oven door, and took out her bread. It was done. It was perfect. Brown and high and hollow when she tapped its bottom with her knuckle. Not caught at all.
She put it on the table.
Moll came in from the front office. He stood in the doorway. His face was white. His hands were at his sides, and they were shaking, both of them, and he did not seem to notice.
"He knows," said Mrs. Marta.
Moll said nothing.
"He knows, Moll. He knew before he came in. He didn't come to find out. He came to let us know he knew."
Moll looked at the bread on the table. At the steam going up from it into the cold air of the back room.
"Yes," he said.
And behind them, by the range, Terrence looked up at the bread too, and smiled at it, the way he always did, as if it reminded him of something.
Going Home
"I'm going home," said Mrs. Marta.
She said it at five o'clock, when the light was going and the lamps were coming on along Rope Street, one by one, and the chestnut man's brazier under the bare tree had become a small red eye in the blue dusk. She said it standing up, with her coat already on and her mother's gray shawl over her head, and her good shoes on her feet, the black ones with the buttons that she wore to church on Saints' days and to the Bank when the rent was due.
Moll looked up from the table.
He had been sitting there since the man in the gray cape left. An hour. More. Not doing anything. Not writing his report about the goose. Just sitting, with his big hands flat on the scarred wood and his eyes on the steam going up from her bread, which had long since stopped steaming. Every now and then his fingers moved. Four. Two. Then they stopped again.
"No," he said.
"I wasn't asking."
"Marta. They're watching the house. Pym said. The Inquisition's got its own man on the door. And now that one—" He jerked his head at the front office, at the street, at the place where the gray cape had stood. "Now he's been here. Now he knows. If you go back to Tallow Street tonight—"
"I'm not going in the front."
Moll stared at her.
"I've lived in that house thirty-four years," said Mrs. Marta. "I know every way in and out of it. I know which floorboards creak and which windows stick and which neighbor keeps a dog. And I know there's a door in my cellar that I never knew was there till Tuesday, because the boy found it. Through the old cellars. All the way to the coal merchant's at sixteen." She pulled on her gloves. "I'm going in that way."
"Through the—" Moll stopped. "Through the cellars? Marta, you're seventy-two years old."
"Seventy-one."
"With your knees."
"My knees are my business."
"What's so important," said Moll slowly, "that you'd crawl through the cellars of Tallow Street in the dark, in your church shoes, with an Inquisitor on your front step?"
Mrs. Marta did not answer at once.
She looked across the room at Terrence. He was in his chair by the range, where he had been all afternoon, with his hands in his lap and his thumb going round. He was looking at the fire through the open door of the firebox. The red light moved on his face. He was smiling at it, a little.
"There's a tin," she said. "In my kitchen. Under the hearthstone. The left-hand corner. It lifts if you know how."
Moll waited.
"It's the boy's rent," said Mrs. Marta. "Some of it. A shilling in four, every month, for six years. I never spent it. I put it under the stone." She pulled her shawl tighter. "I always thought, one day, he'd forget everything. All of it. The whole book. One bad night, one big spell, and he'd wake up in that bed not knowing his own name, and not knowing me, and not knowing where he was. Like—" She stopped, and did not look at Terrence. "And I thought, if that happens, he'll need to start again. Somewhere. With something in his pocket. And nobody's ever going to give it to him but me."
The back room was very quiet.
Moll looked at her for a long time. Then he looked down at his own hands on the table. They had stopped shaking. He turned them over and looked at the palms, as if there were something written on them.
"How much?" he said at last.
"Eighteen shillings and some coppers. I haven't counted it lately."
"For eighteen shillings."
"For the boy," said Mrs. Marta. "Not for the shillings."
Moll let out a long breath through his nose.
Then he got up. Slowly, holding the edge of the table, with his bad leg stiff. He went to the hooks on the wall and took down a gray oilskin cape, and held it out to the bench where Pym was sleeping, and kicked the bench.
Pym sat up with a snort.
"Wake up, lad," said Moll. "You're going for a walk with Mrs. Marta. You're going to carry the lantern and keep your mouth shut and do exactly what she tells you. And if anybody stops you, you're on your way to see your mother on Saltings Lane, and she's poorly, and the old lady's her neighbor."
Pym blinked. "My mother lives in Gullwick, Sergeant."
"Not tonight she doesn't."
The coal merchant at No. 16 Tallow Street was a man called Abner Cole, and he was not pleased to see them.
He was a short square man as black as his own coal, with a black apron and black hands and a black face, and only the whites of his eyes and the pink of his mouth showing in it. He opened the door of his yard at the back, off the little lane behind the houses, a crack, with a lantern held up in one fist and a shovel in the other. And he looked at Mrs. Marta in her church shoes and her gray shawl. And at the large young constable behind her with the lantern and the ears.
"Marta," he said.
"Abner."
"They've had men in my cellar," said Abner Cole. "Wednesday. All day. Gray coats. Poking at the coal with poles. Looking at the walls. Asking me had I ever known there was a door in my back wall, and I said no, which was true, and they didn't believe me, which was their business." He spat. It came out black. "And there's a grating in my floor that's been in my floor for forty years, and now there's a padlock off it that wasn't mine and a new one on it that isn't mine either."
"I'm not going down the grating," said Mrs. Marta. "I'm going home."
Abner Cole looked at her for a long moment. Then at the lane behind her, both ways, very carefully. Then he stood aside.
His cellar was black and hot and stifling, full of the dry bitter smell of coal and the slow red glow of a stove in one corner. The coal was heaped up to the roof along one wall, a great black slope of it, and the light from Pym's lantern made it glitter, as if it were full of tiny stars. At the back, behind the coal, there was the hole. Corin's hole. A ragged black gap in the old brickwork, about the height of a child, where somebody had pushed the bricks through from the other side. The Inquisition had looked at it, Abner said. They had measured it, and drawn it in a book, and gone away again. Nobody had bricked it up.
Mrs. Marta looked at it. Then she got down on her knees on the coal.
It hurt. She had known it would hurt. Her knees had hurt for thirty years, ever since the steps, and the coal was hard and sharp and sliding under her, and she could feel every lump through her good black skirt. She did not care. She went through the hole on her hands and knees, into the dark, with her shawl caught on the broken bricks and the coal dust going up her nose.
Pym came after her, with the lantern. He did not fit very well. She heard him swear, quietly, and then say sorry, missus, and then swear again.
On the other side there was a cellar she had never seen.
Low and long and empty, with an earth floor and brick pillars holding up a vaulted roof, and cobwebs hanging down in gray rags. And beyond it another, the same. And another. The old cellars. Joined, under the houses of Tallow Street, by doorways that had been bricked up a hundred years ago and opened again, very recently, by somebody in a hurry. She went through them one after another with Pym's lantern bobbing behind her and her heart going hard in her chest. She was counting. Fourteen, she thought. Fifteen. Sixteen was Abner's. So the next one, the one after this—
And then she saw her own potatoes.
They were lying on the earth floor in a long tumbled slide, as if somebody had kicked the sacks over in the dark. Her potatoes. Her coal, in its bin. Her sacking. Terrence's barrel of ale, that he had never finished, and that she had never had the heart to pour away.
And above it all, a hole in the roof. Ragged. Splintered. Huge. Where the floor of her scullery had been. The broken ends of her own floorboards hanging down out of it like teeth. And through it, faint, gray, the last of the evening light, coming in through the broken window of her kitchen and falling down through the hole onto her potatoes.
She stood under it and looked up.
Pym held the lantern and did not say anything. He was a good boy, she thought. A good, quiet, foolish boy with ears like handles. Someone had brought him up properly.
"The ladder's there," she said. "Hold it for me."
The ladder was there. Bent, from the weight of the thing that had come down it. But there. Pym held it. And Mrs. Marta climbed up into her own house through the floor, in the dusk, in her church shoes, one rung at a time, and came out on her knees on the cold flagstones of her own scullery, among the broken boards, and knelt there for a moment with her hands flat on the stone.
It was very quiet.
It was her house. It smelled of her house. Onions, and old wood, and soap, and coal. And something else, now, under it. Something cold and wet and new that had not been there before. The smell of a window open to the weather. The smell of a house where nobody has lit the fire.
She got up.
She went into her kitchen.
The purple door hung off its hinge. The three daisies in their jug were dead. One of the chairs was on its side. There was glass on the floor, from the window in the hall, and footprints in the glass, big ones, and her good blue and white plates were still on the dresser, every one, not one broken, which she did not understand at all.
She went to the hearth. She knelt again. She found the corner of the stone, the left-hand one, with her fingers, the way she had found it a hundred times in the dark. She pressed, and lifted.
The tin was there.
An old toffee tin, red and gold, with a picture of a ship on the lid. She took it out and held it in both hands, and it was heavy, and it rattled.
"Got it," said Pym behind her, in a whisper, with enormous relief. "Missus. Now we can—"
"Not yet," said Mrs. Marta.
She stood up, with the tin in her arms. And she looked at the door into the hall. At the foot of the stairs, dark, going up.
Ninety-one steps.
"Wait here," she said.
