Chapter 19: The Rag-Picker, Part 4 of 5: The Arches by the Dye-Works
The Arches by the Dye-Works
Juno did not take them up the lane.
"Watch on the corner," she said, at the bottom of it, by the little humped bridge at the end of the coal wharf. She did not even look. She just said it, the way you say mind the step. "Two of them. By the pump. They've been there since Friday. They've got a brazier." She sniffed. "Chestnuts."
Corin looked up the lane. In the gray morning light it was not black anymore. It was just a lane. Steep and narrow and cobbled, with high soot-dark walls on both sides, and a gutter down the middle full of gray ice, and the two dark lines of the wagon's wheels still in the frost on the stones, side by side, going up. At the top, where it opened out, he could see the corner of a square, and an iron pump, and a thin blue curl of smoke going up from something beside it.
And behind the square, over the roofs, the chimney.
In daylight it was red. Dark red, the color of old meat, streaked with black soot from top to bottom where the rain had run down it for a hundred years. Square. Enormous. So tall that Corin had to tip his head right back to see the top of it, against a sky the color of dirty milk. Near the top, the black iron band. And the little iron rail.
And tied to the rail, hanging limp in the still air, something white.
He could see it now. A flower. A long white flower on a long green stem, tied to the rail with a piece of ribbon, hanging upside down, with its petals drooping.
A lily.
Corin stood and looked at it. He did not need the Ledger. He knew. A white lily tied to the Dye-Works chimney. Not there at the seventh bell. There now. He had written that days ago, at the very start, on a morning he did not remember, with his heart going fast.
He had not known, then, what it meant.
He was not sure he knew now. But he looked at it for a long time, hanging up there in the gray, white and drooping and patient. Like a signature at the bottom of a letter.
"Come on," said Juno.
She took them round.
Along the river road, a little way. Then down. Over the river wall, by a set of slimy iron rungs set into the stone that Corin had never noticed, though he must have walked past them a hundred times. Down onto a narrow ledge of mud and stones at the bottom of the wall, just above the water, where the river had gone out a little with the tide and left a strip of the bank bare. It was covered in frost and broken glass and old rope and the bones of fish. The ice went grinding past, an arm's length away, gray and dirty, with the water black between the blocks.
They went along the ledge in single file. Juno first, quick and light, never slipping, jumping from stone to stone like a goat. Then Corin. Then Oren, slowly, carefully, with one big hand on the wall, swearing under his breath every time his boots slid.
And then the stream.
It came out of a hole in the river wall. A great round hole, as big as a door, with a ring of old brick round it, green with weed and black with slime, and an iron grating across it that had rusted through at the bottom long ago. The stream came out of it in a thin brown trickle, over a long tongue of frozen mud, and ran down into the river, steaming a little in the cold, because it was warm. Warmer than the river. And it smelled.
Corin had never smelled anything like it. Sharp and sour and chemical, like vinegar and rust and something burning. It caught in his throat and made his eyes water. The water was not brown, he saw, when he got closer. It was every color. Streaks of dark blue, and rusty red, and a sick yellow-green, twisting together in the trickle like ribbons, and never quite mixing.
"Dye," said Juno. "From the works. It all comes out here. Been coming out here for a hundred years. That's why it's warm. They've got great vats up there, and they boil it." She ducked under the broken bottom of the grating without stopping. "Mind your heads."
They went into the dark.
It was a tunnel. Low and round and brick, with the stream running down the middle of the floor in a little channel. On either side of the channel, there was a narrow ledge, just wide enough for one foot in front of the other. The bricks were stained every color the water was. Blue and red and yellow and green, in long streaks and blotches up the walls, so that in the gray light coming in from behind them it looked like the inside of a sick rainbow.
It went up. Steeply. They climbed.
After fifty paces, the light changed. Gray light ahead. Coming down from above. And the tunnel opened out.
They came out under the arches.
Corin had not known they were there.
He had walked past the Dye-Works a hundred times. He had seen the chimney, and the long red walls, and the great iron gates on the street, always shut. He had never wondered what was underneath it.
Underneath it was this.
The Dye-Works had been built on a hill. And to make the ground flat, long ago, the builders had raised it up on arches. Great brick arches, one after another, in long rows, like the inside of a church that had been laid on its side. Each one was as tall as two men and as wide as a cart, with a curved brick roof, black with soot and stained with dye, dripping. They went off into the dark in every direction. Row after row after row. Some of them were open to the air at one end, where the hill fell away, and gray daylight came in. Some were dark. Some had been bricked up. Some had wooden fronts built across them, rough planks and old doors and bits of sacking, with stovepipes sticking out of them, and smoke coming out of the stovepipes.
People lived here.
Corin saw that at once. Not many. A few dozen, perhaps, scattered through the arches, wherever there was a dry place and a bit of shelter from the wind. He saw a washing line between two pillars, with a baby's shirt on it, frozen stiff. He saw a brazier, glowing, with a kettle on it and an old woman crouched beside it in a shawl, holding her hands to the heat. He saw a goat. A real one, small and white and dirty, tied to a pillar with a rope, chewing something that looked like a boot. He saw a row of little bottles on a ledge, catching the light. A child's drawing in chalk on the brick of a wall. A tin bath, upside down. A wheelbarrow with no wheel.
And he saw the people.
They were standing. Not in a crowd. Not together. Scattered, here and there, between the arches, in ones and twos. A man in a sack apron. A woman with a baby on her hip. Two boys, about Juno's age, very thin, with no shoes. The old woman by the brazier, who had got up now and turned round. An old man leaning on a stick.
They were all looking the same way. Down the row of arches, to the left. Toward the end. Where the gray light came in from outside, and fell on a wooden front built across the last arch but one, and a door in it, made of old planks, standing open.
And in front of the door, a big man in a dark blue coat and a round black hat, with his back to them, quite still.
Nobody was saying anything.
That was the thing Corin noticed. Twenty people, perhaps, in a place like this, early on a Sunday morning, and not one of them was speaking. Not a word. Not a whisper. The only sounds were the dripping of the arches, and the trickle of the dyed stream in its channel, and the goat chewing, and the far-off Sunday bells, very faint, through the bricks.
They were afraid. He could see it on all of them. On the man in the apron, who had his hands in fists at his sides. On the woman with the baby, who had pulled it in close against her and put her hand over the back of its head, the way you do in a storm. On the two thin boys, standing so close together that their shoulders touched.
They had heard it too. In the night. At a quarter to twelve.
The old woman by the brazier saw them first.
She was very small and very old and very wrinkled, wrapped in so many shawls that she was nearly round, with a black bonnet tied under her chin with a bit of string. Her face was brown and creased like a walnut. Her eyes were small and black and sharp, and they went straight to Juno, and then to Corin, and then up, a long way up, to Oren. They stayed on Oren. On his size, and his beard, and his scar, and the sword at his side.
"No more," she said.
Her voice was cracked and thin and shaking, but it carried. Every head in the arches turned.
"No more of you," said the old woman. She had picked up something from beside the brazier. A poker. Black iron, with a hook on the end. She held it in both hands, in front of her, the way you might hold a broom to keep off a dog. "You've had him. You've had what you wanted. Whatever it was. You leave us be."
"We're not—" said Corin.
"I don't care what you're not." The poker shook. "You come in the night with your knocking. And now you come in the day with your swords." She looked at Oren. "There's nothing here. You hear me? Nothing. We've got nothing. We don't know nothing. We never saw nothing."
"Mother Grue."
It was Juno. She had stepped out in front of Corin, small and sharp in her huge coat, with her chin up.
"Mother Grue," she said again, "it's me. Juno. Terrence's girl. Marta's grand. I bring you your cough drops off Doyle's on a Friday, you know me."
The old woman's black eyes came down from Oren to Juno. They stayed there. Slowly, slowly, the poker came down too, until the hook of it rested on the stones.
"Juno," she said. "Juno Marta." Her face crumpled. All at once, like a paper bag. "Oh, child. Oh, child, you shouldn't be here. Not today."
"I know." Juno went to her. She took the poker out of the old woman's hands, gently, and leaned it against the wall, and took both the old brown hands in her own. "I know, Mother. This is Corin. He's all right. He's—" She stopped. She looked back over her shoulder at Corin, as if she were not sure what he was. Then her chin came up. "He's mine. He's the one who looks for people. And that's Oren. He's big but he's soft."
Oren, behind Corin, said nothing at all. But Corin saw that he had taken his hand off his sword.
The old woman looked at Corin. A long, hard look. Up and down. At his coat, black with coal dust. At his bandaged hand. At his face.
"You're the one," she said slowly. "The harvester. From the Ministry. Wick said."
Corin went cold.
"He said?"
"All week." Mother Grue's voice shook. "All week, he couldn't stop. There was a harvester came, he said. A young one, with a book. He got down on his knees in the muck and looked at me. Right in the eyes. Like I was somebody. And he asked me what color. Over and over." She shook her head. "He was proud of it. Daft old thing. Told everybody. Told them in the arches. Told them at the Bell, on Wednesday, and on Thursday, and on Friday, and last night, the last night—" Her voice broke. "He told them all. About the lane. About the lady and her flowers. About the smoke. Every word. Every night. I said, Wick, hush, Wick, you'll get yourself into trouble. And he said, I'm not afraid. The harvester'll find her. He's got a book."
She looked at Corin.
"He said you'd find her," she said.
Corin could not answer.
He stood under the dripping arch with the dye-stained water trickling past his boots and the Sunday bells going on and on, far away, through the bricks, and he could not say anything at all.
He got down on his knees in the muck and looked at me. Right in the eyes. Like I was somebody.
He did not remember doing it. It was in the Ledger. He had read it. But he did not remember. He did not remember the old man's face, or the lane, or the color of the smoke, or kneeling in the muck, or anything. It was all just ink.
And for Wick, it had been the proudest thing that had happened to him in years. The thing he told everybody. Every night.
The thing that had killed him.
No. Not killed. Corin made himself think it properly. Not killed. Worse than that. Or better. He did not know which.
"Tell me about the knocking," he said. His voice came out rough. "Please. Mother. Anything you heard."
Mother Grue held tight to Juno's hands. She looked down the row of arches, toward the open plank door and the big man in the blue coat standing in front of it. Her old mouth worked.
"I was asleep," she said. "By the fire. In my chair. I sleep in my chair, I can't lie down, my chest won't let me. And I woke up, and I didn't know why. I thought it was the cold. The fire had gone low. And then I heard it." She swallowed. "Down there. At his door. Knock. And then a long wait. And knock. And a long wait. And knock."
"Slow," said Corin.
"Slow," said Mother Grue. "Like a funeral bell. Like somebody who knows you're in, and has all the time in the world." She shivered. "And I couldn't move. I wanted to. I wanted to get up and go to my door and shout, Wick, Wick, don't open it. But I couldn't. My legs wouldn't. I just sat in my chair and held on to the arms of it, and I heard it."
"Heard what?"
"His voice." She shut her eyes. "Through his door. Before it opened. After the third knock. In the wait. He said something. Loud. Clear. Like a man saying a thing he's made up his mind to say, no matter what." She opened her eyes. "I couldn't hear the words. I'm too far. And my ears aren't good. But I heard him say it. Loud and proud. And then the door went. And it was quiet."
"How long?"
"I don't know. Not long." She shook her head. "Not long at all. Not enough time to say a prayer. And then they went. Past my door. Past all our doors. Two of them. Big. Tall. I saw them through the crack. Their heads touched the top of the arches." Her old hands tightened on Juno's. "And the smell."
"What smell?"
"Flowers," said Mother Grue.
The word dropped into the arches like a stone into a well.
"After they'd gone," she said. "After. When I could move again. I got up and I went to my door and I opened it, just a crack, and looked out. And they were gone. And it was dark. And quiet. And the air was full of it." She was whispering now. "Sweet. Heavy. Like a funeral. Like a whole room full of funeral flowers. All down the arches. So thick you could taste it."
She looked at Corin.
"That's what he said. Wick. That's what he always said, about the lady in the lane. She smelled like a funeral." Her voice cracked. "She came. Didn't she. She came herself. To watch."
Corin did not answer.
He looked down the row of arches. At the plank door, standing open. At the big man in the blue coat in front of it, who had heard them now, and turned his head, and was looking back at them over his shoulder with a face the color of porridge.
Sergeant Moll lifted one hand. Slowly. And beckoned.
Corin went.
The Rag-Picker
Sergeant Moll looked as though he had not slept for a year.
His big boiled-ham face, which had been red and shiny and cheerful in the lane behind the Inkwell, was gray now. Gray and loose and damp, like porridge left out overnight. There were dark bags under his eyes. His round black hat was pushed back on his head, and his blue Watch coat was buttoned wrong, one button off all the way down, so that one side hung lower than the other. He had his pipe in his hand. It was not lit. He did not seem to know he was holding it.
"Harvester," he said.
"Sergeant."
Moll looked past him, at Oren, and at Juno, and back.
"I didn't know who else," he said. His voice was low and hoarse. "I didn't know who else to send for. I went to the station first. At six. To tell the captain." He turned the cold pipe over in his fingers. "He said, Old age, Moll. Rag-and-bone men go soft in the head. Write it down as natural decline and get back to your beat. And he gave me a look." He swallowed. "I've had that look before. Twice this year. You don't argue with that look, not if you want to keep your coat."
"I know the look," said Corin.
"So I wrote it down," said Moll. "Natural decline. Like he said. In my book. And then I came out of the station and I stood on the steps, and I thought about that lane, and the lad on the rubbish heap, and you on your knees in the muck." He looked at Corin. "And I tore the page out. And I went to Mother Marta's."
"Thank you."
"Don't." Moll put the pipe in his pocket, at last, as if he had just noticed it. "Don't thank me. Just look at him."
He stood aside.
Wick's home was the last arch but one.
Somebody had built a front across it, a long time ago, out of whatever they could find. Old planks, and a door with no handle, and the side of a cart, and a sheet of rusty tin, and sacks nailed over the gaps. It was crooked and patched and leaned a little to the left. But it had been mended carefully, over and over, Corin saw, with little bits of wood and nails and string, wherever the wind had found a way in. Somebody had cared about it.
The door was standing open.
There was no lock on it. There never had been. Corin could see the place where a bolt had once been, years ago, and the holes where the screws had gone. Nothing there now. Just a loop of string, to tie it shut from inside.
The string was not broken. It had been untied.
He opened it himself, Corin thought. After the third knock. In the wait. He untied the string and opened the door.
He went in.
It was a small place. Smaller than Corin's room at the top of No. 14 Tallow Street. The curved brick roof came down low on both sides, so that you could only stand up straight in the middle. It was dark. The only light came in through the open door behind him, and through a small square hole cut high in the plank front, with a bit of oiled paper stretched over it for a window.
And it was full of paper.
Everywhere. All round the walls, from the floor to where the roof curved over. Stacks of it. Piles of it. Bundles of it, tied with string. Old paper and new paper, white and gray and yellow and brown. Spoiled sheets from the printing houses, with the ink smudged or the lines crooked or the words printed twice, one over the other. Broadsheets. Bills. Notices. Old letters. Old ledgers with their covers torn off. The pages of books.
Corin had thought, when he heard that Wick picked paper, that it would be a mess. A rag-and-bone man's mess. A heap.
It was not a mess.
It was sorted. All of it. Perfectly. Every stack was square, its edges straight, as if someone had knocked them level on a table. The white paper was on the left, by the door. Then the gray. Then the yellow. Then the brown. The printed sheets were in their own piles, by size. The broadsheets were folded, every one, the same way, in half and in half again, and stacked in a neat tower as tall as Juno. The bundles were tied with string, each knot exactly in the middle, each loop exactly the same size.
And on the floor in the middle of it all, on an old sack, sitting cross-legged, with his back to the door, was a small old man in a great many rags, sorting more.
He had a pile of paper on his left. A loose, untidy pile, the kind you might sweep up off a printing-house floor at the end of the day. He took a sheet off the top of it. He looked at it. He smoothed it flat on his knee with both hands, slowly, carefully, pressing out every crease. Then he turned, and laid it on one of the neat stacks in front of him, and knocked the edges level with his palms, tap, tap. Then he took the next sheet off the pile.
He did not look up when Corin came in.
Tap, tap.
Corin stood just inside the door.
He could smell it. Doctor Silas had been right. It was still in the air. Fresh. Like smoke after a fire. Not a smell, exactly. Not something the nose could find. Something behind it. A dryness. A cleanness. Like the air in a room where somebody has just scrubbed the floor with soap and left the window open. Like the air on a high mountain, where nothing grows. It went into him with every breath, and it felt cold, and it felt empty, and it made the hairs on his arms stand up under his sleeves.
And under that, faint and sweet and heavy and sick, the other smell. The one the nose could find.
Lilies.
He breathed through his mouth. Shallowly. Don't breathe too deep.
Then he went and knelt down on the floor in front of the old man.
Wick was clean.
That was the first thing. That was the thing that frightened Corin most, though he did not know why at first. The old man's face had been brown and creased and dirty in the lane, with a white stubble of beard and grime in every wrinkle. Now somebody had washed it. Or he had washed it himself. It was pink. Scrubbed. The white stubble was still there, but the grime was gone. His thin gray hair had been combed flat, wet, with a parting in it, very straight. His rags were the same rags, but they were tidier. Pulled straight, and tucked in, and buttoned where they had buttons.
He looked like a small boy dressed for church.
And his face.
Corin had seen a great many Slates. On the docks. In the line at the intake hall. On the second-floor landing of No. 14 Tallow Street. Felix, on the plank, with ice on his shoulder. Pip, under her charcoal hills. Old Terrence, rubbing a key that was not there. He knew the face. He had written it down so many times that he could have drawn it with his eyes shut. Calm. Gentle. Pleasant. Empty.
He had never seen it on a face he knew.
That was the difference. He did not remember Wick. Not really. Only from the Ledger. But the Ledger had been very full, that first morning, very careful, and he had read it so many times this week that the old man had become almost like someone he knew. Small and bent and thin as a stick. Small bright eyes, frightened. I am right in the head. It's everybody else that's gone wrong.
The eyes were not bright now. And they were not frightened. They were pale blue and clear and wide open, like a baby's. They looked at the paper on his knee. They did not look at anything else.
"Wick," said Corin softly.
Tap, tap.
"Wick. It's me. The harvester. With the book."
The old man took another sheet off the pile. He smoothed it on his knee. And his mouth moved.
Corin had thought he was silent. He was not. He was murmuring. Very softly. So softly that you had to be close, as close as Corin was now, kneeling on the cold brick in front of him, to hear it. The same words. Over and over. In a dry, thin, gentle voice, like the rustle of the paper in his hands.
"The tide weighs it. And it comes up light."
Tap, tap.
"The tide weighs it. And it comes up light."
He took another sheet.
"The tide weighs it. And it comes up light."
Corin knelt very still.
Behind him, in the doorway, he heard Juno make a small sound. And Oren's voice, very low: "Stay back, girl." And Moll, hoarse: "He's been doing that since I came. Since six. Before. I don't know how long. Mother Grue says she heard him at three."
"The tide weighs it. And it comes up light."
"Has he said anything else?" said Corin. He did not turn round. He did not take his eyes off the old man's face.
"Nothing," said Moll. "Not a word. I tried. I asked him his name. I asked him did he know me. I said, Wick, it's Moll, you old devil, I've known you twenty years, I bought you a gin on Midwinter Night." His voice cracked. "He looked at me. Like that. Like I was a nice stranger. And he said it."
"The tide weighs it," said Wick, gently, to his paper. "And it comes up light."
Corin took off his left glove.
He did it slowly, finger by finger. He put the glove in his pocket. Then he reached out with his bare left hand, very gently, the way you reach out to a bird that you do not want to frighten, and took one of the old man's hands. The right one. The one that was smoothing the paper.
It stopped.
It lay in his palm. Small and thin and light, like a bundle of twigs wrapped in cold paper. The skin was very soft. The nails had been cleaned, every one, with something sharp. There was still a little black under one of them, in the very corner, that the cleaning had missed.
Wick looked up.
For the first time, his pale clear eyes left the paper, and came up, slowly, and found Corin's face. And stayed there. And looked at him, mildly, kindly, with a little polite puzzlement, the way you look at a stranger on a bench who has spoken to you without being introduced.
And smiled.
It was a sweet smile. Shy, and gentle, and a little sorry, as if he were apologizing for not knowing who Corin was. Corin had seen that smile before too. On Felix. On the jetty. Evening, sir. You're the gentleman with the pie.
"What color, Wick?" said Corin.
His voice was not steady. He did not try to make it steady.
"The light in the lane," he said. "Behind the printing house. At the fifth bell. When you came round the second corner. What color was it?"
The old man looked at him. Smiling. Kind. Puzzled.
Nothing moved behind his eyes. Nothing at all. Corin watched for it. He watched the way he had watched Felix on the plank, for the reach, for the frown, for the little narrowing of the eyes that meant someone was looking, down in the dark, for something that was gone. He watched for a long time.
It did not come. There was nothing to reach with. Nothing to reach for. The pond was quite still, all the way to the bottom.
Wick's lips moved.
"The tide weighs it," he said, kindly, to Corin. As if it were an answer. As if it were the only answer he had, and he was sorry it was not a better one. "And it comes up light."
Corin did what Doctor Silas had told him not to do.
He knew he was doing it. He did it anyway. He held the old man's small cold hand in his own bare one, and he shut his eyes, and he reached.
Not with the rod. He did not take the rod out. He did not want to cut anything. He did not want to take anything. He only wanted to look. The way you put your hand into a dark cupboard to feel what is on the shelves. Gently. With the very ends of his fingers. With that part of himself, behind the eyes and under the breastbone, that he had used ten thousand times without knowing how.
He reached into Wick.
And there was nothing.
He had known there would be nothing. Doctor Silas had told him. But he had not known what nothing would feel like. He had thought it would be dark. Like a cellar. Like a well. Like the black of the coal hole, pressing on his eyes.
It was not dark.
It was white.
White and smooth and clean and cold, in every direction, as far as he could reach. Like the inside of an egg. Like a room that had been whitewashed, walls and floor and ceiling, so many times, so thickly, that every corner had been filled in and rounded off and there was nowhere left for anything to hide. No shelves. No cupboards. No drawers. No dusty heap in a corner. Not a crumb. Not a thread. Not a single grain of slag. He had cleared ten thousand glasses in the cold room under the hall, and he had never once seen a glass as clean as this.
This was what the needles did. Ten of them. Thin as hairs. In the dark. In the time it takes to say a prayer.
He felt sick.
Out, said Malakor. Sharp and sudden, in his head. Come out, boy. There is nothing for you here. Come out before it gets into you. It is still fresh. It wants more.
Corin did not come out.
Because there was something.
Not much. Almost nothing. So small that he nearly missed it, in all that white. Right at the very bottom. Right in the middle. Where, in a glass, the last of the slag always gathers, in a tiny gray ring, after you have scraped all the rest away.
A pebble.
That was what it felt like. Round and smooth and hard, and very small, and quite alone. Like a pebble left at the bottom of a jar after the water has been poured out. The white had washed all round it, again and again. Polished it. Worn it smooth. But it had not been able to move it. It sat there, at the bottom of everything, quite still, and it was warm. The only warm thing in all that cold.
He touched it.
And heard it. Not in Wick's voice. Not in the dry soft murmur that was coming out of the old man's mouth. In another voice. Deep and rough and cracked and very loud. Shouting. The voice of a small old man in rags, standing in the dark behind a plank door with no lock, in the long slow wait after a third knock. With his hands in fists. With his chin up.
THE TIDE WEIGHS IT, AND IT COMES UP LIGHT! YOU HEAR ME? THE TIDE WEIGHS IT, AND IT COMES UP LIGHT! I'M KEEPING IT! I'M KEEPING IT FOR THE HARVESTER!
Corin's eyes came open.
He was kneeling on the brick floor in the crooked little room full of paper, with the old man's hand in his, and his whole body was shaking. His face was wet. He did not know when that had happened.
Wick was looking at him. Kind. Puzzled. Smiling his shy, sorry smile.
"The tide weighs it," he said gently. "And it comes up light."
So that whoever is on the other side has a moment, Rook had said, in the white room under the Tide Hall, with his shaking hand made into a fist. One moment. To hide something. Or to run. Or to hold somebody's hand. Or to say one thing out loud, so that the last thing they remember is something they chose.
It isn't much. I know it isn't much. It was all I could do.
Wick had not hidden. He had not run. He had nobody's hand to hold.
He had untied the string on his door. And in the moment Rook gave him, he had chosen one thing. Out of all his long life. Out of every morning at the fifth bell, and every sheet of spoiled paper, and every gin on Midwinter Night, and every face he had ever known. One thing.
Not his own name. Not a face. Not a memory of his mother, or a girl, or a summer.
The words he had heard a tall woman murmur in a dark lane, over a dying man. Because a harvester with a book had knelt down in the muck and looked him in the eyes and asked him what color. And he had believed the harvester would find her.
I'm keeping it for the harvester.
Corin bent his head.
He held the small cold hand in both of his, against his forehead, and he stayed like that for a long time, in the dark little room, with the paper all round him in its perfect stacks, and the dry, clean smell of nothing in the air, and the lilies under it.
Nobody in the doorway said anything.
At last he lifted his head. He let go of the old man's hand. He put it back on the paper on Wick's knee, very gently, the way it had been.
At once, the hand began to move again. Smoothing the paper. Pressing out the creases. Turning, laying the sheet on the stack. Tap, tap.
"The tide weighs it," murmured Wick. "And it comes up light."
Corin took out the Ledger. His hands were shaking so badly that the pencil skidded on the page. He did not care. He wrote it down. Every word. In big letters, so that he would never, ever be able to miss it, whatever else he lost.
WICK. Sunday, first light. The arches under the Dye-Works.
Emptied by the Erasers. Three slow knocks at a quarter to twelve. I HEARD THEM.
He opened the door himself. In Rook's moment he chose ONE thing to keep. He shouted it so it would be the last. "The tide weighs it, and it comes up light. I'm keeping it for the harvester."
He kept it for me.
He looked at the last line for a long time.
Then he wrote, under it, slowly, pressing so hard that the pencil went through the paper:
I will find her, Wick.
