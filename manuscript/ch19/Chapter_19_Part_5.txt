Chapter 19: The Rag-Picker, Part 5 of 5: The Scale-Call
The Scale-Call
When he came out, the arches had changed.
The people were still there. But they had come closer. While he had been inside, kneeling on the brick with the old man's hand in his, they had come drifting down the row of arches, one by one, in ones and twos, the way people drift toward a fire on a cold night. Not too close. Not one of them came past the last pillar but one. But they were all there now, in a loose half circle, in the gray light. The man in the sack apron. The two thin boys with no shoes. The old man with the stick. A girl Corin had not seen before, with a broom. Mother Grue, in her shawls, with Juno beside her holding her hand.
And a young woman with a baby.
She was standing a little apart from the others, by the pillar nearest the door. Corin had seen her before, at the start, holding the baby close with her hand over the back of its head. She was perhaps twenty-five. Tall and strong and broad in the shoulders, with big red hands, and a face that had been in a great deal of wind. Her hair was brown and thick, and she wore it in one long rope of a plait down her back, tied at the end with a bit of tarred string. She wore a man's canvas jacket, stiff with salt, over a long brown skirt, and boots like a docker's. There were gold rings in her ears. Small ones. Two in each.
She was looking at the door of Wick's arch. Not at Corin. At the door. And her lips were moving. Very slightly. Without a sound.
Corin knew the shape of the words. He had just heard them a hundred times.
He went toward her.
She saw him coming, and her lips stopped moving, and her big red hand went up and covered the back of the baby's head again. But she did not step away. She stood her ground by the pillar, with her chin up, and waited for him. The baby, wrapped in a gray shawl, with only a small pink face and one fist showing, looked at him with round, wet, solemn eyes, and put the fist in its mouth.
"You know it," said Corin.
The young woman did not answer.
"What he's saying," said Corin. "You know what it means. I saw you."
She looked at him for a long time. Her eyes were gray. Not pale, like the fish-eyed man in the yard. Gray like the sea on a cloudy day. Hard and clear and very tired.
"Who's asking?" she said.
Her voice was strange. Not the voice of the Ink Quarter, or the docks, or the High Quarter. Corin had never heard it before. It was low and slow, and it went up at the end of things, a little, like a question, even when it was not a question. And the words came out rounder than they did in the city. Softer. As if they had been washed smooth by water.
"My name is Corin Vane," he said. "I'm a harvester. I'm the one he—" He stopped. "I'm the one he kept it for."
She looked at him a moment longer. Then at the Ledger in his hand. Then at his face again, at his wet eyes, and the coal dust streaked across his cheeks where he had wiped them.
Something in her own face moved. Not much.
"Hessa," she said. "Hessa Doon. Off the Patient Grace. She's the black barge with the red sail, tied up below the coal wharf, if you're ever looking." She shifted the baby on her hip. "This is Robin. He's mine." She jerked her chin at the arch beside Wick's. A wooden front like Wick's, but newer, with a stovepipe and a strip of blue curtain. "We winter here. When the river freezes and we can't run. Me and Robin and my man, Wat. Three winters now." She looked at Wick's door. "He was our neighbor. Three winters. He used to give Robin bits of colored paper. To look at."
"I'm sorry."
"Yes," said Hessa Doon. She did not say anything else for a while. Then: "You want to know about the words."
"Please."
"They're not his," she said. "He never said them before. Not once in three winters. Not till this last week." She paused. "And then he said them all the time. At the Bell. To anybody. She said the tide weighs it. The lady in the lane. What does that mean? What does that mean? And everybody laughed at him. Because nobody knew. Nobody in the city knows." Her gray eyes were very steady. "Except people like me."
"People like you."
"Marsh people," said Hessa Doon.
She told him standing by the pillar, with the baby on her hip and her voice low, so that the others in the half circle had to lean in to hear. Corin wrote it down while she talked. She watched him do it with a little frown, as though she had never seen anyone write so fast, and did not quite trust it.
"My mam was from the marsh coast," she said. "West. Down past the end of the country, where the land runs out into the water and you can't tell which is which. Marrowmere. You've heard of it?"
"A little."
"Everybody's heard of it a little," said Hessa. "City of canals. City of fog. City of masks. They tell stories about it to children up here, my man says. To frighten them." She almost smiled. "My mam left when she was sixteen. Ran off with a bargeman, my dad, and came up the coast and never went back. But she took me down there once. When I was little. Not much bigger than Robin. To see her own mam before she died."
"And you heard it there."
"I heard it at the Exchange." Hessa's eyes went somewhere else. Somewhere a long way off, and a long time ago. "The Tidal Exchange. It's the biggest building in Marrowmere. On the water. All white stone, with a great green roof made of copper, and steps going down into the canal on every side, so the boats can come right up to the doors. And inside—"
She stopped. She shook her head a little.
"Inside, it's all scales," she said. "Brass ones. Hundreds of them. Big as cartwheels, some of them, and some no bigger than my hand. All hanging from chains from the roof, in rows, up and up into the dark. And underneath them, the floor's not a floor. It's water. Seawater. The tide comes right in under the building, through great stone arches, and goes out again, twice a day. You walk round on wooden walkways over the top of it." She was quiet a moment. "I've never forgotten it. I was five. I'll never forget it as long as I live."
"What do they weigh?" said Corin. He thought he knew.
Hessa looked at him.
"Same as you do up here," she said. "With your glasses. Your Bank. Your lamps." Her mouth twisted. "Memories. Only down there, they don't pour them into glass. They put them in pearls. Little ones. White, and gray, and pink. You hold a pearl in your mouth and think of a thing, and it goes into the pearl. And then you take it to the Exchange, and you put it on a scale."
"And the tide—"
"The tide weighs it," said Hessa Doon.
She said it differently from Wick. Not a murmur. Not a whisper. She said it out, clear and strong, in her low, slow, marsh voice, going up a little at the end. Like a call. Like the cry of a man selling fish in a market, or the call of a boatman going under a bridge. And it rang under the arches.
Somewhere behind Corin, Oren made a small sound.
"That's what the weighers cry," said Hessa. "When they put a pearl on the scale. The scales are tied to the water, you see. To the tide. With chains, and floats, and wheels. I don't know how. Nobody knows how, except the weighers, and they don't tell. But the tide moves the scales. Not the man. The man can't cheat it. The tide does the weighing." She shifted Robin on her hip. "So when the weigher puts the pearl on, he calls it out. The tide weighs it. So everybody in the hall can hear. So everybody knows it's fair."
"And then?"
"And then you wait. And the scale goes down, or it doesn't. And the weigher calls the weight." She paused. "Most times it's a number. A good number, or a bad one. And you take your money and go."
"Most times."
"Most times," said Hessa. "But sometimes the pearl's not what you said it was. Sometimes you come in and say, This is the day my first child was born. The whole day. Every minute. And you put it on the scale. And it's not. Because you've sold bits of it before. Or burned bits of it. Or somebody's taken bits of it, while you weren't looking. Or it was never as big as you told yourself it was." Her gray eyes held Corin's. "And the scale doesn't go down far enough. And the weigher looks at it, and he looks at you. And he calls it out. Loud. So the whole Exchange can hear."
She said it then. The whole thing. In that clear, rising, ringing marsh voice.
"The tide weighs it. And it comes up light."
The words went up into the dripping arches, and rang there, and died.
Nobody in the half circle said anything.
"It means short," said Hessa. Quieter. "It means less than you said. It means something's missing." She looked at Wick's door again. "My mam said it was the worst thing that could happen to you, at the Exchange. Worse than a bad price. Because the whole hall hears. And everybody knows that you came in with a thing you thought was whole, and it wasn't. And either you're a liar, or somebody's robbed you. And nobody can ever tell which."
Corin wrote it all down.
When he had finished, he stood looking at the page for a long moment.
He was thinking about the lane. About a tall woman in a long coat with a fur collar, in a white half-mask, standing over a dying scribe on a rubbish heap with a silver cup in her hand. Catching his blue smoke. All of it. And Pell, on his back, with his boot pulled off, burning the thing she had come for, right in front of her, before she could take it.
She didn't get what she came for, Corin had told Sergeant Moll, in that lane. Pell burned it first.
And she had walked past an old rag-picker in the dark, so close that her coat brushed his arm, smelling of funeral flowers. With the jar in her hand. Weighing it. Feeling, perhaps, how light it was. And murmuring to herself, in the old cry of a hall full of brass scales on the far side of the country, the way a person murmurs a thing they learned as a child, without thinking.
The tide weighs it. And it comes up light.
Short. Less than she wanted. Something missing.
"She's from there," he said. Not to anyone. "Or she learned there. Or someone who taught her did."
"Who?" said Hessa.
Corin did not answer that.
He turned round, instead. To look at Oren.
Oren had not moved from where he had been standing, by the door of Wick's arch, with Moll. But his face had changed. It had gone pale, under the beard. Paler than Corin had ever seen it. And his right hand had come up, slowly, without his seeming to know it, and was resting against the left side of his face. Against the scar. The long white scar, running from his temple down across his cheek to the corner of his mouth.
He was looking at Hessa Doon. At the gold rings in her ears. At her long brown plait with its bit of tarred string.
"Captain?" said Corin.
Oren blinked.
"I've heard it," he said. His voice was rough. Slow. As if he were picking his way across stepping stones in a river, very carefully, one word at a time. "Before. That voice. That—going up, at the end. Like a question." His hand pressed harder on the scar. "In the Thorne house. A long time ago. Somebody—" He stopped.
"Who?"
"I don't know." Oren's big hand came down from his face. He looked at it, as if it belonged to someone else. "I don't know. It's—I had it. Just then. When she said it. I had it, right there." His mouth worked. "I think I burned it. Last year. Or the year before. For a barrier. I've burned so many words." He shook his head slowly, like a bull shaking off flies. "It's gone."
Corin looked at him for a long moment.
Then he wrote, in the Ledger, under Hessa's words, in small letters:
Oren has heard the marsh voice before. In the Thorne house. Long ago. Can't remember who. Burned it?
And under that:
Lady Thorne. "Her real name, given on the water." The brooch. Marrowmere.
Cressida. Lilies.
Both?? Neither??
He drew a line under it. Then another.
Sergeant Moll had come up beside him. The big man was looking at Hessa Doon with his tired porridge face, and then at Corin, and then down at the Ledger.
"There's something else," he said quietly. "I didn't put it in my book. I didn't put it anywhere."
Corin waited.
"The captain," said Moll. "The look he gave me. This morning." He took his cold pipe out of his pocket again and turned it over in his thick fingers. "I said I've had it twice this year. Well. The first time was in the summer. After a fire on Cinder Lane. And the second time was Friday." He looked at the pipe. "Friday afternoon. There was a lady came to the station. In a carriage. To see the captain. They were shut up in his office for half an hour. I was on the desk. I didn't see her face. She had a veil on." He stopped.
"And?"
"And when she'd gone," said Moll, "I went in with the duty book, and the whole office stank of it." He did not have to say what. His face said it. "Like a funeral. And the captain was sitting at his desk with a little cream envelope in front of him. With a white wax seal. And he looked at me. Like that. The look." He put the pipe away. "And Friday evening, the broadsheets came out about the Thorne girl and the doctor. And the orders came down to watch every corner of the Quarter."
Corin wrote it down. Friday. A veiled lady at the Watch station. Lilies. Cream envelope, white wax. Then the broadsheets.
"Thank you, Sergeant."
"I said don't thank me." Moll looked over at Wick's open door. At the small figure sitting inside on its sack, in the gray light, sorting paper. Tap, tap. His big face crumpled for a moment, and then he pulled it straight again, hard, the way you pull a coat straight. "Twenty years I've known that old devil. Twenty years. He used to steal the paper off my desk." He turned away. "Mother Grue says she'll keep him. Feed him. Make sure he doesn't wander into the river. I'll bring coal." A pause. "It's not much."
"It's something," said Corin.
"Is it?" said Moll. "Is it, harvester?"
Corin did not answer.
Inside the crooked little room, among his perfect stacks, Wick smoothed another sheet of paper on his knee, and laid it down, and knocked the edges level.
"The tide weighs it," he murmured, kindly, to nobody. "And it comes up light."
What the Glass Can't Take
Juno saw it first.
They had come out of the dye tunnel onto the ledge at the bottom of the river wall, into a morning that had turned from gray to white. Not sun. The sun had not come out. But the sky had gone pale and bright and even, like the inside of a shell, and the light came off the river ice so hard that it hurt to look at. The tide had come in a little while they were under the arches. The strip of bare mud was narrower now. The ice went grinding past almost at their boots.
Oren went up the slimy iron rungs first. Slowly, swearing, with his big hands slipping on the frost. Then Corin. Then Juno, last, who could have gone up in three jumps and instead waited at the bottom with her hands in the pockets of her huge coat, scowling at the water.
She was halfway up when she stopped.
Corin, at the top, leaning over the wall to give her a hand, saw her stop. Saw her head come round, quick and sharp, the way a bird's head comes round. Saw her look up. Not at him. Past him. Over his shoulder, and up, and up, at the roofs on the hill above the river road.
At the Dye-Works.
"Don't look," she said. Very quietly. Without moving her lips much. "Don't turn round. Just give me your hand like nothing."
Corin gave her his hand like nothing. She came up the last of the rungs and over the top of the wall onto the river road and stood beside him, brushing the frost off her coat, not looking at anything.
"Roof," she said. "The long one. Behind the chimney. By the water tank."
Corin turned, slowly, the way a man turns to look at the weather. He looked up at the sky. At the white light. At the great square red chimney, with its black iron band near the top, and its little rail, and the white lily hanging upside down from the rail by a ribbon, limp.
Then, as if by chance, at the roof behind it.
It was a long roof. Slate, gray, with frost on it, sloping steeply down toward the river. At the far end, where it met the chimney, there was a big square iron water tank, rusty, sitting on stone legs. And beside the tank, on the very top of the roof, where the two slopes met, a figure.
Gray.
Not the gray of the Erasers. Not that pale fog-ash gray. A darker gray. The gray of a slate roof, or of a pigeon's back, so close to the color of the roof that if Juno had not said, Corin would never have seen it. A long gray cloak, with a hood, pulled forward. Sitting quite still on the ridge of the roof, with its knees drawn up, the way someone sits who has been sitting for a long time. Watching.
And on its knees, something small. A book. Corin could just see the edge of it, and something pale moving over it. A hand. Writing.
He looked at it for perhaps two breaths.
Then the figure looked up. Straight down at him. Across the whole long gray slope of the roof, and the river road, and the frozen air between. He could not see a face. Only the dark inside of the hood.
And it was gone.
Not quickly. It did not jump, or run. It simply stood up, smoothly, in one movement, and stepped back behind the water tank, and was not there anymore. The roof was empty. Gray and white and frosty, with the chimney standing over it.
"That's twice," said Juno.
Corin looked at her.
"Friday," she said. "When the Watch came to Pruett's for her." She jerked her chin back along the river, the way they had come, toward Saint Bride's, toward Lyra. "There was somebody on the roof across the street. In gray. Just like that. Writing. I saw them from the gutter when I was running. And when I looked back, gone." She scowled. "I thought I made it up."
"You didn't make it up."
"No," said Juno. "I don't make things up." She pulled her cap down harder. "Who is it?"
"I don't know."
Oren had come up beside them. He was looking at the roof too. At the empty place by the water tank. His hand was on his sword.
"Not one of hers," he said slowly. "Not one of his, either. Not the pale man's." He frowned. "Too careful. Too patient. They'd have sent somebody to knock."
"Then whose?"
Oren did not answer.
Corin took out the Ledger. Right there, on the river road, in the white light, with the ice grinding below the wall. He wrote it down. Gray cloak, hood. On the Dye-Works roof by the tank. Writing in a small book. Saw me. Went. Juno saw the same on Friday, over Pruett's. He paused. Then he added: Watching me? Or watching what happens to the people I talk to?
He looked at that for a moment. It made him feel cold, in a way that had nothing to do with the morning.
Then he closed the book, and they walked back along the river road to the Cut.
He told Lyra everything.
He had promised, so he did. Every word. He sat on the bottom step of the crypt stairs again, where he had sat all night, with the Ledger open on his knees, and he read it out to her, page by page, in the light of the candles and the brazier and the thin white daylight coming down through the crack in the roof. Mother Grue, with her poker. The perfect stacks of paper. The clean pink face and the combed hair. Tap, tap. The whiteness, inside. The pebble at the bottom of it. The shout in the dark, after the third knock.
I'm keeping it for the harvester.
Lyra listened without moving. She sat on an upturned crate in front of him, with her elbows on her knees and her chin on her fists, and her dark eyes on his face. She did not interrupt once. When he came to the shout, he saw her close her eyes, and keep them closed for a long time, and open them again.
He read her Hessa Doon. The Tidal Exchange, with its white stone walls and its copper roof, and the sea coming in under it through the arches, and the hundreds of brass scales hanging in the dark. The weigher's call.
The tide weighs it. And it comes up light.
Lyra said it after him. Very softly. Under her breath. And then she stopped, and frowned, and looked at the floor.
"Say it again," she said.
He said it again.
"No," said Lyra. "Not like that. Like she said it. Hessa. With it going up at the end."
Corin tried. He was not good at it. His voice was a city voice, flat, and it would not go up the way Hessa's had. But he tried, and it went up a little, at the end, light, like a question.
Lyra's hand had gone to her chest.
She did not seem to know it. Her fingers had closed on the front of her coat, over the place where, Corin knew, under the coat and the shirt, she wore her mother's brooch on a ribbon. She was frowning harder now. Looking at nothing. The way Felix had looked at nothing, on the plank.
"I know that," she said. Hardly aloud. "Not the words. The—the way it goes. Up. Like that." Her fingers tightened. "Somebody used to—"
She stopped.
Corin waited. Oren, by the door, had gone very still.
But it did not come. He watched it not come. Her face reached, and reached, and found nothing, and gave up, and went back to being her face.
"No," said Lyra. She let go of her coat. "It's gone." She laughed, a short, hard, shaky laugh with nothing funny in it. "Everyone keeps saying that today. It's gone. Like it's the weather."
Corin wrote it down. Lyra knows the rise at the end of the scale-call. "Somebody used to—" Hand on the brooch. Gone. He did not say anything about it. He did not need to. She was reading it upside down as he wrote, and her mouth went tight.
"My mother," she said quietly. "That's what you're thinking."
"I don't know what I'm thinking."
"Yes, you do." She looked at him. "Her real name, given on the water. The brooch. Madame Hesper said." She swallowed. "And now this. And Oren hearing it in our house. And her." She did not say the name. She did not need to. Lilies. "Either my mother taught it to my aunt. Or my aunt learned it somewhere else. Or—"
She stopped again. This time she stopped on purpose.
"Or," said Corin.
"Or nothing," said Lyra. "Or I don't know. Write it down. Write it all down. We'll find out." Her chin came up. "We'll find out everything."
After that, he made the list.
He did it on a clean page, near the back of the Ledger, while Lyra sat with Pip, and Oren slept for an hour by the door with his sword across his knees, and Juno ate three of Doctor Silas's hard biscuits one after the other and fell asleep on a pile of blankets with the fourth still in her hand. He wrote it slowly, in his best and clearest hand, because he thought it might be important, and because he wanted, very much, to be able to read it again after he had forgotten writing it.
At the top of the page he wrote:
WHAT THE GLASS CAN'T TAKE.
And under it:
1. The moon tune. Briggs sold everything from Oakhaven: the valley, the night, the wall, the soldier's face. The glass took all of it, "like water." It would not take the tune. The clerk tried three times.
2. The moon tune again. Felix sold his WHOLE childhood. His mother sang it to him at the window. He hummed the end of it on the plank. The glass did not take it from him either.
3. Pell's Ash. When I harvested him, it hummed three notes. The same three. (Ledger, first pages.)
4. Pip. The name of the king was cut out. Cut out of the memory itself. But the sky stayed. The gold. "And they shall not come in."
5. Wick. The Erasers took EVERYTHING. Not a crumb. Except the one thing he chose to keep, in Rook's moment, after the third knock. He shouted it so it would be last. It is still there. Like a pebble.
He looked at the list for a long time.
Then he wrote, underneath:
Two different things? Or one?
Wick's is Rook's gift. The knock. A moment to choose. Rook built that in. That is clockwork. I understand that.
The tune is not clockwork. Nobody built that in. Briggs didn't choose it. Felix didn't choose it. Pell didn't. It just STAYS.
WHY?
And then, slowly, because he had only just thought of it, sitting there with the pencil in his hand:
The fiddler. A young fellow by the Bourse paid him a SILVER to learn the tune properly and play it on the quays. "Folk who've lost things. The tune might help them look." And the open eye in his hat. Same as the Bourse kitchen door. Same as Tam.
The Keepers KNOW. They know the tune stays. They are using it. On purpose. To wake people.
ASK TAM.
He underlined the last two words three times.
Boy, said Malakor.
Corin stopped writing.
The voice in his head had been quiet all morning. Since the arches. Since Come out before it gets into you. He had almost forgotten it was there. It came now very dry and very soft, like a page turning in an empty room.
"What?"
A long silence.
Nothing, said Malakor at last. Go on. Write your list.
"You know something."
I know a great many things. Most of them are of no use to you.
"Do you know why the tune stays?"
Another silence. Longer.
I know that you should ask your clerk with the open eye, said Malakor. Before you ask me.
And that was all. Corin waited, and asked again, and waited, and the voice said nothing more. It had gone back down into wherever it went. He sat with the pencil in his hand and felt it there, the way you feel someone standing behind a door, holding their breath.
He wrote, very small, at the bottom of the page: Malakor will not say. Malakor does not lie. So he knows, and he won't say. Why?
Then he closed the book.
The bells of the city rang noon.
Corin counted them, the way he always did now. Twelve. And under them, across the water, three quarters of an hour behind, Old Tide was still striking eleven, and not minding.
Noon on Sunday.
He turned the Ledger over and opened it at the very front. Inside the cover, where he wrote the things that must not be lost, he had made a little box days ago, and in the box, in ink, two lines:
SUNDAY MIDNIGHT. Vivienne. Ledgers I–VI, or the sister vial is gone and Pembrook does not stall.
MONDAY NOON. Fallowfield carriage for Lord Thorne. Unless Lyra brings the brooch, the portrait and the blue stone to No. 11.
Twelve hours. And then twelve more.
He looked up. Across the crypt, Lyra was sitting on the edge of Pip's mattress, under the charcoal hills and the great gold line. She had the little girl's hand in her lap. She was not talking to her. Just holding it. Looking at the drawing on the wall. At the oak, with every leaf drawn.
She felt him looking, and turned her head.
"She tied a lily to the chimney," said Lyra. "Over his door. Did you see?"
"I saw."
"That's for me." Lyra's voice was quite calm. "Not for him. Not for you. For me. So I'd know." She looked down at Pip's small hand in hers. "I can reach anyone. Anyone you talk to. Anyone who helps you. Anyone who says one word." She was quiet a moment. "Bring me the brooch, Lyra. Or I'll tie a lily over every door in this city."
Corin did not say anything.
"She thinks it'll make me quiet," said Lyra.
She stood up. She laid Pip's hand back on the blanket, gently, and pulled the blanket up over it.
"Wick wasn't quiet," she said. "Was he? He was a daft old man in a hole under a dye-works, and he knew they were coming, and he had one moment left, and he didn't hide, and he didn't run, and he didn't beg." Her voice had begun to shake. She let it. "He stood behind his door and he shouted."
She looked at Corin. Her dark eyes were very bright.
"So that's what we'll do," she said. "We'll shout."
He went up the crypt steps at one o'clock.
He had told them where he was going. The Bourse. The kitchen door, in the alley behind it, with the chalk eye on it. To find Tam, and ask him about a fiddler, and a silver, and a tune that the glass could not take. Lyra had wanted to come. Oren had said no. Juno had woken up long enough to say she was coming, and then fallen asleep again in the middle of saying it, with the biscuit still in her hand.
So he went alone.
The Cut was quiet. Sunday quiet. The frozen lane went up the hill between its dark houses, with smoke coming out of all the chimneys and the smell of Sunday dinners coming out of all the doors. Somewhere a dog was barking. Somewhere a child was crying, and a woman was singing to it, low, a song he did not know.
He walked up the middle of the lane, with his collar up and his gloved hands in his pockets, and the Ledger against his heart, and the jar and the rod in his coat, humming. He did not hurry. He did not look round.
At the top of the Cut, where it came out into the wide street by the old horse trough, he stopped to turn up the collar of his coat a little more. And in the black window of the pawnshop on the corner, shut for Sunday, with its iron grille across it, he looked at the reflection of the street behind him.
The Cut. The frozen lane. The houses, the smoke.
And at the bottom of the lane, in the shadow of a doorway, so still that it might have been part of the door, a shape. A long cloak. A hood.
Gray.
Corin looked at it in the black glass for one breath. Two.
Then he finished turning up his collar. And he walked on, along the wide street, toward the river and the bridges and the Bourse. Not fast. Not slow. The way a man walks who has nowhere in particular to be, and all afternoon to get there.
He did not look back.
But at the next corner, he turned left instead of right. And at the corner after that, he turned left again. And he began, very slowly, very carefully, the way Malakor had once taught him, a long time ago, in a lesson he did not remember, to walk in a circle.
Let it follow, he thought.
And then let's see who's following who.
