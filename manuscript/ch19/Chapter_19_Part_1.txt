Chapter 19: The Rag-Picker, Part 1 of 5: The Side Door
The Side Door
Tallow Row was the kind of street the city forgot to light.
There were lamps, once. Corin could see the iron posts, one at each end, black and bent, with their glass long gone and their cages empty. Somebody had hung a washing line between them. Nobody had taken the washing in. It hung there in the dark, frozen stiff, a row of shirts and stockings and one long white nightgown, turning very slowly on the line when the wind came up from the river, like a row of thin people who had stopped to listen.
The street was narrow. So narrow that if Corin had stood in the middle of it and put his arms out, he could almost have touched the walls on both sides. On the left were the backs of the dockers' houses, all crooked brick and blind windows and little yards full of broken barrels. On the right, tall and black and long, with no windows at all on this side, was the side wall of the intake hall of the Lantern Quay.
It went on and on. Brick after brick. Old brick, dark with soot, gone soft and crumbly at the corners where a hundred winters had got into it. Here and there, an iron drainpipe ran down it from the roof, furry with frost. And about halfway along, set deep into the wall, so deep that you could walk past it in the dark and never know it was there, was a door.
It was a small door. Much smaller than the great green doors at the front of the hall. It was the kind of door that is meant for coal men and clerks and boys with buckets, not for the people who come to sell. It was painted green too, like the front doors, but the paint was old and had peeled off in long curls, so that the gray wood showed through underneath, like bone through skin. It had a big iron keyhole and a small iron handle and no knocker at all.
Corin stood in front of it.
He had been walking for nearly three hours. Up and down. Along the river, and back, and along again. He had not wanted to stand still. When he stood still, the cold got into him, and so did the thoughts. So he had walked, with his collar up and his hands deep in his pockets, past the jetties and the warehouses and the dark ships, past the tavern on the corner of Pike Lane where the dockers were singing, past the end of Tallow Row three times without turning in. He had counted the bells. He had heard Saint Agatha's ring nine, and then ten, and then the quarter, and he had come down Tallow Row at last with his heart going like a rabbit's.
Now the bells were ringing the half hour.
He took his right glove off. He held the key Briggs had given him in his bare hand. It was cold, so cold it hurt, and the bent tooth pressed into his palm. He felt for the keyhole in the dark with the fingers of his left hand. Found it. Fitted the key in.
It went in a little way and stopped.
He pushed. It did not move. He took it out and tried again, slowly, turning it a little from side to side, the way you do with an old lock. It went in a little further. Stopped again.
Ice, said a voice inside his head. In the lock. It's been snowing in there all week.
Malakor's voice. Dry and thin and old, like paper rubbing on paper.
Corin did not answer. He took the key out. He put it in his mouth, the iron end of it, and held it there, between his teeth, until the metal had warmed a little. It tasted of rust and cold and old string. Then he put it back into the lock and breathed on the keyhole, long and slow, three times, the way you breathe on your hands on a winter morning.
This time the key went all the way in.
He turned it. It was stiff. It fought him. He used both hands. And then, with a sound like a small bone breaking, it turned, and the lock gave, and the door moved under his hand.
He did not open it at once.
He stood very still in the dark with his hand on the cold iron handle and listened. To the street behind him. To the washing turning on its line. To the river, far off. To the singing in the tavern, very faint, round the corner. To his own heart.
Nothing else.
He opened the door.
Inside, it was black.
Not dark. Black. The kind of black that has weight, that presses on your eyes like a hand. Corin stepped over the high wooden step into it and pulled the door shut behind him, slowly, so the latch would not click. And for a long moment he stood with his back against the door and could see nothing at all.
He could smell, though.
The hall smelled different at night. In the day it smelled of people. Wet wool and cheap soap and fear and the oil the clerks used on their glasses. Now all of that had gone thin and cold, like the smell in a church after everyone has gone home. Under it, the other smells came up. Dust. Damp brick. Mice. Old paper. And, under all of those, faint and sour and sad, the smell that he knew better than any other smell in the world.
Slag.
The smell of memories that had broken. Like burnt sugar, and wet ash, and the inside of an empty bottle. It came up from somewhere below and to the left of him. From the cold room. From the shelves of jars.
He let his eyes get used to it. Slowly, slowly, the black turned gray.
He was in a narrow passage. It ran along the inside of the wall, with the wall on his right and a row of wooden doors on his left. Store rooms, he thought. Clerks' cupboards. At the far end of it, very faint, there was a thin line of yellow light, under another door. The porter's room. Puckett.
He listened.
And there it was. Through the door, through the wall, through the dark. Slow and deep and steady, like the sea going in and out of a cave. A snore.
Deaf in one ear and asleep in the other, Briggs had said. By ten.
Corin began to walk.
He walked the way he had learned to walk in the Ministry, years ago, in the long gray corridors where you were not supposed to be after the bell. Heel first, slowly. Then the rest of the foot, rolling, slowly, feeling for any board that might creak. Weight on it. Then the other foot. He kept his left hand on the wall. The bricks were cold and rough, and in places wet, and once his fingers went into a cobweb, thick and old and heavy with dust, and he had to stop himself from pulling his hand away.
The passage turned. And opened out.
He was in the hall.
He had never seen it like this. In the day it was full of light. Gemstone lamps hung from the high beams in rows, white and humming, and the light came down on the long counter, and the glasses, and the clerks in their gray coats, and the hundreds of people in the line, and the white tiled floor. Now the lamps were out. All of them. They hung from the beams far above his head, dark, like a row of sleeping bats. The only light came in through the tall windows high up in the front wall, from the lamps on the quay outside. It came in pale and thin and yellowish and fell across the hall in long stripes, from the windows to the floor, with the dust turning slowly in them.
In the stripes of light, Corin could see the counter. The long, long counter, all the way down the hall, with its row of glasses standing on it. Fifty glasses. Tall and round and smooth, like great bells made of ice. Each one with its brass plate at the bottom and its little brass number. In the day they woke and glowed and went cloudy and cleared, over and over, as people put their hands on them. Now they were all asleep. Dark. Clear as water. In the yellow stripes of light from the windows they shone a little, like ghosts of themselves.
He found himself looking for number eleven.
The third from the end. There. He could just see it. Just the same as all the others. Dark. Empty. Waiting for Tuesday.
He looked away.
The benches stood in their rows down the middle of the hall, empty, with the long stripes of light lying across them. Somebody had left a glove on one of them. A child's glove, red, knitted, very small. It lay on the end of the bench nearest the door, with its fingers curled up a little, as if it were holding something.
At the far end of the hall, by the top of the slag-room stairs, stood the Warden's ledger stand. Tall and black on its iron foot. The big ledger was not on it. Briggs took it home with him, Corin knew, every night, under his arm, in a canvas bag. Without it the stand looked like a gallows.
Corin went down the side of the hall, in the shadow of the wall, away from the stripes of light. He passed under the windows. He passed the end of the counter. He passed the clerks' high stools, standing in a row with their legs in the air where somebody had put them up on the counter to sweep. He came to the top of the stairs.
The slag-room stairs went down into the floor beside the ledger stand, steep and narrow, with a brick wall on each side and an iron rail on the left. In the day there was a gemstone lamp at the bottom, blue and cold, and the light came up the stairs to meet you. Now there was nothing. The stairs went down into the black like a well.
Not a match, Briggs had said. Not a light.
Corin put his hand on the iron rail. It was so cold it burned. He held on anyway, and went down.
The stairs were worn. He could feel it through his boots. Each step had a smooth dip in the middle, where ten thousand feet had gone up and down, his own among them, every morning at six for a week. He counted them. He had never counted them before. Nineteen. At the bottom, the floor changed from wood to stone, and the cold came up through the soles of his boots like water.
He was in the slag room.
He did not need to see it. He knew it. He knew it the way you know your own bed in the dark. The long low room under the hall, with its arched brick roof, white with old frost. The deep stone sink along one wall, with the iron pump. The wooden racks where the glasses came down in the day to be cleared, on their little carts, rattling. The long table with his tools on it. The rod-rest. The tin of rag. And along the far wall, from the floor to the roof, the shelves. Rows and rows of jars. Brown and green and clear. Pinned with yellow slips. And in every one of them, very faint, the slag, glowing.
That was the only light down here. The slag. A hundred tiny, dirty glows in the dark, like coals in a grate that has nearly gone out. Gray and brown and dull violet and sick green. Some of them flickered. Some of them did not. They lit nothing. They only made the dark look deeper.
And in the middle of the far wall, between the shelves, there was the slag-room door. The outside door. Big and heavy and iron-bound, with two great bolts across it, and steps going up from it to the yard. That was where the crates went out. Every night. Six of them.
Corin did not go near it.
He turned left at the bottom of the stairs. Toward the corner. Toward the place, in the day, where the mops and the buckets stood. He felt his way along the wall. His fingers found the wooden handle of a broom, and then another, and then the cold tin side of a bucket. The big mop bucket. He knew it. It had a dent in the side, and a rusty handle, and it was always half full of gray water that nobody ever changed.
He moved it. Slowly, with both hands. An inch at a time, so that the water inside would not slop. It was heavy, heavier than he expected, and the bottom of it scraped on the stone with a sound that seemed to him as loud as a scream. He stopped. Listened. Far above him, through the floor, through the hall, through the passage, through the wall, Puckett snored on.
He moved it again. A little more. Then a little more. Until there was space.
Behind the bucket, the wall was not brick.
He could feel it. Wood. Smooth, cold, painted wood, with the paint thick and lumpy, as if it had been painted over many times, the same gray as the walls, so that in the daylight you would never see it. A door. Low. Lower than his chin. He ran his fingers round its edges and found the crack between the door and the wall, filled up with paint. He found the hinges, two of them, big and flat. And he found, about halfway up on the left, where a handle ought to be, a small round hole, filled with old paint too.
Nobody's opened it in ten years, Briggs had said.
Corin put his fingertips into the crack along the edge of the door. He pulled.
Nothing happened.
He pulled harder. His fingers slipped. He took his knife out of his boot, the short thin knife he used to scrape the stubborn slag off the bottoms of the glasses, and he ran the blade all the way round the edge of the door, slowly, cutting through the old paint. It came away in dry flakes, falling on his boots. Then he slid the blade into the crack, near the top, and leaned on it.
The door moved. A hair. Then more. With a soft, tearing, sticky sound, like a plaster coming off a cut.
And a breath of air came out through the gap, against his face. Old air. Dead air. Cold, and black, and dusty, and smelling of coal.
He opened the door wide enough to get through, and no wider. He went through sideways, bending low, with his head down and his shoulder first. Then he reached back through the gap and pulled the mop bucket back toward the wall, as close as he could, so that if anyone came down the stairs they would see nothing but a bucket in a corner. And then he pulled the little door shut behind him.
The dark closed over him like water.
He was standing on something that moved under his boots. Something loose and lumpy and hard, that slid and crunched and shifted when he put his weight on it. Coal. Old coal. Ten years of it, at least, left behind when the stoves came in. The smell of it filled his nose and his mouth. Thick and black and dusty. He could taste it on his tongue.
And above him, a long way above, high up and a little to the right, there was a square of gray.
Not light. Not really. Just a little less dark than the dark around it. A square, about as big as a tray, crossed with thin black lines. Bars. The grating. And through the bars, a few stars, very small and very sharp, in a sky as black as ink.
Corin began to climb toward it.
It was slow. The coal slid under his hands and his knees. Every time he moved, a little stream of it went rattling away down the slope behind him into the dark, and every time, he stopped, and held his breath, and waited, until the sound had died away. He climbed on his hands and knees like a child. His gloves were black at once. So were the knees of his trousers. He felt the coal dust settle in his hair, on his eyelashes, in the corners of his mouth.
He came to the top of the heap.
The grating was just above his head now. He could reach up and touch it. Iron. Thick bars, set close together, crusted with frost and rust. Through them came the air of the yard. Clean and cold and sharp after the coal, like a drink of water. He put his face up to it and breathed.
And he could see.
He could see the whole yard.
The Coal Heap
The yard of the Lantern Quay was not a big place.
Corin had crossed it every morning for a week, at six, in the dark, on his way to the slag room. He had never looked at it. It was the kind of place you never look at. Now, with his face pressed to the iron bars and his eyes at the level of the cobbles, he looked at nothing else, and he saw it the way a mouse must see a kitchen floor.
It was a square. Cobbled, with old round stones, worn smooth and humped like the backs of turtles, with frost in the cracks between them. High brick walls on three sides, black with soot. On the fourth side, the back of the hall itself, with its row of small barred windows, all dark.
In the middle of the far wall stood the gate. Two tall wooden doors, painted green once, iron-bound, with a smaller door cut into one of them for people on foot. A thick wooden bar lay across them, resting in iron brackets. Above the gate, on the right, was the porter's window. The one small square of yellow light that Corin had seen from the jetty. It was nearer now. He could see the shape of the lamp through the dirty glass, and a pot of something on the sill, and the edge of a curtain that had once been red.
The water butt stood on the left, just in front of him. So close he could have reached out between the bars and touched it. A big round wooden barrel, taller than a man, with iron hoops, and a lid, and a long icicle hanging from the tap at the bottom, as thick as his wrist. The barrel hid him. That was why Briggs had chosen this place. Anyone in the yard would see the barrel. Nobody would see the grating behind it. Not unless they came round it and looked down.
And on the right, about ten paces from the grating, were the slag-room steps.
They went down into the ground beside the back wall of the hall. Six stone steps, steep and narrow, with an iron rail. At the bottom of them, in its deep brick doorway, the slag-room door. From up here Corin could only see the top of it. The arch of brick over it, and the top of the iron bands, and the frost on them. But he could see the steps. All of them. And the cobbles at the top, where a wagon would stand.
He looked at the cobbles there. There were marks on them. Two long dark lines, side by side, about as far apart as a man's arms can reach. Wheel ruts. Pressed into the frost and the muck, again and again, night after night, until the stone itself had been rubbed a little smoother there than anywhere else. And between them, scattered in a rough half circle, dark round marks on the stones.
Hoofprints.
No. Not hoofprints. He looked again. Too big. Too round. Too deep. As if something very heavy had stood there, on two flat round feet, many times, in exactly the same place.
He did not want to think about what had made them. So he thought about something else.
He settled himself to wait.
It was not easy. The coal heap was not a good place to sit. It shifted under him all the time. Every few minutes a lump would slide out from under his boot or his knee and go clattering away down the slope into the dark, and every time his heart would jump and he would freeze, and wait, and listen, until he was sure nothing had heard. In the end he found a way to wedge himself. His left knee on a big flat lump. His right foot pushed deep into the loose coal, up to the ankle. His left shoulder against the brick wall of the shaft, under the grating. His fingers curled round two of the iron bars.
The bars were so cold they burned through his gloves. After a while he stopped feeling his fingers at all.
He could hear the city. That surprised him. Down here in the coal, in the black, he had thought it would be silent. It was not. Sound came down through the grating with the cold air. The river, slapping at the quay wall beyond the yard. A dog barking, very far off, on the other bank. Somebody laughing in a street a long way away. A cart, going by on the quay, its wheels grinding on the frozen stones. The singing from the tavern on Pike Lane, still going, rough and happy and out of tune. And now and then, the bells.
Saint Agatha's rang the three quarters. Deep and slow.
A quarter of an hour.
He put his hand, very carefully, inside his coat.
The Ledger was there, against his heart, warm from his body. And in the deep inside pocket on the right, side by side, wrapped in a strip of bandage so they would not knock together, the silver rod, long and thin and cold, and the jar.
Pip's jar.
He had carried it all day. In the slag room, at the intake doors, on the long walk to Pike Dock, on the barrel beside Briggs. It was a thick brown medicine jar, the kind the doctors' shops sell cough syrup in, with a cork stopper and a twist of wire to hold it shut. He had wrapped it in a strip of bandage from the crypt. And through the bandage, through the brown glass, through the coat, he could feel it.
It was humming.
It always hummed. One note. Low and soft and steady, so low he felt it in his ribs more than he heard it in his ears. It had been humming since the moment he filled it, on the floor of the crypt under Saint Bride's, with a little girl's empty eyes looking up at him. PIP. THE SIXTH PIT. THE KING'S SKY. In the day, in the noise of the hall, he had nearly stopped noticing it.
He noticed it now.
Down here, in the black, in the silence under the city's noise, it seemed to him very loud. As loud as a bell. He pressed his hand flat over it, through his coat, and it hummed against his palm like a cat purring.
It will not stop, said Malakor. If that is what you are hoping. It has no reason to stop.
"Can they hear it?" Corin said. He did not say it aloud. He did not need to. He shaped the words in his mouth, without breath, and the voice in his head heard them the way it always did.
The constructs? A dry pause. They do not hear, boy. Not the way you mean. They have no ears. Rook saw to that.
"Then how do they find people?"
You know how. The clockmaker told you.
He did know. He had written it down on Thursday, in Rook's ticking workshop under the Tide Hall, with the brass dog lying across his feet.
They smell memory.
He looked down at his coat. At the jar under his hand, humming its one note. At the Ledger beside it, six years of it, every page full. At himself. A man made of borrowed things and copied things and things written down. A man who had been packed full of other people's ash, once, so tight he could not breathe.
"What do I smell like?" he said. "To them?"
Malakor did not answer for a while.
Like a library, he said at last. That has caught fire.
Corin shut his eyes.
But you are sitting, said Malakor, on top of a slag room. Behind a wall. Beside a door that has had six crates of old violet carried out of it every night since summer. This whole yard stinks of memory. Broken memory. Old memory. It is soaked into the stones. A pause. If I were a construct, I would not be able to smell one burning library in all that. Not unless I put my nose right up against it.
"Will they put their noses up against it?"
I have no idea, said Malakor. I have never been a construct.
It was the kind of thing Malakor said. Corin had written down, once, on one of the very first pages of the Ledger, Malakor is not kind. But he does not lie. He had found it again this week, and underlined it. He was not sure, any longer, which half of it was true.
He thought about Felix.
He had not meant to. But in the dark, with nothing to look at but the yard and the stars, his mind went where it liked, the way a dog goes off the path. And it went to the jetty. To the plank, and the ice, and the lantern. To a young man standing still, halfway between a barge and the land, with his hand pressed flat on his chest.
I know that one. Don't I?
Three breaths. Three breaths, and then it was gone, and the smile came back.
Corin wondered what Felix was doing now. Lying in his bunk in the dockers' house on Pike Lane, perhaps, with his coat on its hook and his boots under the bed. Listening to the men snoring around him. Not thinking about anything. Or perhaps thinking, just a little, very faintly, about a tune. Three notes. Somewhere, a long way down inside him, in the dark, where the glass had not been able to reach.
And Briggs. In his little house behind the green door, with three bolts across it. Sitting on the bottom step of his cellar with a candle, perhaps. With four hundred and twelve tiny jars glowing on their pine shelves around him like stars.
And then, because it was dark, and he was tired, and cold, and alone, he thought about the other thing.
You've got her eyes.
A teacher with a bucket and a ladle, going down a line of sweating soldiers on a hot day. You look too young to be carrying that pike. Ink on her fingers. Dark hair. A boy cleaning boots on a step, very serious, like a little old man. A girl of four, in braids, sitting in a big soldier's lap, with her hands on her chest.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
He had read those words a hundred times. He had written them down himself, from somebody else's mouth, a long time ago. They had always been just words. Ink on a page. A fact, like the name of a street, or the price of bread.
They were not just words now.
Now there was a step. And a river. And a big man singing, and a little girl listening. Now there was a picture to put them in. He could not see it, not really. It was not his. It was Briggs's, and most of it was gone, sold for six silver and a coat. But it was there, at the edge of things, like a room he could see through a crack in a door.
He found that his eyes were wet. In the cold the wet went icy at once, on his lashes, and stung. He did not wipe it. His hands were on the bars.
Boy, said Malakor quietly. Not now.
"I know."
There will be time for that. Later. If you are careful.
"I know."
He breathed in. The cold air came through the bars, clean and sharp, and went down into him, and he held it there, and let it out slowly, white, between the bars. He looked at the gate. At the porter's window. At the ruts on the cobbles and the round dark marks between them.
He made himself be nothing but eyes.
The bells began.
They came from far off first, the way they always did. A small one, high and quick, from somewhere over toward the Customs House. Then another. Then Saint Agatha's, on the hill, deep and slow and sure, like the voice of a very old man who has never been wrong in his life.
One.
Corin's fingers tightened on the bars.
Two. Three.
The porter's window went dark.
He did not see it happen. One moment there was a square of yellow light above the gate, and a pot on the sill, and the edge of a red curtain. The next moment there was nothing. Black. As if a hand had closed over it.
Four. Five. Six.
A door opened somewhere. A wooden door, on old hinges. He heard it creak. Then feet. Slow, shuffling feet, an old man's feet, in slippers, coming down wooden stairs inside the gatehouse. One step. Stop. One step. Stop.
Seven. Eight.
Puckett came out of the little door beside the gate.
Corin had seen him before, in the day. A small, round, bald old man with a white walrus moustache and a face like a pink potato. He wore a long brown dressing gown over his nightshirt, with a scarf round his neck and a knitted cap pulled down over his ears. He had no lamp. He shuffled across the cobbles to the middle of the gate in his slippers, with his arms wrapped round himself against the cold, and stood there.
He did not look round. He did not yawn. He did not stamp his feet or blow on his hands or do any of the things that a man does when he has been woken from a warm bed on a freezing night.
He just stood there. Facing the gate. Waiting. Perfectly still.
Nine. Ten.
And Corin saw his face, in the starlight. For just a moment, as the old man turned his head a little toward the water butt. Round, and pink, and pleasant. And quite, quite empty.
He was told once in July, Briggs had said. And that was enough.
Eleven.
The last stroke of Saint Agatha's went out across the river and died away.
In the silence after it, from the quay beyond the gate, Corin heard wheels.
