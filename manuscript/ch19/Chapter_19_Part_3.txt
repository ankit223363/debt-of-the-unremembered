Chapter 19: The Rag-Picker, Part 3 of 5: The Long Way Back
The Long Way Back
He dug the jar out of the coal with his bare hand.
It was still warm. Warmer than before. When his fingers closed round it through the bandage, it began to hum again at once, the one low note, steady, as if nothing had happened. He held it against his chest for a moment, and felt it hum, and was more glad of it than he could have said. Then he put it back in his coat, beside the rod, and pulled his torn glove off the iron bar, and climbed down the coal heap in the dark.
He did everything the way Briggs had told him. Backward. The little painted door. The mop bucket, moved an inch at a time, and moved back. The slag room, with its hundred dirty glows. The nineteen worn steps. The dark hall, with the stripes of yellow light lying across the benches and the red child's glove still curled on the end of the bench by the door. The long black passage. Puckett, snoring again behind his door, slow and deep, like the sea.
The side door. The key, cold in his numb fingers. The lock, stiff as before, turning with that small sound like a bone breaking.
Tallow Row. The frozen washing on its line, turning in the wind like thin people listening.
He stood at the end of the street and breathed.
The air was so cold it hurt his teeth. He did not care. He breathed it in, great gulps of it, clean and sharp, until the taste of coal had gone out of his mouth, and the smell of brass and oil and summer rain had gone out of his nose, and he could think again.
He looked down at his right hand. It was still clenched. He opened it.
The little yellow square lay in the middle of his palm.
He looked at it for a long time. Then he took out the Ledger, and opened it at the back, where the inside of the cover had a pocket made of folded paper, and put the square in there, beside a pressed oak leaf that he did not remember putting there either. He closed the Ledger. He put it away.
He began to walk.
The river road was empty.
It ran along the top of the river wall, east from the Lantern Quay, past Pike Dock and the timber yards and the coal wharves, toward the Cut. In the day it was full of carts and barrows and dockers and dogs. Now there was nobody on it at all. The lamps stood along it in a long line, far apart, each one in its own small round pool of light, with the dark between them. Gemstone lamps, the old kind, on tall iron posts, humming very faintly in the cold. Their light was white and thin and lit nothing but the frost.
On the right, below the wall, the river. Wide and black and slow, with the ice in it, gray shapes turning and grinding against each other in the current, making a low endless sound like a great animal grinding its teeth in its sleep. On the far bank, a few lights. A ship's lantern. A window. Nothing moved over there.
On the left, the backs of warehouses. Then a timber yard, with its great stacks of wood under tarpaulins, white with frost, like a row of sleeping cows. Then a coal wharf, black, with its iron cranes standing up against the stars like the skeletons of birds.
He walked in the middle of the road.
It was the same road the wagon had taken. He knew that. He could see where it had gone. Its wheels had left two long dark lines in the frost on the cobbles, side by side, running away ahead of him under the lamps, perfectly straight, perfectly even, never wavering. He walked between them. He did not mean to. His feet simply went there, the way feet go in the ruts of a path.
He was not following it. He told himself that. Saint Bride's was this way. The Cut was this way. It was the way home. He had promised Briggs, and he was keeping his promise. He was going home to his bed.
But he looked at the lines in the frost, all the same, as he walked. And he listened.
At the end of the coal wharf, the lines turned.
There was a bridge there. A small stone bridge, old and humped, where a lane came down from the streets above and crossed the mouth of a little stream that ran out under the river wall into the river. Beyond the bridge, the river road went on east. And off to the left, up the hill, between two tall black buildings, the lane went up into the town.
The two dark lines went off the river road, and over the edge of the bridge, and up the lane.
Corin stopped.
He stood on the cobbles beside the bridge and looked up the lane. It was steep, and narrow, and very dark, with no lamps at all. The two dark lines went up it, between the walls, and were lost in the black. Somewhere up there, at the top, there was a big shape against the stars. Tall and square, with a chimney. An old works of some kind. He knew the chimney. Everyone in the Ink Quarter knew it. Square, and enormous, and brick, red in the day and black now, going up and up out of the roofs into the sky, with a black iron band and a little iron rail near the very top. The Dye-Works chimney.
There was something on it. Near the top, on the rail. Something small and pale, that moved a little in the wind.
He could not see what it was. It was too far, and too dark.
Boy, said Malakor.
"I'm not following it."
No.
"I'm just looking."
Then look, said Malakor. And then go home.
Corin looked. Up the black lane, at the two dark lines going up into the dark, at the great chimney with the small pale thing on its rail, moving in the wind.
Then he turned, and crossed the bridge, and went on along the river road, east, toward the Cut.
He had gone perhaps a hundred paces when he heard it.
It came down the hill behind him. From the left, and from behind. From up the lane, from the town, from the dark streets around the great chimney. It came very faint, so faint that at first he thought he had made it up. But the night was perfectly still, and sound travels a long way over water on a still, frozen night, and he heard it.
A knock.
Slow. Heavy. Muffled by distance and by walls. Like something very large leaning forward and laying the flat of its hand against a door.
He stopped walking.
A second knock.
The same. Slow. Heavy. Patient.
He turned round. He stood in the middle of the river road, between the two dark lines in the frost, and looked back the way he had come. At the little humped bridge. At the black lane going up the hill. At the great square chimney against the stars.
A third.
And then nothing.
Nothing at all. Not a shout. Not a scream. Not a door banging, or a dog barking, or a window going up. Just the silence of the frozen city, and the ice grinding in the river, and his own heart.
I made them knock, Rook had said, with his shaking hand made into a little fist. So that whoever is on the other side has a moment. One moment. To hide something. Or to run. Or to hold somebody's hand.
Far off, over the water, Saint Agatha's began to ring the three quarters.
Corin stood in the road until it had finished. He did not move. He did not know where the knocks had come from. Somewhere up there. Somewhere in that maze of dark streets on the hill, among a thousand doors. He did not know whose door. He could not have found it if he had run all night.
And he had promised.
He stood there, and he hated the promise, and he hated himself, and he hated Briggs for asking it, and he knew that none of that was fair, and he stood there anyway, with his numb hands hanging at his sides.
There was nothing you could do, said Malakor. Quietly. Not unkindly. Not tonight. Not from here.
"You don't know that."
No, said Malakor. But I have been alive a great deal longer than you, boy, in one way and another. And I have heard a great many knocks.
Corin turned round again. He walked on. East. Along the river. He did not look back.
But he heard the knocks all the way to the Cut. Over and over. Slow, and heavy, and patient, in the back of his head, in time with his feet.
Saint Bride's stood at the bottom of the Cut like a broken tooth.
It had been a chapel once. A small one, for the bargemen and the fishermen and the poor people who lived along the stream. Now it had no roof. The walls were still standing, old gray stone, black with damp, with tall empty windows like eyes with the eyeballs gone. Ivy had grown up them and died, and the dead ivy hung over the empty windows like hair. The little bell tower at the end had lost its bell long ago. Through the empty arch at the top of it, the stars showed.
It looked as if nobody had been inside it for a hundred years. That was the point.
Corin went round the side of it, through the frozen nettles, to the low black door in the end wall. The door that went down. He stood in front of it. He lifted his fist.
And stopped.
Three slow. Two quick. Oren's knock. He had done it last night, and this morning. He had thought nothing of it.
He thought of it now.
Three slow.
He stood in front of the door with his fist in the air for a long moment. Then he lowered it, and opened his hand, and knocked with his fingertips instead. Lightly. Quickly. Not slow at all.
Tap tap tap. A breath. Tap tap.
There was a silence on the other side of the door. Then a bolt slid back. Then another. The door opened a crack, and a sliver of warm yellow candlelight came out, and a smell of woodsmoke and boiled cabbage and carbolic soap and sick children, and above it, a face. A broad face with a short gray beard, and an old white scar running from the left temple down across the cheek to the corner of the mouth.
"That," said Captain Oren, "was the wrong knock."
"I know," said Corin. "I'm sorry. I couldn't."
Oren looked at him. At his face, black with coal dust. At his white, ungloved right hand. At his eyes.
He opened the door all the way.
"Get in," he said.
The crypt was warm.
After the yard, and the coal, and the river road, it was so warm that Corin's face began to burn at once, and then his ears, and then, slowly and terribly, his fingers, as the blood came back into them. He sat on the bottom step of the crypt stairs with his right hand pressed between his knees and his teeth clenched, and waited for it to stop.
Nothing had changed down here since the morning. The low arched roof, whitewashed, with the old stone coffins set into the walls under it, each with its carved name worn away to nothing. The rows of straw mattresses on the floor, between the pillars, with the sick children on them under gray army blankets. Some sleeping. Some lying still with their eyes open, looking up at the roof. Some murmuring, very softly, in voices that were not their own, about lives they had never had. The two braziers, one at each end, glowing red, with the smoke going up through a crack in the roof. The candles, stuck in bottles on the stone ledges. And on the far wall, above the mattress in the corner, the charcoal hills, and the river, and the oak, and the great soft line of chalky gold going over all of it from hill to hill.
Pip was asleep under it. He could see the top of her head, and one small hand, curled on the blanket.
Doctor Silas was sitting beside one of the braziers on an upturned crate, with a tin cup of something steaming in his long clean hands. He looked up when Corin came in. He looked at him for a long moment, with his tired, careful eyes. Then he got up without a word, and poured something from a pot on the brazier into another tin cup, and brought it over, and held it out.
"Drink," he said. "Slowly. Both hands."
It was tea. Black and stewed and so strong it was nearly bitter, with a great deal of sugar in it. Corin held it in both hands, the way he had been told, and the heat of the tin burned his fingers, and he did not care, and he drank.
Lyra came out of the dark between the pillars.
She had been sitting up. He could see that. She was still in her coat, buttoned to the chin, and her short dark hair was standing up on one side where she had been leaning her head on her hand. Her face was pale and tight. She looked at him, and at the coal on him, and at the cup in his hands, and she did not say anything at all. She came and sat down on the step beside him, close, so that her shoulder touched his. And waited.
He told them.
All of it. Tallow Row and the side door. The coal heap. The yard. Puckett with his empty face, standing at the gate before the bell had finished. The wagon with no horse. The two figures on the box, with their hands on their knees, and the cold air going past their white faces without a breath. The six crates. The violet light, and the voices in it, and the one name that went past him like a bird past a window. The white face at the grating, an inch from his own. The needles. The pull, like the tide at his ankles. Leave it, there's a good fellow.
The pale man in the yard. The sweet. The wrapper, folded smaller and smaller.
When he said the name, Doctor Silas set his cup down on the stone floor very carefully, as if it had become heavy.
Oren said nothing. But his big hand had gone to the hilt of the sword at his side, and stayed there.
Lyra said, "Show me."
Corin took out the Ledger. He opened the paper pocket inside the back cover, and tipped the tiny yellow square out onto his palm, and held it out to her in the candlelight.
She did not touch it. She looked at it for a long time.
"He left it on the grating," said Corin. "On the top bar. Right in the middle. Without looking down."
"He knew you were there."
"I don't know."
"He knew," said Lyra. Her voice was flat. "Rook said. Remember? He hates untidiness. He'd never drop a thing like that on the ground. Not him. He'd put it in his pocket. He'd keep it." She looked up at Corin. Her eyes in the candlelight were very dark. "He left it there for you to find. So you'd know he knew. And so you'd know he let you go."
"Or so I'd think that," said Corin. "And be frightened. And make a mistake."
Lyra was quiet.
"Yes," she said at last. "Or that."
Nobody said anything for a while. One of the children by the far brazier began to murmur in her sleep, in an old man's voice, about the price of herring. Doctor Silas got up, and went to her, and knelt, and put his hand on her forehead.
"There was something else," said Corin.
They looked at him.
"Before he went," said Corin. "He told them there was another call. On the way home. He said, An old gentleman who will talk in gin shops. About ladies, and flowers, and things he saw in the dark." He stopped. "He said, Our friend does so hate talk. It makes her anxious."
"Her," said Oren.
"Her."
Lyra's face had gone white. Whiter than before. She knew. He could see that she knew. Lilies. A woman in white. A carriage on Monday at noon.
"And on the way back," said Corin, "on the river road. By the bridge at the end of the coal wharf, where the lane goes up the hill. At a quarter to twelve." He looked down at the tea in his hands. It had stopped steaming. "I heard three knocks."
Nobody spoke.
Corin put the cup down on the step. He opened the Ledger again. Not at the back. At the front. At the very first pages, from nearly two weeks ago, from a morning he did not remember, in his own quick, untidy hand. He turned the pages slowly in the candlelight, one after another, looking for something. He did not know what. Only that it was there. That he had written it. That something in what the pale man had said had touched it, the way a finger touches a bell, and it was still ringing, very faintly, somewhere at the back of him.
And there it was.
Wick, rag-picker. Blue smoke rising, fifth bell. Tall figure, long coat, pale, catching it in a jar. Smelled of flowers. Funeral flowers. Said "the tide weighs it."
He sat and looked at it.
Things he saw in the dark.
An old man on a crate in a lane behind a printing house. Small and bent and wrapped in rags, with small bright frightened eyes. Who came down the lane every morning at the fifth bell, before the boys from Cinder Lane, to pick the spoiled paper and sell it to the fish men. Who had seen a tall woman in a long coat standing over a dying man. Who had smelled her flowers. Who had heard her speak.
I am right in the head. It's everybody else that's gone wrong.
"Wick," said Corin.
He stood up. The Ledger slid off his knee onto the step. He did not pick it up.
Oren was on his feet too. Faster than a man that size had any right to be. He was between Corin and the stairs, with one big hand flat against Corin's chest.
"No," he said.
"Let me past."
"Where?" said Oren. "Where will you go? Do you know where he sleeps?"
Corin opened his mouth. And shut it.
He did not know. He had never known. Wick was a man on a crate in a lane. A name in a Watch report. A line in the Ledger. He had never asked where the old man went when he was not picking paper. He had never thought to.
"There's a thousand doors on that hill," said Oren. His voice was low and steady. "And the Watch is on every corner of the Ink Quarter tonight looking for her." He did not look at Lyra. He did not need to. "And if those things are still out there, still walking, still smelling for whatever they smell for, and you go running up and down the streets on your own in the dark like a hare—" He stopped. "You heard the knocks at a quarter to twelve. It's past one now. Whatever was going to happen up there has happened. You know that. You know it better than me."
Corin stood with Oren's hand on his chest. He could feel his own heart against it, going hard and fast.
He did know.
He sat down again, slowly, on the cold stone step.
Lyra picked up the Ledger from where it had fallen, and closed it, and put it in his hands. She did not say anything. She sat down beside him again, close, with her shoulder against his. And after a moment, without looking at him, she put her hand on his arm, and left it there.
"At first light," said Oren. "I'll go with you. We'll find someone who knows him. Somebody always knows a man like that." He took his hand away. "At first light."
Corin nodded.
He did not sleep. He sat on the step with the Ledger on his knees and Lyra's hand on his arm, and watched the candles burn down in their bottles, one by one, and listened to the children murmuring in voices that were not their own. And all night, in the back of his head, slow and heavy and patient, something knocked.
Sunday Bells
First light came down the crack in the roof like dirty water.
It was not much. A thin gray line, falling on the stone floor between the pillars, with the brazier smoke turning in it. It touched the edge of a mattress, and a gray blanket, and a small bare foot sticking out from under the blanket. Then it crept along the floor, slowly, as the sky outside got lighter, and touched the bottom step of the crypt stairs, where Corin sat.
He had not moved for hours. His back ached. His legs had gone stiff and cold. His right hand, the one that had been bare on the bars, had swollen a little, red and shiny, and the fingers throbbed when he bent them. Doctor Silas had looked at it in the night and put some grease on it that smelled of goose fat and pine, and wrapped it in a bandage, and said, "You'll keep the fingers. This time." The bandage was gray now with coal dust.
Lyra had fallen asleep against his shoulder around four.
She had not meant to. He could tell. She had been sitting up very straight, with her hand on his arm, and her eyes on the candles, and then her head had begun to drop, a little, and jerk up, and drop again, and at last it had stayed down. On his shoulder. She was still there now. Breathing slowly. Her short dark hair was against his cheek, and it smelled of woodsmoke and carbolic and, very faintly, under all that, of something sweet and green that he did not know the name of. He had not moved, so as not to wake her. That was why his back ached.
Oren was asleep too. Sitting up on a crate by the door with his back against the wall and his sword across his knees and his chin on his chest, snoring softly. He had said he would keep watch. He had kept it until about five.
Only Doctor Silas was awake. He was going slowly along the rows of mattresses with a tin bowl and a cloth, kneeling by each child, wiping faces, lifting heads to give them sips of water. He did not hurry. He did not talk. Once, by Pip's mattress, he stopped for a long time and sat back on his heels and just looked at the drawing on the wall above her. At the gold line, going over the hills.
Then, high above them, the bells began.
Sunday bells. Not the hours. Not the quarters. The long, slow, rolling Sunday peal that the city's churches rang at first light every week, to call the faithful. It started at Saint Agatha's on the hill, deep and slow, and then the others came in one by one, near and far, until the whole sky over the city was ringing like a great bowl that someone had struck. The sound came down through the crack in the roof, and through the stones, and filled the crypt. The old stone coffins in the walls hummed with it.
Lyra woke.
She woke all at once, the way soldiers wake, with no blink and no stretch, sitting up straight with her hand already going to her coat pocket where she kept her little knife. Then she saw where she was. And who she had been sleeping on. And her face went pink, from the collar of her coat to the roots of her hair.
"I didn't—" she said.
"It's all right."
"I wasn't—"
"I know," said Corin. "It's all right."
She looked at him a moment longer. Then she looked away, and rubbed her face with both hands, hard, and stood up, and went to the brazier, and stood with her back to him, warming her hands. He saw that the back of her neck was still pink.
Oren opened his eyes. He did not move anything else. Just his eyes. They went round the crypt, once, slowly. The children. The doctor. Lyra at the brazier. Corin on the step. The door.
"First light," he said.
"First light," said Corin.
And somebody knocked on the door.
It was not a slow knock.
It was not Oren's knock, either. It was not any kind of knock that Corin had heard before. It was fast and hard and rattling and went on and on, bang-bang-bang-bang-bang, like a woodpecker on a tree, or a very small fist that was in a very great hurry and did not care who heard it.
Oren was on his feet with his sword half out.
"Oi!" said a voice through the door. High and sharp and cross. "Oi! Open up! I know you're in there! I can smell the cabbage from the top of the Cut!"
Corin let out his breath.
He knew that voice. Everyone in the Ink Quarter knew that voice. It had been shouting at him, on and off, for most of a week, from rooftops and gutters and chimney pots and the tops of walls, telling him he was too slow, and too old, and too stupid to live.
"It's all right," he said to Oren. "It's Juno."
Oren did not put the sword away. But he went to the door, and drew back the bolts, one, two, and opened it a crack.
Juno came through the crack like a cat through a fence.
She was small and thin and sharp, all elbows and knees, in a huge old man's coat that came down nearly to her ankles, with the sleeves rolled up four times so that her hands could come out. Her face was sharp too. A pointed chin, and a pointed nose, red with cold, and two sharp black eyes that went everywhere at once. Her hair was cropped short as a boy's and stuck up in spikes, and she had a knitted cap jammed down over it, and soot on one cheek, and a long scratch on the back of one hand, fresh, where she had caught it on something. Round her neck, on a piece of dirty string, there was a brass key. It bounced on her chest as she came down the steps.
She was out of breath. She had been running. Corin could see it in the way her chest went up and down, and the steam coming off her coat.
"How did you find us?" he said.
Juno stopped at the bottom of the steps and gave him a look of deep scorn.
"How did I find you," she said. "How did I find you. Saints, listen to him." She pulled off her cap and slapped it against her knee, and a little shower of frost fell off it. "You came home Friday night stinking of that doctor's dog-tonic. You went out Saturday morning stinking of it. Gran said you'd been sleeping somewhere with sick people, because you had that look, and you smelled of carbolic. And there's only one doctor in the Ink Quarter who makes tonic that smells like wet dog, and everybody knows he lives in a hole under a church with no roof at the bottom of the Cut." She looked round the crypt. At the mattresses, the braziers, the children. At the charcoal hills on the wall. At Doctor Silas, kneeling by a mattress with his cloth in his hand. "So I came to the church with no roof at the bottom of the Cut. Didn't I."
"Good morning, Miss Marta," said Doctor Silas, without looking up.
"Morning, Doctor." Juno nodded to him, quite politely. Then she looked back at Corin, and her sharp face changed.
The scorn went out of it. All at once. And underneath it, Corin saw, she was frightened.
She was fourteen. He knew that. It was in the Ledger. Juno Marta. Fourteen. Mrs. Marta's granddaughter. Fast. He had forgotten it, the way he forgot most things, and then read it again, and forgotten it again. She did not look fourteen most of the time. She looked like something that had grown up on the rooftops, out of the soot and the pigeons and the wind, and had never been a child at all.
She looked fourteen now.
"Sergeant Moll came," she said. "To Gran's. Before it was light. He was banging on the door so hard he woke the whole house, and Mr. Dobbs from upstairs came down in his nightshirt with a poker. He wanted you." She swallowed. "He looked bad, Corin. Real bad. His face was all gray, like porridge. He said if anybody knew where you were, they had to find you, quick, and give you this."
She put her scratched hand into the pocket of her huge coat. She took out a piece of paper. Small, and folded once, and torn along one edge, as if it had been pulled out of a notebook in a hurry.
Corin took it.
It was a page from a small black notebook. He had seen that notebook before. In a lane behind a printing house, in the gray before dawn, not two weeks ago, in the thick fingers of a big man with a boiled-ham face and a pipe. There were words on it. Not many. In pencil. In big square capitals, pressed so hard that the point had torn the paper in two places.
WICK. ARCHES UNDER THE DYE-WORKS. COME QUICK. — MOLL.
And under that, smaller, as if he had written it afterward, and had not been sure whether to:
HE IS ONE OF THEM NOW.
Corin did not say anything.
He stood at the bottom of the crypt stairs with the scrap of paper in his bandaged hand, in the thin gray light coming down through the roof, with the Sunday bells still rolling and ringing over the city above him, and he read it again. And again.
Lyra had come from the brazier. She was standing beside him. She read it over his shoulder. He heard her breath go in, short and sharp, and stop.
Oren read it too. He did not say anything either. He put his sword back in its scabbard, slowly, with a long soft scrape of steel, and buckled the strap over it. Then he went and got his coat.
"Three slow," said Corin. His voice sounded odd to him. Very quiet. Very calm. "At a quarter to twelve. Up the hill from the coal wharf. Under the chimney." He folded the paper. "I heard it. I stood in the road and heard it. And I walked away."
"You couldn't have—" said Lyra.
"I know."
"Oren was right. You didn't know where—"
"I know." He put the paper inside the Ledger, carefully, beside the little yellow square. "I know. I know all of that." He closed the Ledger. "It doesn't help."
Juno was looking from one of them to the other with her sharp black eyes.
"One of who?" she said. "Who's Wick? What's he mean, one of them?"
Nobody answered her.
Corin looked across the crypt, at the mattress in the corner. Pip had woken with the bells. She was sitting up under the charcoal hills, with her blanket round her shoulders and her hands lying open in her lap. Looking at nothing. Her face was calm, and gentle, and pleasant. Like a pond on a still day. And from her mouth, very softly, in the dry old voice of a shepherd who had died a long time ago, came words.
—and the sheep did not run. That was the strange thing. They stood in the field and looked up at the gold as if it was the sun. And my father said—
Juno followed his eyes. She looked at Pip for a long moment. At her empty face, and her open hands, and her moving mouth.
And her own face changed again. It went very still. Corin saw her hand come up, without her knowing it, to the brass key on its string round her neck. And her thumb began to rub it. Slowly. Round and round. The way an old man's thumb rubs a key that is not there.
"Oh," she said. Very small. "Like Grandad."
"Like your grandad," said Corin.
Juno stood with her thumb on the key. Then she pulled her cap back on, hard, down over her ears, and her chin came up.
"I'm coming," she said.
"Juno—"
"I'm coming." Her voice cracked on it. She did not seem to notice. "I know the Dye-Works. I know the arches. I know every way in and out of that place, top and bottom, and you don't. You'll go in the front like a cow through a gate, and the Watch'll have you in two minutes, and her too." She jerked her head at Lyra without looking at her. "I'm coming. And you can't stop me, because I'm faster than you, and I'll just follow you anyway, and then I'll be on my own, and Gran'll kill you."
Oren, at the door, buttoning his coat, made a small sound that might have been a laugh.
"She's right," he said.
Corin looked at Juno. At her pointed red nose, and her fierce black eyes, and her thumb, still moving, round and round on the brass key.
"All right," he said.
Lyra had already gone to get her coat.
"No," said Corin. "Not you."
She turned round.
"The broadsheets," said Corin. "The Watch. Thorne heiress fled. Your face on every corner. Juno's right. It's two minutes." He said it as gently as he could. "And she—the one who sent them—she'll be waiting for you to come out. That's the whole point. That's what all this is for."
Lyra stood very still with her coat in her hands.
He thought she would argue. She had the look. Her chin had come up, the way Juno's had, and her dark eyes had gone hard and bright, and he could see the words forming in her mouth.
Then she looked past him. At Pip, under the charcoal hills, murmuring about sheep.
And she put the coat down.
"Bring me everything," she said. Her voice was low and fierce and shaking. "Everything you see. Every word he says. Write it all down."
"I will."
"Every word, Corin."
"I promise."
She nodded, once. She went back to the brazier and stood with her back to them again, quite straight, with her arms folded tight across her chest.
Doctor Silas had got up from his knees. He came to the foot of the stairs, wiping his hands slowly on his cloth, and looked at Corin with his tired, careful eyes.
"If he is what the sergeant says," he said quietly, "don't try to take anything out of him. There'll be nothing there. And whatever was done to him will still be in the air. Fresh. Like smoke after a fire." He paused. "You've had a long night, harvester. And you are not as empty as you look. Don't breathe too deep."
Corin nodded.
Oren opened the door. The cold came in, and the gray light, and the sound of the bells, much louder. Up on the hill, Saint Agatha's had begun the last long round of its peal.
They went up the steps into the morning.
