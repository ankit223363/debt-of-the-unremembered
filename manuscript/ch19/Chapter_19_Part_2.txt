Chapter 19: The Rag-Picker, Part 2 of 5: Eleven
Eleven
The wheels came slowly.
Corin heard them a long time before anything else. A low, steady grinding on the frozen cobbles of the quay, coming from the east, from the left, from the direction of Pike Dock and the long dark river road beyond it. Not fast. Not slow. Exactly the same, turn after turn, the way a mill wheel turns, or a clock. No clatter. No rattle of a loose board or a chain. No creak of an axle that needed grease. Just the grinding, steady and smooth, getting louder.
And under it, another sound.
He did not know what it was at first. A soft, heavy, regular thud. Thud. Thud. Like a great slow heart. Like someone beating a carpet, very far away, with a padded stick. It went with the wheels. Each thud came exactly between two turns of the wheels, as if one were counting time for the other. And it did not come from the road. It came from somewhere inside the sound of the wheels, deep and muffled, the way a heart sounds when you lay your ear against a chest.
He had heard horses on cobbles all his life. Every morning, every night, a hundred times a day. Horses did not sound like that. There were no hooves at all. Horses' hooves went clip-clop, quick and light and uneven, four feet, never quite together. This was one sound, over and over. Flat. Heavy. Even. Thud. And thud.
He thought of the round dark marks on the cobbles at the top of the slag-room steps, and his hands went tight on the bars.
The sound stopped outside the gate.
There was a silence. A long one. Corin counted his heartbeats, because there was nothing else to count. Ten. Twenty. Thirty. Puckett stood in front of the gate in his brown dressing gown and his slippers, with his arms at his sides now, not wrapped round himself anymore, not shivering, quite still, facing the gate like a dog facing a door.
Then, from the other side of the gate, there came a knock.
One.
Not loud. Not a hammering, the way the Watch hammered on doors in the Ink Quarter, with their fists and their truncheons. Not a polite tap either. A single, slow, heavy knock. As if something very large had leaned forward and laid the flat of its hand against the wood, once, with all its weight behind it. The great gate shook in its frame. A little frost fell from the top of it and pattered down onto the cobbles like salt.
Corin did not breathe.
One, he thought. Only one.
Puckett lifted the bar.
He did it the way a man does a thing he has done a hundred times. Without looking. Without hurrying. He put both his small fat hands under the great wooden bar and lifted it up out of its iron brackets, though it must have been twice as heavy as he was, and carried it across to the wall, and leaned it there, carefully, upright, against the bricks. Then he went back to the middle of the gate, and took hold of the iron ring on the right-hand door, and pulled.
The gate swung open.
And the wagon came in.
It was black.
That was the first thing. Not painted black, the way the Ministry carriages were painted, glossy and fine, so that the lamps shone in them. This was a dull black, a dead black, the black of soot and old iron, that took the starlight and gave nothing back. It was long and high and closed. A box on wheels. No windows. No doors that Corin could see, except at the back. No name painted on the side, no number, no sign of the Bank or the Ministry or anyone else. On each corner of the roof, where a lamp ought to hang, there was an iron bracket, and the brackets were empty.
The wheels were very big. Bigger than any cart's. They were iron-rimmed, and their spokes were black too, and they turned slowly over the cobbles of the yard without a sound except that low smooth grinding. They left two dark lines behind them in the frost. Exactly in the old ruts. Exactly.
And there was nothing pulling it.
Corin stared. He looked at the front of the wagon, where the shafts ought to be, and the horse between them. There were shafts. Two long black poles, sticking out in front. And between them, where a horse should be, there was—
Nothing. Just the shafts, and the cobbles, and the dark.
And up on the box at the front, where a driver ought to sit, side by side, sat the two figures.
They were not driving it.
There were no reins. There was no whip. They sat with their long gray hands resting on their knees, quite still, the way two people sit in a church pew, and they did not move at all. And the wagon came. That was all. Slowly and steadily across the cobbles, with its big black wheels turning and the slow thud going on somewhere deep inside it, as if it knew the way by itself.
Thud, went the deep slow heart inside the wagon. Thud.
Corin had seen many things in his life. Most of them he did not remember. But he had written them down, a great many of them, in the Ledger, and he had read about them since, and some of them had been very bad. Men emptied on rubbish heaps. A girl with a gold sky in her eyes. A father who bought back his son, two coppers at a time. He had thought that he was ready.
He was not ready.
They were tall. Rook had said seven feet, and Corin had written it down, 7 ft, and thought he understood it. He had not understood it. Seven feet was taller than the doorway of the slag room. Seven feet was taller than the water butt. Seven feet was so tall that when they came in under the arch of the gate, they had to bend their heads, both of them, at exactly the same moment, by exactly the same amount, like two trees bending in the same wind.
They wore long coats. Gray. Not the gray of the Ministry's coats, which was a clerk's gray, a dusty, tired, everyday gray. This was paler. The gray of fog. Of ash after the fire has gone right out. The coats came down nearly to the cobbles, and they were buttoned to the throat with a row of small dull buttons, and they did not move at all when the figures walked. Not a flap. Not a swing. As if they were made of stone, or of something that only looked like cloth.
And their faces.
Rook had said white eggs, and Corin had written that down too.
They were white. And they were smooth. And they were shaped like eggs, a little longer than a man's head, a little narrower at the chin than at the top. And there was nothing on them. Nothing at all. No eyes. No nose. No mouth. No mark of any kind. In the starlight they had a faint shine, like the inside of a shell, or like old ivory, or like the glasses on the counter in the dark hall. Like something that has been polished for a very long time by a very patient hand.
They came across the yard on their high box, thud and thud, and the cold air around them did not move.
That was the thing Corin noticed most. Afterward, when he wrote it down, it was the thing he underlined. The night was freezing. Everything that breathed, breathed white. Puckett, standing by the open gate, breathed white, in little slow puffs. Corin himself, behind the bars, was breathing white, though he was trying not to breathe at all. The yard was full of breath. Frost on everything. The cold so sharp it hurt.
And the two gray figures came through it, and nothing came out of them.
No cloud. No smoke. Not a wisp. Their white faces went through the freezing air like two stones going through water, and left the air behind them perfectly clear.
Briggs was right, Corin thought. Their breath doesn't smoke.
They have no breath, said Malakor very softly. Be still now. Be still as the coal.
The wagon stopped at the top of the slag-room steps.
It stopped exactly in the ruts. Exactly. Corin could see the wheels sitting in the two long dark lines on the stones, as if they had been poured into them. The two figures stood up on the box at the same moment. They stepped down, one on each side, without holding on to anything, the way a man steps off a low wall. Their feet came down on the cobbles together. Thud. The ground shook a little under Corin's knees, through the coal. And their feet came down on the dark round marks between the ruts. Each foot in its mark. Exactly. They walked round to the back of the wagon, one on each side.
They have done this a hundred times, he thought. And every time exactly the same.
The one on the left reached up and opened the back of the wagon.
It had two doors, like a cupboard. The figure took one in each hand and swung them out, slowly, and they opened without a creak. Inside, Corin could see nothing. Only dark. A deeper dark than the night. And from it, rolling out into the yard, a smell.
He knew it at once. It was the smell of the slag room, but stronger. Much stronger. Ten times. A hundred. Burnt sugar and wet ash and the inside of a bottle, and under it something else. Something older and sharper, like metal, like a coin held in the mouth, like the air before a storm. It came across the cobbles toward him, and through the bars, and into his nose and his throat, and his eyes watered with it.
Old violet, said Malakor. Very old.
The two figures turned from the wagon. They went to the top of the slag-room steps. They stopped there, side by side.
And then they went down.
Corin had wondered how. The steps were steep and narrow, and the doorway at the bottom was low. He had seen dockers bang their heads on that arch. The figures were seven feet tall.
They did not bend. Not their backs, not their knees. They simply went down the steps, one behind the other, upright, and as they went down, they seemed to grow narrower. Not smaller. Narrower. Their shoulders drew in. Their long gray coats hung straighter. Their white egg heads stayed exactly the same, but the rest of them went thin, and thinner, the way a shadow goes thin when the sun moves. And they passed under the brick arch of the doorway without touching it, and were gone.
The slag-room door did not open. Corin was sure of that. He had heard it open every morning for a week. It was a big, heavy, iron-bound door with two great bolts, and it shrieked on its hinges like a pig. He had not heard it now. He had heard nothing.
But they were inside.
He pressed his face harder against the bars. Through the wall beside him, through the brick of the coal shaft, through the painted door behind the mop bucket, he could feel them. Not hear. Feel. The way you feel thunder before it comes. A pressure, low and heavy, in his ears, in his teeth, in the jar against his ribs. The jar's humming had changed. It had gone higher. Thinner. Quicker. Like a frightened animal's heart.
He put his hand over it and pressed.
Down, said Malakor. Put it down. In the coal. Deep.
Corin did not argue. He let go of the bars with his right hand. His fingers had stuck to the iron with the cold, and the glove tore a little as he pulled it free. He reached into his coat, slowly, so slowly, and drew out the bandaged jar. It was warm. Warmer than it had any right to be. He held it for a moment against his chest. Then he leaned down, and pushed it into the loose coal at his knee, and kept pushing, until it was buried up to the neck, and then over the neck, and then he heaped more coal on top of it with his hand, a little at a time, so that it would not rattle.
The humming went faint. Muffled. Like a voice through a pillow.
He put his hand back on the bars.
They came up with the first two crates.
One each. On their right shoulders. Each figure walked up the steep stone steps with a crate on its shoulder and its right hand resting lightly on the top of the crate, the way Felix had walked up the plank with the ice. Except that Felix had been bent under the weight, his knees shaking, his face red, his breath steaming. The figures were not bent at all. They came up the steps as if the crates were made of paper. And as they came up out of the doorway, they grew back. Their shoulders widened. They were seven feet tall again.
The crates were wooden. Plain pale boards, nailed, with rope handles at each end. About as long as a child's coffin. Corin had seen them stacked in the slag room in the day, along the wall behind the last row of shelves, under a tarpaulin, where nobody was supposed to look. He had looked, once. On his second morning. He had lifted the corner of the tarpaulin and seen the black stamp burned into the end of each crate. A closed eye. And under it, two letters.
IV.
The figures walked to the back of the wagon. They lifted the crates off their shoulders and slid them inside, into the dark, without a sound. Then they turned, and went back down the steps, and grew narrow, and were gone.
Two.
Corin counted them in his head. He did not dare move his lips.
They came up again. Two more crates. Into the wagon. Down again.
Four.
Puckett stood by the gate, with the frosty air puffing in and out of his round pink face, and watched nothing.
The figures came up the third time.
The one in front had reached the top step. The one behind was two steps below it. And as the one behind came up, its crate, its pale wooden crate, knocked against the iron rail.
It was a small knock. The kind of knock you would not notice in the day. Wood on iron. Tock. But the rail was old, and rusty, and sharp at the top where a bolt had broken off long ago and never been mended, and it caught the corner of the crate as it went past. Corin heard the wood split. A short dry crack, like a stick breaking under a boot.
And light came out.
Violet.
It came out through the crack in the corner of the crate in a thin bright line, like a knife. Not like the slag on the shelves. Not dull. Not dirty. This was bright, and deep, and cold, and pure, the color of the sky in the very last minute before night, when the sun has gone and the stars have not yet come. It lit the side of the crate. It lit the gray sleeve of the figure carrying it, and the long white smooth curve of its egg head, so that for one moment the white face was violet too, and seemed almost to have an expression, though it had none. It lit the steps. It lit the cobbles. It reached, thin and straight and bright, right across the yard, and touched the side of the water butt beside Corin's face, and the frost on the barrel's iron hoops sparkled violet in it like a hundred tiny stars.
And with the light came a sound.
Corin heard it. He was sure that he heard it. Faint and far away, as if from the bottom of a very deep well. Not a hum, like Pip's jar. Not one note. Many. Hundreds. Thousands. Voices. All speaking at once, very softly, so softly that he could not tell one word from another, the way you hear a crowd in a hall when you are standing outside in the street.
And one word, out of all of them, for just a moment, very clear.
A name.
He did not catch it. It went past him like a bird past a window. Short. A man's name, he thought. Called out by a woman's voice, high and frightened, a very long way away and a very long time ago.
Then the figure put its long gray hand over the crack.
The light went out. The voices went out. The yard was dark again, and silent, and full of frost.
The figure stood still at the top of the steps, with the crate on its shoulder and its hand flat over the split corner. It did not move for a long moment.
Then its head began to turn.
The Man on the Box
It turned slowly.
Not the way a man turns his head, quickly, with a little jerk, when he hears a noise behind him. Not the way a dog turns, or a bird. Slowly. Smoothly. Like the hand of a clock. The long white egg of its face came round, a little at a time, away from the wagon, away from the steps, across the yard. Past the gate. Past Puckett. Past the porter's dark window. And on.
Toward the water butt.
Toward the grating.
Corin's whole body had turned to ice. Not the cold. Something else. Something worse than cold. He could not have moved if the yard had been on fire. He knelt on the coal with his fingers round the bars and his face an inch behind them and he watched the white face come round toward him, and he thought, very clearly, the way you think the last thing before you fall asleep: Library. That has caught fire.
The face stopped.
It was pointing straight at the water butt. At the big round barrel with the long icicle hanging from its tap. At the place on its side where, a moment ago, the thin violet light had touched the frost on the iron hoops and made it sparkle. It was perhaps twelve paces away. It had no eyes. But Corin knew, the way you know when someone is looking at the back of your neck in a crowded room, that it was looking.
And then it put the crate down.
It did it gently. It bent, from the knees, and set the split crate down on the cobbles at the top of the steps, very carefully, as if it were full of eggs. It kept one long gray hand flat over the crack in the corner until the crate was on the ground. Then it took the hand away. The crack was dark now. No light came out of it. The figure straightened up, slowly, to its full height.
And took a step toward the water butt.
Thud.
Corin felt it in the coal under his knees.
Do not move, said Malakor. His voice had gone very small and very far away, like a voice from the bottom of a well. Do not breathe. Do not think. Be coal.
Thud.
It came across the cobbles. Not fast. It did not need to be fast. Its long gray coat did not swing. Its white face did not move from the barrel. Its feet came down on the frozen stones, one, and then the other, and with every step the ground shook a little, and a little more coal slid away behind Corin in the dark with a soft trickling sound, and he could not stop it.
Thud.
He could smell it now. He had not thought it would have a smell. It did. Faint and dry and strange. Like the inside of an old clock. Brass and oil and dust. And under that, very faint, something else, that he could not name at first, and then could. Rain. Rain on a summer road. The smell of a hot day when the first drops come down on the dust.
Somebody's memory. Somebody's summer. Inside it.
Thud.
It stopped at the water butt.
It was so close that Corin could not see its face anymore. It was too tall. He could see only the bottom of its long gray coat, hanging straight and still, a foot from the bars. The hem of it was dark with wet from the cobbles, and there was frost on it, a thin white line of frost all along the bottom edge, that did not melt. Beyond the coat, its feet. Big and round and flat. Not boots. Not shoes. Something smooth and gray, like the bottom of a stone jar.
It stood there.
Corin heard a sound above him. Soft. A long, slow, drawing sound. Like air going into a bellows. Like a man breathing in through his nose, very deep, very slow, to smell a flower.
It has no breath, Malakor had said.
It was not breathing. It was smelling.
The sound went on. And on. Longer than any breath could go. And as it went on, Corin felt something pull. Very faintly. Inside him. In his chest, behind his eyes, at the back of his mouth. The way the tide pulls at your ankles when you stand at the edge of the sea. A little tug. And another. As if something were reaching down through the bars and feeling for the edges of him, very gently, with soft fingers, to see if any of him was loose.
In the coal at his knee, under a hand's depth of black lumps, the jar went quiet.
Not muffled. Quiet. As if it were holding its breath too.
The long gray coat began to bend.
It bent from the top. Corin saw the hem lift a little off the cobbles as the figure leaned forward. Down. Toward the barrel. Toward the gap between the barrel and the wall, where the grating lay in the shadow. He saw the gray sleeves come down. He saw a hand. Long and gray and smooth, with no nails, with fingers that were too long by a joint, and on the tip of each finger, catching the starlight, a tiny bright point of silver, thin as a needle.
Ten of them.
The hand came down toward the bars. Slowly. Slowly. Until it was a foot above them. Six inches. And the white face came down behind it, smooth and blank and shining faintly, like the moon coming up behind a hill, and filled the whole square of the grating, and Corin was looking up into it from an inch away, and there was nothing in it, nothing at all, and he understood for the first time what the word empty meant.
"Leave it," said a voice. "There's a good fellow."
The white face stopped.
It hung there above the grating, an inch from the bars, with the long needled hand beside it. It did not move away. It did not move closer. It simply stopped, the way a clock stops when somebody puts a finger on the pendulum.
"It's only the slag," said the voice. Light and pleasant and a little bored. "The whole yard reeks of it. You'll be sniffing at every drain in the place till morning, and I want my bed."
Footsteps. Real ones. Light and quick and neat, on the cobbles. Somebody had come in through the small door in the gate, behind the wagon, while Corin was looking at the white face. Somebody who walked like a man who had never in his life had to hurry for anything.
"Up you get," said the voice. "Come along. Crate."
The white face rose.
It rose straight up, away from the bars, smoothly, the way it had come down. The hand went with it. The needles of silver went out of the starlight, one by one. The long gray coat straightened. And then the hem of it turned, and the big round feet turned, and the figure walked away across the yard, thud, and thud, back toward the steps and the split crate, as if nothing had happened at all.
Corin's fingers would not let go of the bars.
He did not try to make them. He did not try to do anything. He stayed exactly where he was, and he breathed, very slowly, in and out, through his mouth, so softly that no white came out of him, and he looked through the gap between the barrel and the wall.
And he saw the man.
He was standing in the middle of the yard with his hands in his pockets.
He was not tall. That was the first thing. After the two gray figures, he looked almost small. Slim, and straight, and neat, in a long gray coat that fitted him beautifully, the way only very expensive coats fit, so that it seemed to be part of him. Gray gloves. A gray scarf, soft, tucked into the collar. No hat. His hair was pale. So pale that in the starlight it was almost white, though he was not old. Cut short, and brushed back very neatly from a high pale forehead.
And his face was pale, too. Pale skin. Pale eyebrows. And pale eyes. Corin could not see their color, not in the dark, not from there. But he could see that they were pale. They caught the starlight the way water catches it. Like the eyes of a fish that lives very deep down, where the sun never comes.
Slim. Not tall. Pale. Pale hair, pale skin, pale eyes.
Rook's voice, shaking, in the ticking room under the Tide Hall.
The man took one hand out of his pocket. There was a little tin in it. Flat and round and silver, the size of a big coin. He opened the lid with his thumb, with a tiny click that Corin heard quite clearly across the yard, and he took something out of it, and closed it again, and put it back in his pocket.
A sweet. In a little square of yellow paper.
He unwrapped it, slowly, with the tips of his gray gloves. He put the sweet into his mouth and moved it into his cheek with his tongue. And then, while the two gray figures went down the steps and came up again with the last two crates, he stood in the middle of the yard and folded the yellow paper.
In half.
And in half again.
And again. And again. Very neatly. Very carefully. His head a little on one side, like a man doing a puzzle. Until it was a tiny square, no bigger than a fingernail.
Silas Kross.
Corin did not say it. Not even in his head. He was afraid that if he so much as thought it, the man in the middle of the yard would hear.
The last crates went into the wagon. Six. Corin counted them. The split one went in last, with the gray hand still flat over its corner.
The figures closed the back doors of the wagon. They walked round to the front, one on each side. They stepped up onto the high box, without holding anything, and sat, side by side, with their long hands on their knees. And were still.
Silas Kross did not get in.
He stood beside the wagon a moment, sucking his sweet, looking round the yard. The way a man looks round a room he has just tidied, to see that everything is where it ought to be. At the gate. At the slag-room steps. At the porter's window. At Puckett, still standing by the open gate in his brown dressing gown, breathing little white puffs, with his empty pink face.
"Thank you, Mister Puckett," said Kross pleasantly. "Bed, now. And you won't remember a thing, will you? There's a good fellow."
"No, sir," said Puckett.
His voice was quite ordinary. A little hoarse with sleep. He turned and shuffled back across the cobbles to the little door of the gatehouse, and went in, and Corin heard his slow slippered feet going up the wooden stairs. One step. Stop. One step. Stop. After a while, the porter's window came back. Yellow. The pot on the sill. The edge of the red curtain.
Kross watched it light. He nodded, very slightly, as if it had done what he expected.
Then he turned to the wagon, and looked up at the two white faces on the box.
"One more little call tonight," he said. "On the way home. I'm sorry. I know it's late." He did not sound sorry. "An old gentleman who will talk in gin shops. About things he ought to have forgotten weeks ago. About ladies, and flowers, and things he saw in the dark." He sighed. "And our friend does so hate talk. It makes her anxious. And when she's anxious, she's untidy."
The white faces did not move.
"You have his smell," said Kross. "Don't you. You had it on Thursday." He smiled, a little, with one side of his mouth. "Quietly, please. Don't frighten the neighbors more than you must."
He turned away from the wagon.
And he walked, slowly, with his hands back in his pockets, across the yard.
Toward the water butt.
Corin's heart stopped. He felt it stop. One beat, and then nothing, and then nothing, and then a great hard thump against his ribs, like a fist on a door.
Kross did not look down. He did not look at the barrel or at the gap behind it or at the shadow by the wall. He was looking up at the sky, at the stars, with his pale face lifted and his breath going up white in front of it, like a man taking a last look at the weather before he goes indoors. He stopped beside the water butt. He stood there a moment, sucking his sweet.
Then, without looking down, he took his right hand out of his pocket. And he leaned, very slightly, and set something down on the top bar of the grating. Neatly. Carefully. The way you set a cup down on a saucer.
And he walked away.
Back across the yard. To the small door in the gate. He did not look back. He went out through the little door and pulled it shut behind him, and Corin heard his light, neat, unhurried footsteps on the quay outside, going away to the east.
The wagon followed him.
It turned in the yard, slowly, on its big black wheels, with the two still figures on its box, and went out through the open gate, and the deep slow heart went thud and thud somewhere inside it, and the wheels ground on the cobbles of the quay, getting fainter. To the east. Along the river.
After a long time, Puckett came down again, in his slippers, and shut the gate, and put the bar back across it, and went back up to bed.
Corin counted to a thousand.
He had promised. So he did it. He did it slowly, in his head, with his fingers still round the bars and his eyes on the gate, the way a child counts in a game of hide and seek. One. Two. Three. Every hundred, he stopped and listened. The river. The dog on the far bank. The bells, ringing the quarter past. The tavern on Pike Lane had stopped singing. The city had gone quiet, the way it does after midnight, when even the drunks have gone home.
Nine hundred and ninety-eight. Nine hundred and ninety-nine.
A thousand.
He let go of the bars.
His fingers would not open properly. He had to pull them, one at a time, off the iron. The glove of his right hand had frozen to the bar and stayed there, a little piece of black wool, when he pulled his hand away. His fingers were white. He could not feel them at all.
He looked at the top bar of the grating.
It was there. Sitting in the frost, on the iron, exactly in the middle, where Kross had put it. So small that if he had not known, he would have taken it for a bit of leaf, or a flake of rust.
A tiny square of yellow waxed paper. Folded in half. And in half again. And again, and again, and again. Until it was no bigger than a fingernail.
He reached up through the bars with his numb white fingers, and took it, and held it in his palm.
It was quite dry. Quite neat. Not a crease out of place. Somebody had folded it with great care, and set it down with great care, exactly where a man crouching under a grating, looking up, would see it.
Or exactly where a tidy man would put a piece of rubbish he did not want to drop on the ground.
Corin did not know which.
He knelt on the coal in the dark for a long time, holding it, and he did not know which, and he thought that perhaps that was the point.
Boy, said Malakor at last. Very quietly. Get the jar. And go.
