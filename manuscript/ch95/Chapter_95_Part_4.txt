Chapter 95: The Raid on the Arches, Part 4 of 5: The River Ledge
The River Ledge
The night outside the drain was empty.
Juno lay in the mouth of it for a long time, under the sacking, with only her eyes out, and looked and listened. Nothing. No lamps. No boots. No voices. The moon had gone over toward the west and the shadow of the vat had swung round and grown longer, and lay across the platform now in a great black curve. The river went past, black and silver, talking to itself. Somewhere far off, across the water, a dog barked twice and stopped.
She crawled out.
She stood up, behind the vat, in the shadow, and her legs nearly would not hold her. She had been bent double in the drain for so long that her back had forgotten how to be straight. She stood with her hand on the cold stone of the vat and let it remember.
The box was in the snow at her feet, under the sacking. The inks. The needles. The drawings Vespera had chosen. She could not carry it. It was too heavy, and too big, and it would clink at every step, and she had a long way to go.
She could not leave it in the snow, either.
She knelt down again and pushed it back into the drain. All the way in, as far as her arm would reach. Into the dark. And pulled the sacking in after it, over it. And then she found the square stone that closed the drain, the one Vespera had pushed sideways and in that morning, with her two blue hands. It was leaning against the back of the vat. Juno put her shoulder to it and pushed it back. Sideways, and then in. It went with a long grinding sound that seemed loud enough to wake the whole river. And then it was shut, and the back of the vat was only stone again, and nobody would ever know.
She stood up.
She went to the end of the platform, in the shadow, past the place where Brin had stood. She did not look at the snow there. She did not look up at the blue wall, at the dark line going up it, ring by ring. She looked at the river, and at the edge of the platform, and at the narrow ledge of stone that went on from the end of it, just above the water, under the overhang of the next warehouse. The river path. Where the bargemen walked when they were hauling.
There was no snow on it. Vespera had been right. The warehouses leaned out over it, and the quays above leaned out over it, and the snow had never got in.
Juno stepped down onto it.
She walked.
She went west, back toward the city, the way they had come that morning. It seemed a hundred years since that morning. The ledge was narrow, and wet, and slippery with green weed and ice, and there was no rail, and the river was right there at her right hand, black, a step away, moving. She kept her left hand flat on the wall. She did not hurry. She had walked fourteen miles on frozen feet. She could walk a river ledge in the dark.
The coat dragged at the backs of her knees. She could feel the thing sewn into the hem. She had not felt it all day. She felt it now, at every step. A little stiffness. A little weight. Against the backs of her legs.
And the bottle in her inside pocket, against her ribs. Warm.
She passed under the Cut Bridge, where the Mill Cut came out into the river through its low arch, and had to wade, because the ledge stopped. The water came up over her boots, over the straw stuffed in them, over her ankles, cold as knives. She did not stop. She came out the other side and climbed back onto the ledge and went on, with her feet squelching and the cold going up her legs.
She passed the boatyards, black and silent, with the hulls of half-built barges drawn up on the slips like the skeletons of whales.
She passed the Fish Stairs, going up on her left in the dark, wide and hollow, with the rusty rail.
And there, at the top of the Fish Stairs, on the quay, she saw lamps.
She stopped. She pressed herself flat against the wall under the overhang of the quay and looked up.
They were going along the quay. West. The same way she was going, ahead of her, up there on the cobbles. Slowly. Five or six blue lamps on poles, swinging. And men under them, walking. And in the middle of them, a cart. A closed one. Black. With a cover tied down over a frame, and a single horse, and a man on the box.
No blue hand on this one. Plain black. Like a coal cart. Like a hearse.
And walking beside it, at the back, on the side nearest the river, a tall thin man in a long gray coat. Not riding. Walking. With his hands in his pockets and his head bent.
They went along the quay, west, toward the old part of the river, past the fish market, past the net lofts. Toward where the quays went round in a long curve and the old buildings were, very old, black, the oldest in the city, with their feet in the water. Toward a big square black one with a stone eagle over its river door, that had been the Customs House once, a long time ago, when ships came up the river from the sea.
Juno watched them go until the lamps were very small.
Then she watched until they were gone.
She did not cry. She had thought she would. She found that she could not. There was something in her too big and too cold for crying. It sat in her chest like a stone at the bottom of a river.
She turned round and went on west.
Under the Corn Bridge, where the people slept in the arches, wrapped in rags. Past the old man with the blue letters on his hands, still asleep, with his mouth open. She stopped by him. Just for a moment. She did not know why. She looked at his hands, crossed on his chest. At the faded blue letters on the backs of them. She could not read them in the dark.
She took off one of her three shawls. The gray one. The thinnest. She laid it over him. Over his hands.
He did not wake.
She went on.
The river bent. The quays went round with it. The houses on the far bank changed, got smaller and meaner, and the smell changed too, got sharper, a smell like old leather and something worse under it. The tanneries. And ahead of her, low over the water, black against the lamp-glow of the city, she could see a bridge.
Not a fine one. Not like Lantern Bridge, with its white arches and its blue house-shaped lamps. An old one, low and humped, of black stone, with five arches. And under the arches, at the near end, where the bridge came down to the bank, something built in. A long low shape of wood and stone, wedged in under the first arch, with a sagging roof. With no light in it at all.
The Tanners' Bridge.
And under it, the old rope store.
Juno stood on the river ledge and looked at it.
You'll know her. She's got a face like a burned loaf and a tongue like a razor, and she'll tell you to go away. Don't go away.
She went toward it.
Auntie Sorrel
The rope store was built right in under the first arch of the Tanners' Bridge, where the arch came down to the bank, so that the black stone of the bridge was its roof, and its back wall, and half its sides. The rest was wood. Old planks, gray, warped, nailed on anyhow, with tar in the cracks. There was one small window, shuttered. There was one door, low and heavy, made of the same gray planks, with a rope for a handle. And over the door, nailed to the planks, so old and so rusted that it had gone the color of the wood, there was a horseshoe.
There was no light. There was no smoke. It looked as if nobody had been in it for years.
Juno stood in front of the door on the river ledge, with the water slapping at the stones behind her and her wet feet freezing in her boots, and looked at it.
She knocked.
Not the knock. She did not know if Sorrel knew the knock. She knocked the way you knock on a door you have never knocked on before. Three times. With her fist. Not loudly.
Nothing.
She knocked again.
Nothing.
She stood there. The cold went up through her legs. The coat dragged at the backs of her knees.
Then she put her mouth close to the gray planks, to a crack between two of them, and said, very low:
"Granny Sedge says the blue one's back."
The silence on the other side of the door changed.
Juno did not know how she knew. Nothing moved. No sound. But the quiet in there was different. Before, it had been the quiet of an empty place. Now it was the quiet of a place where somebody is holding their breath.
She waited.
Then a bolt went back. Slowly. Rusty. And another. And the door opened. A crack. A hand's width. And no more.
There was dark behind it. And a smell. Tar, and old rope, and a stove that had been put out, and something sharp underneath, like ink, like the blue arch, only fainter.
And a face.
It was high up. Higher than Juno had expected. A tall woman, then. Old. With a long jaw and a long nose and white hair scraped back so tight that it pulled at the corners of her eyes. And down the whole of the right side of her face, from the hairline to the jaw, there was a scar.
Juno had never seen a scar like it. It was not a line. It was a shape. A great shiny patch of skin, smooth and pulled tight and puckered at the edges, pinker than the rest of her face, and darker in places, as if it had been melted and had set again. It went over the cheekbone and round the eye, so that the eye on that side was pulled down a little at the corner, and the eyebrow was gone. It had a shape. Juno could not tell what shape. Not in the dark, through a crack in a door. Something with points. Like a leaf, or a star, or a hand.
She thought: a face like a burned loaf.
She thought it was not kind. She thought it was true.
The woman looked down at her through the crack. At her face. At her coat. At her wet boots.
"Go away," she said.
Her voice was low and dry and very sharp. Like a razor. Like a rope going through your hands too fast.
Juno did not go away.
"Are you Sorrel?" she said.
"I'm nobody. There's nobody here. It's a rope store. Go home, girl, before you freeze, and tell Granny Sedge she's an old fool who can't keep her mouth shut." The door began to close.
Juno put her hand on it.
Not to push. Just to put her hand there. Flat. On the gray planks.
"She said you'd say that," she said. "Vespera did. She said you'd tell me to go away. She said not to."
The door stopped.
The woman looked at her through the crack for a long time. Her one good eye, the left one, was very dark and very bright. The other, the pulled-down one, hardly moved at all.
"She said," said the woman slowly. "Did she."
"She said to show you the lining."
Juno did not wait to be told. She turned round, on the ledge, with her back to the door. She reached behind her, and took hold of the hem of the coat, the brown coat, too long in the arms, with the patch on the elbow. And she turned it up. Inside out. At the back. So that the lining showed. The old brown cotton, thin and patched. And the seam at the bottom, sewn shut in small, tight, even stitches, in blue thread. And above the seam, on the lining, in the same blue, very small and very neat, five letters.
She held it up behind her, to the crack in the door, as high as she could.
She could not see the woman's face. She could only hear.
And for a long, long time, she heard nothing at all.
Then the door opened. All the way. And a hand came out of the dark and took hold of the hem of the coat, gently, out of Juno's fingers. A long thin old hand, with knotted knuckles and short square nails. And on the back of it, very faint, the way they had been on the old man's hands under the Corn Bridge, letters. Blue ones. Inked into the skin.
The hand held the lining. A thumb moved over it. Slowly. Over the five blue letters. One at a time. The way you would touch a name cut in a stone.
S. O. R. R. E. L.
"That's her stitch," said the woman. Very quietly. All the sharpness gone. "That's her stitch. Nobody else in this city sews a back-stitch that small. Nobody ever did." Her thumb stopped. "She taught me, and I never could."
Juno turned round.
The woman was standing in the doorway now. Very tall, and very thin, in a long dark dress with an apron over it, and a man's coat over that, too big. She was still holding the hem of Juno's coat. She was looking down at it. At the blue letters. And her face, in the moonlight off the river, with the great shiny scar down one side of it, was wet.
"When?" she said.
"Tonight. An hour ago. Two." Juno's voice shook. She made it stop. "The gray man. Kross. He came with gray coats and a smith. They broke the grille. She stayed in her chair so I could get out the back. Down the drain. With the coat." She swallowed. "They took her to the Customs House. In a black cart. He walked beside it."
Sorrel shut her eyes. The good one and the pulled-down one together.
"And the big one?" she said. "Granny Sedge said there was a big one with her. An Ashborn."
"Brin. He fought them. He didn't hurt them, not really. One of them cut him. Here." Juno touched her own side. "He climbed the wall. All the way up. And went over the roofs. I don't know where."
Sorrel opened her eyes.
She looked at Juno for a long time.
Then she let go of the hem of the coat. And put her long thin hand, with the blue letters on the back of it, on top of Juno's head. Just for a moment. The way Vespera had, pushing her down into the dark.
"You'd better come in," she said. "Before you freeze to my step and I have to chip you off in the spring."
Juno laughed. She did not mean to. It came out of her short and cracked and wet, because it was exactly what Vespera had said on the forty-sixth step of Saint Bride's Lane, the night she came, with her shawl blowing off her ragged gray head.
Sorrel did not ask why she laughed. She seemed to know.
She stood aside, and Juno went in.
It was dark inside, and cold, and smelled of tar and rope. There were coils of old rope everywhere, piled up against the walls, as high as Juno's head, gray and stiff, like sleeping snakes. And at the back, under the black stone curve of the bridge, there was a little iron stove, cold. And beside it, sitting very still on a coil of rope, there were two people. A man and a woman. Old. Holding hands. Looking at her with frightened eyes.
"Binders," said Sorrel, shutting the door behind them and pushing the bolts across. "What's left. Hiding in a rope store like rats. Waiting for the lamps." She said it without any feeling at all. "There were more of us here last week."
Juno did not say anything.
She put her hand inside her coat. Into the deep inside pocket, against her ribs. And took out the bottle. The black one. With the thread tied round its cork. Warm.
She held it out.
"She said to give you this," she said. "With the coat. She said it's the last of the old black. She said to use it on something that matters."
Sorrel looked at the bottle.
She did not take it at once. She stood in the dark by the bolted door and looked at it, in Juno's hand, for a long time.
Then she took it. In both her hands. And held it against her chest. Over her heart.
"Sentimental old fool," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle.
"She said you'd say that," said Juno. "She said to tell you you're a worse one."
Sorrel made a sound.
It was a laugh. And it was not. It was both. It came out of her all at once, in the dark rope store, under the black stone of the Tanners' Bridge, with the river going past outside. A great cracked sound, like something breaking. And she put her long hand with the blue letters over her mouth, and her shoulders shook, and the two old people on the coil of rope by the stove looked at each other and then at the floor.
Juno stood there in her wet boots and her brown coat and waited.
At last Sorrel took her hand away from her mouth. She wiped her face with the back of it. The good side, and then the scarred side, carefully, the way you would wipe a window.
Then she looked down at Juno.
"Well," she said. In her ordinary sharp voice again. Like a razor. Like a rope. "Take that coat off, girl. Slowly. And give it here. And light that stove. There's a flint on the shelf."
"Why?"
"Because there are sixty-three names sewn into your hem," said Auntie Sorrel, "and half of them don't know yet that the needle's gone." She held out her hand for the coat. "And somebody's going to have to tell them."
The Thread
The stove took a long time to light. The flint was old and the tinder was damp and Juno's hands would not stop shaking. Sorrel did not help her. She stood by the bolted door with the brown coat over her arm and the black bottle held against her chest and watched, and did not say anything, and Juno thought at first that she was cruel, and then understood that she was not. She was letting Juno do something. Anything. With her hands.
On the ninth strike the tinder caught. A little red worm of light, and smoke. Juno blew on it, very softly, the way she had seen Mrs. Doyle blow on the brazier in the crypt. And the worm grew, and became a flame, and the flame caught on the shavings, and the shavings on the sticks, and in a little while there was a fire in the iron belly of the stove, crackling, and the red light came out of its open door onto the coils of gray rope and the black stone of the bridge overhead and the faces of the two old people sitting by it.
The old man was small and brown and bald, with enormous hands. His forearms were covered in pictures. Ships. A great many ships. Juno could see them in the red light, going up both his arms under the rolled-back sleeves, one above another, every one different. Full-rigged ships and fishing boats and a little rowing boat with two people in it. The old woman beside him was smaller still, and had no pictures that Juno could see. Only her hands, which were blue at the fingertips. Not all the way, like Vespera's. Just the tips. As if she had dipped them in ink, once, a long time ago, and it had never quite come off.
"Hatch," said Sorrel, nodding at the old man. "He did the docks, forty years. Ships for sailors, so they'd know their way home. And Liss. His wife. She mixed for him. And for me." She looked at the two of them. "They're on the list. The Office's list. Number twelve and thirteen, last Tuesday. They had a room over the chandler's in Rope Lane and a man came with a paper and knocked on the door and Liss went out the back window in her nightdress with Hatch behind her, and that's the last the Office has seen of them."
Hatch looked at Juno with small bright frightened eyes. He did not say anything.
Liss smiled at her. A little. Shakily.
"Sit," said Sorrel. "By the stove. Get your feet warm. Take those boots off."
Juno sat on a coil of rope by the stove and took her boots off. The straw in them was soaked and black. Her stockings were soaked. Her feet were white and wrinkled and so cold she could not feel them. Liss reached out without a word and took one of Juno's feet in her two small blue-tipped hands, and began to rub it. Gently. Between her palms. The way you would rub a child's hands on a winter morning.
Juno sat very still and let her.
Sorrel sat down on the other side of the stove, on an upturned crate, with the coat across her knees.
She took a pair of scissors out of her apron pocket. Ordinary ones. Black iron, with no birds on them. She turned the coat inside out across her lap and found the seam at the back of the hem. The one in blue thread. And she looked at it for a long time, in the red light of the stove, without cutting it.
"I could leave it," she said. Not to anybody. "Leave it sewn. It's the last thing she'll ever stitch, very likely. I could leave it just as it is."
Nobody said anything.
Then Sorrel put the point of the scissors to the first blue stitch. And cut it.
She cut them one at a time. Very carefully. Not tearing. Each little blue stitch snipped and drawn out with the point of the scissors and laid on her knee, on her dark apron, in a row. Juno watched her do it. It took a long time. When she had done, there was a little row of blue thread on her apron, like a line of writing. And she put the scissors down, and slid two fingers into the open seam, between the lining and the cloth, and drew out the silk.
It was still rolled. Tight. Finger-thin. Tied with its own blue thread.
Sorrel held it in her palm and looked at it.
"Sixty-three," she said.
She untied it. She unrolled it across her knees, over the brown coat, in the red light. The tiny writing, black and blue, line after line after line, like the grain in wood.
She began to read.
She read aloud. Not loudly. Low, and steady, and slow, the way Juno's mother read the Saints' names on a feast day. A name. And then a pause. And in the pause, sometimes, Hatch or Liss would say something. Or Sorrel herself would. Very short.
"Old Mab. Of the Ink Quarter."
"Dead," said Liss. "Ten winters."
"Fennick. The sailor."
"Dead," said Hatch. His voice was a whisper. "Drowned. Off the Salt Quay."
"Ada Hollis. Of the Paint Rooms."
Sorrel stopped on that one. She did not say anything. Neither did Hatch or Liss. After a while she went on.
"Marjory Kell. The third arch."
"The pens," said Juno.
They all looked at her.
"The pens," she said again. "At the Bank. Saturday. The step women said. Her and her boy. Tom."
Sorrel looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked back down at the silk.
"The pens," she said, and went on.
Juno listened. She sat on the coil of rope with her bare feet in Liss's lap and listened to the names. All sixty-three. It took a long time. And after every name, in the pause, there was a word. Dead. Gone. The pens. Hiding. Gone over the river. Gone to her sister's in the fens. Nobody knows. And once, twice, three times, Here. From Hatch. From Liss. From Sorrel herself, on her own name, which came near the end, in Vespera's small sharp crooked hand: Sorrel. Of the Tanners' Bridge. Can do names, and grief, and the deep work. Taught by me.
"Here," said Sorrel, very quietly.
And then the last name.
Sorrel looked at it for a long time before she read it.
"Vespera," she said. "Of the Dye-Works. The last arch. The needle."
Nobody said anything.
The stove crackled. Outside, under the arch of the bridge, the river went past, slapping at the stones.
"Taken," said Juno at last. Because somebody had to.
Sorrel nodded slowly.
She rolled the silk up again. Tight. Tied it with the blue thread. And held it in her closed hand, against her apron, over the little row of cut blue stitches.
"Twenty-one living," she said. "Out of sixty-three. That's what's left of us. Twenty-one. And four of those in the pens, and three gone out of the city, and the rest in holes like this one." She looked at the stove. "Waiting for the lamps."
"What will you do?" said Juno.
Sorrel did not answer at once.
She looked at Juno across the stove. At her wet face and her bare white feet and her shawls, two now. At the brown coat across her own knees, inside out, with its seam open.
"We were girls together," she said. "Her and me. In the Ink Quarter. A hundred years ago. Her mother taught us both. At a table under a window. She had a pair of scissors shaped like a bird, her mother. She used to cut our first threads with them." Her voice was very dry. "Vespera was the needle. Even then. Even at ten. Everybody knew it. And I was the thread. The one that follows after. That's what her mother used to say. One of you goes in first, and the other one holds it all together after." She breathed out. "And then she went away for twenty years and left me holding it."
She was quiet.
"What will I do," she said. "I'll do what thread does. I'll go where the needle went." She looked at the silk in her hand. "I'll find the twenty-one. Every one. I'll tell them the needle's gone. And then I'll tell them she left a list, and the list is in my hand, and we're not finished."
Hatch looked at her with his small bright eyes.
"And then?" he said. It was the first thing he had said that was not a single word.
"And then we'll see," said Sorrel. "There's a boy coming. Isn't there, girl? With gray hands. That she went north for."
Juno looked up.
"Corin," she said. "Yes. He's coming. Five days, she said. Six. Slow."
"Then he'd better be quick," said Sorrel.
And Juno, sitting by the stove in the old rope store with her feet in a stranger's lap, began, at last, to cry.
