Chapter 95: The Raid on the Arches, Part 2 of 5: Brin
Brin
The lamp came round the end of the vat on its pole, swinging, throwing blue light across the snow, and under it there was a gray coat. A young one. With his mouth open. And behind him another. And another. Three of them, four, crowding round the end of the vat into the narrow space between the round stone and the river, with their lamps up and their short heavy sticks out. Cleansing Office men. Not soldiers. Not Enforcers. Clerks' men, who carried sticks for show and knocked on doors.
They saw Brin.
They stopped. All at once. The way you stop when you walk round a corner and find a bear.
Brin did not move. He stood between the vat and the wall with the stone spear held low across his body and looked at them. In the blue lamplight his face was very still and very brown and the gray on his cheeks shone like wet slate.
"Leave him," said the man in gray at the foot of the steps.
He did not say it loudly. Juno heard it. She did not think the gray coats did. They were staring at Brin.
Then a big man pushed through from the back of them. Older. With a sergeant's stripe on his sleeve, and a red face, and a long iron bar in his hand that he must have taken from the smith. He looked at Brin. At the spear. At the gray on his face. And his red face went redder, and something came into it that Juno had seen before, on the faces of boys in Tallow Street when they found a cat in a corner.
"Ashborn," said the sergeant. "Saints. A real one. In the city." He grinned. "That's not on any list. That's a bonus." He lifted the bar. "Come on, lads. He's only got a stick with a stone on it."
"Sergeant," said the man in gray. Louder.
The sergeant did not hear, or did not want to. He came forward. Through the snow. Fast. With the iron bar up over his shoulder, ready to swing. And the four younger men came after him, because he was the sergeant, and because there were five of them and only one of the big man, and because they were frightened, and when men are frightened they do what the loudest one does.
Brin moved.
Juno had never seen anything move like that. Not a man. Not an animal. He had been so slow all day. So slow on the steps, so slow kneeling down to Pip in the crypt, so slow with every word. She had thought he was slow all through.
He was not slow at all.
The spear came up and round in a long low sweep, not the point, the butt end, the plain wooden end, and caught the sergeant's iron bar at the top of its swing and took it out of his hands as if he had been holding a feather. The bar went up, turning over and over in the blue light, and came down in the vat. There was a crack of ice. A splash. And the bar was gone.
The sergeant stood with his empty hands up over his shoulder and his mouth open.
Brin turned the spear in his hands. Not quickly. It seemed to Juno that he had all the time in the world. The butt end came round again, low, and hit the sergeant behind both knees at once, and the sergeant's legs went out from under him and he sat down hard in the snow with a sound like a sack of flour dropped off a cart.
The four young men stopped.
One of them, the one with the lamp, swung it. At Brin's head. Wildly. With both hands. The pole came round, and the blue lamp on the end of it, and Brin did not even seem to look. He put up one big hand and caught the pole. Just caught it. In his fist. And held it there. And looked down at the young man on the other end of it, who was pulling and pulling, with his feet slipping in the snow, and not moving it at all.
Then Brin twisted his wrist.
The pole went sideways, and the young man went with it, because he would not let go, and he went over the edge of the platform, into the river. There was a yell and a splash and a great deal of thrashing, and the blue lamp went in with him and hissed and went out, and the river was black again.
"He can't swim!" somebody shouted. "Tam can't swim!"
Brin looked at the black water. At the thrashing. He reached down with the stone spear, the butt end, and held it out over the edge. Into the water. And something grabbed it, splashing, and held on. And Brin pulled. One-handed. Slowly. And the young man came up out of the river on the end of the spear, streaming water, coughing, white as a fish, and Brin dropped him on the platform in the snow like a wet coat, and stepped back.
The young man lay on his back in the snow and coughed up the river.
Nobody else moved.
The other three young men stood with their sticks in their hands and looked at Brin. At the spear. At their sergeant sitting in the snow holding his knees. At the one called Tam, coughing.
Brin looked back at them. His face had not changed at all. He did not seem angry. He did not seem anything. He stood between the vat and the wall with the stone spear low and his feet planted wide, and waited.
And Juno, behind his legs, with her chin in the snow, understood something.
He had not used the point. Not once. The black stone head, chipped like fish scales, sharp enough to shave with. He had swung the butt end every time. He had not hurt anybody. Not really. A man sitting down. A man in the river, and then pulled out again.
He was not fighting them.
He was only standing in the way.
Like a post.
Then there were more lamps.
They came from the front of the arch, round the end of the vat, three, four, five of them, swinging, and men under them. A lot of men. All the gray coats who had been in the arch, or at the grille, or on the snow out front, coming round to see what the shouting was. Ten of them. More. Juno could not count. They crowded into the space at the end of the vat, and the blue light of all their lamps together filled it like water filling a bucket, and they saw Brin, and they saw the sergeant in the snow, and Tam coughing, and they saw the spear.
And one of them, at the back, had a sword.
Juno saw it. A short heavy one. A real one. Not a stick. It flashed in the blue light as he pushed forward through the others.
"Enough!"
The man in gray. At the foot of the steps. He did not shout it. But it went through the cold air like a bell. Every head turned.
All but one.
The man with the sword did not turn. He was already moving. Already past the others, already in the narrow space, low and quick, with the short sword held out in front of him, point first. At Brin's side. At the place under the arm, where the big coat hung open.
Brin turned.
He turned the way a tree turns in the wind. All of him. And the spear came round. But he was turning to keep the stone head away from the man. Even now. Even with a sword coming at him. Turning so that the butt end would come round, not the point.
And so he was a little slow.
The sword went in.
Juno heard it. A sound she would remember for the rest of her life. A short soft sound, like a knife going into a loaf.
Brin did not cry out.
He made a sound. Low. Deep in his chest. A grunt. The sound a man makes when he lifts something too heavy. And the butt end of the spear came round, and hit the man with the sword across the side of the head, and the man went down in the snow and lay still, and the short sword went with him, out of Brin's side, red.
Brin stood there.
He stood very straight, between the vat and the wall, with the spear in one hand and the other hand pressed flat against his side, under his arm. And between his big brown fingers, in the blue light of all the lamps, something dark was coming. Running down. Dripping onto the snow.
Juno did not make a sound. She did not know how she did not. She lay in the mouth of the drain with her face in the snow and her hands over her mouth and her whole body shaking, and did not make a sound, because his boot was against her elbow still, pressing. Stay down.
"Nobody," said the man in gray at the foot of the steps, "touches him again."
He had not raised his voice. He did not need to. The gray coats had gone very still.
"He is not on any list," said the man in gray. "He is not why we are here. He is a guest of this city, from over the Glass, and he has broken no law that I know of but standing in the snow." He looked at the man lying in the snow with the red sword. "Who is that?"
"Voss, Inquisitor," somebody said. "From the Long Office. He's—he came along. For the—he likes to come along."
"Take him to the Ministry. Put him in Room Nine. I'll see him in the morning." The man in gray did not look at Voss again. He looked at Brin. "Let the Ashborn go."
Over the Roofs
Nobody moved to stop him.
They stood in the blue light of their lamps, all of them, in the narrow space between the vat and the river, and looked at Brin. At the spear. At the big brown hand pressed flat to his side, with the dark coming through the fingers. At the snow under him, where it was going from white to something else, in a widening round stain, like ink on blotting paper.
Brin looked back at them.
Then he looked at the man in gray. For a long moment. Over their heads.
And he said something. In the Ashborn tongue. Low. Two or three words.
The man in gray answered. In the same tongue. One word.
Brin turned.
He did not turn toward the gray coats, or the steps, or the river. He turned toward the wall. The blue brick wall of the last arch, going up beside him into the dark, to the parapet of the Weavers' Road far above, with its little bare tree bent over against the moon.
Juno looked up at it from the snow. It was very high. Three times as high as a man. More. Straight up. Blue-black brick, old and rough, with frost on it.
There were rings in it.
She had not seen them before. She saw them now, in the blue lamplight and the moonlight together. Iron rings. Old ones. Rusty. Set into the brick, one above another, going up. The dyers must have used them once, for ropes, for hauling their great pots of indigo up from the river to the road. Some of them were broken. Some were nearly rusted through. But they went up, one after another, all the way, to just under the parapet.
Brin put the stone spear into his left hand, with the hand that was pressed to his side. Holding both. The spear, and the wound. He reached up with his right hand and took hold of the first ring.
And climbed.
He climbed slowly. That was the worst of it. Juno lay in the mouth of the drain and watched him, and every movement was slow, and she could see what each one cost. He reached up with his right hand to the next ring, and hung there, and found a toe-hold in the brick with his boot, and pulled himself up. And stopped. And breathed. And reached up again. And all the time his left arm was clamped against his side with the spear held along it, and the dark was coming down the blue brick under him in a thin line, from ring to ring.
The gray coats watched him.
Nobody threw anything. Nobody shouted. Nobody went for the steps to meet him at the top. They stood in the snow at the foot of the wall with their lamps up, all of them, the sergeant still sitting holding his knees, Tam still coughing, and watched the huge Ashborn climb the blue wall of the arch in the moonlight, slowly, ring by ring, bleeding.
The man in gray at the foot of the steps did not watch.
Juno saw that. He had turned away. He was looking at the river. At the black water going past. With his bare right hand at his side, and his face very still.
Brin was halfway up.
A ring broke.
Juno heard it. A small dry crack, like a twig. And she saw his right hand come away from the wall with the ring still in it, rusted through, and he swayed out from the brick, backward, over the snow, over the lamps, over all their upturned faces, with nothing holding him but the toes of one boot.
Somebody gasped.
Then his left hand came away from his side. The hand with the spear in it. And the spear went up, and the stone head of it went into the brick, between two bricks, into the old soft mortar, and stuck there. And held. And Brin hung from it, one-handed, with his body against the wall again, and the dark running freely now from his side, down the brick, down his leg, dripping off the toe of his boot into the snow far below.
He hung there for a long time.
Then he reached up with his right hand, past the spear, and found the next ring. And pulled.
The spear came out of the brick with a scrape. He held it against his side again. And went on.
Juno found she was crying. Without any sound. With her face in the snow. She did not know when she had started.
He reached the top.
She saw his big hand come up over the edge of the parapet, by the little bare tree. And his other hand, with the spear. And she saw him pull himself up and over, slowly, slowly, in one long heave, like a seal getting out of the water onto a rock. And then he was on the Weavers' Road. A huge dark shape against the moon, on his knees by the parapet, by the little tree.
He did not look down.
He got up. Slowly. With one hand on the parapet. He stood there for a moment, swaying, in the moonlight, at the top of the wall. And then he turned and went away from the edge, out of sight, across the Weavers' Road.
On the far side of the road, Juno knew, there was a gap. And then the roofs of the warehouses along Tanner Street. Slate roofs, steep, with snow on them, and chimneys, going away into the dark in a long jumbled line. Somebody could get across them, if they were brave. Or desperate. From roof to roof, over the gaps, all the way along to the boatyards and the Cut and beyond.
She heard, very faintly, from up there, a slither of snow on slates. And then a thump. And then nothing.
And then, a long way off, so far she was not sure she had heard it at all, another slither, and another thump.
And then nothing at all.
The gray coats stood at the foot of the wall with their lamps and looked up at the empty parapet and the little bare tree.
On the blue brick, from the bottom to the top, ring by ring, there was a dark line. Going up. Like a seam.
"Saints," said somebody, very quietly.
The man in gray turned round from the river.
He looked at the dark line on the wall. He did not look at it for long.
"Get Voss to the Ministry," he said. "Get the boy out of his wet things before he freezes. Sergeant, get up. Your knees are not broken. I heard them. They're only bruised." His voice was quite flat and quite ordinary. "And bring the smith's sack round to the front. The rest of you, with me."
Nobody argued.
The lamps went back round the end of the vat, swinging, one after another, and the boots with them, and the voices, low now. The sergeant got up, holding his knees, swearing under his breath. Two men lifted Voss by the arms and dragged him, his heels making two long lines in the snow. Somebody put a coat round Tam.
And the man in gray went last.
He went past the end of the vat, past the place where Brin had stood, past the round dark stain in the snow. And as he went past the back of the vat, he did not look down at the mouth of the drain. He did not look at the box in the snow beside it.
But he stopped.
Just for a moment. Beside the box. As if to fix his glove, except that his right hand had no glove on it. And he bent down. And picked up the sacking that lay on the snow, the sacking Brin's spear had been wrapped in. And dropped it. Over the box. Over the dark square of the drain mouth. Over Juno's face.
And went on.
Juno lay under the sacking in the dark, with the snow on her cheek, and the smell of wet hemp and old indigo in her nose, and listened to his boots go away round the end of the vat, crunch, and crunch, toward the front of the arch, toward the broken grille, toward the old woman in her chair.
The Smith
She should have gone then.
She knew it, lying under the sacking with the snow melting against her cheek. It was what Vespera had told her. Out the drain, and along the river path, under the quays, where there's no snow at all. And you don't look back. The gray coats were all round at the front of the arch. Nobody was at the back. Nobody was watching the river. She could crawl out from under the sacking, and along the edge of the platform in the shadow of the vat, and down onto the river ledge at the end of it, and be gone, before anybody came round again.
She did not go.
She lay under the sacking and listened.
She could hear them in the arch. Through the stone of the vat, through the brick, through the drain. Muffled. Far off, and very near. Boots on the old gray boards. A lot of them. And breaking. Glass, mostly. A great deal of glass. Bottles going off the shelves one after another, and smashing, and men laughing. Wood. A shelf coming down. A heavy sound, like a sack of coal tipped out. The smith's sack.
And voices. Excited. Loud.
"Look at this. Look at this lot. Pictures. Hands with pictures on."
"There's a skull. A skull, with a bottle in it. Saints."
"Leave that. Leave it. Get the papers."
"What do we want with—"
"Everything. The Office wants everything. Every bit of paper. In the sack."
And under them, under all of it, nothing. No sound at all. Not one word.
Vespera.
She was in there. Juno knew she was in there. Sitting in her chair, in the middle of the room, over the trapdoor, with her blue hands in her lap. While they broke her bottles and tore her drawings off the walls and pushed them into the smith's sack. While they laughed at her skull. Not saying anything. Not one word.
Juno put her hands over her ears.
She took them away again.
Then she heard him.
The man in gray. She knew his voice now. She would always know it. Quiet and flat and ordinary. He did not raise it. He did not need to. When he spoke, everything else stopped.
"Out."
The breaking stopped. The laughing stopped.
"Inquisitor?"
"Out. All of you. Wait on the snow at the front. With the lamps."
"But the—we haven't finished the—the Office wants every—"
"The Office will have every bit of paper in this room," said the man in gray. "I'll bring it myself. Out."
There was a silence. Juno could feel it, even through the stone. A heavy, uncertain silence. A lot of men looking at each other.
Then the boots. Going out. Slowly. Crunching on broken glass. One pair after another. Through the broken grille, onto the snow. And a man's voice outside, low, grumbling. And another telling him to shut up.
And then quiet.
Juno lay under the sacking.
She had to go now. She knew that. Everybody was at the front. On the snow, with their lamps. Waiting. Nobody was looking. This was the time. This was the only time.
She thought about Vespera, alone in the blue room, in her chair, with the man in gray.
She thought about the trapdoor. Right under the chair. And the drain going up to it. And the cracks in the old gray boards, round the edges of the trapdoor, where the yellow lamplight had come through when she was down there before.
She thought: nobody will ever know what they said.
That was the thing. That was what decided it. Not courage. She did not feel brave. She felt sick and cold and frightened all the way through, like a stone at the bottom of a river. It was only that she thought: whatever happens in that room, now, between them, nobody will ever know. Nobody will ever write it down. Unless somebody hears it.
Corin wrote everything down. So that he would not lose it.
Somebody should hear it.
Juno pushed the sacking off her face. Very slowly. So that it would not rustle.
The night was very bright and very cold. The moon was high. The back of the vat was in deep black shadow. The river went past at the edge of the platform, black and silver, making its small cold sounds. There was nobody there. Only the stain in the snow where Brin had stood. Only the dark line up the blue wall.
She turned round in the mouth of the drain.
She went back in.
