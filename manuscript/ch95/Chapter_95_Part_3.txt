Chapter 95: The Raid on the Arches, Part 3 of 5: Under the Floor
Under the Floor
The drain was the length of a cart. She had done it three times now. She did it a fourth time, on her hands and knees in the black, very slowly, so slowly that it seemed to take all night. She did not let the coat drag. She held the hem of it up in one hand, bunched, so that it would not scrape on the stone. She put each hand down flat, and then each knee, and waited, and listened, before she moved again. The bottle of ink in her inside pocket pressed against her ribs, warm, heavy. She held her breath until her chest hurt and then let it out a little at a time through her nose.
She came to the turn.
She stopped there, crouched at the bottom of it, with the stone curving up over her head. And looked up.
There was light.
Not much. Yellow. In thin lines. Coming down through the cracks round the edge of the trapdoor board, and through the gaps between the old floorboards round it. The lamp. Vespera's little brass lamp, still on the floor, still burning. And moving across the lines of yellow, every now and then, a shadow. Somebody moving about up there. Slowly.
Juno got her feet under her. She stood up, as far as the turn would let her, bent over, with her back against the stone, and her head just under the boards. And found a crack. At the edge of the trapdoor, on the side nearest the shelves, where two old boards had shrunk apart from each other. It was as wide as a finger. She put her eye to it.
She could see.
Not everything. The crack was low. Right down at the level of the floor. So she saw the room the way a mouse would see it. Boards. Dust. Broken glass, everywhere, glittering in the lamplight, and dark spreading puddles of old ink, black and blue and red, with the glass lying in them. A drawing, torn, face down. The leg of a shelf, broken off.
And very close, right in front of her eye, so close she could have touched it if the board had not been in the way, a chair leg.
Dark wood. Old. Carved at the foot into the shape of a claw holding a ball.
The chair. Over the trapdoor. Just as she had heard.
And beside the chair leg, in the dust, a pair of boots. Small ones. Old. Black leather gone gray and cracked, with the laces tied in double knots. Not moving. Flat on the floor, side by side, the toes together. And the hem of a black cloak, pinned up, just above them.
Vespera.
Juno pressed her eye harder to the crack. If she tilted her head, just a little, she could see further. Up. Past the boots. Past the hem of the cloak. The edge of the seat of the chair, black leather, cracked. And two hands, resting in a lap.
Blue hands.
They were lying quite still. One on top of the other. Palms up. The way she had seen them lie when Vespera was asleep.
She was not asleep. Juno could hear her breathing. Slow, and steady, and a little rough. Awake.
And there was somebody else in the room.
Juno could not see him at first. She could hear him. Footsteps. Very quiet ones, on the broken glass. Going slowly round the room. Stopping. Going on. Stopping. As if somebody were walking round a church, looking at the windows.
Then they came near. And she saw his boots.
Tall boots. Gray. With the mud of the north road brushed off them, but still dull, still marked. And the hem of a long gray coat. They came across her crack, and stopped, on the far side of the chair. Facing it.
And then, slowly, he crouched down.
Juno saw his coat come down, folding. And his knee, on the boards, in the dust, among the broken glass. And then his face.
She had not seen it close. Not really. Not in the moonlight, from ten paces, at the foot of the steps. She saw it now, from a finger's width of crack, in the yellow lamplight, a little below and to one side. As if she were lying on the floor at his feet.
It was a long face. Thin. Gray at the temples. Not handsome, not ugly. Tired. So tired. Lines going down from the corners of the nose to the corners of the mouth, deep, like the cracks in the old leather of the chair. And eyes that were gray, and very still.
He was crouching on the floor beside Vespera's chair, so that his face was lower than hers. Looking up at her.
The way you would crouch beside a very old person in a chair, to talk to them. So that they would not have to look up.
Neither of them said anything for a long time.
The lamp hissed. Out on the snow, beyond the broken grille, beyond the smashed boards, the gray coats were talking among themselves, low, and stamping their feet. A lamp swung. Somebody coughed. Far off, on the river, a boat's bell rang, once, and was quiet.
In the blue room nobody moved.
Juno did not breathe. She stood bent double in the turn of the drain with her eye to the crack and her hands flat on the cold stone on either side of her face, and did not breathe at all.
Then Vespera spoke.
"Well," she said. "Silas Kross."
Her voice was perfectly steady. Low and rough, like a file on wood. The voice she had used to the step women, and to Mrs. Doyle at the vestry door, and to Juno on the floor of this room that afternoon.
"You've got old," she said.
The Needle and the Inquisitor
"So have you," said Kross.
"I was always old. I was born old. My mother said so." Vespera's blue hands did not move in her lap. "You were young, once. I remember it. A long thin boy at the back of the Long Office with his collar too big for him. Writing down everything everybody said." She sniffed. "You've not changed as much as you'd like to think."
Kross did not answer that.
He was looking at her hands. Juno could see where his eyes went. Down, to the blue hands in her lap, and then a little up. To her wrist. To the place where the sleeve of her dress had come back, a little, and the edge of the writing showed. Tiny letters, going round. Blue. Black.
"You've been busy," he said.
"Twenty years is a long time to sit still."
"It's all there? On your arms?"
"Everything I need." Vespera did not look down at her wrist. She did not pull the sleeve over it. "Where nobody can get at it. Not you. Not him. Not anybody, unless they take the arm off." Her mouth did something at one corner. "I don't suppose he'll go as far as that. He's a gentleman. He'll want me in one piece."
Kross was quiet for a moment.
"He wants to talk to you," he said.
"I know what he wants." Vespera's voice did not rise. "He wants the best needle in the city. Isn't that what they say? He's got his rods, and his carts, and his Choir. And now he wants somebody who knows the old work. The deep work. Under the skin." She looked down at him. "He wants somebody to write on children, Silas. And I'm the best there is."
Juno, under the floor, went cold all over.
Kross did not move. His face did not change. But he did not say no, either.
"I don't know what he wants," he said at last. "I know where he wants you taken."
"The Vault."
"The Vault."
"Under the old Customs House." Vespera nodded slowly. "I know it. I knew a woman who went in there once. When I was young. She came out a year later and sat by the river every day till she died, and smiled at the boats." She was quiet. "She'd been the best singer in the Ink Quarter."
Kross looked down at the broken glass on the floor.
"I'm not going to take anything from you," he said. "Not tonight. Not here."
"No," said Vespera. "You'll leave that to him."
Kross looked up at her.
He did not argue. He did not say anything at all. He looked at her for a long moment, in the yellow light of the lamp, with his tired gray face. And then he nodded. Once. Very slightly.
"Yes," he said.
Vespera looked at him. Juno could see her face now, if she tilted her head right back against the stone. A little of it. The underside of her jaw. Her sharp chin. The edge of her mouth.
The mouth had gone very thin.
"Well," said Vespera. "At least you're honest. That's something. That's more than I can say for most of the gray coats in this city." She breathed out. "You always were. Even as a boy. Honest and no use to anybody."
"I've been told."
"I'll tell you again."
The lamp hissed. Out on the snow, somebody laughed, and somebody else hushed him.
"Did you send the lamps slow?" said Vespera. "Tonight? Along the front?"
Kross did not answer.
"And the smith," said Vespera. "With his sack swinging. I could hear him from the river end. Clank, clank. Like a pedlar's cart." She looked down at him. "You sent them like that. So I'd hear."
"I sent them how I sent them."
"So I'd have time."
"So you'd have time," said Kross, "to be sitting in your chair when I came in. With your lamp lit. Like a lady at home." He looked round the wrecked blue room. At the broken shelves, the torn drawings, the ink on the floor. "Instead of being dragged out of a drain by your boots by somebody called Voss."
Vespera did not say anything.
Then Kross did something Juno did not expect.
He looked down. At the floor. At the boards. At the edge of the trapdoor, under the chair, where the cracks were. Right at the crack where Juno's eye was.
She stopped breathing.
He looked at it for as long as it takes to count three. The same as at the back of the vat. Gray eyes. Tired. Very still.
Then he looked back up at Vespera.
"You're quite alone, Madame," he said. "Aren't you."
It was not a question. He said it the way you would say it's snowing. Clearly. A little louder than he had said anything else. As if he wanted to be sure it was heard. By the room. By the men outside. By the floor.
Vespera did not look down.
"Quite alone," she said.
"That's what I'll put in my report," said Kross. "The woman was found alone, in her chair, with her lamp lit. She came quietly." He paused. "Every word true."
"Every word but one," said Vespera.
"Every word," said Kross. "I'll leave out the floor. A report doesn't have to say what the floor was made of."
Vespera's mouth moved at one corner. Juno saw it.
"No," she said. "I suppose it doesn't."
They were quiet for a while.
"The boy," said Vespera at last. "The harvester. Your report said he'd gone over the far side."
"I know what my report said."
"He hasn't."
"I know."
"He's coming here. To the city. Slow. But coming. Five days behind me, six."
"I know that too."
Vespera looked down at him.
"Then why did you write it?" she said. "A lie, in the Archon's own report, read out to the Council. Gone on over the far side. With your name under it." Her voice had gone low. "You know what that costs, in this city. You know better than anybody. Why?"
Kross did not answer for a long time.
He looked at the floor. At the broken glass. At the ink.
"Because someone asked me," he said at last. Very quietly. "A long time ago. To keep counting."
Vespera went very still.
"Counting what?"
"The ones who are taken." Kross's voice was quite flat. "Every one. Their names. Where they went. Who took them. So that one day somebody would know." He breathed out. "And I have. For a long time. And he's one I'm not going to count."
Vespera stared down at him.
"Who asked you?" she said.
Kross did not answer.
He stood up. Slowly. The way he did everything. His knees cracked. He brushed the dust off the knee of his gray trousers with his bare right hand, and a little sliver of glass with it, and looked down at the old woman in the chair.
"It's time, Madame," he said.
The Scissors
Vespera did not get up at once.
She sat in her chair a moment longer, with her blue hands in her lap, and looked round the room. Juno could not see her face. She could see her head turn, slowly, from one side to the other. Taking it in. The broken shelves. The torn drawings. The skull on the floor, on its side, with the bottle gone out of it. The ink in the dust. Forty years.
Then she put her hands on the arms of the chair, on the old worn leather, beside the straps. And pushed.
She got up the way old people get up. A little at a time. Leaning forward first. Then her weight onto her feet. Then her knees straightening. Juno saw the old boots shift on the boards, right in front of her eye, and take the weight, and hold it.
And something fell.
It fell out of the folds of the black cloak as Vespera stood up. Out of a pocket, or a fold of the skirt. Small and bright. It hit the boards with a sharp little clatter, very close, so close that Juno flinched, and slid a little way through the dust and the broken glass, and stopped.
Right in front of the crack.
Right in front of her eye.
The scissors.
The bird ones. Juno knew them at once. Small, and fine, and old. Made of some dark metal, iron perhaps, polished smooth by hands. The handles were the bird's wings, curving out, with little lines cut in them for feathers. And the blades were the beak. Long, and thin, and closed, pointing at her. And at the joint, where the blades crossed, there was a tiny round eye.
They lay in the dust a finger's width from her face, on the other side of the board, looking at her with their tiny round eye.
Vespera did not look down. She did not seem to have noticed.
Kross did.
Juno saw his boots move. Two steps. Across the glass. And then his knee came down again, on the boards, right in front of the crack, so close that she could see the weave of the gray cloth over it, and a thread pulled loose. And his hand came down. His bare right hand. Long, and thin, and pale, with the knuckles standing out. It came down into the dust, among the broken glass, and picked up the scissors.
Juno watched his hand close round them.
He did not get up at once. He knelt there, with the scissors in his hand, and turned them over. Slowly. Looking at them. She could see them in his palm, through the crack, the bird's wings and the long closed beak. He opened them, a little, with his thumb. They opened very smoothly, without a sound, the way old good things do. He closed them again.
"These are good," he said.
Vespera looked down then.
She looked at the scissors in his hand for a long time.
"They were my mother's," she said.
Kross did not say anything.
"She cut my first thread with them," said Vespera. "When I was six. At her table, in the Ink Quarter, under the window. The first thread I ever sewed into skin. Into my own thumb." She held up her left hand. Juno could see it, just. The blue thumb. And on the ball of it, very faint, so faint you would never see it unless you were looking, a tiny mark. A little blue knot. "She cut it for me, and tied it off, and said now you're one of us."
She let her hand down.
Kross stood up. With the scissors.
He looked at her. Then he put them in the pocket of his coat. Not the waistcoat. The coat. The outside pocket, on the right, where his glove was.
"I'll keep them," he said. "Safe."
"You'll keep nothing for me," said Vespera. Quite sharply. "I'm past keeping things for. Give them to somebody who needs to cut something."
Kross looked at her.
"Who?"
"You'll know," said Vespera. "You always know, don't you. That's your trouble."
Kross did not answer that.
He held out his arm. His left one. Crooked at the elbow, the way a gentleman holds out his arm to a lady to take her in to dinner. The long gray sleeve.
Vespera looked at it.
Then she put her blue hand on it. On the gray cloth. Lightly. The way a lady would.
And they stood there for a moment. The tall tired man in gray, and the small old woman in black, arm in arm, in the middle of the wrecked blue room, among the broken glass and the spilled ink and the torn drawings of hands. In the yellow lamplight.
Then Kross bent down. And picked up the lamp from the floor.
The little brass one. With the brown glass chimney. He held it up. The light swung across the room, across the blue walls, the empty shelves, the chair with its straps.
He looked at the chair. At the place where it stood. At the floor under it.
And he said, quite quietly, not to Vespera, not to anybody, in the voice of a man talking to an empty room:
"Mind the step. There's ice."
Then he blew out the lamp.
The dark came down all at once.
Juno, under the floor, could not see anything. Not the crack. Not the chair leg. Not her own hands on the stone. Only black. And she heard their feet. His boots and Vespera's old cracked ones. Going across the boards. Slowly. Crunching on the glass. Away from the chair. Toward the front of the arch. Toward the smashed boards, and the broken grille, and the snow, and the lamps.
She heard them stop at the front. She heard men's voices outside go quiet.
And she heard Vespera say something. Clearly. To the men. To the snow. To the whole cold night.
"Well, don't stand there gawping. Have none of you ever seen an old woman before?"
And somebody, outside, laughed. Not unkindly. Surprised.
And then the boots. All of them. A lot of them. Going away along the front of the arches, slowly, crunch and crunch, toward the river end. And the clank of the smith's sack. Heavy now. Full of drawings, and papers, and whatever else they had pushed into it. And the creak of a cart, somewhere, and a horse blowing.
And then, a long way off, and then further off, and then very far, and then nothing.
Juno stood bent over in the turn of the drain, in the pitch dark, with her face against the cold boards and her hands flat on the stone.
She did not cry. She did not do anything. She stood there in the black for a long time, listening to nothing.
Then she whispered it. Out loud. So she would have said it. So it would be somewhere, even if it was only in the dark.
"You're quite alone, Madame. Aren't you."
And: "Quite alone."
And: "Give them to somebody who needs to cut something."
She said them over three times. Every word. Exactly. So she would not lose them.
Then she got down on her hands and knees in the dust, and turned round, and crawled back down the drain toward the river.
B. A. N.
She did not get very far.
Halfway down the drain, where the box had been, where the stone sloped a little, she stopped. Her arms would not go on. They simply stopped, on their own, the way your legs stop at the top of a long hill. And she lay down. On her side, in the dust, in the black, with her knees drawn up inside the brown coat and the bottle of ink pressing into her ribs. And lay there.
She did not mean to. She knew she had to go. She knew the river was out there, and the ledge, and the long way home in the dark. She would go. In a moment. She only had to lie here first, for a little while, in the dark, where nobody could see her, and nobody could ask her anything, and nobody needed her to do anything at all.
It was very quiet in the drain.
She could hear her own heart. She could hear the river, very faint, through the stone. She could smell the blue. The old dry dusty sharp blue smell, and under it the smell of dried flowers. And under that, now, something else. Something new. From the arch above. From the broken bottles. A thick sweet heavy smell, like a hundred different inks all spilled together, running into each other in the dust.
She lay and breathed it.
And she thought about what Vespera had been going to say.
She had not meant to think about it. It came. The way things come in the dark when you are lying still. Vespera's face close to hers over the hole in the floor, with every line in it. Vespera's voice. Low and quick.
Tell him I said not to be stupid. Tell him I said be quick. Tell him I said he has a sister and she's at the—
And then the clang. And the cheer. And the gate. And Vespera had stopped. And looked at the front of the arch. And looked back at Juno. And had not finished.
She's at the—
At the what?
Juno lay in the dark and turned it over.
She did not know Corin had a sister. Nobody had said. Not Vespera, in the crypt, by the brazier, last night. Not anybody. She had thought Corin was like her. Like Gideon. Like the six under the last arch. Somebody with nobody. Who had come to number fourteen Tallow Street with nothing, and paid his rent on the first of the month, and gone up the stairs to his room under the roof, and come down again in the morning with his gloves on.
A sister.
She thought about the inside of Vespera's arm, above the elbow, in the yellow lamplight. The soft pale skin. The space that had been kept clear. And the needle going in and out, very fast, like a bird pecking. And the letters coming.
S. I. S. T. E. R.
And then a gap. And then the needle still going.
B. A. N.
And then Vespera's blue hand, coming down flat over the rest.
B. A. N.
Juno said it, in the dark, under her breath. The way she said the letters on the floor of the crypt with Gideon, with the blue chalk. B. A. N. She said it slowly. She tried what came after.
Band. A band of what? Banner. Like the old torn banner in the colored glass at Saint Bride's, with the saint holding it. Banns. Like the banns at a wedding, that Mrs. Doyle read out in the empty church on Sundays to nobody, from an old book, because she said somebody ought to.
Bank.
She stopped.
She's at the—
Bank.
She lay very still.
She thought about Granny Sedge at the Coal Street pump, that morning, leaning on her brush. And the Auditor sits up all night. At the Bank. Fourth Desk. Up at the top, at the river end. I see her lamp. She never used to.
She thought about the one time she had seen the Auditor. From the Bank steps. A long way off. A woman at a high window, in a green coat, with her hand flat on the glass.
She thought about Corin, once, in the crypt, before he went north. Sitting by the brazier with his gloves on. Saying something about the Auditor. And then stopping. As if he had said something he did not mean to. As if he had touched something hot.
She did not know. She did not know anything. It was only letters. Three of them. On an old woman's arm, in the lamplight, before a hand came down over them. B. A. N. It could be anything. It could be band. It could be banner. It could be a name. Somebody called Bannock, or Bantry. She did not know.
But she lay in the dark in the drain and felt it go into her, the way the cold goes into you, all the way through. And stay.
Tell him he has a sister. And she's at the Bank.
She did not know if it was true.
She knew she would tell him.
She would tell him exactly. The way Vespera had said it, and the way it had stopped, and the letters on the arm, and the hand coming down. All of it. Every word. So he could decide. So he could put it in his book. That was what you did. That was what the books were for.
She said it over, in the dark, three times. Like the other things. So she would not lose it.
He has a sister and she's at the—
S. I. S. T. E. R. B. A. N.
Then she got up on her hands and knees again. Her arms went, this time. And she crawled on, down the drain, toward the gray square at the end, and the river.
