Chapter 95: The Raid on the Arches, Part 5 of 5: Tallow Street at Dawn
Tallow Street at Dawn
Sorrel sent her home before first light.
"Not to the crypt," she said, at the bolted door, with the gray beginning in the sky over the tanneries across the river. "Not yet. Home. To your mother. Your mother will be sitting up." She had sewn the hem of the brown coat shut again in the night, with plain brown thread, so it looked like nothing. The silk was not in it anymore. Juno did not know where it was. She did not ask. "Go home and let her see you. And then go to your crypt and tell them. All of it. Except where you've been tonight."
"Why not that?"
"Because what nobody knows," said Sorrel, "nobody can be made to say." She looked down at Juno with her one good eye. "Not even by a man in gray with his glove off."
Juno went home.
She went by the back ways. Up from the river by the Tanners' Steps, and through the warren of lanes behind the tanneries where the smell made her eyes water, and across Coal Street in the dark, past the pump, where the step women were not yet. And up Pewter Lane, which was the quickest way to Tallow Street from the river. She did not know until she was in it that it was Pewter Lane.
There was a house in Pewter Lane with a black cloth tied to the knocker.
She stopped and looked at it. A narrow house, like all the houses in Pewter Lane, with a green door. And on the green door, on the knocker, a strip of black cloth, tied in a bow. For somebody dead. And a candle in the window, lit, though it was almost morning.
And on the step, in the snow, a little girl's wooden doll. Fallen face down.
Juno did not know whose house it was. She did not know anybody in Pewter Lane. She stood and looked at it for a moment, at the black bow and the candle and the doll, and then she went on, because she was cold, and because her mother was sitting up.
Tallow Street was quiet. Gray. With the snow lying smooth on the cobbles and the roofs, and nobody about. No carts. No step women. Only, at the far end, by the corner, under the blue Ash lamp, a man.
Juno knew him. Everybody in Tallow Street knew him now. A thin man in a brown coat with the collar turned up, who had come in the autumn and taken a room over the pawnbroker's at number nine, and sat at his window all day, and walked up and down the street all night, and never seemed to work. Fenwick, he said his name was. He said he was a clerk between places. Mrs. Gammon, who knew everything, said he was a watcher. A Ministry man. For number fourteen.
He was leaning against the lamp post, with his hands in his pockets, eating something out of a paper. He looked up when Juno came round the corner. He looked at her for a moment. Then he looked away again, and went on eating, as if she were not worth looking at.
Juno went past him to number fourteen.
It was a tall house, old, of dirty yellow brick, with a broken railing and a front door that opened onto the stairs to the lodgers' rooms. Corin's room had been at the top. Under the roof. Empty now. Juno did not look up at it.
She went down the side passage. It smelled of coal and cats. At the end of it, at the back of the house, at street level, there was a purple door.
It opened before she touched it.
Her mother was standing in it.
Mrs. Marta was a big woman, broad and soft, with gray hair coming out of its pins and a face like a kind loaf, and an apron over her dressing gown, and a shawl over that. She had been sitting up. Juno could see it in her face. In the dark under her eyes. In the way she stood in the door, with one hand on the frame, as if she had been standing there a long time, listening for feet in the passage.
She looked at Juno.
At her face. At her wet boots. At the brown coat with its hem sewn up in plain brown thread. At her two shawls, where there had been three.
She did not say anything.
She opened her arms.
And Juno went into them, and her mother's arms came round her, and held on, hard, so hard that it hurt, and Juno did not care. She stood in the purple doorway in her mother's arms with her face in her mother's shawl and the smell of soap and tea and coal smoke and her mother, and behind her mother, in the warm room, all round the walls, she could hear the clocks.
Forty-one of them.
Ticking.
Her father's clocks. That he had mended, all his life, for other people, so that when he died, four winters ago, the room was full of the ones nobody came back for. On the dresser. On the shelves. On the walls, on nails. On the mantelpiece over the range. Big ones and small ones. Brass ones and wooden ones and one shaped like a church. None of them set to the right time. None of them set to the same time as each other. All of them going. Her mother wound them every Sunday, with Juno, one by one. Forty-one.
All ticking.
All but one.
On the mantelpiece, at the end, there was a little black clock. Plain, and square, with a painted bird on its face. A blue bird, faded. It had never gone. Not in Juno's whole life. It said quarter to midnight. It had always said quarter to midnight. Her mother wound it every Sunday with the rest, and it never went, and she never stopped winding it.
Juno looked at it over her mother's shoulder.
Quarter to midnight.
Still.
"Where've you been?" said her mother, into her hair. Very low. "Where've you been, my duck, all night, where've you been?"
Juno did not answer.
What nobody knows, nobody can be made to say.
She held on to her mother in the purple doorway, and over her mother's shoulder she looked out through the passage, at the gray light of Tallow Street. And at the far end, by the corner, under the blue lamp, she could just see him. A thin man in a brown coat, with his collar turned up. Not eating anymore. Standing very still. Looking down the passage at number fourteen.
At her.
She did not look away.
After a while, he did.
Saint Bride's
She went to the crypt at the second bell, when the step women were out with their buckets and the streets were full enough to hide in.
Her mother did not want her to go. Her mother had sat her down at the scrubbed table by the range, among the forty-one clocks, and given her porridge, and dry stockings, and her own old boots, which were too big, stuffed with fresh straw. And then sat across from her with her arms folded and watched her eat. And when Juno got up and put on the brown coat, her mother had not said anything. She had only stood up too, and gone to the purple door, and stood in it.
Then she had turned the collar of the coat down at the back. Where it had stuck up. A little tug. A little pat.
Juno had stood very still and let her.
Now she was on the two hundred and twelve steps of Saint Bride's Lane, in the gray morning, with the snow trodden to slush in the middle and frozen at the sides. Old Tull was not on the landing. It was too early for Old Tull. The pump at the hundredth step had a skin of new ice on its trough, and the lion's mouth had a little white beard of icicles.
She went up past it. Up to the top. Round the side of the church, between the wall and the old graves, past the yew. To the vestry door.
She knocked. Three, and then two.
The door opened at once. Mrs. Doyle was there with her broom. She looked at Juno's face, and her own face changed, and she did not ask anything. She stood aside.
Juno went down the twenty-three steps with her hand on the rope.
They were all there. They were always all there. Doctor Silas at his table, with his cracked spectacles, grinding something in a bowl. Big Gideon against the wall under the word DA, with his hands on his knees. The six children under the last arch, awake, some of them, sitting up in their blankets. Pip with her thumb in her mouth.
And Gideon. By the brazier. Whittling. With the fleet of flat boats along the wall beside him, and a new one in his hands, half-made, pale, and shavings all round his knees.
He did not look up when she came down the stair. He never did.
But his knife stopped.
"Where's Vespera?" he said.
He said it in his flat voice. His ordinary voice. The voice with nothing in it. He did not ask where Brin was. He did not ask where Juno had been all night. He asked where Vespera was, as if he already knew, and only wanted to hear somebody say it.
Juno stood at the bottom of the stair.
She had thought all the way up the two hundred and twelve steps about how she would say it. She had thought she would say it slowly, and carefully, in order, from the beginning. The arch. The inks. The list. The girl on the road. The lamps. She had thought she would sit down by the brazier and tell it properly.
She did not sit down.
"The gray man took her," she said. "Kross. Last night. At the arch. To the Customs House. The Vault."
The crypt went quiet.
Doctor Silas put down his pestle. Very carefully. In the bowl. As if it might break.
Mrs. Doyle, who had come down the stair behind Juno, stopped on the last step.
The children under the arch did not understand. Pip took her thumb out and looked at everybody. Nobody looked back at her.
Big Gideon's eyes moved. From Juno, to his son. And stayed there.
And Gideon sat by the brazier with his knife in one hand and the half-made boat in the other, and did not move at all.
"And Brin?" said Silas at last. Very low.
"He fought them. He didn't hurt them. Not really. A man cut him." Juno touched her side. "Here. With a sword. He climbed the wall. Up to the old road. And went over the roofs." She swallowed. "I don't know where. Nobody followed him. The gray man said let him go."
"The gray man said that?"
"He said Brin wasn't on any list."
Silas looked at her over his cracked spectacles for a long time.
Then he sat down. On the stool behind his table. Slowly. Like a man whose legs have stopped.
"Saints keep her," said Mrs. Doyle, on the bottom step. And made the sign. And then made it again.
Juno looked at Gideon.
He had not moved. He sat by the brazier with his knife and his boat. His pale pointed face did not show anything. It never did. There was nothing in him to show it. Vivienne had taken it, every Tuesday, for four winters, in her cold room. Every warm thing.
But his hands were shaking.
Juno saw it. The knife in his right hand. The little pale boat in his left. Both of them, shaking. Not much. A fine fast tremble, like the hands of an old man. The way they had shaken the day he gave his da back his name, and looked down at them, and said, Why are they doing that?
He was looking at them now. The same way. As if they belonged to somebody else.
"The Customs House," he said.
"The Vault. Under it. Kross said."
Gideon did not say anything for a while.
Then he put down the boat. Very carefully. On the floor beside the others. At the end of the row. Half-made. With its little cabin not yet cut.
And he put down the knife beside it.
And he looked up at Juno. With his pale eyes that never showed anything.
"I can go anywhere," he said. "Without making a sound. You know that. I went into the Bourse. I went into the vaults under it. I went into Vivienne's cold room every Tuesday for four winters, and she never once heard me come in." He said it quite flatly. Not boasting. The way you would say I have two hands. "I can go into the Customs House."
"Gideon—" said Silas.
"I can go in, and find where she is, and come out again. And nobody will know." Gideon looked at his shaking hands. "I don't feel anything. So I won't be frightened. So I won't make a mistake." He looked up. "That's what it's for. Isn't it. Not feeling. That's what it's good for."
Nobody answered him.
Big Gideon, against the wall under the word DA, lifted one huge hand off his knee. Slowly. And laid it on his son's head. The way he did. The one thing he knew how to do. His big hand on the small dark head.
Gideon did not look round.
But his hands stopped shaking.
"Not yet," said Juno.
She did not know where it came from. It came out of her the way go had come out of Vespera. Short and sure.
They all looked at her.
"Not yet," she said again. "Corin's coming. Vespera said. Five days, six. Slow." She looked at Gideon. "Wait for him. Wait till he's here. Then go."
Gideon looked at her for a long time.
"Why?"
Juno thought about it. About a man in gray kneeling in the dust among broken glass. About a voice in the dark saying You're quite alone, Madame. About a drain, and three letters on an arm, and a hand coming down.
"Because he'll need to know," she said. "Where she is. And he'll want to be the one who's there when you come back and say it."
Gideon was quiet.
Then he picked up his knife again. And the half-made boat. And went back to whittling. Slowly. Carefully. The little cabin at the back. One shaving at a time.
"Five days," he said. Not looking up. "Then I go."
"Five days," said Juno.
And she sat down at last by the brazier, on the brick floor, in her brown coat, beside him. And held her hands out to the coals. And told them all of it, from the beginning, slowly, in order, the way she had meant to. The arch. The inks. The girl on the road. The lamps. Brin on the wall, ring by ring.
Everything except the rope store.
Everything except the letters on the arm.
The Wall
That night, when the children were asleep and Doctor Silas had gone up the stair to sit in the cold church and say whatever he said to the Saints in there, Juno got the blue chalk out of the tin.
Gideon watched her.
He was sitting where he always sat, by the brazier, with the new boat in his hands. Finished now. With its little cabin at the back, and its flat front, for pushing. He had set it on the floor at the end of the row and then picked it up again, and he was holding it, not doing anything with it. Just holding it. Turning it over, slowly, in his gray cut-down gloves.
Juno went to the wall.
The long curved brick wall of the crypt, red and old, with the chalk on it. The words. White, and blue. Big and crooked. LYRA. READ. V. DA. SAUL, with its two hard lines under it. And low down, between Big Gideon's boots, where he sat asleep now with his chin on his chest, GID.
She stood and looked at them.
They had all been written by somebody who could not write very well. She knew that now. She had not known it before. Before, they had just been words, the words on the wall, the way the saints in the colored glass at the top of the church were just the saints. Now she could read them. And she could see that whoever wrote LYRA had pressed so hard that the chalk had broken in the middle of the R. And whoever wrote SAUL had been crying. You could see it in the shape of the letters. They went downhill.
She found a place. Near the end of the wall. By the last arch, where the children slept. High enough that nobody would rub it out with their back, sitting against it.
She held up the chalk.
And stopped.
She knew the letters. Every one. From A to the end. Lyra had pointed to them in this room with her finger, and said their names, and Juno had said them back. She had been teaching them to Gideon on the floor with this same blue chalk for weeks. She knew them.
She had never written anybody's name on a wall.
Gideon had got up. She had not heard him. She never did. He was standing beside her, with the little boat in one hand, looking at the wall where she was looking.
"What are you going to write?" he said.
"Her name."
Gideon looked at the bare red brick.
"I don't know her letters," he said. "I've only got nine."
"I know them."
She put the chalk to the brick.
V.
That one was easy. It was on the wall already, further along, small, from before. Somebody's. Corin's, she thought. She had never asked. Two lines, coming together at the bottom. Like a bird's beak. Like the blades of a pair of scissors, closed.
E.
Three lines out from one line down. Like a comb with three teeth.
"That one's in my nine," said Gideon.
"I know."
S.
She did it badly. The top curve was too big and the bottom curve was too small, like a snake that had eaten something. She looked at it. She did not rub it out.
P.
E.
"That's the same as the second one," said Gideon.
"Yes."
R.
That one was in his nine too. He did not say so. He watched her make it. She pressed hard on it, without meaning to, the way whoever wrote LYRA had pressed. The chalk did not break.
A.
She stepped back.
It was crooked. All of it. The letters went uphill at the start and downhill at the end and the S was a snake that had eaten something. It was the most badly written word on the whole wall.
It was there.
VESPERA.
Gideon looked at it for a long time.
"What does it say?" he said. "All of it. Together."
Juno looked at him. At his pale pointed face, that never showed anything. He knew what it said. She had told him what she was going to write. He was asking anyway.
She understood that he wanted to hear it said. Out loud. In this room. The way you say the names at a funeral.
"Vespera," she said.
Gideon nodded slowly.
Then he held out his hand. Not the one with the boat. The other one. With the cut-down gray glove on it.
For the chalk.
Juno gave it to him.
He did not write anything. He held the chalk in his gloved fingers for a long moment, looking at the word on the wall. And then he reached up, very carefully, and drew something under it. Small. Under the P and the first E.
It was a boat.
A little flat one. With a cabin at the back. The kind he whittled. The kind that pushed.
He drew it with four lines, and a square for the cabin, and stepped back, and looked at it.
"So she knows where to go," he said.
Juno did not ask what he meant. She did not think he knew either. She thought it was the first thing he had ever drawn that was not a letter, and that it had come out of somewhere in him that Vivienne had not got to, all those Tuesdays, in her cold room. Some small far place. Where a boy had sat on his da's shoulders on the docks and watched the barges go by, flat at the front, pushing.
She took the chalk back. She put it in the tin.
They stood side by side in front of the wall for a while, in the red light of the brazier. Not saying anything.
Then Gideon went back to his place by the fire, and sat down, and put the finished boat at the end of the row with the others. Carefully. Straight. So it was the same as the rest.
And Juno lay down on her straw under the second arch with the book under her and her coat over her, and looked at the wall until her eyes closed.
VESPERA.
And under it, small, a boat.
