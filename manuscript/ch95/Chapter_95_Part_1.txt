Chapter 95: The Raid on the Arches, Part 1 of 5: The Coat
The Coat
Juno did not take the coat.
She stood in the middle of the blue arch, in the yellow light of the little brass lamp, in her three shawls, and looked at it. At the brown coat in Vespera's two blue hands. Too long in the arms. With the patch on the elbow, in a different brown, that her mother had sewn on in the autumn by the light of the range. With the thing sewn into the hem, against the backs of the knees, that nobody would ever feel.
She did not take it, because she knew what it meant if she did.
"No," she said.
Vespera's face did not change.
"Juno."
"No. I'm not going. Not without you." Her voice came out high and thin and much too loud. She could hear it go up into the curve of the blue roof and come back. She did not care. "You can come too. You can fit. You came in. You can go out. We can both go, now, down the drain, before they get here, and along the river, and—"
"And they'll follow the drain," said Vespera. "In about as long as it takes to say it. They'll break that grille and come in and find the hole in the floor, and the board up, and the dust on the board with your knees in it, and they'll put a lamp down the hole, and two men after it." She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. "And they'll come out the other end behind the vat, where Brin is. And then they'll have Brin, and me, and you. And the coat."
Juno stared at her.
"If I'm here," said Vespera, "sitting in my chair, with my lamp lit, waiting for them, they won't look for a hole in the floor. Why would they? They've found what they came for." Her sharp black eyes did not leave Juno's. "They'll take me, and they'll be pleased with themselves, and they'll go home. And you'll be under the quays by then. And Brin will be gone over the roofs. And the coat will be wherever you're going."
"But they'll take you."
"They were always going to take me." Vespera said it quite simply. "Since the day I walked back into this city. Since before. I've known it for twenty years, girl. I put it on my arm."
Outside, behind the gray boards, behind the grille, along the front of the arches, the boots were coming. Crunch, and crunch. And the clank of the smith's sack. Nearer. Juno could hear them quite clearly now. She could hear men's voices, low. She could see, through the finger's-width gap at the top of the blanket, a faint blue light on the brick at the top of the arch. Moving. Getting stronger.
"Put on your coat," said Vespera again.
Juno's eyes had gone hot and blurred. She wiped them on her sleeve, hard.
"You promised," said Vespera. "In this room. On that floor. You said it back to me. If anything happens to you, I take the coat to Sorrel." She held the coat out a little further. "Something is happening. Right now. Put it on."
Juno put out her hands.
She took the coat.
It was warm. Vespera had been under it, asleep, for an hour, and it had kept her warmth. It smelled of her now. Of ink, and dust, and something like dried flowers. Juno put her arms into the sleeves. Too long. They came down over her hands. She did up the buttons, all of them, from the bottom to the top, with her fingers shaking so that she missed two and had to do them again.
Vespera watched her do it.
Then she reached out and turned the collar down at the back, where it had stuck up. The way Juno's mother did, every morning, at the purple door, before Juno went out. A little tug. A little pat.
"There," said Vespera.
Juno could not speak.
Vespera looked at her for a moment longer. Then she bent down. Slowly, the way old people bend. And picked something up off the three-legged table by the chair. And held it out.
It was the bottle. The black one. The forty-year ink, from the bottom of the box, that had felt warm in Juno's hand, like something alive and sleeping. Vespera had put the cork back in it after she used it, and tied a bit of thread round the cork so it would not come out.
"Take this," she said. "Give it to Sorrel with the coat. Tell her it's the last of the old black. Tell her I said to use it on something that matters." Her mouth did something at one corner. "She'll say I'm a sentimental old fool. Tell her I said she's a worse one."
Juno took the bottle. She put it in the deep inside pocket of the coat, where it lay against her ribs, heavy and warm.
"And the box," she said. "The inks. And the needles. And the drawings. We packed them. They're in the drain."
"Leave them."
"But we came for them!"
"We came for the list," said Vespera. "The rest was an excuse. An old woman wanting her things." She looked round the blue arch, once, quickly. At the shelves. At the drawings on the walls. At the chair. "They can have the rest. It's only ink. I can make more ink."
The boots stopped.
Just outside. Right outside the grille. Juno heard them stop all at once, the way soldiers stop. And a man's voice, giving an order she could not make out. And then, very close, so close it made her jump, a sound like a bell. Iron on iron. A great heavy clang.
The smith. On the padlock.
The arch rang with it. Dust came down from the curve of the roof in a soft gray rain. The bottles on the shelves chattered against each other.
Clang.
"Go," said Vespera.
Juno went.
The Drain
The hole in the floor was where it had been, by the shelves, with its board lifted and leaning against the wall. A black square in the dusty gray boards. Cold air came up out of it.
Juno got down on her knees beside it.
She looked back.
Vespera had not moved. She was standing in the middle of the blue arch beside the old chair, small and straight, with her ragged gray hair and her blue hands at her sides, in the yellow light of the lamp on the floor. She was not looking at the grille, where the clanging came from. She was not looking at the door. She was looking at Juno.
Clang.
The whole arch shook. A bottle fell off a high shelf and broke on the boards with a small bright sound. Ink spread from it, black, slowly, into the dust.
"Go on," said Vespera. "In you go. Feet first, this time, so you can put the board back over yourself if you have to. No. I'll do the board."
Juno put her feet into the hole. Her legs. She sat on the edge with her legs dangling in the cold dark and looked up.
"Vespera—"
"Don't say anything," said Vespera. "Whatever it is. Don't. Save it." She came across the boards, quickly, without a sound, and knelt down by the hole. Her face was very close. Juno could see every line in it. "Say it to Corin, if you like. When you see him. Tell him I said not to be stupid. Tell him I said be quick." She paused. "Tell him I said he has a sister and she's at the—"
CLANG.
Something gave, outside. A great tearing screech of iron. And a cheer, short, from a lot of men's throats. And the rattle of a gate, swinging.
Vespera stopped.
She looked toward the front of the arch. At the gray boards behind the blanket. At the blue light coming through the gap at the top, much stronger now, flickering, moving. Then she looked back at Juno.
And she did not finish what she had been going to say.
"Down," she said. "Now."
She put her blue hand flat on the top of Juno's head. Not hard. And pushed.
Juno went down into the drain.
She went down all at once, sliding, the stone scraping her back through the coat, and landed at the bottom of the turn on her hands and knees in the dust, in the pitch dark. She heard the board come down over her head. A scrape. A soft thud. And the square of yellow light above her was gone, and there was nothing. Only the black. And the cold. And the smell of blue.
She stayed where she was, on her hands and knees, and could not move.
Above her, through the board, through the floor, she heard Vespera stand up. She heard her feet go across the boards, quick and light. And then she heard a sound she did not understand at first.
A long, slow shriek. Wood on wood. Heavy. Grinding.
The chair.
Vespera was dragging the chair. The big old chair, with its high back and its straps, that they had dragged off the trapdoor together that morning, both of them, pulling with all their strength, with their heels dug in. That had been too heavy for the two of them. Vespera was dragging it back. On her own. Juno could hear her breathing. Short and hard and harsh, like a saw. And the chair coming, a little at a time, in jerks. Shriek. Stop. Shriek. Stop. Across the boards. Toward the trapdoor. Toward the place right over Juno's head.
Shriek.
And stop.
And the creak of the old leather, very close above her. As somebody sat down in it.
Juno put both her hands over her mouth.
She understood. The chair was over the trapdoor now. Its four legs round the square of the board. Its seat over it. Vespera in the seat. Nobody coming into the arch would see the board at all. They would see an old woman sitting in a chair. In her own chair. In the middle of her own room. With her lamp lit. Waiting.
They won't look for a hole in the floor. Why would they?
Juno knelt in the black dust at the bottom of the turn with her hands over her mouth and her shoulders shaking.
Then she heard the boards.
Not the floor. The front. The gray wooden wall behind the grille. Something hit it, outside. Hard. Something heavy, swung. An axe, or a bar. The whole arch boomed like a drum. And again. And on the third blow there was a crack, sharp, like a branch breaking, and a man shouted, and the blue light came in.
She could see it. Even from down here. A faint blue glow came in round the edges of the board over her head, through the cracks in the floor. Lamplight. Ash light. Cold and blue and moving.
And voices. A lot of them. Coming in.
Juno took her hands off her mouth.
She turned round in the drain. On her hands and knees. Toward the straight part. Toward the far end, the square of gray night, the back of the vat, the snow, the river. And Brin.
She began to crawl.
It was the length of a cart. She had done it twice today. It had seemed short both times. It seemed to go on forever now. The stone was rough under her hands and freezing and gritty with dust. The coat dragged. The bottle of ink knocked against her ribs at every movement, heavy, warm, like a second heart. She could hear her own breathing, loud in the stone. She could hear, behind her and above her, getting fainter as she went, the voices in the arch. And boots on the boards. And a man laughing.
Halfway, she hit the box.
The wooden box, with the inks in it, wrapped in straw. They had pushed it into the drain that afternoon, ready, before the whistle. It filled the drain from side to side. She could not get past it.
She put her shoulder to it and pushed.
It did not move. It was heavy, full of glass, and the drain went a little uphill here, and her knees slipped in the dust. She pushed again. It moved a hand's width, scraping. She pushed again. Another hand's width. Her shoulder hurt. Her knees hurt. She did not care. She put her head down and pushed with her whole body, the way she had pushed her mother's mangle when she was small and wanted to help, and the box went scraping and rattling along the stone ahead of her into the dark, a hand's width at a time, and she went after it.
She could see the end now. A square of gray. Less dark than the dark.
And she could hear, from out there, beyond the square of gray, from the snow and the river and the night, a sound she had never heard before.
It was a voice. A deep one. Very deep. Saying something, slowly, in words she did not know. Not shouting. Not loud. Saying it the way you would say a prayer, or the words of a song you had known all your life.
And under it, a long low sound, like wind. Like a sword coming out of a scabbard. Like sacking being unwound from something long, and let fall.
Brin.
Behind the Vat
She pushed the box the last little way and it tipped out of the mouth of the drain onto the snow with a soft thump and a clink of glass. And she put her head out after it, into the night.
The cold hit her face like water.
She was at the back of the vat. Low down, on the platform, with the round stone side of the vat curving up on her right, black and blue in the moonlight, and the blue brick wall of the arch going up on her left, and between them a narrow space of trodden snow, no wider than a doorway. And in that space, with his back to her, filling it from side to side, there was Brin.
He had taken his hood off.
His round shaved head was bare to the cold. The moonlight lay on it, white, and on the gray of his cheeks, smooth and dull. He was standing with his feet wide apart in the snow, very still. And in his two hands, held across his body, there was the thing he had carried all day wrapped in sacking.
The sacking lay at his feet now, in a heap.
It was a spear.
Juno had never seen a spear before. Not a real one. Only in pictures, in the colored glass at Saint Bride's, where the saints held them, very straight and thin and gold. This one was not gold. It was long, taller than Brin, and the shaft was some pale wood gone dark with hands, wound round near the middle with strips of leather. And the head of it was stone. Black stone, long and thin and leaf-shaped, chipped all over its two faces into tiny ridges like the scales of a fish, so that the moonlight ran on it in little lines. It was tied to the shaft with something pale. Gut, or sinew. Wound and wound.
Brin was holding it low, the stone head down near the snow, pointing out past the end of the vat.
And he was singing.
That was the sound she had heard in the drain. Not a prayer. A song. Very low and very deep, in the Ashborn tongue, slow, the same few notes going round and round, the way a wheel goes round. He was not singing loudly. He was hardly singing at all. It was more like breathing, with a tune in it.
Juno did not know what it was. She thought it was something you sang before a thing you might not come back from.
She stayed where she was, half out of the drain, on her elbows in the snow, behind his legs. She did not make a sound. She did not think he knew she was there.
Then, without turning his head, without stopping the song, Brin moved one foot. Back. A little. Until the heel of his big boot was against her elbow. Pressing. Gently.
Stay down.
She stayed down.
She could see past his legs. Not much. A slice of the platform between the end of the vat and the wall. Snow. The edge of the river, black and silver. And the stone steps that came down to the platform from the Weavers' Road above, at the vat end. Old steps, steep, cut into the side of the last pier, with snow on them.
And there was a man on the steps. Coming down.
Not fast. Not slow. One step at a time, with one hand on the wall. A tall thin man in a long gray coat buttoned to the throat, and a gray hat. His right hand was bare. She could see it on the wall, pale in the moonlight, against the blue-black brick.
He came down to the bottom of the steps. Onto the platform. Into the snow. And stopped.
He was looking at Brin.
They were perhaps ten paces apart. The man in gray at the foot of the steps. The huge Ashborn between the vat and the wall, with his stone spear held low.
Neither of them moved.
From round the other side of the vat, at the front of the arch, Juno could hear the gray coats. A lot of them. Voices. Boots on boards. The splintering of more wood. Somebody laughing. A lamp swinging, blue, so that the light went back and forth across the snow at the end of the vat like a pendulum. None of them had come round to the back. None of them had seen. They were all at the front, where the grille was, and the broken boards, and the old woman in her chair.
Only the man in gray had come this way. Down the steps at the vat end. As if he had known exactly where to come.
Brin's song stopped.
The quiet was very loud.
The man in gray looked at Brin for a long time. At the spear. At the gray on his cheeks. And then, very slowly, he lifted his bare right hand, and held it out, palm up, empty. Toward Brin. The way you would hold out your hand to a horse you did not want to frighten.
And he said something.
Juno did not understand it. It was not the southern talk. It was the Ashborn tongue. Slow, and careful, and with the words in odd places, the way somebody speaks a language they learned a long time ago and have not used since. But she knew what one of the words was. She had heard it all day, from Brin. From Vespera, mocking him. This one.
Brin went very still.
He did not answer at once. He stood with the stone spear low, looking at the man in gray. Juno could feel him, through the heel of his boot against her elbow. Like a wall. Like a tree. Not moving at all.
Then he answered. In the same tongue. Short. Three or four words. Very deep.
The man in gray lowered his hand.
He said something else. Shorter. And then, in the southern talk, quite quietly, in a voice Juno had never heard before and knew at once, the way you know a voice you have heard about, he said:
"Go home, big man. There's nothing here for you to carry."
Brin did not move.
"This one stays," he said.
The man in gray looked at him a while longer.
And then his eyes moved. Not much. Not far. Down. Past Brin's legs. To the snow behind Brin's boots. To the back of the vat, low down, where the round stone met the platform. To the square black mouth of the drain. And the box in the snow beside it. And a face, half out of the hole, with snow on its cheek, looking up at him.
Juno stopped breathing.
He looked straight at her. In the moonlight. From ten paces. She saw his eyes. Gray. Tired. Very still.
He looked at her for as long as it takes to count three.
Then he looked away. Back up at Brin's face. As if there had been nothing there at all. As if there had only been snow.
And from round the front of the vat, a voice shouted. A gray coat. Loud and hoarse and pleased with itself.
"Inquisitor! She's here! The old woman, in a chair, she's just sitting here—"
And then another voice. Closer. At the end of the vat. Where the swinging blue lamp was.
"Saints. Sergeant! Sergeant! There's one round the back! A big one! He's got a—"
The lamp came round the end of the vat.
