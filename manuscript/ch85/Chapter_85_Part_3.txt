Chapter 85: Sola's Hands, Part 3 of 5: Four, Two, Nine, Three
Four, Two, Nine, Three
It went up out of him like a fire going up a dry hill.
All of it. All at once. Everything of her that was in him. He saw it go. He could not stop it. He could not even slow it. He could only stand behind his own eyes and watch it go past, the way you stand at a window and watch a house burn.
The valley.
The whole green valley, the way she had seen it every morning of her life, from the top house at Haverstock. The river, slow and brown. The strip fields going down to it, each one a little different green. The orchards on the slopes, white and pink in spring. Old Dunnock's field. The widow Ashby's barley. The mill wheel turning. The smoke going up from the chimneys of Applegarth in the evening, straight up, blue, on a still day.
Gone.
The horse.
Not the iron one. The real one, before. A big gray mare with a white blaze, called something, he never knew what, that she had ridden as a girl and as a woman, whose blanket she had kept, whose shoe hung over the forge in the smithy that had been her house. The warm smell of its neck. The way it put its nose into her pocket for apples.
Gone.
And the iron one too. The great red beast with its riveted plates and its frame of a seat and its hot oily breath, that her smiths had made for her out of plow iron and cart springs, that creaked and ticked and that the children followed everywhere. The feel of it moving under her. The height of it. The sound.
Gone.
Her braid. Long and iron-gray, pinned up under the helmet. The weight of it on her neck. Her mother's hands braiding it when she was small, every morning, on the back step, in the sun.
Gone.
The scar on her jaw. How she got it. A burn. A forge. Her brother's fault, Aldric's, when they were children, and how she had never let him forget it, and how they had laughed about it, after, when they were old.
Gone.
The ditch. The men in the ditch with their pikes. Their faces. Each one. She had known every face. To the ditch! And don't you dare look back for me, because I'll not be there!
Gone.
The road. The long road south, made of light. The charge. The gold pouring out of her chest like burning honey and laying itself down in front of the hooves. The ditch men's faces lifting, not afraid. The last of her going out of her into the light like the last held note of a song.
And her hand. Her free left hand, at the very end. Four fingers. Two. The fold. Three, to the heart.
Gone.
Gone. Gone. Gone.
It all went up out of him, every bit of her, one thing after another, faster and faster, the way the pages of a book go up when you throw the whole book on the fire. And as it went, it went into him. Into his arms. Into his legs. Into his chest. Not strength, this time. Something more than strength. Something he did not have a word for.
Speed.
He was moving.
He did not feel himself move. He did not feel his feet on the glass. He felt the glass go past under him, black and fast, with the drowned village a blur far down. He felt the air on his face like a wind, hot, though there was no wind. He felt the gray hands at the ends of his arms, out in front of him, open now, reaching. He heard the ticking get louder. Louder. Close.
And then he was there.
He did not know how. It had been too far. It had been a few long strides for the iron man to reach the child and a long way more than that for him to reach the iron man, and he had seen it, laid out like a road, the whole of what was going to happen. And it had not happened. He had crossed all that glass in a heartbeat. In less than a heartbeat. In the time it takes to draw one breath, he was behind the iron man.
Its arm was up. Over the child. At the height of her head. Coming down.
And his hands were on its back.
The plate came off. He did not feel it come off. He heard it. The four cracks of the rivets, so close together that they were one sound. The scream of the brass tearing. And his hand was in the wheels, and on the drum, and on the tail of the spring.
And pulled.
The spring came out of it screaming, the loudest of all three, a long shriek of steel that went out across the whole of the silent Eye and came back from nowhere. It lashed out past the child. It went over her head. He saw it go over her head, a dark flat whip, a hand's width above her hair, and he saw her hair move in the wind of it.
And the iron man's arm stopped.
In the air. Over her head. A hand's width from her hair.
And its ticking began to go wrong.
Corin heard it. He was standing right behind it, with the spring lying on the glass beside him and his gray hand still half inside the hole in its back, and he heard it. The ticking did not stop. Not at once. It went on. But it went on slower. And slower. And it began to stumble.
Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick.
Four.
A stop.
Tick. Tick.
Two.
A stop.
Corin's hand came out of the hole in its back. Slowly. He did not take it out. It came out by itself.
Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick.
Nine. He could count nine. He could just hold it. He counted it on the fingers of his gray hand without knowing he did it, each tick a finger, until he ran out of fingers on that hand and went on to the other.
A stop.
Tick. Tick. Tick.
Three.
And then a long stop. A longer one.
And then again. Slower. Fainter.
Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Four. Tick. Tick. Two. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick. Nine. Tick. Tick. Tick.
Three.
And then nothing.
The iron man fell.
Forward. Over the child. And Corin, behind it, without thinking, with whatever was left in his hands, took hold of its back and pulled. Sideways. So that it went down on its face on the black glass beside her, not on her. With a crash that shook the glass under his feet.
And lay there.
The child had stopped singing.
She was standing exactly where she had been, on the black glass, with Oren's coat half off her shoulder and her bare feet and her fists still clenched. Her eyes were still shut. Tight. She had not opened them. She did not know.
The silence came down over the Eye like snow.
Corin stood beside the fallen iron man and looked at it.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
Witness. Hold. Fold. Home.
The calls. The old rebel signs. Sola's hand, at the very end, on the road of light, making them one after another to the men in the ditch. Oren's drum calls, from the war. Lyra's father, tapping them on the arm of his chair in the dark after he had burned everything. The silent man, knocking them on the iron door every month for two winters, and nobody answering.
And now an iron man, a thing of brass and wheels with nobody inside it at all. Made by somebody, somewhere, who had perhaps once scratched under protest inside its chest. Ticking them out, at the end, as its spring ran down.
He did not know why.
He did not think anybody would ever know why.
He stood there, and he could not think about it, because there was something happening to his face.
The Cold
It started at his shoulder.
He knew the shoulder. He had known it for weeks. On both sides, the left and the right, the gray came up his arms from the black fingertips, over the wrists and the elbows and up the long muscles of the upper arm, and stopped. At the round of the shoulder. In a line. He had seen it at the Edge, washing in the meltwater with his sleeves rolled up. A line at the top of each arm, like the mark the high water leaves on a wall.
It was not stopping now.
He felt it go. On the left side. Not the right. The left. He felt it the way you feel cold water creep up your back when you lower yourself into a river. A cold. Not a pain. A cold so deep and so still that it felt like nothing, like the absence of something, the way the place where a tooth has been feels like nothing. It came up over the round of his left shoulder, under his coat, under his shirt, and kept coming.
Onto his neck.
He stood beside the fallen iron man on the black glass in the bright morning and felt it climb his neck. Slowly. A finger's width at a time. Up the side, under his left ear, past the place behind his left ear where the hair grew thin and where something had been once, and the lamp had taken it. Cold. Up. Over the corner of his jaw.
He put his hand up.
His left hand. He did not decide to. It came up by itself, the way your hand goes to a place that hurts. And his gray fingers touched his own face. His jaw. The left side. Below his ear.
He could not feel them.
He could not feel his fingers on his face. That was not new. He had not been able to feel his fingers on anything for weeks. But he could not feel his face either. He could not feel his fingers touching it, from the inside. He could feel his fingers press, a kind of weight, a kind of pushing, the way you feel somebody lean against a door you are standing behind. But not touch. Not skin on skin. His jaw, where his fingers were, was not there anymore. It was cold, and smooth, and far away, like a stone in a stream.
The cold went on.
Over his jaw. Onto his cheek. Along the bottom of it, toward the front. Toward his mouth.
He felt it reach the corner of his mouth.
And stop.
He stood there with his gray fingers against his gray jaw and waited for it to go on. Into his mouth. Into his words. That's where it goes, Vespera had said, at the bottom of the stair. It's in your mouth, harvester. He stood and waited for it to take his lips, his tongue, the back of his throat. He waited to open his mouth and find nothing there.
It did not go on.
It stopped at the corner of his mouth. At the very edge, where the lips meet. He could feel the line of it there. A line of cold, at the corner, the way you can feel a line of frost on a window with your fingertip, where the warm stops and the cold begins.
He took his hand away from his face.
He looked at it. His gray hand, in the sun. The same as it had been.
He opened his mouth.
"Corin," he said.
His voice came out. Rough. His own. He could feel his lips move round it, most of them. The right side. And the left side, near the corner, a little numb, a little slow, the way your mouth is when you come in from the cold in winter. But it moved. The word came out.
His name.
He said it again, to be sure.
"Corin."
It was there. It was still there.
He did not know how long it would stay there.
And beside him, on the black glass, the child opened her eyes.
She opened them slowly. The way you open your eyes when you have been sure, for a long time, that the thing you were afraid of is standing right in front of you, and you would rather not see it. She opened them a crack. And then wider. And she saw the iron man lying on its face on the glass beside her, a hand's width from her bare feet, with the hole in its back and the spring lying beside it in a long loose curl.
She looked at it for a long time.
Then she looked up. At him. At his face.
And her own face changed.
He did not know what she saw. He did not have a mirror. He would not have one for days. But he saw her see it, and he saw her face go from white to whiter, and her mouth come open, and her eyes go very wide. Not afraid of him. He did not think it was that. Something else. The way a child looks at a grown-up who has come in from outside with blood on his shirt.
"Your face," said Tansy.
Her voice was very small.
"I know," said Corin.
"It's— it's gone gray. It's gone gray, on the side. Like your hands. Like his hands." She did not look at the silent man, who was coming toward them across the glass now, fast, with Kestrel. She looked at Corin. "Does it hurt?"
"No."
"Is it cold?"
He thought about that. "Yes."
Tansy looked at him a moment longer.
Then she did a thing he did not expect. She stepped forward, round the end of the fallen iron man, over the curl of the spring, in her bare feet, with Oren's coat dragging off her shoulder. And she put her hand up. Her small hand, dirty, with bitten nails. And laid it flat on the side of his face. On the gray. On the cold.
He could not feel it.
He could not feel it at all. He saw her hand there, at the edge of his eye, small and pale against the gray of his jaw. He knew it was there. But he could not feel it. It was like watching somebody touch a statue of you.
"It is cold," said Tansy. "It's like the glass at night."
She left her hand there.
And then Kestrel was there, and the silent man, and Kestrel went down on her knees on the glass and took hold of the child, both arms, and pulled her in, hard, against her chest, and the child's hand came away from his face. And Kestrel held her. Did not say anything. Held her, with her shaved head bent over the child's sticking-up hair, and her eyes shut, and her arms round her so tight that the child squeaked.
The silent man stood over them.
He did not touch the child. He did not touch anybody. He stood there with his long arms hanging and the blood drying in his beard, and he looked down at the child in Kestrel's arms. For a long time. And then he looked at Corin.
At his face.
And for a moment something moved behind the silent man's eyes. A long way back. Like somebody in the last room of a big house, lifting their head because they have heard a voice they know.
Then it was gone.
Oren on His Knees
Across the glass, at the dome, Oren was on his knees.
Corin saw him past Kestrel's bent head, past the child in her arms, past the silent man's long legs. Oren was kneeling on the black glass in front of the iron door, where the gold wall had been. On one knee. The way a man kneels when his legs have gone and he will not let the rest of him go down after them. His sword was point-down on the glass in front of him, and both his hands were on the hilt, and he was leaning on it. Like a stick. Like a crutch.
His head was bowed.
Corin had never seen Oren kneel. It was not in the book. He did not think he needed it to be. You could see, looking at him, that Oren was not a man who knelt.
He went to him.
It was a long way. It was not a long way. It was more of his own slow steps than he could count, on a lame leg with no crutch, across the glass, past the kneeling iron man and the first one on its face. He did it slowly. His leg shook. Twice he thought it would fold. It did not. And then he was standing over Oren, and Oren lifted his head.
His face was gray. Not the gray of Corin's hands. The gray of a man who is very tired and very hurt. His beard was wet with sweat. His eyes were red at the rims.
They looked at each other.
Oren looked at Corin's face for a long time. At the left side of it. At the jaw. At the corner of the mouth.
He did not say anything about it.
"You did it, lad," he said instead. Hoarse. "Three. All three. I watched. You did it."
"She did."
"Aye. Well." Oren breathed out through his nose. "You let her."
Corin did not know what to say to that.
"You held the door," he said.
Oren's face did something.
It was very small. Corin almost did not see it. A kind of flinch. Not of the mouth or the eyes. Of the whole face, all at once, for half a breath. The way a man flinches when somebody says the name of somebody he has lost.
Then it was gone.
"I held the—" said Oren. And stopped. His mouth stayed open. He looked at Corin with his mouth open, and Corin saw him reach for it, and not find it. Again. And close his mouth.
"Aye," said Oren. "I held it."
He looked over his shoulder. At the iron thing set into the curve of the dome behind him. The one with the gap down its side. With his own handprint still dark on the carved sun in the middle of it, from two days ago, when he cut his palm and pressed it there and said Aunt. I'm here.
He looked at it for a long time.
"That," he said. Quietly. "I held that."
Corin looked at it too.
"It's a door," he said. He did not mean to. It came out.
"Aye," said Oren. "So you say."
He did not try to say it back.
Corin stood there over him. He did not know what to do. He wanted to put his hand on Oren's shoulder. He did not. His hand was gray and his reach was awake, here in the Eye, and he did not trust it. He stood with his hands at his sides.
And then Oren put out his own hand. His left. The one with the cut across the palm bound up in a strip of cloth. And laid it, for a moment, flat on the back of Corin's gray hand. On the skin. Bare.
Corin felt nothing. He saw it there.
"It's all right, lad," said Oren. "It's only a word. I've a good few left."
And took his hand away.
The Last of the Room
It was quiet inside him.
He noticed that after a while. He was standing on the black glass in front of the dome, a pace from Oren, with two of the fallen iron men lying near him, and far across the glass Kestrel still on her knees holding the child and the silent man standing over them, and the sun on all of it, and he noticed that inside him it was very quiet.
Not the calm. The calm was gone.
He had not felt it go. It had gone with her. With the last of her, with the road of light and the hand making the calls. One moment it had been there, filling him, wide and still and silent, so big that there was no room for anything else. And now it was not. And in the place where it had been there was only him. His own body. His own legs.
His lame leg.
He felt it now. The knee that would not bend. The ache in it, deep, all the way up into the hip. He had not felt it all morning. He had run on it. He had turned on it like a door on a hinge. He had crossed the whole wide glass on it in a heartbeat. And now it was his again. Stiff, and aching, and shaking a little under him, the way it shook at the end of a long day.
He did not have his crutch. He had left it at the bottom of the stair.
He stood on his good leg, mostly. And waited.
Inside, the quiet. It was not the quiet of the Glass, where every voice in him had been held still like a bird in a hand. It was a different quiet. Emptier. The quiet of a room after the furniture has been taken out. You walk into it and your footsteps sound too loud.
He listened to it.
Hask was there. A little. He could feel him. Far back. The three-note hum, not humming, but there, the way a cat is there under a bed, curled up, not making a sound. And Arran. A little. A warmth, small, like a coal under ash. Waiting to laugh.
And behind his ear.
Boy.
The old voice.
It was very small. Smaller than he had ever heard it. Not dry, the way it was dry when the old man was being clever. Not sharp. Thin. Like a voice coming from the bottom of a well, or from the far end of a very long passage, through a door at the end that is almost shut.
Malakor, he thought.
Here. A pause. Still here. Don't fret.
Corin stood on his good leg with the sun on his gray face.
She's gone, he thought.
Yes.
All of her.
Yes. A long pause. Longer than Corin liked. That was the last of the room, boy.
Corin did not answer.
The one where she used to be, said Malakor. Very quietly. That room in you. The one with the door off its hinges. You remember. Hask said it. Broken from the inside. Somebody went out of it in a hurry.
I remember.
She was in it. These last days. Since the horse. She was sitting in it, in the dark, with her hands on her knees, the way she sat at that table. Taking up all the room. And now she's not.
A silence.
It's empty now, said Malakor. Except for me.
Another silence.
And I'm not much.
Corin did not know what to say.
He stood there on the black glass and felt the room inside him. He could feel it. He had always been able to feel it, a little, since Hask touched his chest at Last Thorn. A room with the door off its hinges. Somewhere deep, behind his ear, under everything. He had felt it full, when Lyra was in it, long ago, before he knew her. He had felt Vivienne in it, shouting, for six days, until Hask breathed her out. He had felt Sola in it, these last days, big and silent and still.
Now it was empty.
Almost.
There was the old man. In a corner. Small. Folded up.
And there was something else. Something Corin had not noticed before, because the room had always been full of somebody, and you do not notice the floor of a room when there are people standing on it. Something at the back. Hard. Small. Square. Like a stone in a shoe. Like a fold of paper pressed so flat and so tight that it shone.
The crease.
It was right there. In the empty room. He could feel it, the way you feel a splinter in your palm when you close your hand. He had always known it was there. Malakor had told him. There's a crease, boy. I can't see past it. But he had never felt it so clearly. It had always been under somebody's feet.
Now there was nobody to stand on it but the old man.
And the old man was not much.
Malakor, he thought. Is that—
Not now, said the old voice. Very tired. Not here. Not with all these people about. A pause. Look up, boy. Your girl's coming. And you'll want to be on your feet for her.
Corin looked up.
