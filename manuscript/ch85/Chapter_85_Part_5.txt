Chapter 85: Sola's Hands, Part 5 of 5: Written Down
Written Down
After a while he took the Ledger out.
He did it without thinking. His hand went inside his coat, on the left, where it always went, and found the green book in its oilcloth against his ribs. It was still there. It had been there all morning. Under the coat. Under his shirt. Against the place where her name was written on his skin. It had gone under iron arms and over the black glass and through the screaming of the springs, and it was still there.
He unwrapped it on his knee. Slowly. With the crutch lying across his lap.
He found the page. He found the stub of pencil. He held it in his gray fist.
And he wrote.
He pressed hard. He could not feel how hard. He watched the letters come out of the end of the pencil, gray and crooked and leaning back, going down the page like a man going down a stair in the dark.
WEDNESDAY.
I BURNED ALL OF SOLA.
He stopped. He looked at that line for a long time. It did not look like enough. It did not say the valley or the horse or the braid or the boys in the orchard with the blossom coming down, or Hob in the wet grass. It did not say the hand on his shoulder. It said all. He did not know how else to say it.
He went on.
THE GRAY IS ON MY FACE NOW.
And then, after a long time, below it:
SHE SAID NOTHING. THAT IS HOW I KNEW.
He looked at it.
Then he turned the book round on his knee so that Lyra could read it. The way he always did. So she could check his letters.
She read it.
He watched her read it. Her eyes going along the first line. And the second. And stopping at the third. And staying there.
She did not say anything about the letters.
She did not say anything at all.
And then, very slowly, she put out her left hand. The one that was not bleeding. And laid one finger on the page. Not on him. On the paper. Under the last line. Under the words SHE SAID NOTHING. And left it there.
As if to say, yes.
As if to say, I know.
As if to say, that's right. That's how you knew.
Then she took her finger away, and left a small mark there on the paper, red, from where the blood had run down from her other hand onto this one. A spot. By the N in NOTHING.
She looked at it.
"Leave it there," she said. Very low. The first thing she had said since she came out of the gap. Her voice was rough. "It's mine. My mark."
Corin looked at the red spot by the N.
There was another one like it. Somewhere. Further back in the book. He knew there was. He did not remember it. But he knew it, the way he knew her voice. A red spot. By a letter. On a page that said something about her coming after.
"I'll leave it," he said.
And he wrapped the book up again in its oilcloth and put it back inside his coat, on the left, against his ribs.
"Lyra," he said.
She did not look at him. She was looking across the glass. At the man by the dome. The bearded one. The one she had cut.
"Is he—" said Corin.
"Yes."
She said it quite flatly. The way you say it's raining. She did not take her eyes off the man.
Corin did not know what to say. He did not know what you said. He thought of the book, and all the things in it, and he could not think of a single line in it that would help.
"He was coming at me," said Lyra. Still flat. Still looking. "With his sword. And a stone in his other hand. He was going to throw the stone at the gap. At Vespera, on the stair. I saw him look at it." She was quiet. "So I did it. The way Oren showed me. From the hip. I didn't think. I just did it."
"Yes."
"It's a finishing stroke," said Lyra. "Oren said. In the horse's shadow. He said it's not for fencing. It's for finishing." Her voice cracked, very slightly, on the last word, and then was flat again. "He was right."
Corin looked at her hand. The right one. Held against her chest. The bandage red through, and dripping, slowly, onto the black glass beside her knee. A small red pool, growing.
"Your hand," he said.
She looked down at it as if she had forgotten it was there.
"Oh," she said. "Yes." And then, after a moment: "I couldn't do it with the left. I tried, in the Ridges, with Oren. It doesn't come. It has to be the right. It has to come up from the right hip." She turned the hand over and looked at the palm, at the red bandage. "So I used the right."
"Vespera told you not to."
"I know."
"Does it hurt?"
She thought about that. Then she shook her head.
"Not yet," she said. "It will."
They were quiet.
Across the glass, the men in gray were nearly at their camp now, with the bearded man between them. Corin watched them go. He thought about the young one with the big ears. Are you him? And the big one, kneeling, saying a name.
"Do you know his name?" said Lyra suddenly. "The one I— Do you know what he was called?"
"No."
"No." She was quiet. "I don't either."
She was still looking at the dark smear on the glass where they had dragged him.
"I'd like to know it," she said. "I don't know why. I'd like to know it."
Corin did not say anything. But he took the book out again, the green one, and opened it on his knee, and found the page, and under the last line, under her red mark by the N, he wrote in small crooked capitals:
THE MAN SHE CUT. ASK HIS NAME.
And showed her.
She read it. She did not say anything. But she nodded, once, and something in her face that had been very hard for a long time went, very slightly, less hard.
The Pale One
He looked at the pale one then.
He had not, all morning. Not properly. He had seen him, off to the left, alone, writing. A shape at the edge of the fight. A white coat. He had not looked.
He looked now.
The pale one was standing a long way off across the glass, near where the men in gray had stood at the start. By himself. Not near the camp. Not near Kross. Not near anybody. He stood very straight in his long near-white coat, with his gray hat on and his gray gloves on, and the black book open on one hand, and he was writing in it with the silver pencil. Calmly. As if he were standing in a garden on a summer afternoon, making a note of which roses had come out.
He was not looking at the dead man the others were carrying. He was not looking at the iron men on the glass, broken, his iron men. He was looking at the dome. At the line of Ashborn. At the child in Marrit's arms.
And at Corin.
Their eyes met, across the glass. Corin saw the pale one see him look. And the pale one smiled. A small, kind, pleasant smile. The way a doctor smiles at a patient across a ward.
Then he looked down at his book and went on writing.
Corin's hands began to shake.
He did not know why. He looked down at them. His gray hands, on his knees, on the crutch. They were shaking. Not much. A tremor, small and quick, like the shake of a dog's leg in its sleep. He could see it. He could not feel it. He watched his own gray fingers tremble on the wood of the crutch and did not know what was making them do it.
He was not afraid. He did not think he was afraid. He had been afraid at the bottom of the stair, and on the bench in the night, and Hask's hum had stopped. But he was not afraid now. There was nothing to be afraid of. The iron men were broken. The men in gray were carrying their dead away. The pale one was only a slight man in a white coat, a long way off, with a book.
His hands shook.
He looked at the pale one again. At the black book. At the silver pencil. At the white coat.
At his left hip.
There was a rod there. In a loop of leather on his belt. A silver rod, as long as a forearm, as thick as two fingers. Corin knew rods. He had carried one himself, for years, at the Ministry. He had one now, in his pack, wrapped in a shirt. The old man's rod. The one Malakor had lived in, before there was room in Corin for him.
This one was different.
He could not have said how, at first. It was too far off. And then the sun caught it, as the pale one turned a page, and he saw. The groove. The fine bright spiral that ran round the silver from one end to the other, the way all the rods had it. The rod turns to the right, somebody had told him, once, long ago. Always. Like the sun. So the thing goes in and does not come out.
This one went the other way.
To the left.
His hands shook harder.
He put them flat on his knees and held them there. Pressed them down. The shaking went on under them. Up his arms. Into his shoulders. He did not know what it was. He did not have the memory. There was a hole where it would be, a great dark room in him, and the old man folded up small in a corner of it, and the crease. But his hands had something. The way his feet had the silent man's stride. His hands knew that rod. Or one like it. Or the hand that made it.
And his hands were afraid of it.
Malakor, he thought.
Nothing.
Malakor. That rod. The one on his hip. Why are my hands—
Nothing at all. Not even the feeling of somebody listening. The corner of the room where the old man was folded up was very, very quiet. As if the old man had heard the question and had made himself even smaller, and smaller, so as not to be found.
Corin pressed his hands flat on his knees until the shaking stopped.
It took a long time.
And when he looked up again, the pale one had stopped writing, and had closed his black book, and was walking across the glass. Not toward the camp. Toward Kross.
The Glove
Kross had not moved.
Corin saw that when the men in gray were halfway back to the camp with their dead. He lifted his eyes from them and looked past them, across the glass, and Kross was standing exactly where Corin had seen him standing from the bottom of the stair, through the gap, at the very beginning. A short way back from where the iron men had been. Alone. With his hands at his sides.
He had not moved all morning.
Not when the stones were thrown. Not when the wall went up. Not when the child sang and the iron stopped for one tick. Not when the wall went down, or when Corin went out onto the glass with a dead woman in his hands, or when the springs came out screaming one after another. Not when the big man lifted his carbine at the silent man's back. He had said one word, then, Lyra said afterward. One name. Harrow. And the big man had lowered it. And that was all Kross had done.
He had stood and watched.
And one step behind his left shoulder, the cadet.
She had not moved either. She was standing very straight in her white coat with its silver buttons, with her fair hair coming down out of its pins and her hands at her sides. And she was looking at the glass. At the iron man lying on its face near the dome. At the one kneeling with its arm lifted. At the one by the child. At the dark wet smear on the black glass where the bearded man had been dragged.
And her face, from this far, was the face of a girl writing something down in her head. Exactly. So that she could write it on paper later.
Corin knew that face. He did not know how. Something in the shape of it.
The three men in gray reached the camp with their dead. Corin watched them lay him down on the glass by the low lanterns. Gently. The big one knelt beside him again. The young one sat down suddenly, all at once, as if his legs had gone, and put his face in his hands.
And the pale one came across the glass.
He had been off to the left all morning, alone, writing. Corin had not looked at him. He looked at him now. A slight man in a long coat so pale it was nearly white. A gray hat. Gray gloves. A black book in one hand and a silver pencil in the other. And at his left hip, in a loop of leather, a silver rod.
He was walking toward Kross.
Not hurrying. He never seemed to hurry. He walked across the glass the way a man walks across a ballroom to ask somebody to dance, neatly, without a mark on him, without a speck of dust on his coat, and he stopped beside Kross. A pace off. On the side the cadet was not on.
And he spoke.
Corin could not hear him. It was too far. He only saw the pale one's mouth move, and his head tilt a little, politely, and one gloved hand lift, palm up, toward the dome. Toward Corin.
Kross did not look at him.
The pale one spoke again. A little longer this time. His hand was still lifted. And then he let it fall, and waited.
And Kross took off his glove.
His left one. Corin saw that his right hand was already bare. It had been bare all morning. A pale shape against the gray of his coat. Now he lifted his left hand, the gloved one, and took hold of the fingertip of the glove with the fingers of his bare right hand. And drew it off. Not quickly. One finger at a time. The way a man takes off his gloves when he has come in from the cold and is in no hurry, and the fire is lit, and there is somebody waiting for him by it.
The little finger. The ring finger. The middle. The first. The thumb.
The glove came off.
He folded it. Corin saw that too. He folded it in half, and in half again, neatly, along the seams, and put it in the pocket of his coat with the other one.
And stood there with both his hands bare.
The pale one said something else. Corin saw his lips move. And this time, because the pale one had turned his face a little toward the dome to say it, or because the Eye carried sound so far in its silence, he heard it. Faint. Across the glass. In a soft, pleasant, patient voice, like a man reminding a friend about an appointment.
"Inquisitor. The Archon's orders."
A pause.
"If you would."
And behind Corin, at the dome, Oren made a sound.
Bare Hands
Corin turned his head.
Oren was standing a few paces behind him, in front of the iron door, where he had got up off his knees. He was not leaning on his sword now. He was holding it. Low. In both hands. And he was looking across the glass at Kross with a face Corin had not seen on him before.
"Both," said Oren.
He said it to nobody. Low. Through his teeth.
"Both of them," he said. "Bare."
Lyra had heard. She turned her head. "What?"
"His hands." Oren did not take his eyes off Kross. "He had the one off all morning. The right. You don't fight with a bare right hand, lass. You don't fight bare-handed at all, not if you've a sword, not unless you're a fool or a farmer. Glove gives you grip. Glove saves your skin." He breathed out through his nose. "He's not taken it off to fight with. He's taken it off to touch."
Corin looked at Kross. At the pale bare hands at his sides, very white against the gray coat.
"He's a taker," said Oren. "Like you, lad. Like the cadet. Only more. A great deal more. I saw him in the city. I've seen men come out of rooms where he'd been, and stand in the street afterward, and not know which way was home." He shifted his grip on the sword. "On the Glass he couldn't. Nobody could. That's why he kept his gloves on and walked behind his iron and waited. But here."
He did not finish.
He did not need to. Corin could feel it. The Eye, all round them, clear and black and open. Where things could be taken. Where his own reach was awake in his gray hands, and Wren's in hers, and Kross's.
"He'll call one of us out," said Oren. Very quietly. "You watch. That pale one's told him to. He'll call one of us out, and if we don't go, he'll come in."
"Then we don't go," said Lyra. "We stay here. With the spears. He's one man."
"He's one man with both his hands bare and nobody's word to stop him but his own." Oren's face did not change. "And he's let us go, lass. Every time. You know that. On the dyke road. At the Bourse. On the Glass, in the dark, with his lanterns, when he could have walked into the middle of us any night he chose. He's let us go and let us go." He paused. "And that one's been writing it down."
Corin looked at the pale one. Standing beside Kross. With his book open.
"If Kross goes home with nothing," said Oren, "that one writes it. And then it's Kross on a list. Not us." He was quiet a moment. "So he'll call one of us out. To show he did. To give that book something to write."
"Then who?" said Lyra.
Oren did not answer.
But Corin saw his hands, on the hilt of the sword. Big and scarred. The left one with the strip of cloth round the palm where he had cut it to wake the door. They were not shaking. Corin's own hands had been shaking, a moment ago, looking at the pale one's rod. Oren's were steady. They held the sword the way you hold a thing you have held every day of your life.
And Corin knew who.
So did Lyra. He felt her know. He felt her go still beside him on the glass.
"No," she said. Very low. "Oren. Not you. You can't even— your back—"
"My back's been worse."
"You burned a word an hour ago. Look at you. You can hardly stand."
"I'm standing."
"Oren—"
He looked down at her then. For the first time. Away from Kross. Down at the girl kneeling on the black glass beside the lame man, with her bleeding hand against her chest and her bent sword on the glass by her knee and her face white.
And his face was very gentle.
"Lass," he said. "If he asks for me, by my name. My family's name. On her glass." He nodded, very slightly, at the dome behind him. At the carved sun with his own blood on it. "I'll go. That's all. That's all there is to it. You don't say no to that. Not a Vance. Not here."
Lyra opened her mouth.
And across the glass, Kross began to walk.
If You Would
Kross walked forward.
He did it the way he had done everything that morning. Without hurry. Without any sign. He walked across the black glass with his bare hands at his sides, away from the pale one, away from the cadet, toward the dome. The cadet came after him. One step behind his left shoulder. Exactly. The pale one did not come. He stayed where he was, and opened his black book, and lifted his silver pencil, and watched.
Kross walked past the first iron man.
The one Corin had broken first. Lying on its face on the glass with the plate gone from its back and the spring beside it in a loose dark curl. Kross did not look down at it. He stepped over the end of the spring, neatly, the way you step over a puddle in a street.
He walked past the second. The one kneeling, upright, with its arm still lifted and its head turned toward the place where the silent man had been. He walked past it close enough to touch. He did not touch it.
The third one was off to the side, by the child. Out on the open glass. He did not go near it.
And he came on toward the dome.
Corin was still on his knees. He did not get up. He did not think he could. His lame leg would not hold him and his good one was shaking, and the cold was on his face, and the warm place on his shoulder where her hand had been was going cold too, slowly, the way a chair goes cold after somebody gets up from it. He knelt on the black glass beside Lyra with his hand open on the glass beside hers, and watched Kross come.
The silent man was watching too. He had not moved from where he stood, out on the glass. His head had turned to follow Kross. His face did not change.
The Ashborn swung their spears toward him as he came. Kestrel and Sef and Oda, and Brin with nothing. They stood in their line in front of the dome and lowered their spears and did not move.
Kross did not look at them either.
He stopped.
A short way off from the dome. A few long paces. Close enough that Corin could see his face clearly now. A thin face. Tired. Lines from the nose to the mouth, deep, like cuts. Gray eyes. Dark hair going gray at the sides, under the plain gray hat. He was not a big man. He was slight and plain and gray, like a clerk, like a man you would pass in the street and not see. He stood on the black glass with his bare hands at his sides and the drowned village under his feet, and the cadet one step behind his shoulder, and he looked past the line of Ashborn spears.
Not at Corin.
Not at the child, or Kestrel, or the silent man.
At Oren.
Oren was standing in front of the iron door. He had been standing a little bent, a moment ago, with his sword held low. He was not bent now. He had made himself stand up straight. Corin saw it cost him. He saw Oren's face go white and then gray with it, and his mouth set, and his free hand go to the small of his back and then away again, quickly, as if he did not want anybody to see. And then he stood there. Straight. With his sword in his hand. Looking at Kross.
They looked at each other across the few paces of black glass.
And Kross spoke.
He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. On the Eye, in the silence, it carried. Quiet and level and polite, without any weight on it at all, the way Corin had heard it in the city, long ago, at the bottom of some stair, saying Good evening to somebody.
"Captain Vance," said Kross.
A pause.
"If you would."
Nobody moved.
Corin felt Lyra go rigid beside him. He felt it through the hair's breadth of air between their hands. She did not move her hand. But every part of her went still and hard, the way a bird goes still when the hawk's shadow goes over.
"No," she said. Very low. "No. Oren, no—"
Oren did not look at her.
He was looking at Kross. And his face was strange. Corin had seen Oren's face a great many times, every day, written about in the book. Angry. Tired. Laughing in his beard. Standing in a church. He had never seen it like this. It was not afraid. It was not angry. It was the face of a man who has been waiting for somebody for a very long time, and has heard at last their step on the stair.
Oren lifted his sword.
Not high. Not in a guard. He lifted it the way you lift a sword to show it to somebody. Point up. Before his face. The flat of it toward Kross. The notch near the tip catching the sun.
And held it there a moment.
Then he brought it down. Into the low guard. Point down, by his left boot.
And he walked out.
Past the end of the line of spears. Past Kestrel, who turned her head to watch him go and did not try to stop him. Past where Corin and Lyra knelt. He did not look down at them. Out onto the open black glass in the sun, with his bad back and his notched sword and his cut left hand bound up in its strip of cloth, toward the slight gray man with the bare hands.
And stopped.
A few paces from him. Face to face. On the glass. Over Applegarth.
The old soldier who had stood beside a door all his life, and could not say the word for it anymore. And the Inquisitor, who had let them go, every time.
Corin knelt on the black glass with his gray face and his open hand, and watched them, and could not breathe.
