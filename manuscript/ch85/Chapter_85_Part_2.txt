Chapter 85: Sola's Hands, Part 2 of 5: Feet
Feet
For a moment, only a moment, Corin did not look where the silent man was looking.
He looked down. At his own feet. At the silent man's feet. On the black glass, a pace apart, with the kneeling iron man between them.
He had boots. Old ones, cracked across the toes, laced with a bit of string where the lace had broken in the Fold Hills. The silent man had boots too. Older. Worse. The soles were coming away from the uppers at the toes, so that they gaped like mouths, and there was a hole in one heel you could have put a thumb through.
They were standing the same way.
Corin saw it, and something in his chest went tight.
The weight on the right foot. The left foot a little forward, and turned out. Not much. A finger's width. The way his left foot always turned out, because of something, he did not know what, something before he could remember. Lyra had told him once. It was in the book. Your left foot turns out when you stand still. Like you're getting ready to walk off to the left.
The silent man's left foot turned out too. The same. A finger's width.
He thought about the footprints.
They had found them on the Glass, days ago. He did not remember finding them. It was in the book, in his own crooked capitals, and Lyra had read it to him that morning, and every morning since. SMALL FOOTPRINTS. MELTED INTO THE GLASS. A CHILD. GOING THE OTHER WAY. And under it, later, in the chapel, after Tansy said what she said: THE LEFT ONE TURNS OUT. LIKE MINE.
A child's feet. Going the other way. Melted into the glass long ago, when the glass was warm enough to take a print.
And beside them, Lyra said, there had been no big footprints. Only the small ones. As if the child had walked alone. Or as if whoever walked with the child had walked so lightly that the glass did not notice.
Or carried him.
Corin did not know where that thought came from. It came up from somewhere deep, from the room with the door off its hinges, and went past him like a fish going past in dark water, and was gone before he could look at it.
He looked up.
The silent man was looking out across the glass. Toward the men in gray. His face was the same as it always was. Empty, or not empty. Far back. A man in the last room of a big house.
But his feet were Corin's feet.
Corin had walked in step with him from the Fold Hills to the Sun Road. All that long hot day. He had not known why. He had thought it was only that the silent man was copying him, the way a child copies, because Tansy said he never kept step with her. He had thought it was a strange thing about a strange man, and written it down, and forgotten it.
It was not the silent man who had learned to walk like Corin.
Corin did not know how he knew that. He did not know anything. He only stood on the black glass beside the kneeling iron man and looked at their two pairs of feet, the same, the left one turned out a finger's width, and knew it the way you know a word you cannot say.
Somebody had taught a small boy to walk.
A long time ago. On a road. By holding his hand. A big dry hand round a small one. Not why. But it's the right way. Step, and step, and the small left foot turning out because the big left foot turned out, and the small boy copying, the way small boys copy, until the small feet did it on their own and forgot they had ever learned it.
He did not have the memory. He had the feet.
Malakor, he thought.
Nothing. The old man was very quiet. Far back. Folded.
Malakor. Was it you? Did you—
Nothing.
And the silent man's lips had moved, a moment ago. Twice. A short shape and a longer one. Corin did not know what they made. He thought, if he could only hear it, he might know.
He did not hear it.
The blue-white fire came past his ear, and his body moved, and there was no more time.
The Men
The blue-white fire went past Corin's ear so close that he heard it.
He had not known you could hear it. You could. It went past him with a sound like a sheet being torn, and a heat like an oven door opening, and the smell came after it, burnt sugar, so sweet it made him gag. It hit the kneeling iron man on the shoulder and the iron there went black and spat sparks.
His body had already moved. Sideways. Away from the line of it.
The men in gray.
They had stood back while the wall was up. Corin had seen them from the bottom of the stair, through the gap, in pieces, the way you see a fight through a keyhole. Four men in gray coats on the black glass behind the iron, with their hands in their coats, throwing their stones at the gold and watching the stones burst on it in blue sparks. And then stopping, when they saw it did no good. And standing.
They were not standing now.
The wall was gone, and two of the iron men were down, and they were coming. Not fast. Not together. Not the way soldiers come, in a line. The way men come who are used to people getting out of their way. In a loose bunch, a little too close, with short swords out and stones in their other hands.
Three of them. The fourth, the big one with the broken nose, was hanging back. Corin saw that. He was standing where he had stood, a little in front of Kross, with his carbine on his back and his hands empty, and he was not coming. He was watching.
The other three came.
The young one with the big ears was in front. His face was white. His lips were split and bleeding. He had a stone in one hand and a short sword in the other and he was holding the sword wrong, too tight, too far up the hilt, the way a boy holds a sword the first time somebody gives him one. He was coming at Corin.
And Corin's body went to meet him.
Not fast. Not slow. It went the way it had gone at the iron men. As if it had done this before, a great many times, on a great many mornings, and knew exactly how it would go.
The young one swung.
It was a big swing, wild, from too far back, and Corin's body saw it coming from the moment it began and was not there when it arrived. His body was inside it. Close. Too close for the sword. And Sola's hands came up.
They did not hit him. Corin waited for them to hit him, somewhere far back behind his eyes, and they did not. They took hold of the young one's sword arm, at the wrist and above the elbow, one gray hand in each place. And turned.
That was all. A turn. The way you turn a key in a stiff lock. The young one's arm went round, and his body went round after it because it had to, and his feet went out from under him on the smooth glass. And the sword was not in his hand anymore. It was in Corin's. And the young one was on his back on the glass, staring up at the sky with his mouth open and his split lips bleeding and the stone rolling away from his open hand.
Corin stood over him with the sword.
He looked down at the young one's face. White. Very young. Younger than him. With big ears, and blood on his chin, and his eyes wide. Frightened.
Sola's hands did not do anything else.
They could have. Corin felt that. He felt her in his hands the way you feel a dog straining at a rope, and he thought she would. He thought the sword would come down. And then he felt her look. Through his eyes. At the young one's face. At the big ears and the split lips and the fear.
And her hands opened.
The sword fell on the glass beside the young one's head with a clang.
Corin did not know if she had done that or he had. He would never know. He only knew that the young one was lying on the glass with his eyes shut tight, waiting, and the sword was lying beside his head, and nothing came down.
And somewhere behind Corin, out of the gap in the dome, Lyra came.
He did not see her come. He heard her. Her feet on the glass, quick and light. And her voice, once, not a word, a sound, the sound she made in the Ridges when she went at the first iron man. And then the ring of steel.
He turned his head.
She was a few paces off, to his left, between him and the dome. She had come out of the gap with her bent sword, and one of the men in gray had come at her. The second one. A man Corin did not know, with a beard, square and heavy, with a short sword in his hand and a stone in his other. He had come at her hard, and she had met him.
She had her sword in her right hand.
Corin saw that, and something in him went cold that was not Sola. Her right hand. The bandaged one. The one with the skin torn off the palm in the Ridges, that Vespera had salved and bound and told her not to use. The bandage was already dark. He could see it from here. Dark and wet and spreading.
She was holding the sword in it anyway.
The man in gray swung at her. She took it on her blade, low, and gave ground. One step. And he swung again, harder, and she took it again, and gave again. Another step. Back toward the dome. Toward the gap. And the man came after her, pressing, the way a man presses when he thinks he is winning.
And Corin saw her sword go down.
Not dropped. Down. The point low, behind her, by her left heel. Her weight on her back foot. Her left hip turned toward the man. As if she were tired. As if she were giving up.
He knew that. He did not know how he knew it, but he knew it. It was in the book. The lie. Oren had taught it to her in the shadow of the iron horse, and the silent man had done it once, faster than Oren ever, and handed her the sword back hilt first. The Vance cut.
The man in gray stepped in for the kill.
And she rose.
From the hip. From the foot. Like a spring let go. Her sword came up out of the lie by her heel and across her body and up, from her left hip to above her right shoulder, under the man's guard, through the gap between his elbow and his ribs. Corin heard it. A sound like a sheet being torn.
The man in gray stopped.
He stood there for a moment, very still, with his sword still lifted. He looked at her. His face was surprised. Not angry. Surprised. As if somebody had told him something he had not expected to hear.
Then he went down.
He went down on his knees on the black glass. And then onto his side. And lay there. And the black glass under him began to go dark.
He did not get up.
Lyra stood over him with her sword in her right hand and the bandage on it soaked through, red, dripping. She was looking down at him. Her face was perfectly white.
Behind her, the Ashborn had come out of the gap. Kestrel and Sef and Marrit and Oda, with Brin behind them empty-handed. They had come out in a line, with their spears low, and they stood between Lyra and the third man in gray. And the third man in gray, who had been coming up behind the bearded one, stopped. He stood there with his short sword out and his stone in his other hand and looked at the line of spears, and at the man on the glass, and at Lyra.
And took a step back.
Corin watched Lyra.
She did not move. She stood over the man she had cut with her sword hanging from her bleeding hand, and her face was white and still, and she did not look away from him.
Corin did not know if she had ever killed anybody before. It was not in the book. But he looked at her face, and he thought that she had not. He thought it the way he knew her voice.
She had now.
The Young One
The young one with the big ears was still on the glass.
He had not got up. Corin had put him down, or Sola had, with a turn of the wrist and elbow, and taken his sword away and dropped it beside his head, and he was lying exactly where he had fallen. On his back. With his arms out. His eyes were open now. He was staring up at the sky.
Corin did not mean to go to him. His body was not his anymore, not all of it, but it was not all hers either. Some of it was his. Enough to walk. And it walked him over there, a few steps, slowly, while the third man in gray stood back from the line of spears and the big one with the broken nose stood back from all of it and Lyra stood over the bearded man with her bleeding hand.
He stood over the young one and looked down.
The young one looked up at him.
His face was very white. His lips were split down the middle and there was blood on his chin and his teeth. His ears stuck out under his gray cap, which had come half off, so that you could see his hair was the color of straw and cut short like a cadet's. He was young. Younger than Corin had thought. His eyes were wet.
They looked at each other.
"Are you him?" said the young one.
His voice cracked. He had to stop and try again.
"The one," he said. "At the Bourse. Who held the roof. My— Harrow's sister-in-law. She said a lame man held the roof up. She said she saw his face." He was looking at Corin's face. At the gray on it. "Are you him?"
Corin thought about it.
He did not remember the Bourse. He did not remember holding anything up. He remembered nothing about a roof or a lame man or anybody's face. But it was in the book. Lyra had read it to him. MIDWINTER. THE BOURSE. I HELD UP THE DOME. In his own capitals. And his arms were gray to the shoulders, and now his face, and Lyra said that was why.
"I don't know," he said. "It says so. In my book."
The young one stared at him.
Then he began to cry. Not loudly. He lay on his back on the black glass and his face crumpled up and the tears came out of the corners of his eyes and ran down sideways into his big ears, and he did not make a sound.
Corin did not know what to do.
He stood there. Then, because he did not know what else to do, he went down on his good knee beside the young one. Slowly. With his lame leg out stiff. And he put out his hand. His gray hand. Not to touch him. Not to take anything. Just held it there, a little above the young one's chest, the way Lyra held her finger above the lines on his ribs.
The young one looked at the hand.
"You're not going to—" he said. "Take it? The thing? Like she—"
"No."
"I asked her to. The cadet. Last night. I asked her to take it out. A girl. A little girl, at the burning, at the bread racks. With a red thread in her hair. I keep seeing her." His voice went thin. "She wouldn't. She said I had to keep it. So I wouldn't do it again."
Corin did not say anything for a while.
He thought of the bread racks at Last Thorn. He did not remember them. They were in the book. The night of the burning. Lyra's cut. The iron man against the racks, and three children in front of it. And a girl, the serious one, who had painted Lyra's gray line on her face at the rite, with a red thread in her hair.
"She was right," he said.
The young one shut his eyes.
"You have to keep the ones that hurt," said Corin. He did not know where the words were coming from. They came from somewhere, slow, the way the words came when Lyra read him the book and he wrote them down. "Some of them. Somebody told me that. Or I read it. I don't know." He looked at his gray hand, held above the young one's chest. "I've taken a great many things out of people. In the city. For a long time. I took the ones that hurt, mostly. That's what they asked for." He paused. "I don't think I did them any good."
The young one opened his eyes and looked at him.
"Go back," said Corin. "To your camp. Your friend's hurt. The big one will want you."
The young one lay there a moment longer.
Then he rolled over. Slowly. Onto his side. With his hand pressed to his ribs where Sola's throw had put him on the glass. And he got up onto his knees, and then onto his feet, swaying. He did not pick up his sword. He looked at it, lying on the glass, and then at Corin, and then he left it there.
And he went.
Back across the glass, toward the big one with the broken nose. Limping. With his big ears and his split lips and his straw-colored hair.
He did not look back.
Tansy
He heard her before he saw her.
The song. Thin and high and shaking, coming across the glass from somewhere to his right. Out past the end of where the wall had been. Out on the open Eye.
The moon song. The three notes, and the fourth going up and hanging, and the rest. In a small voice, too high, too fast, with the breath catching in the wrong places. The voice of a child singing in the dark to keep the dark off.
Only it was not dark. It was the middle of the morning, and the sun was bright on the black glass, and the child was out in it.
Corin turned his head.
She was a long way off. Too far. He saw that at once, the way you see at once that a cup is going to fall off a table and you are too far away to catch it. She had come out round the end of the wall, the narrow place by the iron rib, after the silent man. He could see the way she had come. She had gone the way the man had gone, out along the curve of the dome and past it and out onto the open glass, toward where she had seen him thrown. And then on. Further. Looking for him, perhaps. Or lost. Or too frightened to know where she was going.
She was standing on the black glass all by herself, far out, with Oren's big coat dragging off one shoulder and her bare feet and her hair sticking up and her hands clenched at her sides. With her eyes shut.
Singing.
And between her and Corin, a long way off from him and not far at all from her, there was the third iron man.
It had been at the gap. In the middle. In front of Kestrel. Corin had seen it from the bottom of the stair, through the narrow opening beside the iron door, pounding on the gold. When the gold went, it must have gone forward. Into the gap. And found the Ashborn there, Kestrel with her spear, and the line of them. And stopped, the way they stopped for a person in the way.
And then it had heard the song.
It had turned. Corin could see that it had turned. Its egg head was toward the child. Its body was coming round after its head, heavy, slow, its great feet shifting on the glass. It was walking. Away from the gap. Away from the Ashborn. Out across the open glass. Toward the sound.
Toward her.
Its ticking was loud. Louder than the others had been. As if it were hurrying.
And she had her eyes shut. She could not see it. She was standing there singing with her eyes screwed shut and her fists clenched and she could not see it coming.
Corin's body was already moving.
But it was too far. He knew it was too far. He could see it the way you can see, in the moment before a horse bolts, the whole of what is going to happen, laid out like a road. The iron man was between him and the child, and closer to her than he was to it. It was walking at a walk, and it would reach her before he reached it. Long before. His body was fast, now, Sola's body, light on its feet. But it was a man's body, not a horse's. It could not cover that much glass in time.
Kestrel was running. He saw her at the edge of his eye, out of the line of Ashborn, with her white cape flying out behind her and her spear in her hand. Too far. Further than him.
The silent man was running. Corin saw him too. Coming across from the left, long-legged, fast, faster than Corin had ever seen him move. Too far.
Nobody was close enough.
And the iron man was only a few long strides from the child. Fewer. It lifted its arm.
And in the calm, in the great still silence inside him, Sola turned.
He felt her turn. All of her. Not a shift of weight this time. Not a woman in the next room getting up out of her chair. The whole of her. Turning. The way the whole of a river turns at a bend.
Toward the child.
And he knew what she was going to do.
He had known it, a little, at the bottom of the stair, with Vespera's hand on his chest. He had known it on the bench in the night, knocking heard with Oren in the dark. He had known it since the iron horse, if he was honest. Since the gold. Since the moment, at the very end, when he was inside her on the long road of light charging south, and he felt her give the last thing she had. He had known that she would always give the last thing. That was what she was. That was all she was. A woman who gave everything, at the end, to stand between her people and the gray.
And the child was hers.
She was standing on the glass over Applegarth singing the moon song with her eyes shut, and she was hers. They were all hers.
Wait, he thought. Far back. Behind his eyes. Wait. Not all of you. Please. Not all—
She did not wait.
She had never waited for anybody in her life.
She gave it all.
