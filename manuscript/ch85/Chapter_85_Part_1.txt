Chapter 85: Sola's Hands, Part 1 of 5: Out
Out
Corin walked out onto the clear glass with a dead woman's calm spreading through him, and his hands moved before he had decided anything.
No. That was not how it began. It began a breath before that, at the edge of the gold.
He was standing with his face a hand's width from the wall of light. He could see through it. It was thin now, so thin, like honey with too much water in it, and a crack ran up the middle of it from the glass to the rim, crooked and pale. On the other side of the crack, a pace away, there was an iron man. Its arm was drawn back. Its egg head was tilted. Its slit, at the height of his eyes, had a glint at the back of it.
And behind him, Oren. He did not look round. He did not need to. He could hear Oren's breathing, harsh and ragged through his teeth, and he could feel the old man's eyes on the back of his neck. And he could still hear the sound Oren had made, a moment ago, when he tried to say the word and the word was not there.
Hold the—
Corin had seen Oren's face. He had seen the place where the word should have been. He knew that place. He had a great many places like it himself. He knew what it felt like to reach for a thing and close your hand on air.
Oren had done that for him. For all of them. For the wall.
The iron man's arm came down.
It hit the gold in front of Corin's face. And the gold did not shake this time. It did not jar. It broke.
Not like glass. Glass breaks with a sound. This broke the way a soap bubble breaks. All at once, silently, from the crack outward. One moment there was a wall of pale gold light in front of him, curving away on both sides to the dome. And the next there was nothing. A shimmer in the air, like heat over a road. A few gold sparks, drifting down onto the black glass like the last sparks from a fire when you kick it out. And then not even that.
Behind him he heard Oren go down. A grunt. A heavy sound. A knee hitting glass.
And the iron man's arm, which had been coming down onto the wall, came down onto nothing, and swung through the space where the wall had been, and the weight of it pulled the iron man forward a half step. Toward him.
And Corin's body moved.
That was how it began.
He did not move it. That was the thing he would try to tell Lyra, afterward, and not be able to, and write down in the book instead in shaky capitals that still did not say it. He did not decide. He did not think go left or duck or now. He thought nothing at all. There was a great wide calm spreading through him, from his chest outward, into his arms and his legs and his hands, like cold water spreading through cloth. And in the calm, his body went.
It went down. Under the arm. Low and fast, with his knees bent and his weight on the balls of his feet, the way a man ducks under a beam in a low barn. The iron arm went over his head. He felt the wind of it in his hair. And his body went on, forward, under it, past the iron man's hip, and turned, on one foot, the bad one, the lame one, and the lame one did not fold. It held. It turned him like a door on a hinge, all the way round, so that he was behind the iron man.
And he was out.
Out on the open glass. Out past where the wall had been. With the dome behind him and the iron man's back in front of him and the whole wide black Eye going away on every side.
And the calm.
He had felt it before. In the Ridges, standing up in front of the second iron man, when he did not fall down. At the iron horse, inside her, for a long gold moment before the end. But he had never felt it like this. It filled him. All of him. It was so wide and so quiet and so still that there was no room in him for anything else. No fear. No thinking. No words.
She had no words. She had given them all away. Long ago, at the little lake at the top of this valley, the one that was under his feet now, under the Eye, under the clear black glass. Every word she had. Her people's names, one by one. Her own name last. And what was left of her was this.
Silence. And hands. And feet that knew where to go.
He was in it. He was in it the way you are in a river when you have stopped swimming and let it carry you. He could see out. He could see everything, very clearly, the way you see things in a dream. But he was not the one looking. He was somewhere behind his own eyes, a little way back, the way you stand a little way back from a window so that the people outside cannot see you. Watching.
Watching his own hands.
They were gray. Gray to the shoulder, under the coat, and he could not feel them. He had not been able to feel them for weeks. But he could see them, out in front of him, closed into fists, easy, loose at the wrist. And they were not his. They had never moved like this. They had never in his life been held like this, a little away from his body, a little forward, ready.
They were hers.
And under them, under his feet, the light.
It was the middle of the morning, now. The sun had come up off the rim of the bowl and stood a little way up the sky, and the long red light of the dawn had gone gold and short. It came down onto the Eye and went into it. Down into the clear black, the way it had gone down into it at sunset, deep, deeper, so that the whole floor of the Eye was lit from inside. And far below him, under his feet, there was the village.
Applegarth. The roofs. The square tower of the church. The well. The farm cart with its horse caught in the middle of a step. The people with their faces turned up.
Toward him.
He was standing on their faces. Kestrel had said so. We do not walk on the Eye. It is like walking on their faces. And he was standing on them now, on the black glass, with an iron man in front of him and the sun coming down past him into the deep.
She did not mind. He felt that too. Sola did not mind. They were hers. They had been hers when they were alive, and they were hers now, and she was standing over them the way she had stood over them long ago, in a ditch, at the bottom of the valley. Between them and the gray.
And all round him, loud, louder than anything, the ticking.
Three iron men. Close. Tick. Tick. Tick. All in one beat. Coming out of their chests like hearts.
The First One
The iron man in front of him turned.
It had swung its arm through the place where the wall had been and found nothing. And then it had stood a moment, a half step forward, the way a man stands when he leans on a door that is not there. And now it was turning. Its egg head came round on its brass neck with that little whirring click, and its body began to come round after it, slow, heavy, its great feet shifting on the glass.
It had heard him. Or felt him. He did not know how they knew things.
His body did not wait for it to finish turning.
It went in. Close. So close that he could smell the iron man, the old oil and rust and hot brass, the smell of the cellar under the Records yard that he had never smelled and somehow knew. His body went in against its back, as it turned, the way a dog goes in against the back legs of a bull. Under the swing of its arm. Too close to be hit.
And his hands went up.
To its back. To the small of its back, where the brass plate was. The square plate, the size of a big book, with the little hole in the middle of it where the bird key went. Rivets at the corners. He saw it very clearly. He saw the scratches on it, and a dent in one corner, and a thumbprint, small and black, in the oil, that somebody had left there a long time ago.
His gray fingers found the edge of the plate.
And slid off.
He felt it, somewhere far back behind his eyes. Not in the fingers. The fingers felt nothing. But his body felt it, the way your body feels a stair that is not there. The fingers had gone to the edge of the plate and closed on it, and nothing had happened. They had not gripped. They could not grip. They were dead. Black from the tips to the second joint, dead as wood, dead as stone. They could close, if he watched them close. But they could not feel what they closed on, and so they could not hold it, and they slid off the brass like wet hands off a rope.
The iron man was still turning.
Its arm was coming round.
And in the calm, in the great still silence that filled him, something moved.
Not him. Her. He felt her move, inside him, the way you feel somebody shift their weight in the next room through a thin wall. A turning. A decision. Not in words. She had no words. But he knew what it was. It was the same thing he had felt in her on the horse, at the very end, at the lake, before the gold came out. A woman looking down at what she had, and choosing what to give.
Not that one, he thought. Far back. Behind his eyes. Not—
She gave it anyway.
She did not ask him. He had known she would not. He had known it on the bottom step, with Vespera's hand on his chest, when he said I'll give her back to them. He had known that once he let her have his hands, she would do with them what she had always done with her own. Fight. And spend whatever it cost.
And what it cost, here, in the Eye, was her.
A morning.
That was what she gave. One morning. He did not choose it. He did not see it until it was going, and then he saw it all at once, the way you see a whole room in the flash of lightning.
An orchard. Spring. Apple trees in rows, old ones, gnarled and gray-barked, with the blossom thick on every branch, white and pink, and the petals coming down in the wind like snow. Long grass between the rows, wet with dew, soaking her boots. The sun low and gold through the trees. And boys. A line of boys, a ragged line, twenty of them, more, farm boys and miller's boys and a smith's boy with burns on his arms, holding pikes too long for them, their faces red and frightened and proud. And her, walking up and down in front of them in the wet grass with her braid down her back and a stick in her hand, shouting.
Feet! Feet, you lump! Look at your feet! A pike's no good to a man who's on his back in the grass!
And a boy at the end of the line falling over his own pike, sprawling, face down in the long wet grass with the blossom coming down on him. And the whole line laughing. And her laughing. Throwing her head back and laughing, a great loud crow of a laugh, the laugh of a woman who did not laugh often and did it with her whole body when she did. And going to the boy and hauling him up by the back of his collar with one hand and setting him on his feet and brushing the petals off his shoulders.
Again. From the top. And this time, Hob, try and remember which end's which.
Hob.
Hob, who was on the cross at Haverstock, the first of the Vance names on the north side, in the second ring. Hob, who she had given at the lake, first of all of them, before the river and the horse and the bread.
Corin saw it. All of it. The orchard and the blossom and the boys and the laugh and Hob in the wet grass with petals on his shoulders. He saw it the way you see a page of a book in the moment before somebody holds it to a candle.
And then it burned.
It went up. All at once. Not like pain. Like a breath. Like a long breath going out of him, out of his chest and down his arms and into his hands. The orchard went white, and then gold, and then it was gone. The blossom. The boys. The laugh. Hob. Gone. And where it had been, in his arms, in his dead gray hands, there was strength.
He had never felt anything like it.
It came into his arms the way water comes into a dry ditch when they open the sluice. All at once. Rushing. Filling. His arms were full of it. His hands were full of it. And his dead black fingers closed on the edge of the brass plate in the small of the iron man's back, and he could not feel them, he still could not feel anything, but they held.
They held like iron.
And his arms pulled.
The plate came off.
It did not come off the way it had come off in the Ridges, when the silent man tore it with his bare hands, slowly, bending it back like the lid of a tin. It came off all at once. With a scream of metal. The rivets at the corners sheared, one, two, three, four, with four sharp cracks like sticks breaking, and the whole square plate came away in his hands, and his body threw it aside without looking, and it went skidding away across the black glass, spinning, flashing in the sun.
And behind it, in the hole, the wheels.
More than he could count. Brass. Small and big. Turning. All turning, all at once, every one in its own time, the way the wheels turn in the back of a clock when you take the back off. Ticking. And in the middle of them all, at the heart, a drum. A round brass drum, the size of his palm, with a dark coil of steel wound round and round inside it.
The spring.
His hand went in among the wheels.
He watched it go. His own gray hand. Into the turning brass, the teeth of the little wheels catching at his gray skin and not cutting it, because gray skin did not cut, it was like stone, like river stone. His black fingers found the drum. They found the end of the spring, where it came out of the drum, a little hooked tail of dark steel.
They closed on it.
And pulled.
The spring came out screaming.
It came out of the drum the way a snake comes out of a hole when you pull its tail, uncoiling, whipping, a long flat ribbon of dark steel unwinding all at once in a great shrieking flash. It went out past his face and he felt the wind of it. It lashed across the glass. It rang. It sang, a high terrible note, like a saw on a stone, and then it was lying on the black glass beside him in a long loose curl, quivering. And still.
The iron man stopped.
Not slowly. All at once. Its arm, coming round at him, stopped in the air. Its head stopped. Its ticking stopped. Every wheel in its back stopped, at once, with a sound like a hand closing on a fistful of coins.
And it fell.
Forward. Away from him. The way a tree falls when the last of the trunk gives. Slowly at first, and then not slowly. It went down onto the black glass on its face with a crash that went out across the whole silent Eye, and lay there.
Corin stood over it.
His hands hung at his sides. Gray. Easy. Loose at the wrist.
The calm was still there. Wide and still and silent. But there was something missing from it now. A morning. An orchard. A laugh.
He did not know what he had lost. He had never had it. It had been hers. He had only been carrying it.
But he felt the place where it had been. And he knew, the way you know when you have put your foot through a rotten board in the dark, that it was not coming back.
In Step
The second one was already coming.
It was the one with the stuck leg. The one the silent man had jammed with Brin's spear. Corin saw it as his body turned away from the first one, lying on its face on the glass: it was a few paces off, to the right, coming at him at a limp, dragging its right leg stiff behind it with the broken stub of the spear shaft still jammed in the back of the knee. Its ticking was crooked. Every few ticks there was a catch in it, a stumble, like a man with a stone in his shoe.
It was slower than the others. But it was coming. Its egg head turned toward him. Its good arm was coming up.
And beside it, walking toward it, a pace behind, there was the silent man.
Corin had not seen where he came from. He had been out on the glass, Corin knew that much. He had heard Oren say so, from the top of the stair, with the gold going thin. He's up, lass. Look. He's up. He had gone out round the edge of the wall and been thrown. And here he was. Walking. Behind the iron man with the stuck leg, a pace back, on its left. Not hurrying. With his bound hands loose at his sides and blood drying in his beard.
He was not looking at the iron man.
He was looking at Corin.
And Corin's body went.
It went toward them. Not straight. At a slant. Toward the iron man's right side, the side of the stuck leg, while the silent man was on its left. And as his body went, he felt the silent man go too. Not saw. Felt. The way you feel somebody start to walk beside you in a crowd. They went toward the iron man from either side at the same moment, at the same pace.
And in step.
Corin did not know how he knew that, either. He could not see the silent man's feet. He was looking at the iron man. But he knew that when his own left foot came down on the glass, the silent man's left foot came down at the same instant. And his right with Corin's right. The same stride. The same length. And when Corin's left foot came down, it turned a little outward, the way it always did, the way it had done since before he could remember, the way it had made the child's footprints in the glass that he had walked beside and not known. And the silent man's left foot turned outward too.
The same.
As if they had walked together a long way, a long time ago, and their feet had learned each other.
The iron man felt them coming. Its head swung, left, toward the silent man, and then right, toward Corin, and then back. It could not watch both. It lifted its good arm. The left one. Toward the silent man.
And the silent man went in under it.
Corin saw him go. Low and fast, the way Corin's own body had gone under the first one. The same move. Exactly the same. Sola's move, in a man who had never had Sola in him. Under the arm, past the hip, and round. And as he came round, his long leg came up and back and drove the heel of his boot into the back of the iron man's good knee. The left one.
The knee went.
It did not bend. Iron knees did not bend that way. It locked, the way the other one had locked when Brin's spear went in. Both legs stiff. The iron man rocked on them like a table with the legs nailed wrong. It lurched. It tipped. It went forward and down, slowly, onto its stiff knees, like a man kneeling who cannot kneel.
And its back was in front of Corin.
The brass plate. A little below the height of his chest, now, with the iron man on its knees.
His hands went to it.
And this time she did not need to burn anything. He felt that. He felt her not burn. The strength from the orchard was still in his arms, a little, not all of it, but enough. His dead fingers found the edge of the plate. They held. His arms pulled.
The plate came off. The rivets went, crack crack crack crack. The wheels inside were turning. His hand went in. The drum. The tail of the spring. He pulled.
The spring came out screaming.
It lashed out past him across the glass in a long dark flash and he heard the silent man step back from it, quick, one step, and it went past them both and fell ringing on the glass.
The iron man stopped.
It did not fall. It stayed where it was, on its stiff knees, upright, its arm still half lifted, its head still turned toward the place where the silent man had been. Stopped. Silent. Kneeling on the black glass in the sun like a man at prayer who has forgotten the words.
Two.
Corin stood behind it with his hands hanging.
And on the other side of it, the silent man stood and looked at him.
They looked at each other across the kneeling iron man's shoulder. In the bright gold morning on the black glass, with the drowned village under their feet and the ticking of the last iron man going on somewhere behind them, loud.
The silent man's face did not change. But his eyes were not empty. They were not looking through Corin, the way they had looked through everybody in the Fold Hills. They were looking at him. At his hands. At his feet. And then at his face.
And Corin thought, I know you.
He did not know where it came from. It was not a memory. He did not have the memory. There was a hole where the memory would be, he knew that, a great dark room in him with the door off its hinges, broken from the inside, Hask had said, somebody went out of it in a hurry. He did not have it. But his feet had it. His feet had known. They had walked in step with the silent man across the glass from the Fold Hills, and his feet had known him the whole way.
I know you.
The silent man's lips moved.
Twice. The way they had moved at the edge of the Sun Road, looking at Sola's helmet. Two shapes. A short one and then a longer one. No sound.
And then he turned away and looked out across the glass. Toward the men in gray.
