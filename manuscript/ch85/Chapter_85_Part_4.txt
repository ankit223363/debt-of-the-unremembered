Chapter 85: Sola's Hands, Part 4 of 5: Sola Goes
Sola Goes
Lyra was coming across the glass.
She was walking. Not running. He saw her come out from the place by the dome where the man in gray lay on his side, and step over his outflung arm, and come toward him. She had her bent sword in her left hand now, hanging, point down, dragging a little on the glass. Her right hand was held against her chest. The bandage on it was red all through. Dripping. She did not seem to know.
Her face was white. Whiter than the bandage had been before the blood.
She was looking at him.
And as she came, something happened inside him.
It was not the cold. The cold had stopped at the corner of his mouth and stayed there. It was not the old man. The old man had gone quiet in his corner. It was something else. Something he had not felt before, in all the days since the iron horse, in all the days of carrying her.
It was her.
Not all of her. All of her was gone. He knew that. He had watched it go, every bit of it, the valley and the horse and the braid and the road. There was nothing of her left in him to burn. The room was empty.
But she was not gone yet.
He felt her. Not in the room. At the door of it. The door that was off its hinges. Standing there, the way she must have stood at the top of her own stair, long ago, with her maps under her arm, looking back down into the dark before she went up into the light. Not in him anymore. Not quite out. On the step.
The last breath is a door, Kestrel had said, at Last Thorn, on the morning Hask died. They wait on the step until the sun is up.
The sun was up.
And she was going.
She did not have any words. She had never had any words, in him. She had given them all away before he ever knew her. She could not say goodbye. She could not say thank you, or well done, lad, or mind the girl, or any of the things somebody says on a step before they go. She did not have a single one left.
So she did the only thing she had.
She left a silence.
He felt it come. Not like the calm. The calm had been huge, a wide still flood that filled him and took him over and moved his hands. This was small. It came into him quietly, the way somebody comes into a room where a sick person is sleeping. It settled in him. Somewhere near his left shoulder, where the cold began. And it was warm.
It was the only warm thing in all that cold.
It was like a hand.
Like a big hand, heavy, rough, with a scar across the back of it from a forge, set down on his shoulder from behind. Not gripping. Not pushing. Just set there. The way you put your hand on the shoulder of somebody who has done a hard thing well, on your way past them, without stopping, without a word. The way an old captain puts his hand on a young soldier's shoulder, going past, at the end of a long day.
That way.
It rested there.
And then, very slowly, it lifted.
And she was gone.
Corin went down on his knees.
He did not decide to. His good leg folded under him, and his lame leg would not, so he went down crooked, sideways, onto one knee with the other leg stuck out stiff, on the black glass, beside the fallen iron man with the hole in its back. He put one gray hand flat on the glass to stop himself going over. And knelt there.
And wept.
He did not know he was going to. It came up out of him all at once, the way it had come up at Last Thorn, when Hask breathed Vivienne out of him and the noise stopped. From somewhere deep, under everything. Great dragging sobs that shook him from the belly up and would not stop. He knelt on the black glass in the sun with the drowned village far below under his hand and the tears running down his face, down the right side, warm, and down the left side, over the gray, where he could not feel them. Where he could only see, at the edge of his eye, that they were wet.
He wept for a woman he had known for five days.
He had counted them. He could count them. Five was a hand. Friday, the iron horse. Saturday, the grounding. Sunday, the chapel and the Ridges. Monday, the Eye. Tuesday, the book. And today. A hand of days. He had never seen her face, except from inside, in the dark, in a mirror of somebody else's eyes. He had never heard her voice, except once, through his own mouth, saying Vance in the dark so that Oren went white. He did not know what she had looked like when she laughed.
He had known her better than he had ever known anybody.
And she had given him all of it, every bit, and he had burned it, every bit, here on the glass over her people's faces. To stand between them and the gray. And she had gone. And she had left him a hand on his shoulder, for a moment, the only way she had left to say anything at all.
He knelt there and wept for her, and could not stop.
And nobody told him to.
Vespera
Somebody put the crutch down beside him on the glass.
He heard it. The knock of the wood. He did not look up at first. He was still weeping. And then he did look up, through the water in his eyes, and it was Vespera.
She had come up the stair. Corin did not know how long it had taken her. Her knees were bad and the stair was steep and she had been carrying his crutch. She was standing over him now on the black glass, small and gray and sharp, in her crushed dark coat with her rope belt of needles and bottles, breathing hard. Her blue hands were empty. She had set the crutch down beside his knee and straightened up and she was looking at him.
At his face.
She looked at it for a long time. Longer than the child had. She looked at it the way she looked at everything, with her head a little on one side and her sharp old eyes narrowed, the way she had looked at the back of his ear at Last Thorn before she said Ah.
She did not say Ah.
She did not say I told you, either. He waited for it. He thought she would say it. She had told him. At the bottom of the stair, with her hand on his chest. Your face. Into your mouth. Into your words. He thought she had every right to say it.
She did not.
She got down on her knees in front of him. Slowly. He heard them crack. And she put out one blue hand and took hold of his chin. The way she had at Last Thorn. Her fingers cold and hard and strong, like the fingers of somebody who has spent her life with her hands in dye.
She turned his head. To the right. So that she could see the left side.
He let her.
He could not feel her fingers on his chin. Not on the left side. On the right side he could. A little. Cold.
She looked at the gray. Up his neck. Over his jaw. To the corner of his mouth.
And then she did a strange thing. She put her thumb, very gently, at the corner of his mouth. Right at the edge. Where the gray stopped. And pressed. Just a little.
"Say your name," she said.
Her voice was flat. Not sharp. Not gruff. Flat, the way a voice goes when it is holding something down very hard.
"Corin," said Corin.
He felt her thumb move with his lips. On the right side of her thumb, he felt it. On the left side, nothing.
"Again."
"Corin."
"Your other name."
He had to think. It was on his chest. Under his collarbone. Where he could not read it. Your name is Corin—
"Vane," he said.
"Again."
"Corin Vane."
Vespera took her thumb away.
She sat back on her heels on the glass and looked at him. And for a moment her face was not sharp at all. For a moment it was only the face of an old woman, very tired, who had walked a long way across the Glass and up a steep stair with bad knees, carrying a crutch for a fool.
"Then it's still in your mouth," she said. "Your name. Just. It's sitting right on the edge." She looked at the corner of his mouth. "Keep it there."
"How?"
"Don't burn," said Vespera. "Not one thing more. Not a crumb. Not a breath of anybody's. Not here. Not anywhere, for a long while. Not if they come at you with every iron man in the world." Her voice went hard again. "Do you hear me, harvester? Not one more thing. Next time it goes in. Next time I'll be reading your name to you off your own chest every morning, and you'll be saying it back to me like a parrot, and you won't know what it means."
"Yes."
"Say it."
"I won't burn."
"Good."
She looked at him a moment longer. Then she reached out and picked up the crutch from the glass beside his knee, and put it into his gray hand, and closed his fingers round it, one by one, little finger first, thumb last. The way you close a child's hand round a coin.
"There," she said. "Hold that. It's yours. You'll want it."
And she got up, slowly, with a hand on his shoulder to push herself up, the shoulder where the warm had been, and stood a moment with her hand there. Then she took it away, and went to Oren, who was still kneeling a few paces off, without looking back.
Applegarth
He looked down.
He did not mean to. He was kneeling on the black glass with the crutch in his gray hand where Vespera had closed his fingers round it, and his face was wet, and his head was bent. And so he was looking down. Through the glass. Into the Eye.
And there was the village.
He had seen it before. From the shore, when they first came down onto the Eye, and the hum stopped. From the dome, last night, when Kestrel played the flute and the sun went into the glass sideways and lit it up like a fair. He had seen it every time he looked down. Roofs, a long way off. A square. A tower.
He had not seen it like this.
He was right over it. Two of the fallen iron men lay close by him, on the open glass in front of the dome, and the place where he knelt was between them. And under him, straight down, so far down that it made his stomach drop, there was the square of Applegarth.
He could see the well. Round, with a little roof over it on four posts. A bucket on the rim. He could see the stones of the square laid out round it in rings, the way the stones are laid round the market cross at Haverstock. He could see a cart, the cart with the horse caught in the middle of a step, its front hoof lifted, its head up. And he could see the people.
He had not looked at the people before. Nobody did. Kestrel did not walk on the Eye because of them. It is like walking on their faces.
He looked at them now.
There were a great many. More than he could count. More than his hands could hold. Standing in the square, round the well, in the doorways of the houses, in the road. Still. All of them still. And all of them with their faces turned up.
Toward the light.
Toward where the light had come from, long ago. From the north. From the head of the valley. From the little lake that was under his knees now, under the dome, under everything. They had been standing in their square on a spring morning, going about their business, and they had heard something, a roar, a sound like the sky tearing, and they had turned and looked up. All of them. At once.
And the light had come down on them, gold, and they had stayed looking up.
He could see a woman.
One. Near the well. He did not know why his eye went to her. She was standing with a basket on her arm. A big round basket, with something in it he could not make out. Apples, perhaps. It was spring, so not apples. Bread. Eggs. Her other hand was up. Not shading her eyes. Not reaching. Just up, a little, open, the way you lift your hand when somebody calls your name across a market and you are not sure, yet, who it is.
Her face was turned up toward him.
He could not see it clearly. It was too far down, and the glass between them was too deep, and the light from the sun went down into it and broke up into many soft pieces on the way. He could only see that it was a face. A woman's. Looking up.
At him.
Not at him. At the light. At what had come down the valley, long ago, on an iron horse, with a road of gold pouring out in front of it.
At her.
She had known that woman. Corin knew it. Not because he remembered. He did not remember anything of Sola's now. It was all gone, every bit, burned on the glass in front of him to tear three springs out of three iron men. But he knew it anyway. The way you know, walking into a house you have never been in, that somebody you love has lived there.
Sola had known every face in that square.
And she had given them all away, at the lake, one by one, the names of them, Hob and Talan and Annis and all the rest. And then she had given herself. And they had stood in their square with their faces turned up and watched her come, and been turned to glass, every one of them, with their hands up and their baskets on their arms.
And she had stood on an iron horse for all those years at the end of a road made of her own light, waiting. For somebody to come. For one of hers.
And Oren had come. And then Corin had come, and put on her helmet, and taken her into him. And she had lived in him for a hand of days. And this morning she had come out onto the glass over her people's faces one last time, and stood between them and the gray, and given everything she had left.
To keep the gray off them.
They were already glass. They had been glass for longer than anybody alive could remember. Nothing could hurt them now. Not the iron men, not the men in gray, not anybody.
It had not mattered to her.
They were hers.
Corin knelt on the glass and looked down at the woman with the basket, a long way below, with her hand up and her face turned toward the light.
And he lifted his own hand. His gray one. The right. He did not know why. He lifted it a little, open, palm out, the way she had lifted hers. As if somebody had called his name across a market, and he was not sure, yet, who.
He held it there.
The woman far down in the glass did not move. Of course she did not. She was glass.
He put his hand down.
The Men Fall Back
When he could see again, the men in gray were going.
He lifted his head and wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist. The right one. The left one, on the gray, he could not feel, and he did not try. And he looked out across the glass.
There were three of them left. Standing. The big one with the broken nose. The young one with the big ears, on his feet again now, swaying, with his short sword gone and his hand pressed to his side. And the one who had stepped back from the line of spears. Three.
And the fourth was on the glass.
The one Lyra had cut. The square heavy one with the beard. He lay on his side by the dome where he had fallen, and the glass under him was dark, a wide dark pool on the clear black, spreading slowly. He had not moved. Corin knew, looking at him, that he would not move again.
The big one with the broken nose came across the glass.
He came slowly. With his hands empty and open at his sides, held out a little, so everybody could see them. He did not look at the Ashborn. He did not look at the line of spears that had swung toward him as he came. He walked straight past them, a pace off, as if they were not there. To the man on the glass.
And knelt.
He knelt the way Oren knelt. Slowly. With a hand on his knee. And he put his big scarred hand on the bearded man's shoulder. And turned him, a little. And looked at his face.
For a long time.
Then he said a name. Quietly. Corin did not hear it. He saw the big man's lips move, once, and then shut.
And then he got his arms under the bearded man, under the shoulders, and lifted. Not all of him. The top half. He could not lift all of him. He was too big and the bearded man was too heavy. He lifted him under the arms, with the bearded man's head lolling against his chest and his boots dragging on the glass, and began to walk backward. Away from the dome. Away from the spears. Toward the camp.
The other two came and took the bearded man's legs.
They did it without a word. The young one with the big ears, with his hand still pressed to his side and his face gray, and the third one. They took a leg each. And between the three of them they carried him. Slowly. Across the black glass. Away. The bearded man's head on the big one's chest. His arms hanging. A long dark smear on the glass behind them where his blood ran out.
Nobody tried to stop them.
The Ashborn stood in their line in front of the dome with their spears held low and watched them go. Kestrel had got up from her knees and given the child to Marrit, and stood at the end of the line, with her spear upright, and her face perfectly still. Sef. Oda. Brin, empty-handed. And Oren.
Oren had got up off his knees. Corin saw him now. He was standing a little in front of the iron thing in the side of the dome, the way in, with his sword hanging in his hand. He was not standing straight. He was leaning, a little, to one side, the way a man leans when his back has gone. And he was watching the three in gray carry the fourth away, and his face was the face of a man watching soldiers carry their dead off a field. Corin had seen that face somewhere before. He did not know where. On a lot of men, perhaps.
The silent man stood alone, out on the glass, a little way off, and watched too. His hands hung at his sides. He did not move.
And Lyra was beside him.
Corin did not know when she had got there. He had been weeping, and then he had been watching the men in gray, and then she was there. Kneeling on the glass at his side. Close. A hand's width away. Not touching. With her bent sword lying on the glass beside her knee, and her red bandaged hand held against her chest, and her face.
She was looking at his face.
He turned his head and let her.
He knew what she was seeing. He had seen it in the child's face. He had felt it with his own fingers, from the outside, cold and smooth and far away. The gray. Up the side of his neck. Over his jaw. To the corner of his mouth.
She looked at it for a long time.
She did not say anything.
That was the thing. She did not say anything. She did not say Oh, Corin. She did not say it's not so bad. She did not say Vespera can do something, or it'll stop, or you'll be all right, or any of the things somebody says. She did not even make a sound. She knelt on the black glass beside him with her bleeding hand against her chest and looked at the gray on his face, steadily, all of it, from his collar to the corner of his mouth. And her face did not change.
And she said nothing at all.
And that was how he knew.
He did not know her. He would never know her. He did not know what she had been to him, before, or what she was to him now, except what was written in the book and on his ribs in Vespera's ink. Partner. No lies between you.
But he knew her silence.
He knew it the way he knew her voice. She was not a person who said nothing. She was a person who said things. Too many things, sometimes, too fast, like a kettle on the boil. You talk like a kettle. It was in the book. And she was kneeling beside him now with her face white and her mouth shut, saying nothing, because there was nothing she could say that would not be a lie.
And she would not lie to him. It was on his ribs.
So she said nothing.
And he knew, from the nothing, how bad it was.
He looked at her. She looked at him.
And then, very slowly, he turned his right hand over on the glass. Palm up. His gray hand, with the dead black fingertips. And held it there. Between them. On the black glass. Not reaching for her. Not touching. Just there. Open.
She looked at it.
They had said, in the Eye, no touching. Not here, where his reach was awake and might go into her by itself. They had kept it, two days. Hands in pockets.
She looked at his open hand for a long time.
Then she put her left one down on the glass beside it. Not on it. Beside it. Palm up too. Her little finger a hair's breadth from his.
Not touching.
They knelt there on the black glass in the sun, side by side, with their hands open beside each other, not touching, and watched the men in gray carry their dead away.
