Chapter 77: Ferrand's Notebook, Part 4 of 5: Ferrand Comes Back
Ferrand Comes Back
And now it was Saturday. And she was lying under the canvas, in the heat, remembering. And it was noon.
She knew it was noon because the ticking had come. The Inquisitor's two, walking up along their own prints from the place where he had left them frozen at dusk. She heard them come closer, and closer, tick tick tick, and then slow, and stop, a little way off from the canvas. Standing in the sun. Waiting for the word.
And then, a little after, she heard footsteps.
Not iron. A man's. Light and quick and even. Coming across the glass from the northwest.
She sat up. The Inquisitor was already sitting up beside her. She had not heard him wake. He was looking out under the edge of the canvas, toward the sound, with his hat on and his face shut.
Master Ferrand came out of the glare.
He was alone. He was walking as neatly as ever, in his near-white coat and his gray hat and his gray gloves, with his silver rod at his left hip and his notebook in its pocket. His face was as calm as ever.
But he was pale. Paler than usual, which was very pale indeed. His lips were thin. There were two small spots of color, high up on his cheeks, as if somebody had pinched them. And when he came under the edge of the canvas and took off his hat, Wren saw that his fine fair hair was dark with sweat, and stuck to his forehead in points, and that his hand, holding the hat, was gripping the brim so hard that the gray kid of his glove had gone tight across the knuckles.
He was angry.
Wren had never seen him angry. She did not think anybody had. And he did not look angry, exactly. His voice, when he spoke, was as soft and polite as it had always been. But the anger was there, under the politeness, the way the heat was there under the glass. You could not see it. You could feel it coming off him.
"Good afternoon, Inquisitor," he said.
"Ferrand." The Inquisitor did not get up. "You're alone."
"I am." Master Ferrand sat down on a water cask. Carefully. As if he was afraid it might break. "My three are a few miles that way." He nodded east. "Frozen at dusk on the open glass, between the hills and the road. They'll be walking now. On the trail. The right trail." A pause. "I was not with them."
"No."
"No." Master Ferrand smiled. It was not a nice smile. "I was following a man who limps."
The Inquisitor said nothing.
"Yesterday," said Master Ferrand. "At noon. The trail split. Most of the prints went east, toward a road in the glass. A very straight road. And two sets went northwest. Close together. And one of them limping. Badly. A stick, I thought. A foot that doesn't bend. Just as you said, Inquisitor, at the trench. The lame one, and a friend to help him along." He turned his hat round in his hands. "And my three, being iron, and very stupid, went straight on east after the rest. They didn't even look at the other trail. And I thought, how clever of him. How very clever. He's sent the others on, and gone off the other way with one friend, to throw us off. And the iron is too stupid to see it."
He stopped turning the hat.
"So I followed them," he said. "Alone. All afternoon. Into the evening. Out into the open glass, a very long way. Dropping crumbs, they were. Oatcake. As if they wanted to be followed." His mouth went thin. "I should have thought about that."
"And?" said the Inquisitor.
"And it got dark," said Master Ferrand. "And cold. And I went on, with a lantern. And sometime after midnight, I found the end of the trail." He reached into the pocket of his near-white coat and took something out and threw it down on the glass between them.
It was a goatskin.
An old one. Brown and white, with the hair worn off in patches. Tied in a bundle with a cord, and the cord frayed and broken at one end, where it had been dragged. Dragged a very long way, over hot glass and cold. The underside of it was scraped smooth and shiny.
"That," said Master Ferrand, "is what I followed. For eleven hours. A goatskin on a string. Being pulled along by a man who was pretending to limp. Because the next prints, the ones after the skin, when he dropped it and turned for home, didn't limp at all." He smiled again. "Well. A little. At the end. I think he hurt himself. The felt wore through. There was blood on the glass, the last mile. That was some comfort."
Wren looked at the goatskin on the glass.
She did not know what she felt. Something. Something she did not have a name for, that made her want to laugh and made her want to cry, both at once. A man had walked all day on the burning glass, pretending to limp, dragging a goatskin, dropping crumbs, so that Master Ferrand would follow him the wrong way. And Master Ferrand had. For eleven hours.
And the man had hurt himself doing it. And had walked home anyway.
The Inquisitor looked at the goatskin. Then at Master Ferrand.
"The iron wasn't stupid," he said. Quietly. "The iron went where the word said."
Master Ferrand's face did not change. But the two spots of color on his cheeks got brighter.
"Yes," he said. "Thank you, Inquisitor. I had worked that out."
He stood up. He put his hat on the cask. He took off his coat, slowly, because it was hot, and folded it neatly over his arm. And he took his notebook out of the inside pocket, and opened it, and laid it down on a folded hide at the edge of the canvas, open, with the silver pencil across it, the way a man puts down a thing he is about to write in, and will be back for in a moment.
Then he went out into the sun, to the iron men, to say his word to the Inquisitor's two.
Wren got up too. She did not know why. To get water, perhaps. To get away from the canvas, and the goatskin, and the feeling in her chest. She got up and went past the folded hide at the edge of the canvas, toward the water casks.
And as she passed, she looked down.
She did not mean to. It was only that the notebook was there, open, on the hide, at the edge of her eye. White pages in the gray light under the canvas. And her eyes went to it the way eyes go to any writing. Without asking.
She saw the square, unjoined letters. Master Ferrand's hand. Like a child's.
She saw the top of the page.
There was a name there. Written larger than the rest, and underlined. A heading. The way you would head a page in a ledger for a new account.
Kross, S.
And under it, lines of writing. Small. Neat. Dates, she thought. Places. She could not read them. She did not stop to read them. She did not dare.
But beside the name, in the margin, she saw something else.
A small circle. Drawn in pencil. Neat and round and closed. Not filled in. Just a circle. The way the Archon's seal was a circle. The way a circle was drawn on a Ministry list, in the margin, beside a name, when the name was finished.
Already drawn.
Wren kept walking. She did not stop. She did not look back. She walked to the water casks and took the tin cup that hung on the side of one and filled it and drank, and her hands did not shake, and she was proud of that, later. Very proud. Prouder than of anything she had done in two years and five months.
When she came back, Master Ferrand was under the canvas again, writing in the notebook. On a different page.
He looked up at her as she passed. Mildly. With his pale colorless eyes.
"Thirsty work, the Glass," he said.
"Yes, Master Ferrand," said Wren.
And lay down in her place, and closed her eyes, and saw the circle.
Harrow
She did not sleep. She lay with her eyes shut for an hour, and saw the circle, and then she gave up and got up and went out from under the canvas to the water casks again, because it was something to do with her hands.
Harrow was there.
He was sitting on the glass in the narrow strip of shade behind the casks, with his back against one of them and his big legs stuck out in front of him, and his carbine across his knees. He was cleaning it. Slowly. With a rag and a little tin of oil. He did not look up when she came. But he moved his legs, a little, to make room.
She sat down. In the shade. Not close. There was not much shade, behind the casks. It was hot even there. The heat came up off the glass through the seat of her coat and she had to keep shifting.
For a while neither of them said anything.
Harrow was older than she had thought. She saw that, close to, in the daylight. She had thought of him as one of the Enforcers, big and gray and all the same, the way you think of the furniture in a room. But he was not the same. He was older than the others. Fifty, perhaps. His broken nose had been broken more than once. There was gray in his stubble and deep lines round his eyes, from screwing them up against weather. His hands on the rag were huge and scarred and very careful.
"You were sick," he said. Not looking up. "At the farm."
Wren's face went hot.
"Yes," she said.
He nodded. Rubbed the rag along the barrel. Looked down it, toward the sun, with one eye shut.
"I was sick my first time too," he said. "Twenty years back. A house on the docks. A man who wouldn't open his door." He shrugged. "We opened it for him. He was behind it." He put the rag down. "I was sick in the gutter. In front of everybody. The sergeant laughed."
Wren did not say anything.
"I'm not sick anymore," said Harrow. "Haven't been for years. You stop." He picked up the little tin of oil and looked at it as if he did not know what it was. "I used to think that was a good thing."
He was quiet for a moment.
"I've got a boy," he said. "About that one's size. The one at the window. At the farm." He held his big hand out flat, a little way above the glass. "Seven, he is. Eight next spring. He's at home with his mother. In the Ink Quarter. Above the baker's." He put his hand down. "I kept thinking. Walking up that hill after. With the iron in front and the clerk behind and that woman. I kept thinking, that's my boy at the window. If I stood in my own door. And somebody said a word."
He looked at Wren then. For the first time. His eyes were small and pale and red at the rims from the glare.
"And I'd have gone through," he said. "If I'd been told. That's what I kept thinking. If he'd said it to me, instead of the iron. Through. I'd have gone. I'd have done what the word said. Because that's what we do." He looked down at the carbine across his knees. "No different. Me and the iron. No different at all."
Wren did not know what to say. She had never had an Enforcer talk to her like this. Enforcers did not talk to cadets. They did not talk to anybody, much. They stood at doors, and walked behind, and did what they were told.
"You're different," she said at last. It came out small.
"How?"
She thought about it. She thought about it hard, because she wanted to give him a true answer, and not just a kind one.
"You thought about it," she said. "After. The iron doesn't think about it after."
Harrow looked at her for a long moment.
Then he laughed. Short. Not a happy laugh. But not an unkind one either.
"That's not much," he said.
"No," said Wren. "But it's something."
He picked up the rag again and went back to the carbine. Rubbing. Slowly. Looking down the barrel.
"The Inquisitor gave her all his money," he said after a while. "The widow. I saw."
"Yes."
"I've been with him eleven years," said Harrow. "On and off. I've stood at a lot of his doors. I never saw him do that before." He paused. "Mind, I never saw anybody killed in front of him before, either. Not like that. Not by accident." He looked across the glass, at the canvas where the Inquisitor lay under his hat. "He doesn't do accidents. He never has. Every door I ever stood at for him, he knew exactly what was behind it, and what he was going to take, and what he was going to leave. He's careful." He spat to one side, onto the hot glass, where it hissed. "That other one isn't careful. That other one just writes it down."
Wren went cold. In the heat. All at once.
She looked at Harrow. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the carbine. But she knew he had said it on purpose. To her. Because she was the only one he could say it to.
"Harrow," she said. Very low.
"Mm."
"If somebody said a word to you. Out here. Not the Inquisitor. Somebody else." She swallowed. "Would you go through?"
Harrow's hands stopped on the carbine.
He sat there for a long time. With the rag in one hand and the oil in the other, not moving. The heat shook the air over the glass. Somewhere out in the glare, the Inquisitor's two iron men stood in the sun, ticking softly, waiting for their word.
"I've got a boy at home," said Harrow at last. "Seven. Eight next spring."
That was all he said.
He did not say yes. He did not say no. He went back to cleaning the carbine.
But Wren sat beside him in the narrow shade behind the water casks, with the heat coming up through her coat, and thought that perhaps it was an answer. Perhaps it was the only kind of answer a man like Harrow had. And perhaps, out here, where nothing could be taken, it was enough to be going on with.
Walking with Lanterns
They walked at dusk again. All of them, this time. Master Ferrand too.
He did not go ahead. Wren had thought he might. She had thought he would want to get back to his three, out on the open glass to the east, and be with them when the sun came up. But he did not. He walked with the Inquisitor's party, at the back, behind the last Enforcer, with his near-white coat buttoned to the chin and his hat pulled down. He did not carry a lantern. He did not speak to anyone. He walked as neatly as ever, but slower than Wren had ever seen him walk, and once, when the line stopped, she looked back and saw him standing with his eyes shut, swaying very slightly, like a tree in a wind that nobody else could feel.
He had not slept, she thought. Not since Thursday. Two nights and two days on the Glass, walking. Even Master Ferrand had to sleep sometime.
She did not feel sorry for him. She tried to, a little, to see if she could. She could not.
She kept seeing the circle.
The Inquisitor walked at the front, at the edge of the light, as always. He stopped often, and crouched, and looked at the ground. The trail was clear here. Lots of prints, felted and soft. The drag, here and there, of the stick and the stiff foot. And then, in the second hour, the place where the trail split.
Wren saw it when Harrow held the lantern low. Most of the prints went straight on, north and east, the way the iron had gone. But two sets went off to the left. Close together. Into the dark. One of them with a long swinging drag beside it, like a man with a bad leg. And beside those, the long scraped smear of something pulled.
And following them, neat and small and even, one more set. A man's boots. Narrow. Precise. Each print exactly the same distance from the last.
The Inquisitor crouched over the small neat prints for a long time. He did not touch them. He did not say anything. Then he looked back, over his shoulder, at Master Ferrand standing at the back of the line with his eyes shut.
And Wren thought she saw, in the lantern light, the corner of the Inquisitor's mouth move. Just a little. Not a smile. Not quite.
She thought of the goatskin. Of a man walking all day on the burning glass, pretending to limp, so that someone would follow him the wrong way. And then, at the end, not having to pretend. She hoped somebody had helped him walk home. She found that she hoped it very much. And she did not even try to be ashamed of that.
The night went on. The cold came. The stars came down. The lanterns swung in their four small pools of gold, and the glass gave them back, upside down. The hum went on in Wren's teeth. Her feet went numb, and then her ankles, and then she stopped thinking about her feet at all. Mrs. Oddie's scarf grew stiff with frozen breath over her mouth.
And after three hours, perhaps, or four, they came to the iron men.
Master Ferrand's three.
They were standing on the open glass in a rough line, a little apart from each other, the way they had stopped when the cold caught them at dusk. Tall and gray and still. Their blank heads turned north. Their great shovel hands hanging. One of them had one foot a little off the ground, caught in the middle of a step, and stood there on the other foot, perfectly balanced, as if it had been carved that way.
The lanterns came up to them. And the light ran up their iron legs and their iron chests and their blank oval faces, and shone in the thin dark slits.
They did not move. They could not.
The Enforcers went round them in a wide circle. Nobody wanted to go close. Not even Harrow. Pike, the young one, held his lantern up high and stared at the nearest one's face as he went past, with his mouth open, and walked into Harrow's back.
The Inquisitor did not go round them. He walked straight up to the line, and between two of them, and through. As if they were posts. As if they were nothing. He did not look at them.
Master Ferrand stopped.
He stood in front of the middle one, the one with its foot off the ground, and looked up at it. For a long moment. His face, in the lantern light from behind him, was in shadow. Wren could not see it.
Then he put out one gray-gloved hand and laid it flat on the Eraser's chest. On the cold iron plate, over the place where the wheels were. And left it there. The way you might lay your hand on the neck of a horse.
"Tomorrow," he said to it. Very softly. Wren only just heard.
Then he took his hand away and walked on.
Wren went past them last. She did not look at their faces. She looked at the glass under her boots. And she found that she was counting her steps, without meaning to. The way she sometimes did in corridors at the Ministry, when she did not want to think about where she was going. One. Two. Three. Four.
On the far side of the line, she let out her breath. She had not known she was holding it.
Ahead of her, at the edge of the lantern light, the Inquisitor had stopped. He was standing quite still, with his head up. Looking north. Into the dark.
Wren came up beside him.
She could not see anything. Only the stars, and the faint gray shine of the glass going away, and a long low swell a mile or two off, a darker line against the dark.
"Sir?" she said.
He did not answer at once. He stood looking at the swell. As if he could see something on top of it that she could not. A man, perhaps. Standing. Looking back.
"They're close," he said at last. Very quietly. "Closer than they were." He was silent a moment. "They know we're here."
"How can you tell, sir?"
The Inquisitor did not answer that.
He only went on looking at the swell for a little while longer. And then he lifted one hand, in its gray glove. Not high. Only to the height of his chest. And held it there, open, toward the north. The way you lift your hand to a man across a street, when you know him, but you are not going to cross.
Then he lowered it. And walked on.
