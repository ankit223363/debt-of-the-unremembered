Chapter 77: Ferrand's Notebook, Part 5 of 5: The Vial
The Vial
In the small hours the Inquisitor called a halt.
He did not say why. He stopped, and turned round, and said, "Two hours," and that was all. But Wren saw him look, as he said it, at the back of the line. At Master Ferrand. Who had stopped when the line stopped and was standing very straight with his eyes shut, and had not opened them.
The Enforcers did not argue. They were too cold to argue. They put the lanterns down in a ring and spread the blankets inside it and lay down on them, all four of them, in a heap, like puppies, with their carbines underneath them and their coats over the top. Master Ferrand lay down a little apart, on his own blanket, on his back, with his hat over his face and his hands folded on his chest. He was asleep before Wren had finished unrolling her own blanket. She heard his breathing change.
She did not mean to sleep. She meant to sit up again, as she had on the first night, and keep watch.
But she had been walking for six hours in the cold, and she had not slept properly for three days, and she lay down on her blanket just for a moment, just to rest her back. And the stars came down round her, and the hum came up through the glass into her bones, and she was gone.
She woke because she was cold.
That was all. Not a sound. Not a dream. Only cold. Cold so deep it had gone right into the middle of her and found the warm place there and begun, very slowly, to put it out. She opened her eyes and could not, for a moment, feel her face. She could not feel her hands. She lay there in the dark and the cold and thought, quite calmly, I should get up. And did not.
Then she turned her head.
And saw the Inquisitor.
He was sitting up. A little way off from the others, by the lantern at the edge of the ring, which had burned down very low, to a small blue bead of flame. He was sitting on his blanket with his knees drawn up and his cape round his shoulders. He did not see her. His eyes were shut.
His right glove was off.
She saw that first. The bare hand, very pale in the faint blue light. Thin. Long-fingered. And in it, held up close to his ear, between his finger and thumb, something small.
A vial.
One of the tiny glass ones. Wren knew them. She had filled a great many. The small ones, no longer than a finger, with a cork stopper sealed in wax. The ones the Ministry used for the little memories. The light ones. A song. A smell. A word. A moment.
This one had a ribbon tied round its neck.
A thin ribbon, tied in a bow. It had been pink, once. Wren could see that, even in the blue light. It had faded now almost to white, the way a ribbon fades that has been handled a great deal, and kept close, and taken out often, and put back.
The Inquisitor was holding it to his ear.
Wren lay very still and watched him.
She knew what he was doing. Everybody who worked with the jars knew. You did not need Extraction for it. You did not need anything. If you held a small one close to your ear, very close, in a quiet place, you could hear it. Faintly. The memory inside. Not all of it. Not the way you would if it was put into you. Just the sound of it, if it had a sound. Like hearing a voice through a wall. Like hearing the sea in a shell.
The clerks in the Long Office did it sometimes, when they thought nobody was looking. It was not allowed. They did it anyway.
Wren had done it herself. Once.
The Inquisitor held the vial to his ear with his bare hand and his eyes shut.
And his face had changed.
She had seen it go smooth when he slept. She had thought that was the strangest thing she would ever see on the Glass. It was not.
This was not smooth. This was open.
That was the only word she could find for it. Open. Like a door. Like a window, in the morning, when somebody pushes the shutters back and the light comes in. All the lines were still there, the deep hard ones from nose to mouth, the ones round the eyes. But they had all, somehow, turned the other way. As if his whole face, which had been shut tight against something for as long as she had known him, had for this one moment stopped holding on.
He looked like a man who has come home.
That was what she thought. Lying there frozen on her blanket, watching him. He looked like a man who has been away a very long time, on a hard road, in the cold. And has come home at last, late at night, and stopped in the dark hall with his coat still on. And heard, from the kitchen at the back of the house, somebody laughing.
And has stood there. In the hall. Not going in. Just listening. Just letting it be there.
Wren could not hear what was in the vial. She was too far away. But she did not need to. She could see it on his face.
Somebody was laughing.
She did not know how long she lay there watching. Long enough for the cold to go a little further into her. Long enough for her to stop feeling her feet altogether.
Then the Inquisitor opened his eyes.
He did not look at her. Not at first. He lowered the vial, slowly, from his ear, and looked at it in his bare palm. The tiny glass. The faded ribbon. He looked at it the way you look at a letter you have read so many times you know every word, and read again anyway.
Then he looked up. Straight at her.
He had known she was awake. Of course he had. He always knew.
And the open face did not close. Not at once. It stayed open, for a moment, in the blue light, looking at her. As if he had been caught, and did not mind being caught. As if, for once, he did not have the strength to put the mask back on, and did not want to.
Somebody
"Who is it?"
She had not meant to say it. It came out of her mouth before she knew it was there. Hoarse, through Mrs. Oddie's frozen scarf. Too loud in the silence. One of the Enforcers in the heap stirred, and muttered, and was still.
Wren lay frozen on her blanket and wished she could take it back.
You did not ask the Inquisitor questions. Not like that. Not about himself. In two years and five months she had asked him a great many questions. Shall I file this, sir? Shall I bring the carriage, sir? Shall I tell him you are out? She had never once asked him anything that was not about work. Nobody did. It was not done. It was like asking a wall what it had for breakfast.
She waited for his face to close.
It did not.
He looked at her across the small blue light of the lantern. For a long time. With the vial in his bare palm, and the faded ribbon trailing over his fingers. And she saw him think about it. About whether to answer. About what to say. She saw him think about not saying anything at all, and turning away, and putting the vial back in whatever pocket it lived in, and the glove back on, and the mask.
He did not do any of those things.
He did not hide it. That was what she would remember. He did not close his hand over it. He did not put it away. He let it lie there, open, in his palm, in the light, where she could see it.
"Somebody," he said.
Quietly. Not unkindly. As if it was the answer, and the whole answer, and there was nothing to add.
Then he looked down at it again. At the tiny glass. At the ribbon.
"Somebody I would not like to see on another form," he said.
And he closed his fingers over it, gently, the way he had closed the farmer's widow's fingers over the coins. And held it like that, in his closed bare hand, against his chest. And did not say anything else.
Wren lay on her blanket and did not say anything either.
She was thinking.
She was thinking of his desk. In the glass office at the end of the Long Office, with the green lamp and the tea tray and the paper of barley sugar. She had stood beside that desk a thousand times. She knew everything on it. Every pen. Every paperweight. Every stack of files, and the order they went in.
And she knew the list.
It lay on the left side of the desk, under the black book, squared to the edge. A long sheet of good paper, with names on it. In his hand. Not many. A dozen, perhaps. Some with lines through them. Some with small marks beside them that she did not understand. She had never been meant to see it. She had seen it anyway, the way you see things on a desk you stand beside every day. Without reading. Just knowing what shapes were there.
And near the bottom of the list, she had once seen two words.
Not a name. Not quite. A first letter, and then a word.
E. Auditor.
She had wondered about that. At the time. She had wondered who E. was, and what kind of Auditor, and why an Auditor should be on a list in the Inquisitor's hand, under the black book, squared to the edge of the desk. And then she had stopped wondering, because it was not her business, and there were forms to file.
She thought about it now.
She thought about it lying on her back on the Glass, in the cold, looking at the Inquisitor's closed bare hand against his chest, with a faded pink ribbon trailing out between his fingers.
Somebody I would not like to see on another form.
She did not say anything.
She could have. She could have asked. Is it E., sir? Is it the Auditor? The words were there. Right there, behind her teeth. She could feel them.
She did not say them.
It was not because she was afraid. She thought about that afterward, a long time, and she was sure. It was not fear. She had been afraid of him for two years and five months, a little, the way everybody in the Long Office was afraid of him. And she found, lying there, that she was not afraid of him now. Not at all. Not since the farm.
It was something else.
It was that he had not hidden it. He had let her see. He had held it out in his bare hand in the light and let her look, and he had answered her, and the answer had been true. She knew it had been true. She had heard enough lies in the white rooms under the Long Office to know a true thing when she heard one.
And you did not take a true thing like that, held out to you like that, and ask for more.
You let it be.
So she let it be. She lay on her back on her blanket and said nothing, and after a while the Inquisitor put his glove back on, slowly, one finger at a time, with the vial still in his other hand. And then he put the vial away inside his cape, on the left side, over the heart. And lay down again on his blanket, on his back, with his cape over him.
And she heard his breathing slow, and slow, and go deep.
Asleep. Again. As easily as a child.
Her Book
Wren could not sleep after that.
She made herself sit up. It hurt. Everything had gone stiff with cold, her neck and her shoulders and her knees, and when she moved it was like moving a door that has rusted on its hinges. But she sat up. And pulled the blanket round her. And rubbed her hands together inside her gloves until she could feel her fingers again, a little, as hot needles. And then she reached into the inside pocket of her white coat, on the left, and took out her book.
It was a small brown book. Very plain. Cheap brown card covers, a little bent at the corners now, and a dozen sheets of rough paper sewn in down the middle with brown thread. She had bought it in the autumn from a stall on Quill Row for fourpence. Master Ferrand knew about it. He had told her so, in the cellar, in his pleasant voice. You keep a book, I believe. Fourpence, on Quill Row. She had not known how he knew. She had not asked.
She opened it now, by the last blue bead of the lantern.
She did not need to read it. She knew what was in it. Every line. She had written every one.
It was a list. Of the things she had taken.
Not all of them. She had taken too many to write them all down. Just some. The ones that stayed with her, afterward. The ones she could not stop thinking about in the night. She had started it in the autumn, after a bad day, without really knowing why. And she had gone on. One line for each. Small, in pencil, in her neat cadet's hand.
Boy, about seven. Fish Stairs. Health cart. His first fish. Given back to nobody.
Woman, old, the river stairs. Her husband's voice saying goodnight. Given back to nobody.
Man, about forty, the Ministry cells. The name of the street he grew up on. Given back to nobody.
That was how they all ended. Given back to nobody. She did not know why she wrote that. It was true, that was all. The things she took went into jars, and the jars went into the Ministry's cellars, or the Bank's vaults, or the Health's carts, and none of them ever went back to the people they came out of. Not one. Not ever. So she wrote it down. Every time. As if writing it was a kind of keeping.
She turned the pages. Slowly. In the blue light. Past all the lines. Past the place where she had once started to write Sergeant Moll, the night she brought him in, and stopped after the first letter, because nothing had been taken from him and nothing ever would be, not while the Inquisitor said so. To the end. To the last line. To the first empty page.
She took the stub of pencil out of the spine.
And she sat there with it in her stiff gloved fingers, over the empty page, for a long time.
Because the thing she wanted to write did not belong in this book.
This was a book of things taken. Things she had taken, with her own hands, out of other people's heads. That was what it was for. That was the whole of it. And she had not taken anything from Corwen Lusk. She had not touched him. She had not reached for him. She had only stood in the snow and watched while a word said through, and a machine went through, and a man was not there anymore.
That was not taking. That was something else.
She did not have a word for it.
But she found that she wanted it written down. Somewhere. In something. So that it was kept. So that it did not just go out of the world the way he had gone out of it, in a yard in the hills, with nobody's name for it and a sack over his boots.
The Inquisitor had learned his name. She had seen his lips move. But the Inquisitor would not write it down. He did not keep a book like hers.
Master Ferrand had written something. She had seen him. But she did not think it was the man's name.
Nobody else would.
So she did.
She wrote it slowly. Pressing hard, because her fingers were stiff and the pencil kept slipping. Her neat cadet's letters came out shaky and big, bigger than any of the other lines in the book. Like a child's.
Corwen Lusk.
She stopped. Then went on.
Farmer.
She stopped again. She looked at it. She thought about the gate. The five gray bars. The pitchfork across his chest. The slow thick voice. Nobody comes through my yard. She thought about how he had not hurried. How he had walked across his own yard to his own gate, slowly, the way he must have done every morning of his life, and stood in it.
And she wrote the last line.
He stood in his own gate.
Then she stopped.
She did not write given back to nobody. It did not belong. Nothing had been taken. Nothing had been given. A man had stood in his own gate, and that was all, and that was the whole of what she wanted kept.
She looked at the three lines for a long time. They were the only lines in the whole book that were not about something she had taken.
They were the first lines of something else. She could feel that, sitting there in the cold. Like the first step on a new road. She did not know where the road went. She did not know what kind of list this was, or what it was for, or who else might go on it.
But she knew it had begun.
She closed the book. She put the pencil back in the spine. She put the book back in the inside pocket of her white coat, on the left, over her heart. The way the Inquisitor had put away the vial.
And she lay down.
The Fourth Note
She lay on her back on her blanket and looked up.
The lantern had gone out. She had not seen it go. The last blue bead of flame had shrunk, and shrunk, and then it had simply not been there anymore, and the dark had come in over the little ring of sleepers all at once, like water closing over a stone.
But it was not really dark. Not on the Glass. It was never really dark on the Glass. There were the stars.
Wren had never seen so many. She had not known there were so many. In the city you saw a handful, between the roofs, through the smoke, on a clear night in winter. Here they were everywhere. So thick that in places they ran together into long pale smears, like spilled milk. And they came all the way down to the edge of the world on every side, and the Glass took them, and gave them back. So that when she turned her head on the blanket and looked along the ground, she saw stars there too. Under her. Faint and blurred and upside down, in the dark mirror of the glass.
She lay in the middle of a great black ball of stars, on a blanket, on the cold, and listened.
The hum. Always the hum. Thin and high and endless in her teeth.
The breathing of the Enforcers in their heap. Slow and heavy. Harrow snoring a little.
Master Ferrand's breathing, a little way off. Very quiet. Very even. Like a clock.
And the Inquisitor's. Close by. Slow and deep. The breathing of a man at the very bottom of sleep.
No ticking. The iron was all frozen, far behind them now. Hers. His. All of it. Standing in the dark like posts, waiting for the sun.
She was so tired. She was more tired than she had ever been in her life. Her whole body ached with it, down to the bones. But her mind would not stop. It kept going round. The gate. The coins. The ashes. The trench. The goatskin. The circle in the margin. The ribbon. Somebody. The three lines in her book. Round and round and round, like a wheel that has been set spinning and has nothing to stop it.
And without meaning to, without even knowing she was going to do it, she began to hum.
Very softly. Under her breath. Into Mrs. Oddie's scarf.
Three notes.
The same three notes she always hummed. She did not know where they came from. She had never known. She had hummed them all her life, as far back as she could remember, when she was frightened, or tired, or could not sleep. Low. Then high. Then a little lower, not quite as low as the first. And then stop. Short. As if there should be something more, and there was not.
She had hummed them in the Long Office once, without thinking, and the whole room had gone quiet. Forty clerks, and not a pen moving. She had hummed them to Sergeant Moll, in Room Nine, and he had hummed back. She had hummed them in the Inquisitor's own house, at the bottom of his scrubbed pale stairs, and Lady Lyra Thorne had looked at her as if she had seen a ghost, and hummed a fourth. It was my mother's.
Low. High. A little lower.
And stop.
She hummed them into the dark. Once. Twice. It helped. It always helped. It was like holding something. Like a hand round a warm cup. The wheel in her mind slowed, a little. Her breathing slowed.
Three notes. Into the dark.
Low. High. A little lower.
And stop.
And from the dark, very softly, somebody hummed the fourth.
It came from close by. From her left. From the blanket where the Inquisitor lay on his back in the starlight with his cape over him and his hands folded on his chest. A low, quiet, husky sound. Hardly a sound at all. A breath with a note in it.
It went up. From where hers had stopped. Up, and up, a little higher than her high note, and then hung there. Held. Not finishing. Not coming down. Hanging in the cold air over the Glass like a question nobody was going to answer.
And then it stopped.
Just stopped. In the middle. The way a man stops when he hears himself doing something he did not mean to do.
Wren lay very still.
She did not turn her head. She did not breathe. She lay on her back on her blanket under the great black ball of stars and listened as hard as she had ever listened to anything in her life.
Nothing.
The hum of the Glass. The Enforcers, breathing. Harrow's small snore. Master Ferrand's clock-breath.
And from her left, from the Inquisitor's blanket, nothing at all. No breath. Not for a long moment. As if he too was lying awake in the dark, very still, not breathing, listening.
Then, at last, his breath came again. Slow. Careful. Too careful. The breath of a man pretending to be asleep.
Wren closed her eyes.
She did not hum again. Neither did he.
