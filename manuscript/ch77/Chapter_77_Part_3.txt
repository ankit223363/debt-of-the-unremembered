Chapter 77: Ferrand's Notebook, Part 3 of 5: Kross Sleeps
Kross Sleeps
They walked again in the afternoon, when the worst of the heat had passed. And at dusk the Inquisitor stopped.
He stopped the way he did everything. Without saying anything first. He was walking at the front, with his empty hands and his gray cape. And then he was not walking. He was standing still on the glass, looking at the sky, where the sun was going down red and huge on the western edge. And the Enforcers behind him stopped too, and bumped into each other, and stood there.
Behind them, the ticking of his two iron men slowed. And slowed. And stopped.
Wren turned round to look. She could see them, a little way back, two tall gray shapes on the darkening glass. Standing still. Frozen. With one foot a little in front of the other, in the middle of a step. Like men in a picture.
"We stop here," said the Inquisitor.
They stopped there.
The Enforcers did not know what to do. Nobody had told them what you did on the Glass at night. They stood about in the cold that was already coming up out of the ground, stamping their feet and blowing on their hands, while the light went. Then Harrow, the big one with the broken nose, said they should put the canvas up for a windbreak, and they did, though there was no wind. And they got out the blankets. And they lit two of the lanterns and set them on the glass, and the light from them ran out across the dark ground in long yellow streaks, and shone back up off it, so that it looked as if the lanterns were standing in water.
The cold came fast. Faster than Wren could have believed. One moment the glass under her was warm through her boots. The next it was cool. The next it was cold. And then it was so cold that her feet hurt, and she could see her breath, and the Enforcers had stopped talking and were huddled together under their blankets like sheep in a storm.
And the Inquisitor lay down.
He did it simply. He took one of the blankets and spread it on the glass, a little apart from the others, by the middle lantern. He took off his hat and set it beside him, upside down. He lay down on his back on the blanket, with his gray cape over him. He folded his gloved hands on his chest.
And he went to sleep.
At once. Wren saw it happen. She was sitting a few paces off with her own blanket round her shoulders, watching him, because she did not know what else to do. And she saw him close his eyes. And she saw his face change. All at once. The way a field changes when a cloud goes off the sun.
It went smooth.
She had never seen his face smooth. In two years and five months. His face was a face of lines. Deep ones, from the nose to the corners of the mouth. Little ones, round the eyes. A line between the eyebrows that never went away, even when he smiled, which was not often. A tired face. A face like a page that has been folded and unfolded too many times.
And now the lines went. Not all of them. But most. The line between his eyebrows went. The corners of his mouth came up, a little, out of their long hard grooves. His breathing went slow and deep and even, almost at once, the breathing of a man so tired he has gone straight down to the very bottom of sleep without stopping on the way.
He looked young.
That was what Wren thought, sitting in the cold, looking at him by the lantern light. He looked young. Much younger than he was. Like a picture of himself from a long time ago. He looked like somebody's son. Like a boy who has been out all day in the fields and come home and fallen asleep in his clothes in front of the fire, and his mother has put a blanket over him and let him be.
She watched him for a long time.
The Enforcers went to sleep, one by one, in their huddle. Wren heard their breathing change. Somebody snored. The lanterns burned low and steady. The stars came out, more of them than she had ever seen, more than she had known there were, and came down to the edges of the Glass and went on below it, in the dark mirror of the ground, so that she was sitting in the middle of a great black ball of stars.
And the Inquisitor slept.
Wren did not. She sat with her blanket round her and her knees up and watched him. Nobody had asked her to. Nobody had said keep watch. The Enforcers had not set a guard. They had not thought of it. There was nothing out here to guard against, they thought. And the iron men were frozen.
But Wren sat up. And watched the slight gray shape on the blanket by the lantern. And every time she started to nod off, she pinched the back of her own hand, hard, and sat up straighter.
Twice in the night, when the cold got so bad that she could no longer feel her face, she got up and took another blanket from the pack and laid it over him, on top of the cape, and tucked the edges in under him the way you tuck in a child. And she moved the middle lantern closer to him, so that its small warmth was near his head. And each time he did not wake. And each time she went back and sat down again, with her back against the water casks and her knees up, and pinched her hand, and watched.
She did not know why.
She thought about it, in the long cold hours, and she still did not know. It was not her duty. She was not a guard. She was a cadet. She took dictation and carried tea and collected memories in jars. Nobody had told her to sit up all night in the cold watching the Inquisitor sleep.
But she did.
Perhaps it was because she had never seen him asleep before. Perhaps it was because he looked so young. Perhaps it was the shake in his hand, at the farm. Once. Only once. Or the hat, held against his chest. Or the coins rolling on the step.
Perhaps it was because, for the first time since she had known him, he looked like somebody who might need watching over.
She watched over him until the sky in the east went gray. Then he woke, all at once, and sat up, and put his hat on, and looked at her.
He did not ask if she had slept. She was sure he could see that she had not. But he did not ask.
He only looked at her for a moment, with his tired face coming back over his young one, line by line, like a mask being put on.
"Thank you, Halloway," he said.
And she did not know how he knew.
Two Ways
Master Ferrand came back to them that morning. Friday. He came walking out of the dazzle from the north an hour after sunrise, alone, in his near-white coat, as neat as if he had stepped out of his own front door. Behind him, far off, Wren could hear the ticking of his three, coming on slowly at the speed of a man.
He had walked all night. Wren saw that at once. Not because he looked tired. He did not look tired. Master Ferrand never looked anything. But there was frost on the brim of his gray hat, white and crisp, that the sun had not yet melted. And his lips were faintly blue.
"Good morning, Inquisitor," he said. "I trust you slept."
"I did." The Inquisitor was sitting on a water cask, drinking from a tin cup. He did not get up. "You walked."
"I walked." Master Ferrand smiled. A small, pleasant, polite smile. "My three froze at dusk, of course. Rook was quite right about that. They stood like posts all night. But I found I could walk on without them. So I did, a little way, with a lantern, to see what there was to see." He took out his notebook. "The trail is clear. They're walking by night too, it seems. Eight or nine of them, and the harvester. The lame one leaves a very clear print." He turned a page. "And there's a trench, Inquisitor. A great long trench in the glass, full of dead soldiers, standing up. Rather extraordinary. They went down into it."
The Inquisitor drank his tea.
"And so," said Master Ferrand, "I wondered how you would like to go on."
The Inquisitor did not answer for a while. He sat on his cask with his cup in both gloved hands and looked out at the glass going away north, shining.
"We'll walk at night," he said at last. "With lanterns. As they do. The iron freezes at dusk. Let it. Leave my two standing where they stop, every evening. They'll walk on in the morning, when the sun warms them, along our prints, and catch us up by noon. They always do."
"That's very slow, Inquisitor."
"It's very steady."
"With respect," said Master Ferrand. Pleasantly. "Steady isn't what His Excellency asked for." He closed his notebook. "I thought I might take my three ahead. By day. When they're warm and walking. On the trail. And let you follow by night, at your own pace."
The Inquisitor looked at him.
Wren, standing a little way off by the canvas, held her breath. She did not know why. Nothing was happening. Two men were talking about how to walk. But the air between them had changed. The way the air in the Long Office changed when somebody important came in, and forty clerks all at once stopped scratching with their pens.
"Two ways," said the Inquisitor quietly.
"Two ways," agreed Master Ferrand. "One fast. One steady. Between them, surely, we can't miss."
The Inquisitor was quiet a moment longer.
Then he nodded.
"Go on, then," he said.
"Thank you, Inquisitor." Master Ferrand touched the brim of his frosty hat. Then he turned to go. And then, half turned, with his pale face over his shoulder and his pale eyes very mild, he said one more thing.
"If you would, Inquisitor," he said, "do try to keep up."
And he walked off north, into the dazzle, toward the sound of his three. Neat and small and pale, getting smaller. Until the glare took him, and he was gone.
The Inquisitor sat on his cask and finished his tea.
He did not say anything. He did not look after Master Ferrand. He drank his tea slowly, to the bottom, and then he turned the tin cup upside down and shook the last drops out onto the glass, where they hissed, and were gone.
Then he looked up at Wren.
"Halloway," he said.
"Sir?"
"How many people do you think His Excellency sent north, to bring back one harvester?"
Wren did not understand the question. She thought about it. "Six iron men, sir. Four Enforcers. You. Me. Master Ferrand."
"Yes," said the Inquisitor. "And which of us do you think he sent to watch the others?"
Wren opened her mouth. And shut it.
The Inquisitor did not wait for her to answer. He got up off the cask, stiffly, and put the tin cup in his pocket, and pulled his gloves tight at the wrists.
"Get some sleep," he said. "We walk at dusk."
Under Protest
She did not sleep that day either, not much. She lay under the canvas in the heat and thought about Old Rook.
She had not thought about him for days. Not since the cellar. But now, with Master Ferrand gone off north into the glare with his three, and the Inquisitor's question going round in her head, she found she was thinking about him. About his hands.
They had shaken. All the time. That was the first thing anybody noticed about Thaddeus Rook. An old, bent, bald man in a black skullcap and an apron stained with oil, with a magnifying lens hanging round his neck on a ribbon, and hands that shook like leaves. They shook when he held a cup. They shook when he pointed. They shook when he took off his skullcap to scratch his head.
Except when he was working.
Wren had watched him wind the Erasers in the brick vault under the Records yard, by the light of the caged lamps. Six of them, lying on their trestles in their open crates, like iron men laid out for burying. And Rook had gone from one to the next with the bird-shaped brass key, and fitted it into the hole in the brass plate in the small of each one's back, and turned it. Click. Click. Click. Many clicks. And his hands had not shaken at all. Not once. They had been as steady as stone. As if the work went into them and held them still from the inside.
And before he closed the fifth one up, Wren had seen him do something.
He had taken a little sharp steel point out of his apron pocket. And he had bent over the open chest of the fifth Eraser, over the wheels, with his lens screwed into his eye, and scratched something on the inside of the chest plate. Quickly. Where nobody would ever see it once the plate was shut.
She had seen two words of it, upside down, before he closed it.
UNDER PROTEST.
She had not understood, then. She had thought it was a joke. An old man's joke, the kind old men make to themselves.
She did not think it was a joke now.
She thought of Rook bent over his iron men in the cellar, with his steady hands, building them as well as he knew how, the best work of his life perhaps, because that was the only way he knew how to work. And knowing what they were for. And scratching his two words inside where nobody would see, so that at least the iron itself would know, so that at least it would be written down somewhere, that one man had not wanted this.
They do what the word says, Rook had told the Inquisitor. Not what you mean.
He had been warning him. She saw that now. Standing in the cellar in his oily apron with his hands shaking again, now that the work was done. He had been telling the Inquisitor, as plainly as an old clockmaker could tell an Inquisitor anything: Be careful what you say to them. Be careful who says it.
And the Inquisitor had listened. She remembered that too. He had stood with his hands behind his back and listened to every word, and nodded, and said Thank you, Master Rook.
And then Master Ferrand had said Through, in a farmyard in the hills.
Wren lay under the canvas with the heat pressing down and wondered which of the six it had been. The one that walked through the gate. Whether it was the fifth. Whether it had UNDER PROTEST scratched inside its chest, over the wheels, while it walked through Corwen Lusk.
She hoped not.
She hoped it was.
She did not know which she hoped.
The Trench at Night
They walked at dusk. And all that night. And it was the strangest night of Wren's life.
She would try to remember it afterward, in order, and she never could. It came back to her in pieces. Like pictures in a book, with the pages between them torn out.
The lanterns. That was the first piece. Four of them, carried by the four Enforcers, one each, swinging on their handles at the ends of their arms. They made four small pools of yellow light that moved across the dark glass, and the glass took the light and threw it back up, so that each Enforcer walked in a puddle of gold with his own legs shining underneath him, upside down. And beyond the lantern light, nothing. Black. And then the stars.
The cold. That was another piece. The cold came up through her boots and into her bones and stayed there. She had put on everything she had. Her white coat, and her gray cloak over it, and both pairs of gloves, and a scarf of Mrs. Oddie's knitting round her face. It made no difference. The cold went through all of it as if it was paper.
The Inquisitor at the front. That was another. A slight dark shape walking just inside the edge of the first lantern's light, never in the middle of it, always at the edge. He did not carry a lantern himself. He did not need one. He walked as if he could see in the dark. Every now and then he stopped, and the whole line stopped behind him, and he crouched down, and Harrow brought the lantern close, and the Inquisitor looked at the ground.
At the prints.
Wren looked too, the first time. She had not known you could see prints on glass. You could not, mostly. But here and there, where the glass was a little softer, or where it had been warm when the walkers passed, there were marks. Faint. Rounded. The prints of feet wrapped in something soft. Lots of them. Going north.
And among them, every so often, a different mark. A small round dent. And beside it, a drag. A long smear, as if something had been pulled along the ground.
"A stick," said the Inquisitor quietly. Touching the round dent with one gloved finger. "And a foot that doesn't bend." He touched the smear. "He's still walking on it."
He stood up. And walked on.
And then the trench. That was the biggest piece. The one that came back to her most often, in the nights after.
They came to it a little after midnight. The Inquisitor stopped at the edge of the lantern light, and the line stopped behind him, and nobody said anything. And Wren came up beside Harrow and looked past his lantern. And the ground in front of them was gone.
Not gone. Cut. A great long gash in the glass, zigzagging away to the left and the right into the dark, as far as the lanterns could reach. Deep. Deeper than a man. With steep shining walls. And standing in it, all along it, as far as she could see, in rows, with their faces turned north, were men.
Wren made a sound. She did not mean to.
They were not men. Not anymore. They were glass. Pale green glass, clouded, like the glass of an old bottle that has lain in a river for a hundred years. And inside the glass, faint and dark and blurred, there were bones. And the shapes of coats. And belts, and buckles, and the long hard shapes of pikes. Standing. Hundreds of them. Shoulder to shoulder. Frozen in the act of looking north, waiting for something to come over the rim of the world.
The lanterns' light went down into the trench and lit them up from below. All those faces. All those almost-faces. Long noses and open mouths and eyes that were only darker places in the green.
Nobody moved. Not even the Enforcers. Pike, the young one, had his free hand over his mouth.
The Inquisitor stood at the very edge of the trench and looked down into it for a long time.
Then he took his hat off.
Wren had stopped being surprised. She stood beside Harrow in the cold and watched him do it, the slight gray man at the edge of the great dark gash, with his hat held against his chest and the lantern light coming up from below onto his face. Making it all shadows. Making it look older than it was, and younger, both at once.
He stood like that for so long that Wren's feet stopped hurting and went numb.
Then he put his hat back on. And found the place, a little way along, where the wall of the trench had slumped down in long melted ridges like the side of a candle. And went down it. Carefully. Sitting. Sliding. The way, Wren supposed, the others had gone down it before him.
And they went through the trench. Among the dead. In the dark. With the lanterns swinging and the glass men standing on either side so close that Wren could have touched them, and did not, and kept her arms tight to her sides, and her eyes on Harrow's back, and walked.
On the far side, at the top, there was chalk on the wall.
The Inquisitor saw it first. An arrow. A white chalk arrow, drawn on the glass with a big clumsy point. And under it, in letters as clumsy as the arrow, the letters of a child, words. He held the lantern up to it. And read them. And Wren, beside him, read them too.
THIS WAY IF YOURE NICE.
The Inquisitor looked at the words for a long moment.
Then he did a thing Wren had never seen him do. Never, in two years and five months.
He laughed.
Not loudly. Hardly at all. A breath out through his nose, short and soft, and the corners of his mouth going up for a moment out of their deep lines. And then gone.
"Well," he said. Very quietly. To the chalk. "We'll see."
And he went the way the arrow pointed.
