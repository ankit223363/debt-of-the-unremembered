Chapter 77: Ferrand's Notebook, Part 1 of 5: Saturday Morning
Saturday Morning
The farmer's name was Corwen Lusk, and he stood in his own gateway with a pitchfork, and Wren would remember for the rest of her life that the machine had not meant anything at all.
She was remembering it now. She did not want to. She was lying on her back under a sheet of gray canvas on the Glass, in the morning, with the heat already coming down through the canvas like a hand pressing on her face, and she was trying to sleep, and she could not, and so she was remembering.
She had been remembering it for five days. In the carts. On the road. In the ashes. On the Glass. Every time she closed her eyes it was there, waiting, like a picture hung on the inside of her eyelids. The yard. The snow. The gate. The man.
She opened her eyes instead.
The canvas was a hand's width above her face. It was stretched on four short iron poles that the Enforcers carried in a bundle on their backs, and it was not big. Big enough for three people to lie under, if they lay close. There were three of these shades, set out on the glass in a row, with their edges nearly touching. Wren lay under the middle one. On her left, the Inquisitor. On her right, nobody. One of the Enforcers had been meant to lie there. But the Enforcers did not like to lie near the Inquisitor, and they had all crowded under the other two shades instead, and so the space on her right was empty.
The light came through the canvas gray and thick, like light through a dirty window. It made everything under it look the same color. Her hands. Her white coat, gone gray. The Inquisitor's face.
She turned her head and looked at him.
He was asleep. He was always asleep, now, in the day. That was the strangest thing. Stranger than the Glass. In two years and five months of standing beside his desk, and walking behind him down corridors, and waiting outside doors for him, and carrying his tea, she had never once seen Silas Kross asleep. Not once. She had not been sure he did it. Mrs. Oddie said he sat on the fifth step all night, some nights. Wren had believed her.
And then they had come out onto the Glass, and on the very first night he had lain down on a blanket and gone to sleep as if somebody had blown him out like a candle.
He was lying on his back now, with his gray cape over him like a blanket and his plain gray hat over his face to keep the light off. His hands were folded on his chest. His gloves were on. He was breathing slowly and deeply. A thin man. Not tall. Middle height, and slight, so that under the cape he hardly made a shape at all. Wren could see the edge of his hair under the brim of the hat. Fair once. Graying now. Thin.
She watched him breathe.
Outside the canvas, the Glass hummed. It never stopped. It was a sound so high and so thin and so steady that after the first day you stopped hearing it, mostly, the way you stop hearing a clock. But it was always there. Under everything. Like a held breath.
And under the hum, further off, there was the other sound.
Tick. Tick. Tick.
The two Erasers that were theirs. The Inquisitor's two. They were somewhere out on the glass behind them, to the south, walking. They had been frozen all night, where the Inquisitor had left them at dusk, standing in the dark with their faces to the north. And now the sun was up and had warmed them, and they were walking again, at the speed of a man, along the line of their own frozen footprints, to catch up. They would be here by noon. They always were.
Wren listened to them tick and did not want to.
She closed her eyes again. And there it was, waiting for her. The yard, and the snow, and the gate.
She let it come.
The Gate
It had been Monday. The third day out of the city.
They had come up out of the fens in the night, the two black carts and the four Enforcers riding beside them, and in the morning they had been in the hills. Wren had never seen hills like that before. Low and round and white, one behind another, like loaves of bread under a cloth. Snow on all of them. Stone walls running over them in long gray lines, up one side and down the other, dividing the snow into fields. Sheep in the fields, gray-white against the white, standing still and looking at the carts go by. A sky as pale as milk.
It had been very quiet. Wren remembered that. The quiet of the hills. No bells. No carts but theirs. No voices. Just the creak of the wheels and the breath of the horses and, from inside the crates, very faint, the ticking.
Master Ferrand had let the Erasers out that morning.
He had stopped the carts on the road below a village called Thistle Ford, a little cluster of stone houses on a hill with a squat church in the middle. He had climbed down from the second cart in his near-white coat and his gray gloves, and stood in the road, and looked at the snow on the verge for a long time. Then he had opened his black notebook and written something in it with his silver pencil. And then he had told the Enforcers to open the crates.
Wren had watched them come out. She had watched them come out in the cellar, too, under the Records yard, when Old Rook wound them. But this was different. In the cellar there had been lamps and walls and Rook's shaking hands. Here there was nothing but the white hills and the milk sky, and they came out of the crates into it one by one, and stood up in the road, tall and gray and iron, with snow settling on their shoulders, and turned their blank oval heads all together toward Master Ferrand, the way a field of sunflowers turns.
He had said his word to them. The worn word, the one Wren could never quite catch. And then another. And they had turned again. Toward the north. Toward the hills.
And walked.
They walked at the speed of a man. That was what Rook had said, and it was true. Not fast. Not slow. Never faster, never slower. Up the road. Off the road. Over the verge. Through the snow. In a line, one behind the other, with their great three-fingered shovel hands hanging at their sides and their heads all turned the same way. They left deep round prints in the snow, like the prints of something very heavy that did not care what it stepped on.
The carts could not follow them over the fields. So Master Ferrand had walked behind them. And the Enforcers had walked behind him. And Wren had walked behind the Enforcers, because Master Ferrand had asked for her, in the city, because she knew the face, and so she had to be where he could find her.
They had walked for an hour. Up over one white hill. Down the other side. Over a stone wall. The Erasers did not climb the wall. They walked into it, and through it, and the stones fell down round them, and they walked on. Wren had looked back and seen the gap they made, and the stones lying in the snow, and the sheep running.
And at the bottom of the next hill there was a farm.
It was a small farm. A low stone house with a slate roof, and smoke coming out of the chimney, and one window in the front with a red curtain. A barn beside it, bigger than the house. A yard between them, with a pump in the middle and a wooden trough, frozen. Chickens. A dog on a rope, barking. All of it inside a stone wall, chest high, with one wide gate in it. A farm gate. Five wooden bars, gray with age, hung on iron hinges between two square stone posts.
The Erasers were walking straight at the gate.
Not round the wall. Not along it. At the gate. Because the gate was in the way, and the way they had been told to go went through the yard, and out the other side, and on north.
And a man came out of the house.
He was big. That was the first thing Wren saw. A big slow man in a brown smock and a leather jerkin and heavy boots, with a red face and a short brown beard and great thick arms. He came out of his door and saw the Erasers, and stopped, and stared at them. For a long time. The way you would stare at anything you had never seen before and did not understand.
Then he went back in.
And came out again with a pitchfork.
Wren remembered that he did not hurry. That was the thing she remembered most, after. He did not run. He did not shout. He walked across his own yard, past his pump and his frozen trough and his barking dog, slowly, the way a man walks across his own yard every morning of his life. And he went to his gate. And he stood in it. In the middle of it. With the five gray bars behind him and the pitchfork in his two hands across his chest.
And he looked at the iron things coming toward him over the snow, and he said, quite loudly, in a slow thick voice from the hills:
"Nobody comes through my yard."
Through
The Erasers stopped.
Wren had not expected that. She had thought they would just keep walking. But they stopped, all of them at once, a few paces from the gate, in the snow, in their line. And stood. Ticking. Their blank oval heads turned toward the man in the gate, and the thin dark slit in each one, the width of a finger, seemed to look at him.
They had stopped because he was a man, Wren thought afterward. They did not walk through walls of people the way they walked through walls of stone. Rook had built something into them for that. Something that made them slow, and stop, and wait, when there was a man in the way. So they could be told. So someone could decide.
The farmer stood in his gate and looked at them. His face had gone from red to white, all at once, under the brown beard. But he did not move. The pitchfork did not move.
"Nobody," he said again. Slower. As if they had not heard. "Comes through. My yard."
Behind him, in the house, the red curtain in the window moved. A face came to the glass. A small one. A boy. Six, perhaps. Seven. Pressing his nose flat against the pane, so that it went white at the end, the way children do. Looking out at his father in the gate and the gray iron men in the snow.
Master Ferrand walked up past the Enforcers.
He walked without hurrying, the way he always walked, as if he was crossing a room to look at a picture. His near-white coat was spotless. There was no snow on it. There never seemed to be anything on Master Ferrand. His gray hat was straight. His gray gloves were buttoned at the wrist. His pale face was calm and kind and mild, and his pale eyes, that had no color in them at all, went over the farmer in the gate, and the pitchfork, and the house, and the boy at the window, the way they went over everything. Noting it.
He stopped beside the first Eraser. Not in front of it. Beside.
"Good morning," he said to the farmer. Politely. "I'm so sorry to trouble you. We're on Ministry business. We need to pass."
"Not through my yard, you don't."
"I'm afraid the way we need to go is through your yard."
"Then go round."
"They don't go round," said Master Ferrand. Quite gently. As if explaining something to a child. "That's rather the point of them."
The farmer looked at the Eraser. At the great iron chest and the brass joints and the shovel hands. Up at the blank head, so high above his own. Wren saw him swallow.
"Then they can wait," he said. "Till I'm dead."
Master Ferrand looked at him for a moment longer. Mildly. With his head a little on one side.
Then he took out his black notebook, and opened it, and wrote something down.
And without looking up from the page, in a voice no louder than a man asking for the salt, he said one word to the Erasers.
"Through."
That was all. Not kill him. Not move him. Not break the gate. Wren heard it very clearly. She would hear it every night for the rest of her life, she thought, as clearly as that. One word. Through. Said as a man might say it to a dog that has stopped at a door. Through. Go on. Through.
And the first Eraser walked.
It did not raise its hands. It did not strike. It did not hurry. It simply walked. Forward. At the speed of a man. In a straight line. Toward the gate, and through the gate, because the word said through and the gate was in the way.
And the man was in the gate.
Wren did not see all of it. She would be grateful for that, later. Grateful and ashamed of being grateful. She saw the farmer brace. She saw the pitchfork come down, the iron tines striking the Eraser's chest with a clang like a bell, and skidding off, and the long wooden handle snapping like a twig. She saw the big man's arms go up. She heard him say something. Not a word. A sound.
And then the Eraser was there. Where he was. And the gate was in pieces round it, the five gray bars broken and spinning off into the snow. And the Eraser walked on into the yard, at the speed of a man, past the pump and the frozen trough, toward the far wall. With the others behind it. One after another. All six. In a line.
And the farmer was lying in the gateway.
Wren did not look at him. She looked at the Eraser's back. At the square brass plate in the small of it, with the hole for the bird-shaped key. At the snow on its shoulders. It had not even slowed down. It had not even known. It had walked through the gate, and through what was standing in the gate, the way water goes through a sieve. Because the word said through.
It had not meant anything at all.
That was what Rook had said, in the cellar, with his shaking hands. They do what the word says. Not what you mean. What the word says. If the word says through, they go through. Through whatever's standing in the gate.
Rook had been right.
Nobody had ordered the man killed. Wren knew that. Master Ferrand had not ordered it. He had said through. The Erasers had not wanted it. They did not want anything. They had walked through. And the man was dead in his own gateway, and nobody at all had done it.
The dog was still barking. On its rope. Barking and barking at nothing.
And in the window, behind the red curtain, the small white face was gone.
Ferrand Writes
The woman came out of the house.
Wren did not know her name. She never found it out. She was a small woman, much smaller than her husband, in a blue apron with flour on it, and her hair was coming down out of its pins on one side. She came out of the door at a run. And then she saw the gateway, and stopped. In the middle of the yard. By the pump.
And she screamed.
Wren had heard people scream before. In the city, in the Health, in the cells under the Ministry. She had heard men scream when they were having things taken out of them that they did not want to lose. She had thought she knew what screaming sounded like.
She had not known.
It went on and on. It did not stop for breath. It went up into the milk-white sky and out over the white hills and came back off them, thin and far, so that it seemed to come from everywhere at once. The sheep in the next field ran. The chickens ran. The dog on its rope stopped barking and began to howl instead, in time with it, as if it was trying to help.
The Erasers walked on through the yard. At the speed of a man. They did not turn their heads. They reached the far wall, and walked into it, and through it, and the stones fell down round them into the snow, and they walked on, up the next white hill, in a line. Toward the north.
The woman ran to the gateway. And knelt down in the snow. And put her hands on him.
And Master Ferrand stood beside the broken gatepost, in his near-white coat, and wrote.
Wren watched him do it. She could not stop watching. She stood behind the Enforcers in the snow with her hands hanging at her sides, and watched Master Ferrand write in his black notebook with his silver pencil while the woman screamed. He wrote neatly. Quickly. Without any hurry. His pencil moved in short, square, careful strokes, the way it always did, the letters separate and unjoined, one after another, like a child's. He looked up, now and then. At the gateway. At the woman. At the broken bars of the gate in the snow. At the house. And then down again, and wrote.
He looked at the window.
Wren looked too. She could not help it.
The boy was back. The small white face behind the glass. The red curtain held aside in one small hand. Not pressed against the pane now. Further back. Standing in the middle of the room, perhaps, looking out. Looking at his mother kneeling in the snow, and the thing she was kneeling beside. Not crying. Not moving. Just looking. With his mouth open a little.
Master Ferrand looked at the boy for a moment. Then wrote something else.
Wren wanted to know what. She wanted to know so badly that it was like a pain in her chest. What do you write, at a time like this? What words do you put down? Farmer. Gate. Pitchfork. Through. Wife. Boy at window. What is there to say that needs to be written down?
She never found out. Not then.
Master Ferrand closed the notebook. He put the silver pencil back in its loop on the spine. He put the notebook in the inside pocket of his near-white coat, on the left, over his heart. Then he turned round, and looked at Wren, and at the Enforcers, mildly, with his head on one side.
"Shall we?" he said. "They'll be getting ahead."
And he walked off through the broken gate. Past the woman. Not looking at her. Not stepping round her, either. Just walking past, close, with his coat brushing her shoulder, the way you walk past a stranger in a crowded street. Through the yard. Past the pump. Through the gap in the far wall. Up the white hill, after the Erasers. His prints went neatly across the snow behind him, one after another, small and precise, beside the great deep round ones of the iron.
The Enforcers looked at each other.
Then they followed him.
They had to. That was what Enforcers did. They went where they were told. And they had been told to go with Master Ferrand.
And so had Wren.
She stood there. In the snow. With the woman screaming and the dog howling and the boy in the window looking out. She stood there for longer than she should have. Long enough that the last Enforcer, at the far wall, looked back at her, and frowned, and jerked his head. Come on.
She went.
She walked through the broken gate. She did not look down. She walked through the yard. She did not look at the woman. She walked past the pump and the frozen trough and the chickens huddled under the trough, and the dog howling on its rope, which saw her coming and stopped howling, for a moment, and looked at her with its yellow eyes as she went by.
And as she passed the window, she looked up. She could not help that either.
The boy was looking at her.
Not at the Erasers. Not at Master Ferrand. At her. At the girl in the white coat with twelve silver buttons, the uniform of the Ministry, walking through his yard after the men who had killed his father. He looked at her through the glass with his mouth open a little and his eyes very wide and very dark.
She looked away first.
Behind the Barn
She got as far as the corner of the barn.
Then her stomach turned over, all at once, like a fish flipping in a bucket, and she knew she was going to be sick. She knew it the way you know you are going to fall, a moment before you fall. And she did not want the Enforcers to see. She did not want anybody to see. So she turned, quickly, and went round the corner of the barn, out of sight of the yard, and put one hand flat on the cold stone wall, and bent over in the snow.
And was sick.
It went on for a long time. Long after there was nothing left to come up. Her whole body kept trying anyway, folding over and over itself, the way a hand keeps closing on a thing it has already dropped. Her eyes ran. Her nose ran. Her knees shook. She held onto the wall with her hand and her forehead pressed against the stone, and the stone was so cold it burned, and she was grateful for it, because it was something else to feel.
When it stopped, she stayed there.
Bent over. Breathing. Looking at the snow between her boots, and the mess she had made in it, steaming a little in the cold. Behind the barn it was very quiet. The wall cut off the yard. She could still hear the woman, but far off now, and muffled, as if through a door. And the dog. And, further off, up the hill, the ticking going away.
She wiped her mouth on the back of her glove.
And the thing that came into her head, then, bent over in the snow behind a barn in the hills with a dead man on the other side of the wall, was not a man is dead. It was not the boy saw. It was not what have we done.
It was: I hope the Enforcers didn't see me.
She stood up straight.
She stood there with her back against the barn wall and her face burning and her mouth sour, and she was ashamed. Deeply. Hot all the way through, the way you are ashamed when you have been caught out in something. And she knew exactly what she was ashamed of.
She was ashamed of being sick.
Of being weak. Of bending over in the snow like a little girl. Of not being able to stand in a yard and watch a thing happen and keep her breakfast down, the way the Enforcers could, the way Master Ferrand could. She was a cadet of the Ministry. Two years. More. She wore the white coat with the twelve silver buttons. She had stood beside the Inquisitor's desk while he signed forms that took the memories out of people's heads. She had carried the jars. She had collected Sergeant Moll herself, with her own two hands, and taken him to Room Nine. She was supposed to be able to do this.
That was what she was ashamed of.
Not the gate. Not the word. Not the boy at the window. Not walking past the woman in the snow. Not following Master Ferrand up the hill. None of that. She did not feel ashamed of any of that at all.
Only of being sick.
And she stood behind the barn and felt that, very clearly, the way you feel a stone in your shoe. And some small cold part of her, at the very back of her mind, a part she had not known was there, looked at it. Looked at the shame. Looked at what it was for, and what it was not for.
And said, very quietly: That's wrong. That's the wrong way round.
She did not know what to do with that. So she did nothing. She scooped up a handful of clean snow from the top of the wall and put it in her mouth, and held it there till it melted, and spat. Then she did it again. Then she straightened her white coat, and pulled her gloves tight at the wrists, and tucked a strand of fair hair back into its pins where it had come loose.
Then she went round the far end of the barn, the long way, so as not to pass the gateway again, and climbed over the broken place in the far wall, and went up the hill after the others.
The last Enforcer was waiting for her at the top. A big man called Harrow, with a broken nose and a carbine on his back. He looked at her face as she came up. He did not say anything. Then he turned and walked on.
He had seen. She knew he had seen.
She found, walking behind him up the next white hill, that she did not care as much as she had thought she would. And she did not know whether that was better, or worse.
