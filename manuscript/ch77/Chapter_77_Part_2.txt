Chapter 77: Ferrand's Notebook, Part 2 of 5: Kross Comes
Kross Comes
They did not get far that day.
The Erasers walked until the light began to go. Then they slowed. Rook had said they would. They don't like the black dark. They slow, and then they stop. And so as the sun went down behind the white hills and the sky went from milk to gray to a deep cold violet, the six iron men slowed, and slowed, and at last stopped, all together, in the middle of a snowy field two hills north of the farm. And stood there. Ticking very softly. Like clocks in a house where everybody has gone to bed.
Master Ferrand looked at them. Then he looked at the sky. Then he wrote something in his notebook.
"We'll go back to the road," he said. "The carts will be waiting. There's no point standing in a field all night."
The nearest way back to the road went past the farm.
Wren had known it would. She had walked the whole afternoon knowing it. And when they came down the hill in the dark, with the Enforcers carrying lanterns, and she saw the low shape of the house below them with a light in its one window, she felt her stomach start to turn over again, and made it stop.
The carts were waiting in the lane. The drivers had brought them round by the road. And somebody had come from the village. Wren could see lanterns moving in the yard. People. Two men from Thistle Ford, big slow men like the farmer, with their caps in their hands. And an old woman with a shawl over her head, holding the farmer's wife by the shoulders in the doorway. And the gate was still broken. Nobody had mended it. The five gray bars still lay in the snow where they had fallen.
And in the gateway, there was a shape under a sack.
Somebody had put it over him. An old brown grain sack, split open and laid flat. It did not cover all of him. His boots stuck out at the end, toes up, with snow on them.
Nobody in the yard looked at the Ministry people. Not once. The two men from the village turned their backs. The old woman took the farmer's wife inside and shut the door. As if the people in the lane were not there. As if they were a bad smell, or a cold wind, that you could only wait for to pass.
Master Ferrand climbed into his cart and pulled the door shut.
Wren stood in the lane. She did not know where to go. She could not get into the cart with Master Ferrand. She did not want to get into the other one, with the crates. So she stood in the lane in the dark beside the wall, with her back to the yard, and waited for somebody to tell her what to do.
And that was when the Inquisitor came.
She heard the horse first. A long way off, down the road toward Thistle Ford. Hoofbeats on frozen mud. Not fast. Steady. Coming on through the dark. Then a single lantern, swinging, bobbing up and down, getting bigger. Then the horse itself, a tall bay, steaming in the cold, its breath coming out of it in great white clouds. And on its back, a slight gray shape in a gray cape and a plain gray hat.
He was filthy. Wren had never seen him filthy. He had been riding for two days, since the funeral, she found out afterward, with hardly a stop. Down the south road, round the fens, up into the hills. And the road had been snow and then mud and then snow again, and he was mud to the thighs, his boots and his breeches and the hem of his cape all caked thick and brown and frozen stiff. His face, under the hat, was gray with tiredness.
He pulled up the horse in the lane beside the carts. And sat there for a moment, looking down at Wren.
"Halloway," he said.
"Sir."
He looked past her. At the yard. At the lanterns in it, and the two men with their backs turned, and the shut door. At the broken gate.
At the sack.
He did not ask. Wren saw that he did not need to ask. He looked at the sack in the gateway, and at the broken bars of the gate in the snow, and at the great deep round prints going in through the gateway and out through the far wall. And at the small neat prints going beside them. And she saw him understand all of it. The whole thing. In the time it took to look.
He got down off the horse. Slowly. Stiffly. Like an old man. His legs nearly gave when his feet touched the ground, and he put a hand on the saddle to hold himself up.
Then he walked into the yard.
The two men from the village saw him coming and stepped back. They did not know who he was. But they could see what he was. The gray. The way he walked. One of them said something under his breath. The other put a hand on his arm.
The Inquisitor did not look at them. He walked to the gateway. And stopped. And stood looking down at the sack.
He stood there for a long time.
Wren could not see his face. He had his back to her. She could only see the slight gray shape of him, standing very still, with the lantern light from the yard on one side of him and the dark on the other, and his head bent, looking down at the boots sticking out from under the sack, toes up, with snow on them.
She did not know how long. A long time. Long enough for her feet to go numb in her boots. Long enough for the two men from the village to stop watching him and look at each other.
Then he took his hat off.
He held it in his two hands, against his chest. And stood there with his thin graying hair bare to the cold and the snow beginning, very lightly, to fall on it.
And then he put his hat back on, and turned, and went to the door of the house, and knocked.
Every Coin
The door opened a crack. The old woman with the shawl. She looked at him through the gap with her mouth set hard, and Wren saw her see the gray, and the mud, and the Ministry in the way he stood, and her face shut like a box.
The Inquisitor said something. Wren could not hear it. It was too quiet. A few words.
The old woman did not answer. But she did not shut the door either. And after a long moment, she stepped back. And the farmer's wife came into the gap.
Wren saw her face in the light from the room behind. It was gray and swollen and wet and quite empty. Not angry. Not anything. Like a house with all the furniture taken out. She stood in her doorway in her floury apron and looked at the slight gray man in front of her without seeming to see him at all.
The Inquisitor took off his right glove.
Wren had seen him do that before. Not often. He did it when something mattered. When he was going to touch somebody, or something, and wanted to feel it. He pulled the gray glove off one finger at a time and put it in his pocket. And then he reached inside his cape, and took out his purse.
It was a plain purse. Brown leather, worn soft. Wren had seen it on his desk a hundred times. He kept his money in it for the cab, and for the boy who brought the papers, and for the barley sugar he bought from the woman on the corner of Mercer Street.
He opened it. He did not count what was in it. He turned it over above the woman's hand, and shook it. And it all came out. Every coin. Silver and copper and two gold pieces, Wren saw them shine. All of it, spilling into the woman's open palm, so much that some fell over the edges of her fingers and rolled on the step.
The woman looked down at it. As if she did not know what it was.
The Inquisitor bent, and picked up the coins that had fallen on the step, one by one, with his bare hand. And put them in her palm with the others. And closed her fingers over them, gently, with his own.
And Wren saw his hand shake.
Once. Only once. A small quick tremor, there and gone, as his bare fingers folded hers shut. So small that nobody else would have seen it. The village men did not. The old woman did not. The farmer's wife did not see anything at all.
But Wren saw it. She had stood beside his desk for two years and five months, and she had watched his hands sign a thousand forms that took the insides out of people's heads, and she had never once seen them shake.
They shook now.
Then he let go. And stepped back. And the old woman shut the door.
He stood on the step a moment longer, looking at the shut door. Then he pulled his glove back on, one finger at a time, and turned round, and walked back across the yard. Past the gateway. Past the sack. He did not look at it again.
He came out into the lane. Master Ferrand's cart was there, with its shutters closed and a thin line of lamplight round the edge of the door. The Inquisitor walked past it. He did not knock. He did not look at it. He did not say one word to Master Ferrand. Not then. Not that night. Not the next morning.
He walked to his horse and stood beside it with one hand on its neck, in the falling snow, and looked at nothing.
Wren came and stood near him. Not close. A little way off. The way she stood outside his office door.
After a while he spoke. Without turning his head.
"What was his name?"
"I don't know, sir."
"Find out."
She did. She went to one of the village men, the one who had put his hand on the other's arm, and asked. He looked at her white coat for a long time before he answered. And then he told her, in the slow thick voice of the hills, as if he was laying something down on a table between them that she would have to pick up.
"Corwen Lusk," he said. "His name was Corwen Lusk."
She went back and told the Inquisitor.
He nodded. Once. And did not say anything else. But she saw his lips move, a little, under the brim of his hat. As if he was saying it to himself. Learning it. Putting it somewhere.
Ashes
Two days later they came to the place where the Ashborn had lived. Or what was left of it.
Wren had not been there for the burning. That had been Master Ferrand's night, the night before. He had gone on ahead with four of the iron men, on foot, fast, following the trail over the last of the hills. And the Inquisitor had stayed behind with the carts and the other two, and Wren, and two of the Enforcers, because the carts were slow on the bad roads and he would not leave them. Or that was what he said. Wren had wondered, after, whether he had stayed behind so as not to be there.
She would never know. She did not ask.
They came up over the last hill in the morning, the Wednesday, and there it was. The edge of the world.
That was what it looked like. The hills just stopped. They came to a long ridge, a high bare lip of rock and thin grass and snow, and then on the far side of the ridge the ground fell away, down a long steep slope covered in thorn bushes. Every one of the bushes leaned the same way. Away from the bottom. As if they were all trying to get away from something. And at the bottom of the slope, a line of black trees, dead and leafless and twisted. And beyond the trees, the Glass.
Wren had never seen it before. Nobody she knew had seen it. It was a word in a book, a line on a map, a thing the old soldiers in the Ministry corridors talked about in low voices when they thought no one was listening. She had imagined it a hundred times. She had not imagined it right.
It was flat. Flatter than anything. Flatter than water. It went away from the bottom of the slope toward the north, green-black and shining, without a single hill or tree or stone, without anything, until it met the sky. And there was no snow on it. Not one flake. All the hills behind them were white, and the slope was white between the bushes, and the Glass lay below them dark and bare and glistening, as if the snow had come to its edge and stopped, afraid.
And a warm breath came up off it. Wren felt it on her face. In the middle of winter. Warm and faintly sweet, like burnt sugar.
And under that, a different smell. Coming up from the slope, from halfway down, where a thin gray smoke still hung in the still air.
Burnt honey. Burnt cloth. Burnt hair.
They went down.
The Ashborn camp had been on a shelf of flatter ground, halfway down the thorn slope. Wren could see where it had been from a long way off, because nothing on it was white. The snow had melted for fifty paces round. And in the middle of the black mud were the hoops.
That was all that was left of the tents. The poles. They had been bent into hoops, Wren saw, long springy poles bent over and tied at the top, the way you would make a basket. The hides had burned off them. The poles had not burned through. They stood up out of the black ground in rows, dozens of them, blackened and bare, like the ribs of great animals that had lain down there and died and rotted away to bone.
There were goats.
She had not expected the goats. She did not know why. They lay where they had fallen, here and there among the hoops. Brown and white and black, their coats singed, their legs stuck out stiff. Some had got out. She could see their prints going off up the slope through the snow. Most had not.
There were no people. Wren looked. She could not stop herself looking. But there were no people. Not one. Wherever the Ashborn had gone, they had taken their dead with them. Or there had been no dead. She did not know. She did not ask.
And there, in the middle of the camp, by a great black ring of stones where a big fire had been, there was an Eraser.
It was lying on its face. In the mud. Its long iron arms flung out on either side, its shovel hands open, its blank head turned a little to one side, as if it had fallen asleep there. And its back was open.
The square brass plate in the small of its back, where the bird-shaped key went in, was gone. Torn off. Wren could see it lying a little way off in the mud, bent nearly double. And where it had been there was a hole. A square dark hole into the iron chest, with the edges of the iron plates around it twisted out like the petals of a flower. And inside the hole, wheels. Dozens of little brass wheels, all still. Some of them broken. Some of them hanging loose on their pins.
And a space. In the middle of the wheels. A long round empty space, where something had been, and was not anymore.
The spring, Wren thought. That was where the spring went. The great coiled iron spring that made it go. Somebody had reached in through that hole and taken it out.
She stared at it.
Somebody had done that. With their hands. In the middle of a fire. To a thing that had walked through a stone wall in front of her eyes, and through a gate, and through a man.
The Inquisitor got down off his horse.
He walked to the broken Eraser and stood over it. He did not touch it. He did not bend down to look in the hole. He just stood there and looked at it, the way he had stood and looked at the sack in the gateway.
Then he looked round. At the hoops. At the goats. At the black mud and the gray smoke and the prints going off up the slope.
And he took off his hat.
He held it against his chest again, in both hands, the way he had at the farm. And stood there in the middle of the burned camp with his head bare. And did not move. For a long time. Longer than at the farm.
Master Ferrand came walking up through the hoops.
He had been somewhere further down, near the black trees. He came up through the burned camp in his near-white coat, picking his way neatly round the dead goats, with his notebook already in his hand. There was a smear of soot on one of his sleeves. Just one. It was the first mark Wren had ever seen on him.
He came to the broken Eraser. He looked at it for a moment, with his head on one side.
Then he put the toe of his boot under its shoulder, and pushed.
The Eraser was very heavy. But he pushed hard, and it rolled. Slowly. Over onto its back, with a great iron clang, and lay there with its arms flung out and its blank face to the sky. The thin slit in its head was full of mud.
Master Ferrand looked down at it. Then he opened his notebook and wrote.
The Inquisitor did not look at him. He put his hat back on. Then he got back on his horse, slowly, stiffly, and rode up the slope. Up through the thorn bushes. All the way to the top, to the lip of the ridge. And there he stopped, and sat on his horse, and took his hat off again, and looked out over the camp and the black trees and the Glass for a long, long time.
Wren watched him from below. A small gray shape on a bay horse, against the white sky, with his hat in his hand.
Then he put it on, and turned the horse's head west, and rode along the ridge. Looking down. Looking for something. Looking for the place where they had gone.
Onto the Glass
They went down onto the Glass the next morning, Thursday, at first light.
The carts could not come. Nor the horses. The drivers took them back over the hills to Thistle Ford, to wait. Everything else had to be carried. The Enforcers carried the canvas and the short iron poles and the water casks, two each, on wooden frames on their backs. Wren carried her own pack and a lantern. The Inquisitor carried nothing at all. He walked with his hands empty at his sides, as he always did, and his gray cape hanging still in the still air.
Master Ferrand had gone ahead in the night, with his three. Wren had heard them go. The ticking going away down the slope in the dark, and the soft polite voice saying the word, and then nothing.
She walked down through the dead black trees at the bottom of the slope. Their branches were bare and twisted and smooth, as if something had licked all the bark off them. They creaked, though there was no wind. And then the trees were behind her, and there was a line on the ground, a sharp clean line like the edge of a table, where the dead grass stopped.
And she stepped over it. And she was on the Glass.
The first thing was the sound.
She had not been ready for it. Nobody had told her about it. As soon as her boot came down on the glass, she heard it. Not with her ears, at first. With her teeth. A thin high hum, coming up out of the ground, through her boot, through her bones, into her jaw. So high it was almost not a sound. Like a finger going round and round the wet rim of a glass, very fast, a long way off. And it did not stop. It never stopped. It went on under everything, under her breath and her footsteps and the creak of the Enforcers' packs, so that after an hour she could not tell whether she was hearing it or only remembering it.
The second thing was the light.
The sun came up over the eastern edge of the world while they were still near the trees. And the Glass took it, all of it, and threw it back up at her. Not like water. Water shines in one place, where the sun is. The Glass shone everywhere. Every part of it at once. A great flat dazzle of dark green and black and gold and smoke, going away on every side, and the light coming up off it into her eyes from below, under the brim of her hat, so that there was nowhere to look that was not bright. She screwed her eyes up. It did not help. Tears ran down her face. She pulled her hat down to her eyebrows and walked looking at her own boots. And even her boots shone. Even the shadow of her own body, on the glass, had light in it.
One of the Enforcers, a young one called Pike, took his glove off at the first rest and put his bare hand flat on the glass to see what it felt like. He snatched it back and swore. There was a red mark across his palm already, like a burn from a stove.
That was the third thing. The heat. By the middle of the morning she could feel it through the soles of her boots. By noon they had to stop, and put up the canvas, and sit under it on their packs, because if you sat on the glass it burned you through your coat.
And under the canvas, in the gray thick light, with the hum in her teeth and the heat pressing down and the sweat running into her collar, Wren did the thing she had been thinking about since the edge.
She did it very quietly. She did not tell anyone. She did not even really decide to do it. She only sat there with her hands in her lap, and closed her eyes, and reached.
It was the thing she was for. The thing the Ministry had trained her to do, for two years and more, in the white rooms under the Long Office. Extraction. Reaching out, with the part of you that reaches, for the shine of a memory in somebody else. Finding the thread of it. Taking hold. Drawing it out, gently, slowly, like drawing a long thread out of a coat, until it came free. It was not hard, once you knew how. It was like a hand closing. She had done it a hundred times.
There was nobody to do it to now. Nobody near enough. The Inquisitor sat at the far end of the canvas with his back very straight and his eyes shut, not asleep, only waiting, the way he waited in his office. The Enforcers were under the other canvas. She was not reaching for anybody.
She was reaching for nothing. Just to see.
And nothing happened.
Not nothing the way it was when there was nobody there, and the hand closed on empty air. Not that. Something else. She reached, and the reaching itself did not go anywhere. It went out of her a little way, and stopped. As if it had walked into a wall. As if the air itself had gone thick, like honey, like glass, and her reaching could not move through it. She pushed. A little harder. It did not move. She could feel it there, the reach, the hand that was not a hand, stuck in the hot air a few inches from her chest, closing on nothing, unable to close.
On the Glass nobody can take anything, Wren.
The Inquisitor had said that. On the fifth step, in the city, a long time ago. A week ago.
He had been right.
She let go. She let the reach come back into her. It came back slowly, like a stiff arm lowered after holding something up for too long. And she sat there under the canvas with her eyes shut and her hands in her lap.
And what she felt first was relief.
Great warm relief. All through her. Like putting down a heavy bag at the end of a long road. She could not take. Nobody could take. Not here. Not her, not Master Ferrand, not anybody. For as long as they were on the Glass, nobody's memories could be drawn out of them like threads out of a coat. Not by her hands. Not by anybody's.
She was glad.
And then, a moment after the relief, she was ashamed of it.
Because she was a cadet of the Ministry. And the thing she was for was taking. And she had sat here in the heat and found that she could not do the one thing she was for, and she had been glad.
What kind of cadet was glad of that?
She did not know. She sat with it. With both of them. The relief, and the shame of the relief, sitting side by side in her chest like two strangers on a bench who have nothing to say to each other.
And she thought of the barn. And the snow. And being ashamed of being sick, and not of anything else.
And she thought, It's happening again. The wrong way round.
And she did not know, anymore, which way round was right.
