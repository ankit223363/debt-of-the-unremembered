Chapter 96: The Long Way Back, Part 4 of 5: The Copybook
The Copybook
The cart went into the ditch just as the light was going.
They heard it from the house. They were sitting at Uncle Pim's long kitchen table, by the range, eating eels. Corin and Lyra and Oren and Garrett, and Uncle Pim at the end with his straw, which he took out to eat and put back in between mouthfuls. The eels were stewed, with onions, and they were the best thing Corin had ever eaten, the way everything was the best thing he had ever eaten since the Glass. Kestrel and Sef were in the barn. They would not come into the house. Uncle Pim had taken them a bowl each, himself, and the heads on a plate, and come back and said, "Hm," and sat down.
And from outside, from the dyke track that ran past the end of the lane, there came a crash. And a horse screaming. And men shouting.
Uncle Pim did not get up at once. He finished his mouthful. He put his straw back in. Then he got up and went to the window and looked out through the gap in the shutter.
"One of the book carts," he said. "Gone in the ditch. Off the dyke." He chewed. "Always happens. They don't know the track. Lot of soft places on that dyke."
He went to the door and took his coat off the peg.
"Stay in," he said. "All of you. They'll want my horses. They always want my horses. I'll go and be helpful." He put his hat on. "Garrett, you come. Look stupid."
"I don't look stupid," said Garrett.
"You will by the time I've finished telling them about you."
They went out.
Corin sat at the table with his eels.
He did not mean to get up. He knew he should not. He knew what was out there. Gray coats. Ministry men. With a list. And his face on the posters. Too fat, and no gray, but his face. He knew he should sit at the table in Uncle Pim's kitchen with the shutters closed and eat his eels and wait.
He got up.
Lyra looked at him.
"I'm only going to look," he said.
"That's what you always say."
"I know."
She looked at him a moment longer. Then she got up too, and took her coat off the back of the chair.
Oren did not get up. He sat at the end of the table with his big hands round his bowl and said, "If you're not back by the time I've finished these eels, I'm coming after you." And went on eating. Slowly.
They went out by the back. Round the side of the house. Along behind the midden, in the shadow, to the end of the yard where the lane went out to the dyke. And stood behind the corner of the barn and looked.
It was nearly dark. The marsh was blue and gray, and the sky over it was going from deep blue to black, with the first stars. The dyke ran across the end of the lane, a long raised bank of earth and stone with a track on top, and a ditch on either side, wide, full of black water with a skin of ice.
And in the ditch on the near side, there was a cart.
One of the black ones. With the white eye painted on its side. It had gone off the edge of the track, where the bank was soft, and slid down, and turned half over, so that it lay on its side in the ditch with one wheel in the air, still turning, slowly. The horse was out of the shafts, somehow, standing on the track above, shaking, with its head down and its legs splayed. And the back of the cart had burst open.
The books had gone everywhere.
All down the bank of the dyke. Into the ditch. Onto the ice. Out across the snow at the edge of the lane. Books, and papers, and bundles tied with string, and loose pages blowing a little in the wind, and a wooden box broken open with its lid off, and more books. Dark shapes on the white snow and the gray ice. Like birds that had been shot.
There were three gray coats. Two of them were down in the ditch, up to their knees in the black water, swearing, trying to get the cart up. The third was on the track with a lamp, holding the horse, shouting at them. And Uncle Pim was coming up the lane toward them, slowly, with his hands in his pockets and Garrett behind him, calling out, "Now then. Now then. What's all this."
Nobody was looking at the end of the lane.
Corin stood behind the corner of the barn and looked at the books in the snow.
There was one near him. Not far. A few paces. At the very end of the lane, where it met the foot of the dyke. It had come further than the others. Flung, perhaps, when the cart went over. It lay face down in the snow, open, with its pages spread out under it like a bird's wings.
It was small. Thin. Not a real book. A copybook. The kind children have at dame school, with soft gray-brown covers and lined pages inside, for practicing letters.
Corin looked at it.
And felt it pull.
Not hard. Not like the rod at the bottom of the bunker stair, which had made his hands shake so badly he could not hold a pencil. Not like the left-hand road at the Two Sisters. Softer. A small warm tug. The way the spoon pulled, in Lyra's coat. Like a thread tied to something.
He went and picked it up.
He did not decide to. He went out from behind the corner of the barn, three steps, in the dark, at the end of the lane, and bent down, and picked it up out of the snow in his gloved hand, and came back. Nobody saw. The gray coats were shouting at Uncle Pim. Uncle Pim was shouting back, very slowly, about his horses.
Lyra was staring at him.
"Corin—"
He held up the copybook.
It was wet. The snow had soaked into the soft gray-brown cover and made it go dark at one corner. On the front, in big wobbly letters, in pencil, somebody had written a name. WILL TUCKETT. HIS BOOK. REEDHAM SCHOOL. And under that, smaller, in the same hand, NOT YOURS.
Corin opened it.
Inside there were letters. Rows and rows of them. A, A, A, A, all down the first page, big and careful and leaning to the left. B, B, B, on the second. C. Then words. CAT. DOG. MUM. WILL. Then sums. Then a page of nothing but the letter W, over and over, very hard, as if Will Tuckett had been very proud of his W.
And then, on the next page, a picture.
It filled the page. It was drawn in pencil, very hard, very carefully, the way children draw the thing they draw best. A tree.
A straight trunk. Roots going down, spread out, like fingers. Branches going up and out. And at the top, where the branches came together, a row of points. Like the teeth of a comb.
Like a crown.
Corin stood behind the barn in the blue dusk and looked at it.
He did not move.
Beside him, Lyra had gone very still. He could feel it. The way you feel somebody stop breathing.
He turned the copybook toward her.
She looked at it. At the tree with the crown on top. For a long time.
Then her good hand went to her coat. To the front of it. Over her heart. Where the spoon was. And pressed there. Flat.
"That's—" she said. And stopped.
"I know," said Corin.
"That's the same. That's exactly the—"
"I know."
They stood and looked at each other over the copybook.
From the dyke, Uncle Pim's voice, very loud and very slow: "—and the brown mare's lame in the off fore, so you can have the gray, but you'll bring him back by the first bell, mind, or I'll be at the Ministry myself—"
"Why would a child in Reedham draw that?" said Lyra. Very low.
Corin did not know.
He did not know anything. He stood with the wet copybook open in his gloved hands, at the page with the tree on it, and felt the small warm pull of it go into him, into his hands, up his arms, and settle somewhere in the middle of him, next to the other pull, the one from the spoon. Two threads. Tied to the same thing.
He shut the copybook.
He put it inside his coat. Against his shirt. On the left side.
"They'll miss it," said Lyra.
"They won't. They've got a cart full." He looked at the books in the snow, all down the bank of the dyke. "They won't miss one copybook. They don't even know what's in them. They just have a list."
Lyra did not argue.
They went back round the side of the house, in the shadow, to the back door, and in. Oren was still at the table. His bowl was empty. He was holding his spoon. He looked at them as they came in, and then at the spoon, and frowned at it, as if he could not think what it was called.
"Just in time," he said.
Uncle Pim came back an hour later, with Garrett, and his gray horse gone, and a paper in his hand from the Ministry promising to return it by the first bell, which he put on the dresser behind a jug and did not look at again.
He sat down at the end of the table and put a new straw in his mouth.
Corin took the copybook out of his coat. He laid it on the table, open, at the tree.
Uncle Pim looked at it.
He did not seem surprised. He did not seem anything. He chewed his straw, and looked at the tree with its crown of points, and then at Corin.
"That's Will Tuckett's," he said. "Old Tuckett's grandson. At Reedham." He chewed. "They took the whole school's books. Every child's. Dame Aske kept them in a cupboard and the gray coats said it was a library and took the lot."
"The tree," said Corin.
Uncle Pim looked at it again.
"Oh, that," he said. "That's the old tree." He moved his straw. "Kids draw it. Round here. Always have. My granda drew it. I drew it, on my slate, when I was a boy. You draw your letters, and your cat, and your mum. And then you draw the old tree. Everybody does."
"Why?"
Uncle Pim shrugged. His big shoulders went up and down.
"Nobody knows," he said. "It's just what you draw. It's the old tree. From the old king's time." He took the straw out of his mouth and looked at it. "My granda used to say it was on the gates once. On the big gate. Before they put the eye up."
The kitchen was very quiet.
The range ticked. The wind moved the reeds on the roof.
Corin looked at the tree on the page. At the roots, going down like fingers. At the crown.
He did not say anything.
Across the table, Lyra had put her good hand flat over her heart again. And kept it there.
Where Lyra Saw It
Lyra could not sleep that night either.
Corin knew because he went out to the midden, late, before Ned's punt came for the Ashborn, and when he came back in she was sitting at Uncle Pim's long table on her own, in the dark, with the copybook open in front of her at the page with the tree.
She did not look up when he came in.
He sat down at the other end of the table. More than a hand's width. The whole length of the table. And waited.
"I've seen it," she said. "I know I have. I keep almost getting it. Like a word you can't think of. It's right there, and then it's gone."
"On the Hill?"
"Somewhere on the Hill." She put her finger on the page, beside the tree, not touching it. "I thought it was a book. In my father's library. One of the old ones, on the top shelf, that I was never allowed to take down. With the leather all cracked. I thought it was stamped on the front of one of those." She frowned. "But I don't think it was a book."
"What, then?"
"I don't know." She shook her head. "Something small. Something I held. In my hand. When I was little."
Corin did not say anything.
"It's the spoon," said Lyra. "Isn't it. I keep thinking about the spoon, and holding it, and that's why I think I held the other thing. It's only the spoon." She looked up at him at last, down the long table, in the dark. "Except I don't think it is."
She put her good hand inside her coat. And took it out again. With the spoon in it, wrapped in the end of her scarf.
She did not unwrap it. She laid it on the table, wrapped, beside the copybook. A little bundle of gray wool, the size of a child's hand.
Corin looked at it.
It pulled. From the length of the table. Like a thread.
He did not move.
"Is it now?" he said. Very low. "Am I ready?"
Lyra looked at him for a long time. Down the table. In the dark.
"No," she said.
He nodded.
"Why not?"
"Because you're tired, and you're frightened, and you want to burn something, and there's an old woman under a stone bird." She said it quite plainly. "And if you open that, and something comes up, something big, something about your sister or the cart or the tree, you'll go out of that door tonight and walk to the city and do something stupid. And she said not to." She paused. "And I said the second one."
Corin looked at the little wrapped bundle on the table.
"You're right," he said.
"I know I'm right."
"I still want to."
"I know you do." She put her hand flat over the bundle. Not picking it up. Just covering it. "You can want to. You wrote it down. I want to keep asking. You can keep asking. I'll keep saying no. Till it's yes."
He almost smiled. Half of his mouth did.
"How will you know?" he said. "When it's yes?"
Lyra did not answer at once.
"Abbe said I would," she said. "Or I wouldn't, and then it wouldn't matter." She looked at the copybook. At the tree. "I think it'll be when you're not frightened. When you can open it and sit with whatever comes up, the way you sat with the quiet in the loft this morning. Not doing anything. Just sitting."
"That might be never."
"Then it'll be never." She picked up the bundle and put it back inside her coat, over her heart. "And I'll carry it about for the rest of my life and be buried with it, and you'll have to dig me up."
The laugh came up out of him. Arran's. Very quiet. In the cold dark kitchen.
Lyra closed the copybook.
She slid it down the long table toward him. All the way. It came to a stop by his hand.
"Keep that," she said. "It's yours. You found it."
"It's Will Tuckett's."
"Then give it back to him. One day. When this is over." She stood up. "Go to Reedham and find Will Tuckett and give him his copybook back and tell him his tree's the best one you ever saw."
Corin picked up the copybook. He held it in his gloved hands.
"I will," he said.
She looked at him for a moment longer. Then she went to sit by the dead range, with her back to the warm bricks, and drew her knees up, and shut her eyes. And he sat at the end of Uncle Pim's long table with the copybook in his hands, in the dark, and did not open it again. He did not need to. He could see the tree with his eyes shut.
Roots, like fingers.
A crown.
The Salt Bed
Uncle Pim took them out to the cart shed after supper to show them where they would lie.
He went first, with a lantern, a small round man in a straw hat that he did not seem to take off even indoors, and a coat with straw in every seam of it. Lyra went after him, with her bound hand held against her coat. Corin came last, with Silas's crutch, slowly, over the frozen ruts of the yard. Oren stayed in the kitchen. He said he had seen the inside of enough carts.
The shed was long and low, with a roof of old black thatch that came down almost to the ground at the back, and it smelled of salt and damp wood and horse. There were harnesses on pegs along one wall, stiff with cold. There was a grindstone in the corner, and a heap of sacks. And in the middle, filling most of the space, there was the salt wagon.
It was a big wagon. Bigger than Garrett's little cart. It had high sides of gray boards, crusted white along the bottom where the salt had dried into the wood, and four tall wheels with iron rims, and a bench at the front for the driver. In the back it was full to the top with sacks. Fat gray sacks, stitched along the necks with twine. Salt for the city. And on top of the salt, a great heap of turnips, purple and white, with the earth still on them.
"Turnip day tomorrow," said Uncle Pim. He set the lantern on the corner of the wagon and patted the side of it, the way you might pat a horse. "Every week, turnip day. Salt and turnips. Same as my father. Same as his. The gate men know this wagon. They've known it longer than they've known their own wives." He sniffed. "They still turn it over, mind. Every cart, now. Since the eye went up on the poster. Every cart, every sack. They put their sticks in."
Lyra looked at the sacks. "Then how—"
Uncle Pim grinned. He had a gap in his front teeth that you could have posted a letter through.
"They put their sticks in the sacks," he said. "Not in the wagon."
He went round to the back. He pulled out two of the bottom sacks, grunting, and dropped them on the floor of the shed in a puff of white. Behind them, where the floor of the wagon should have been, there was a board. An ordinary board. Gray, salt-crusted, the same as all the others. He put his thumb on one end of it and pressed.
The board tipped up. Under it there was dark.
Corin leaned on the crutch and looked.
It was a space between the true floor of the wagon and the false one. Long and very flat. Not much deeper than his hand was wide, from the heel to the tips of the fingers. It ran the whole length of the wagon, from the back to the driver's bench, and it was lined with old sacking and smelled so strongly of salt that it made his eyes water.
"My granda built it," said Uncle Pim, with great pride. "In the bad years. Before the old king went. For the tea that didn't pay the tax." He winked. "And sometimes a man that didn't pay it either."
"We have to lie in there," said Lyra. Not a question.
"On your backs. Flat. Like a pressed flower." He showed them with his hands, flat, one on top of the other. "Three of you. Head to foot. The big one in the middle, the lady at the front by the bench, where it's a bit higher. You at the back, mister. With your stick." He looked at the crutch. "It'll go in. Just. Long ways."
Corin looked at the dark space.
He thought about the crate. He had not meant to. It came up on its own, out of the place inside him where the old things were kept now, the things Malakor had given back. A wooden box. A wagon. Dark. The smell of salt fish. A small hand in his. Being very still. Being very quiet. Because if they heard you—
He found that he was holding the crutch very tightly.
"It's not long," said Uncle Pim. He was looking at Corin's face, and his own face had gone kinder. "From the farm to the gate is the long bit, and you'll hear it all. The wheels. Me singing. I always sing. They'll know something's up if I don't sing." He scratched under his hat. "And then the gate. That's the short bit. They'll take the turnips off and look at them, and push their sticks in the sacks, and ask me about my wife's knees. They always ask about her knees. And then they'll put it all back, and wave me on. And then it's the Market Road, and the yard behind the Saltmarsh Inn, and I knock three times on the boards, and you come out." He spread his hands. "Easy."
"And if they don't wave you on?" said Lyra.
Uncle Pim looked at her. He stopped grinning.
"Then I'll sing louder, miss," he said. "And you'll lie very still. And we'll see."
Nobody said anything for a while.
The lantern hissed a little on the corner of the wagon. Outside, in the dark, one of the geese said something rude to the night.
"Why are you doing this?" said Corin.
Uncle Pim looked surprised. As if nobody had ever asked him, and he had never asked himself.
"That copybook," he said slowly. "Will Tuckett's. Old Tuckett came to me at the market, before Midwinter, before they took the books. And he said, his grandson had come home from Dame Aske's and asked him what the tree was. The one Dame Aske drew on the board for them to copy." He looked at the salt sacks. "And Tuckett didn't know. So he asked me. And I told him. I told him, that was on the big gate when I was a boy. Before the eye. My granda showed me it. Carved in the stone. Roots like fingers. A crown on top." He was quiet. "And then Tuckett asked me why they took it down. And I didn't know."
He put the board back down over the dark space. Carefully. It clicked into place.
"I'm an old man," he said. "I grow turnips. I don't know anything about anything. But I'd like to know that. Before I die. Why they took the tree down." He looked at Corin. "And I'd like Will to know. When he's grown. That's all."
He picked up the lantern.
"First light," he said. "Don't eat too much breakfast. And don't sneeze. The salt makes you want to." He went to the door. "My wife will put a bit of wool in your ears for the wheels. They're loud, underneath. Like being inside a barrel rolling down a hill."
He went out across the yard, with the lantern swinging, singing something under his breath about a girl and a goose.
Lyra and Corin stood in the dark shed with the salt wagon.
"You went white," said Lyra. Quietly. "When he lifted the board."
"I know."
"Was it the crate?"
He did not answer. He did not need to.
She did not say anything else. She only moved a little nearer, in the dark, and put her good hand on the side of the wagon next to his, on the gray salt-crusted boards. Not on his hand. Just next to it. Close enough that he could feel the warmth of it through the air.
They stood like that for a while. Then they went in.
