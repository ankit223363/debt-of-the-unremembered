Chapter 96: The Long Way Back, Part 3 of 5: The Salt Wagon
The Salt Wagon
Garrett had a plan. Garrett always had a plan. He told it to them in the morning, in the barn, sitting on an upturned bucket with a heel of bread in one hand and his red cap in the other, waving them both.
"Not my cart," he said. "My cart's no good. My cart's got a false floor you could hide a letter in. Or a sausage. Not a person. Not three persons." He looked at Oren. "Not a person the size of the Captain. My cart's for paper."
"Then what?" said Lyra.
"My uncle's wagon." Garrett took a bite of bread and went on talking round it. "My uncle Pim. My mother's brother. He's got a farm out on the Saltmarsh, under the north wall, right by the gate, you can see the gate from his midden. And he's got a wagon. A big one. Four wheels. Two horses. For salt." He swallowed. "Salt comes in from the marsh pans, and there's a tax on it at the gate, a big one, a penny a bushel. And my uncle Pim doesn't like paying a penny a bushel. Nobody does. So his wagon's got a bed in it."
"A bed," said Oren.
"A false one. Under the real one. The whole length of the wagon." Garrett held his hands apart, as far as they would go. "As deep as your arm. You lie down in it, flat, on your back, and he puts the boards over you, and then the salt on top, sacks and sacks, and you go through the gate under a ton of salt and nobody ever knows. He's been doing it for twenty years. Never been caught." He grinned. "He took a whole family of Fallow Street weavers through once, in the plague year. Five of them and a goat."
"A goat," said Oren.
"It was a small goat."
Lyra looked at Corin. Corin looked at Oren. Oren looked at the roof.
"Three of us," said Lyra. "Corin, and the Captain, and me. In the bed. Under the salt."
"Three's easy. Three's nothing. The Captain's two, but still."
"And the others?"
They all looked at Kestrel.
She had been sitting by the wall all through it, with her spear upright beside her and her eyes on Garrett, not saying anything. Now she looked at Brin, lying on his side in the hay with Oda beside him. Then back at them.
"Oda stays," she said. "With Brin. Three days. Here." She did not ask anyone. "If he moves, he dies. So he does not move. Oda will see to it."
Oda, by Brin, did not look up. She nodded once.
"And you?" said Corin.
"This one does not lie under salt," said Kestrel. "This one does not lie under anything." She said it quite simply. "This one and Sef go by the water. At night. The way the man with the red cap knows."
Garrett went red.
"I don't—I mean—" He looked at the Ashborn woman. At her shaved head with the gray line over it from the nape to the nose. At her long hide coat and her white fur cape and her spear. "I mean, yes. There's a way. By the fen drains and the Mill Cut and then the river. My cousin Ned's got an eel punt. He goes in and out every night, under the bridges, with his traps. Nobody stops an eel punt." He hesitated. "But you'd have to lie down in it. In the bottom. Under the traps. Under a tarpaulin. It smells something terrible."
"Lying under eels," said Kestrel, "is not the same as lying under salt."
Garrett opened his mouth. He shut it.
"It isn't," he agreed weakly.
And so it was decided.
They left the barn at noon.
Corin went to Brin before they went. He did not mean to. His feet took him. Across the hay, to where the big man lay on his right side with his eyes shut and Oda's hand on his chest, and the bound place under his left arm dark again already, a little, through the strips of blanket and shirt.
Corin knelt down in the hay beside him. Not close. A hand's width.
Brin opened his eyes.
They looked at each other.
Corin did not know what to say. He had never known what to say to Brin. He had walked beside him across the Glass for days, and the big man had carried everybody's things, and said almost nothing, and what he said had always been in the Ashborn tongue, or in a handful of southern words, slow and careful, like stones.
"Thank you," said Corin. "For staying. For the wall."
Brin looked at him for a long time.
Then he said something. In his own tongue. Very low.
Corin looked at Kestrel. She had come to stand behind him.
"He says," said Kestrel, "this one stood in the way. That is what this one is for." She paused. "And he says, the old woman did not need this one to stand. She was already sitting."
Corin did not understand. Then he did.
She stayed in her chair. With her lamp lit. Waiting for them.
"She was braver than any of us," he said.
Brin closed his eyes.
"Yes," he said. In the southern talk. "Than this one. Than you." He breathed. The catch in it. "Be quick, heron."
Corin stood up.
He went to the door of the barn, where the others were waiting. Lyra. Oren. Kestrel and Sef, with their spears. Garrett with the pony. And he looked back, once. At the big man in the hay, and the small wiry woman beside him with her hand on his chest, in the dim gray light under the reed roof.
Oda looked up. Her quick black eyes. She lifted one hand, the one that was not on Brin. And made a small gesture. Like pushing something away. Go.
They went.
Across the Marsh
Garrett talked the whole way.
He walked at the pony's head, with the reins in one hand, and the cart creaked along behind him on the frozen track, and he talked. Across the fen road. Down a side track between two ditches. Along the top of a dyke, with the black water on both sides and the reeds rattling. Out onto the Saltmarsh, where the land went white and flat and strange, with salt crusted on the edges of the pools and the wind coming off the sea a long way to the east, smelling of something Corin did not know.
Garrett talked about all of it. About his uncle Pim, and his uncle Pim's geese, which had bitten him every summer of his life. About his cousin Ned, and Ned's eels, and the time Ned caught an eel as long as a man and it got loose in the kitchen. About the pony, whose name was Duchess, and who had been his grandmother's, and who was the stubbornest animal in the fens and possibly in the world.
And about the city.
Corin walked beside the cart, on the far side from the ditch, with his crutch, and listened.
"It's the lists," said Garrett. "That's what everybody's talking about. Every day now. Not just Thursdays. Every single day, Sundays too, a big black case from the Ministry to the Bank, first bell, and back again the next morning. My mate Dickon's a porter at the Bank, he carries the case up the back stair sometimes when the sergeant's legs are bad. He says it's fat as a pudding. He says it's got fifty names in it. Sixty." Garrett shook his head. "And every name's somebody. Somebody's mum. Somebody's uncle who writes letters on the Corn Steps."
Lyra, on the other side of the cart, said nothing.
"And the Auditor," said Garrett. "That's the other thing. That's the funny thing. Dickon says."
Corin's foot came down wrong on the frozen track. He caught himself on the crutch.
"What about the Auditor?" he said.
"She's sending them back." Garrett grinned over his shoulder. "The lines. You know, the lines? The ones that say what the Bank does with you? Dickon says she sends half of them back to the Ministry. Every day. Wrong address. Wrong name. No such room. He says the clerks downstairs can't stop talking about it. He says she found some old rule, some dead Auditor's rule from a hundred years ago, that says you can't write a line against somebody without their proper name, and now the Ministry's going spare." He laughed. "Dickon says the sergeant who brings the case has started looking like a man who's been hit on the head with a ham."
Corin walked on.
He did not say anything. He did not know what to say. He felt something go through him. Not a pull. Not like the spoon, or the copybook. Something else. Something like the moment under the Eye when Malakor had first said there was a sister, in the back of his head, very quietly, and then stopped.
"Who is she?" he said. "The Auditor?"
Garrett shrugged.
"Nobody knows. Nobody ever knows, with the Auditor. She just is." He thought about it. "Young, Dickon says. Younger than you'd think. Green coat. Never goes out. Lives up under the roof of the Bank, at the top, in rooms nobody else is let into. Has a great big silent man who follows her everywhere, like a shadow, with a slate round his neck." He clicked his tongue at the pony. "Dickon's frightened of the big man. Everybody is. He says he's never heard him make a sound."
Corin did not answer.
I passed my brother on a stair. He did not know where that came from. It was not his. It was not in the Ledger. It came up out of nowhere, in somebody else's voice, a girl's, and went away again, like a fish turning in deep water. He did not know whose it was. He did not know if it was anybody's. He thought perhaps he had made it up.
He did not ask anything else about the Auditor.
Lyra was looking at him across the cart. He did not look back.
"And my brother?" she said. To Garrett. "Have you heard anything? About Rowan? Rowan Thorne?"
Garrett looked round at her.
"The young lord?" he said. "Saints, yes. Everybody's heard about him." He looked a little awkward. "He's at the Long Office. In the Ministry. A cadet. He goes in every day, Dickon's cousin says, she does the stairs there. Every single day, regular as a clock, in his cadet's coat with no badge on it. And he carries all his family's papers in his coat pocket. Inside. All the time. Every day. He won't put them down." Garrett paused. "They say his aunt's gone quiet. Up on the Hill. Lady Thorne. The old one. They say she just sits by her window."
Lyra had gone very still.
"Cressida," she said.
"That's her. And they say," Garrett lowered his voice, though there was nobody for a mile in any direction but ducks, "they say the Bank came to weigh her house. After. And the Auditor herself went up the Hill to do it. In a carriage. And the young lord wouldn't give her the papers. Stood in the hall with his arms folded and said No." He grinned again. "And she let him keep them. Wrote some line that meant he could. Dickon says the clerks talked about that for a week."
Lyra did not say anything for a long time.
They walked. The cart creaked. The pony, Duchess, put her head down and leaned into her collar on a rise in the dyke, and the wheels ground on frozen stones.
"He said no," said Lyra at last. Very quietly. Not to Garrett. To the marsh.
"That's what they say."
"He never says no. He never says anything. He's sixteen and he folds paper into little squares and he doesn't talk." Her voice had gone odd. "He said no to the Bank."
Corin looked at her across the cart then. She was not looking at him. She was looking straight ahead, at the white flat land and the gray wall coming up out of it, very far off, with her bound hand pressed flat against the front of her coat.
Her face was wet.
She did not wipe it.
Garrett, who was not a fool whatever his uncle said, stopped talking. For a whole mile. Corin counted the ditches. He got as far as nine, and then he lost count, and he started again.
The North Gate
Uncle Pim's farm sat on the Saltmarsh like a toad on a wet stone.
It was low and dark and squat, built of old brick gone green with damp, with a roof of black tarred reeds that came down nearly to the ground on all sides. There was a yard of frozen mud, and a midden steaming gently in one corner, and a long barn, and a pond with ice on it and three gray geese standing on the ice looking cross. And all round it, flat, white, going away to the edge of the sky, there was the marsh. Pools of black water with salt crusted white round their edges. Low dykes. Reeds. And here and there the long low shapes of the salt pans, square, shallow, frozen, like great windows laid flat on the ground.
And beyond the marsh, to the south, a long way off and very near, there was the wall.
The city wall. Corin had never seen it from outside. He had lived his whole life inside it, as far as he could remember, which was not very far. He had left the city by water when he went north, lying in the bottom of a punt under a tarpaulin, and seen nothing. He saw it now.
It was huge. Gray stone, very old, going up out of the marsh as high as a church, and going away to left and right as far as he could see, in a long slow curve, with towers on it every so often. Square towers, squat, with dark slits for windows. Snow lay along the top of the wall in a long white line. Smoke went up behind it from a great many chimneys, more than he could count, and hung over the city in a brown haze. And through the haze, far off, faint, he could see the shape of the Spire on its hill. Black. Thin. With a red light at the top.
And in the wall, straight ahead, at the end of the road across the marsh, there was the gate.
They stood behind Uncle Pim's midden and looked at it.
Uncle Pim stood with them. He was a big man, wide, with a red face and small pale eyes and a straw in his mouth that he chewed on and never took out. He had not said anything when Garrett brought them into his yard. He had looked at them. At Oren. At Lyra. At Corin's face, the gray side. At the two Ashborn with their spears, who had stopped at the edge of the yard and would come no further. And he had moved the straw from one side of his mouth to the other and said, "Hm." And that was all.
He moved the straw now.
"Look at that," he said.
They looked.
The gate was an arch. A great dark one, with a tower on either side, and the road going into it and through it like water going down a drain. Corin had expected it to be open. It had always been open, as long as anyone could remember. The north gate was never shut.
It was open now. But there was a line.
Carts. Wagons. People on foot. A long dark line of them, going back from the mouth of the gate along the road across the marsh, further than he could see. Not moving. Or moving so slowly that he could not tell. Farm carts with their loads under tarpaulins. A carrier's van. A man with a donkey. Women with baskets on their heads, standing in the snow, waiting. Waiting.
And at the mouth of the gate, there were gray coats.
A great many. He could see them from here, small and dark against the pale road. Around the first cart in the line. Climbing on it. Pulling off the tarpaulin. Lifting things down. Opening sacks. And a white barrier across the road, the same as the ones on the bridges, and a brazier, glowing, and a table with a lamp on it, blue, though it was not yet dark.
"Every cart," said Uncle Pim. "Since Monday. Every single one. Unload and load again. Look in every sack. Count every turnip." He chewed his straw. "Used to be, they'd wave you through. Look at the top of your load and wave you through. Since Monday, they've a man from the Ministry at the table. A thin one. Writes everything down."
Garrett had gone pale.
"The salt," he said. "Uncle. If they're looking in every sack—"
"They look in the sacks," said Uncle Pim. "They don't look under the floor." He moved the straw. "Nobody looks under the floor. They're not built to."
Nobody said anything.
Corin looked at the line of carts. At the gray coats, small and busy, at the mouth of the gate.
And he saw that there were other carts. Not in the line. Coming the other way. Out of the side roads across the marsh, from east and west. From the villages. From the farms. Black carts. Closed. With the open eye of the Ministry painted on their sides in white. Three of them. Four. He did not count. Coming slowly along the dykes toward the gate. And they did not wait in the line. They went straight past it. Past the farm carts and the women with their baskets. Up to the barrier. And the gray coats lifted the barrier for them, and they went through, into the dark arch, into the city.
"What are those?" said Lyra.
Uncle Pim spat. Neatly. Round the straw. Into the midden.
"Books," he said.
They looked at him.
"Books," he said again. "And papers. And letters. And pens and ink and whatever else. From the villages round. They go round with a list, the gray coats, and they knock on doors, and they take whatever's on the list, and they put it in those carts, and they bring it in to the Ministry." He chewed. "My neighbor over at Reedham. Old Jem Cutler. They took his family book. His grandmother's. In its box. With all the births and deaths in it, back to the old king." He paused. "Jem can't read. He just liked to have it."
Corin stood very still behind the midden.
The old king.
He did not know why that caught on him. It did. Like a sleeve on a nail.
"What old king?" he said.
Uncle Pim looked at him. For the first time, Corin thought, really looked. His small pale eyes went over the gray side of Corin's face, slowly, and back.
"Just a saying," he said. "Back to the old king. Back to the beginning. Means a long time." He moved the straw. "My granda used to say it."
Corin did not answer.
He looked at the black carts with the white eyes on them, going into the dark mouth of the gate one after another. Full of other people's books. Other people's letters. Other people's births and deaths, in boxes, back to the beginning.
"Tomorrow," said Uncle Pim. "Not tonight. Tonight they're sharp, it's the end of the day, they want their supper, they're cross. Tomorrow's turnip day. First light. Every cart from here to Reedham'll be in that line with turnips, and the gray coats'll be sick of turnips by the third bell, and they'll start waving them through." He took the straw out of his mouth at last, and looked at it, and put it back in. "And I'll be in the middle. With salt. Like always."
He turned and went back across the yard toward the house, slowly, with his hands in his pockets.
"Supper's eels," he said, over his shoulder. "Ned brought them. The tall one with the spear can have the heads. I'm told they like heads."
Kestrel, at the edge of the yard, did not move. But Corin, looking at her, saw something happen at the corner of her mouth.
Oren's Page
Oren found him by the pond, after.
Corin had gone down there because it was the only place on Uncle Pim's farm where you could not see the gate. The pond lay in a hollow behind the barn, with a low bank of frozen reeds round it, and the three gray geese standing on the ice in the middle, looking at him. He stood at the edge and looked back at them. One of them hissed. He did not mind. It was a clean simple sound, and it meant exactly what it said.
Oren came round the end of the barn and stood beside him. Not close. Oren never stood close to anybody. He stood a little way off with his hands in the pockets of his old army coat and looked at the geese too.
"They don't like you," he said.
"They don't like anybody."
"Sensible birds."
They stood there for a while.
"My brother's in there," said Oren. He did not look at the city. He did not need to. "Harlan. Somewhere. At the Gate Barracks, likely. On the river side of the Ministry. With his whole battalion." He breathed out. "If they're turning over every cart at that gate, it's his men doing it. Some of them. The ones with the round things the boys beat, on their belts." He frowned at the geese. "Or it's the Cleansing lot, the clerks with sticks. I don't know. I don't know anything anymore. I've been gone too long."
Corin did not say anything.
"I'll go to Crown Walk," said Oren. "When we're in. To the Duke. Marrow. His house guards. I drilled them, before. Old men, most of them. Bearded. Half an ear on one of them." He was quiet. "I can't drill them now. I haven't got it to give. But they might listen to me anyway. Old soldiers do. They listen to the voice, after they've forgotten the man."
"Oren—"
"I'm not stupid, lad. I know what I am." Oren took one hand out of his pocket. The right one. Big and square and scarred across the knuckles. He looked at it. "I can hold a sword. I can sharpen one. I can stand in the way of a thing. I can tell a girl her back foot's off the ground from twenty paces." He put the hand back. "That's what's left. That, and a few words. Not many. Fewer every time."
The goose hissed again.
"Will you do something for me?" said Oren.
Corin looked at him.
"Read me my page," said Oren. "In your book. The one you've got about me. Lyra told me you've got one. For everybody." He did not look at Corin. He looked at the geese. "I can't read it myself. Never could. Letters go about like ants when I look at them. My mother tried. My father tried. Harlan tried, once, the winter he taught me the calls with two of—" He stopped. Something went across his face. "With two of the—with the little ones for soup. Harlan tried. And I never could."
Corin took the Ledger out of his coat.
He did not have to look for the page. He knew where it was. He had written it on the Glass, in the dark, by a fire of thorn sticks, the night after Oren lost his swordplay under the Eye. And he had added to it since. A line at a time.
He opened it. He held it so the last of the light from the sky fell on the page.
He read.
He read it slowly. The way he read himself, every morning. With his gloved finger moving along under the words. Out loud. Not loudly. So that only Oren would hear. And the geese.
"OREN VANCE. CAPTAIN. OF THE CITY GUARD. AFTER, OF THE DUKE OF MARROW'S HOUSE."
Oren did not move.
"HIS FATHER WAS A CAPTAIN. HIS FATHER'S FATHER CAME FROM THE NORTH AND NEVER SAID WHERE. HE SWORE BY SOLA'S TEETH."
"SOLA VANCE WAS HIS FATHER'S FATHER'S SISTER. SHE WAS A GENERAL. SHE MADE THE GLASS. HE FOUND HER UNDER THE EYE."
"HIS BROTHER IS HARLAN. OLDER. COMMANDER OF A BATTALION. THEY MET ON A BRIDGE. HARLAN KNOCKED BACK. WITH ONE FINGER."
"HE HAS LOST LOCK, ICE, DRUMS, CHAIR, SPOON, LILIES, DOOR. HE SAYS THE SHUT. THE HARD COLD. THE ROUND THINGS THE BOYS BEAT. THE THING YOU SIT ON. THE LITTLE ONE FOR SOUP. WHITE FLOWERS. THE WAY IN. DO NOT ASK HIM FOR THE WORDS. GIVE HIM HIS OWN."
Oren's face did something. Corin did not look at it. He went on.
"HE LOST HIS SWORD UNDER THE EYE. KROSS TOOK IT. HE STILL HAS HIS HANDS. HE STILL HAS HIS EYES. HE SAW LYRA'S BACK FOOT."
"HE CARRIED MY PACK ACROSS THE GLASS AND SAID IT WAS NOTHING."
"HE SAT ON A CONSTABLE ONCE. FOR LYRA'S FATHER."
"HE STANDS IN DOORWAYS SO NOBODY CAN GET PAST."
"HE IS THE BRAVEST MAN I KNOW."
Corin stopped.
That was the end of the page. He had not meant to read the last line out loud. He had forgotten it was there. He had written it on the Glass, in the dark, and not looked at it since.
He shut the book.
Oren stood by the pond with his hands in his pockets and looked at the geese.
He did not say anything for a long time.
Then he took his right hand out of his pocket again. And wiped his face with it. Once. Quickly. With the back of his scarred knuckles. The way a man wipes rain off.
"Sat on a constable," he said. His voice was rough. "You put that in."
"Lyra told me."
"She would." Oren breathed in, through his nose, long. "He had his hand on her father. On Lord Thorne. In the street. Shaking him. An old man." He looked at the ice. "I didn't think about it. I just sat on him. He was very surprised."
Corin's laugh came up. Arran's. Short and quiet, over the pond. One of the geese turned its head and looked at him with great disgust.
Oren's mouth moved. Under his short gray beard.
"Thank you, lad," he said.
He put his hand back in his pocket. He turned to go back to the house. And then stopped, at the end of the barn, and looked back.
"If I lose more," he said. "Words. When I lose them. Will you put them in? On my page? What I say instead?"
"Yes."
"So somebody can read them to me." He nodded slowly. "So I know what I'm saying."
He went round the end of the barn.
Corin stood by the pond with the Ledger in his hands and the geese looking at him, and the gray light going, and did not move for a long time.
