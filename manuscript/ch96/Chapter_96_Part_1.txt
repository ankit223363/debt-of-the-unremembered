Chapter 96: The Long Way Back, Part 1 of 5: Haverstock
Haverstock
Corin read himself in the stable loft at the Sun and Pike, with the snow-light coming in through a crack in the boards and the horses moving about below him in the dark.
He read the way he read every morning now. Out loud. Under his breath. With one gloved finger on the page, moving along under the words. He did not need the finger. He knew the words. He had written them himself, in big square capitals, pressing hard. But Vespera had said read, not remember, and so he read. And the finger helped. It kept him from going too fast. It kept him from skipping. When he skipped, he was afraid he would skip over something that was not there anymore and not notice.
MY NAME IS CORIN VANE.
That was the first line. Always. He had put it at the front of every Ledger since the first one.
I AM A HARVESTER. I WAS A HARVESTER OF THE MINISTRY. I LIVED AT NUMBER 14 TALLOW STREET, AT THE TOP, UNDER THE ROOF. MY LANDLADY IS MRS. MARTA. HER DAUGHTER IS JUNO.
He read it slowly. He saw the words and heard them and something in him said yes, each time, quietly, the way you say yes when somebody calls your name in a crowd. Yes. That's me. That's still me.
LINUS HALE TOOK ME IN WHEN I HAD NOTHING. HE TAUGHT ME. HE IS EMPTIED NOW. HE IS AT THE LAMPLIGHTER'S REST.
Yes.
LYRA THORNE. SHE SINGS FLAT. HER MOTHER SANG. HER MOTHER HAD A GARDEN WITH A RED BRICK WALL.
Yes. Yes.
OREN VANCE. CAPTAIN. HE HAS LOST LOCK, ICE, DRUMS, CHAIR, SPOON, LILIES, DOOR. DO NOT ASK HIM FOR THEM. GIVE HIM OTHER WORDS. HE HAS LOST HIS SWORD. HE STILL HAS HIS HANDS.
Yes.
He went on. Through the first part. Twice, the way he always did. Then the new parts, the parts from the Glass, from the Eye. I AM A BRIGHT. I AM THE ONE WHO GOT OUT. THE GIRL WAS KEPT. HER NAME IS ELARA. And MALAKOR. HE GOT ME OUT. HE WAS GLAD. And the newest of all, from the sheep pen and the loft at Thistle Ford, in his own hand, which had got slower and squarer and more careful even in the last few days, as if every letter was a stone he had to lift.
ABBE GAVE LYRA A SPOON. IT HAS A TREE ON IT. IT PULLED. LYRA SAYS WHEN. DO NOT KEEP ASKING.
I WANT TO KEEP ASKING.
He stopped on that one. He always did. He looked at it for a while.
Then he closed the book and did up the strap and sat with both hands flat on the cover.
It was very quiet in the loft.
Below him, the horses shifted and blew. Somewhere in the inn yard a bucket rattled on the pump. A woman's voice called something, and a man's answered. The snow-light through the crack in the boards lay across the hay in a thin white line, and the dust turned in it, slowly.
Haverstock.
They had come in at dusk last night, the six of them, up the long road round the hill, past the rings of houses one above another, to the top, to the cross and the church and the inn. The Sun and Pike. The same inn as before. The same landlord, a round red man with a bald head, who had looked at Oren in the doorway and gone white, and then red, and then said, Sola's teeth. Captain. You came back. And given them the stable loft, because the rooms were full of soldiers, he said, gray ones, going north, two days ago, and some had come back and some had not.
The stable loft was better anyway. The Ashborn would not sleep in rooms. Kestrel had looked at the stairs going up inside the inn, narrow and dark, and said, No, and that was that.
Corin could hear them now. Oda, at the far end of the loft, in the hay, snoring her little clicking snore. Sef, near her, breathing like a child. Oren by the ladder, sitting up, because Oren always sat up by the way down. And Kestrel somewhere, awake. He could not hear her. He never could. But he knew she was awake. She always was.
And Lyra.
She was asleep. A little way off. A hand's width more than a hand's width. Lying on her side in the hay with her back to him and her knees drawn up and her bound hand tucked under her cheek. He could see the back of her head. Her hair, growing out, dark, in tufts. Her shoulder, under the blanket, going up and down.
He did not look at her for long. He looked at the line of snow-light on the hay instead.
She had the spoon.
In her coat. Inside, over her heart, wrapped in the end of her scarf. He knew it was there. He could feel it. Not with his hands. With something else. With whatever it was in him that had pulled toward the left-hand road at the Two Sisters, and pulled toward the rod at the foot of the bunker stair, and pulled, in the long hall at Thistle Ford, toward a little piece of honey-colored wood in an old woman's hand.
He could feel it now. Across the hay. A small warm tug. Like a thread tied to something.
Lyra says when.
He looked at the snow-light.
He did not ask. He had written that he would not. He had written that he wanted to. Both were true. He sat with both of them, in the quiet loft, with his hands flat on the cover of the Ledger, and let them both be true, and did not do anything about either.
That was new too.
He did not know when that had started. Being able to sit with two things at once and not do anything. He thought perhaps it had started under the Eye. Or on the Glass after, walking south, with the gray up his face and Malakor gone. He did not know.
He thought perhaps it was because there was nobody in his head anymore to tell him what to do.
The Quiet in My Head
For a long time there had been a voice.
Not his own. An old man's. Dry and sour and clever, with a laugh in it like a door creaking. It had come into him in a room under the city, long ago, from a jar of old Ash, and it had stayed. It had sat in the back of his head like a man in a chair by a fire, and talked. All the time. About everything. It had told him which way to turn and which way not to. It had told him when he was being a fool, which was often. It had told him about the old work, the slow ways, the rods that turned right. It had told him about a girl in a blue wagon.
And at the end, under the Eye, it had told him to go down a stair. Go on, boy. Down you go. I'll know it when I see it.
And then it had spent itself. All of it. On a door that would not open. On getting him through. And gone quiet. And not come back.
Malakor.
Corin sat in the stable loft at Haverstock with the Ledger on his knees and listened to the place where the voice had been.
It was not empty. That was the strange thing. He had thought, at first, on the Glass, walking south, that it would be empty. A room with nobody in it. A chair by a dead fire. And it was a little like that. But not all the way.
There was a hum.
Very low. Very slow. Down at the bottom of him, under everything, the way the river runs under the ice. An old man's hum. Not Malakor's. Somebody older still. Hask, the Ashborn had called him, at Last Thorn. Old Hask, who had been blind, and gray all over, and taught them the notes. Who had died in the camp with Corin's hand on him and gone into Corin with all his years, and his hum had come with him.
It was slower now than it had been. Corin had noticed that, on the Glass. It hummed and stopped, and hummed again. As if it were looking for something. As if it were an old man walking slowly through a house, room by room, opening doors.
And there was a laugh.
Not often. Not loud. A sudden short warm bark of a laugh, like a fox in a hedge. It came up out of him sometimes without warning, when something was funny, or when something was very sad and he did not know what else to do with it. It was not his. It was Arran's. Arran, who had got in the way, and died under a cairn of glass on the edge of the Glass, and laughed at the end. Corin had his laugh now. He did not know what else of Arran's he had. Not much, he thought. A laugh, and a warmth, like a coal in the bottom of a stove.
A hum, and a laugh, and a coal.
And himself.
That was what was in his head now. That was all. No voice telling him which way to go.
He sat with it.
"You're doing it again," said Lyra.
He looked round.
She was awake. She had turned over in the hay and was lying on her back now, with her bound hand on her chest and her eyes open, looking at him across the loft. Across more than a hand's width. In the gray snow-light.
"Doing what?"
"Listening." She sat up. Hay in her hair. "You get a look. Like a dog when somebody's whistled a long way off."
"I wasn't—"
"You were." She pushed the hay out of her hair with her good hand. "Is it him? Is he back?"
Corin did not answer at once.
"No," he said. "It's the quiet."
She looked at him.
"I keep thinking he's going to say something," said Corin. "I'll be walking along, and I'll come to a fork in a road, and I'll wait. For him to say left or right or you're a fool, boy, it's obviously left. And he doesn't." He looked down at the Ledger. "And then I have to decide. On my own."
"Is that bad?"
"I don't know." He thought about it. "It's slow."
Lyra was quiet for a moment.
"Everybody else does it on their own," she said. "Decides. Every day. Without an old man in their head."
"I know."
"So you'll get used to it."
"I know."
"And you've got me." She said it quite flatly. Not looking at him. Picking hay off her blanket. "I'll tell you when you're being a fool. I'll do it better than he did. I'll do it out loud, so everybody can hear."
The laugh came up out of him before he could stop it. Arran's. Short and sharp and warm. A fox in a hedge.
At the far end of the loft Oda stopped snoring and said something cross in her sleep.
Lyra looked at him. Her mouth had gone up at one corner.
"That one's not yours," she said.
"No."
"I like it anyway."
He did not know what to say to that. So he did not say anything. He looked at the line of snow-light on the hay, and she went back to picking the hay off her blanket, and neither of them said anything for a while. And it was all right. The quiet was all right.
Below them, the stable door opened, and cold came up through the floor, and the landlord's voice called up the ladder.
"Captain? Captain Vance? There's porridge. And there's men in the yard asking for you."
Oren, by the top of the ladder, sat up very straight.
"What men?"
"Old ones." The landlord's voice had changed. "From the town. They heard you was back. They're standing in the snow in their good coats." A pause. "Some of them haven't had their good coats on in twenty years."
Oren did not move for a moment.
Then he got up, slowly, with one hand on the beam. He put his sword on his back. He pulled his old army coat straight. He looked at Corin, and at Lyra, and at nobody.
And went down the ladder.
Corin went to the crack in the boards and looked out.
The inn yard was white and trodden. The pump had icicles on its spout. And in the middle of the yard, in the snow, in a line, there were old men. Eight of them. Nine. He did not count. Old men in long coats, some brown, some gray, some a faded red like Granny Abbe's, with brass buttons that had been polished till they shone. Standing straight. As straight as they could. With their breath going up in white clouds.
One of them was the old man Corin remembered from the first time. The one who had saluted Oren in the square by the cross, crying. In his moth-eaten coat with the polished buttons.
Oren came out of the stable door into the snow.
And the line of old men lifted their hands to their foreheads. All together. Slowly. Not quite together. Some of their hands shook.
Oren stood in front of them.
Corin could not see his face. He could see his back. His broad back in the old army coat, with the sword on it. Very still.
Then Oren lifted his own hand to his forehead.
And held it there.
Nobody said anything. Nobody moved. The snow came down very lightly on all of them, on the old men's shoulders and their gray heads and their polished buttons, and on Oren's shoulders, and on the sword.
Corin found that his eyes had gone hot. He did not know why. He did not know these men. He did not know what war they had been in, or who had led it, or why it mattered to them so much that a Vance had come back over the Glass. He knew only what was in the Ledger. OREN VANCE. CAPTAIN. And a woman sitting very straight in a chair under the Eye, with her hands on a map, who had been dead since before any of those old men were born, nearly, and had sent her soldiers home.
It was enough.
He turned away from the crack in the boards.
Lyra was watching him.
She did not say anything. She did not need to.
The Fen Road
They left Haverstock a little before noon, by the south road, down the hill through the rings of houses.
People came out to watch them go. Not many. A few in every doorway. Women with shawls over their heads, and children holding on to their skirts, and the old men in their good coats, standing along the road in the snow. Nobody said anything. Nobody waved. They only watched. And when Oren went past, at the front with Kestrel, with his sword on his back, the old men lifted their hands to their foreheads again, one after another, as he passed. Like a wave going along a beach.
Oren did not look at them. Corin could see the back of his neck. It had gone very red.
At the bottom of the hill the road went out through a gap in an old wall and turned south, and the town was behind them, and there was only the road.
It was a different road from the one in the north. Corin could feel it under his boots. Wider. Flatter. With stones under the snow, and ruts, and here and there the frozen dung of horses. A road that went somewhere and came back again. A road that was used.
And the land was different.
The hills had gone. They had been going for a day, getting lower, softer, rounder, like a blanket somebody was slowly smoothing out. Now they were gone. The land in front of him was flat. Flat and white and wide, going away to the edge of the sky in every direction, with nothing to stop the eye. A few trees, here and there, bent and black and bare. Long straight lines of dark that were ditches, full of black water with ice on it, cutting the white into squares. A farm, a long way off, low and gray, with a thread of smoke going up. And reeds. Great beds of them, dead and pale and rattling in the wind, taller than a man, along the sides of the ditches.
The fens.
He had come through them going north. He remembered the cold. He remembered the punt, and the black water, and a heron, standing on one leg in the reeds, that had looked at him as he went past. He remembered Lyra, on the first night out, saying You walk like a heron. Even when you fall over.
He walked. He leaned on the ash crutch. His leg was better. Hild had said so. But the road was long and the crutch helped, and he had not given it up.
"Fen," he said. Under his breath. "Ditch. Reed. Ice. Road."
Lyra, beside him, a hand's width away, did not say anything. She knew what he was doing. She had stopped telling him not to.
"Farm. Smoke. Tree."
They walked.
In the middle of the afternoon they began to meet people.
The first was a man with a handcart. Coming north. Toward them. Alone. A thin man in a long black coat gone green at the seams, with a scarf wound round his face and a hat pulled down, pushing a two-wheeled cart piled high with something under a tarpaulin, tied down with rope. He was pushing it very hard. Leaning into it. His breath going up in clouds.
He saw them coming down the road toward him. Six people, three of them with gray on their faces and spears. He stopped dead.
He did not run. He could not run, with the cart. He stood in the middle of the road with his hands on the cart's handles and stared at them, and his face above the scarf went the color of the snow.
Kestrel stopped. Everyone stopped.
The man looked at Kestrel. At the spear. At the gray line over her bare head. Then he looked past her, at the rest of them. At Oren. At Lyra. At Corin.
At Corin's face.
Corin saw him see it. The gray. On the left side, from the collar to the corner of the mouth. And he saw the man's face change. Not to fear. To something else. Something Corin did not have a word for.
"You're him," the man said. Very low. Through the scarf. "Aren't you. The one on the posters."
Nobody answered.
"They've drawn you too fat," said the man.
Arran's laugh came up out of Corin before he could stop it. The fox in the hedge. Short and sharp, in the cold white silence of the fen road.
The man flinched. Then he stared at Corin. Then, slowly, something in his face loosened.
"Everybody says that," said Corin.
"I'll bet they do."
The man let go of one of the cart handles. He pulled his scarf down off his face. He was not young. He had a long gray face with a long gray nose and spectacles, mended at one side with thread.
"Fallow," he said. "Edmund Fallow. I keep—I kept—a school. In Thimble Lane. Twelve children. Letters and sums and the names of the Saints." He put his hand on the tarpaulin over the cart. "These are my books. All of them. Every book I own. Forty years of them."
He did not take the tarpaulin off. He did not need to. Corin could see the shapes under it. Square. Stacked. Tied.
"They came on Tuesday," said Edmund Fallow. "To my neighbor. The scribe across the lane. Margery. She wrote letters for people, for a penny. They came with a paper, and took her, and took her pens and her ink and every scrap of paper in her room, and her husband's old letters that she kept in a box under the bed." He breathed in. "And then one of them looked across the lane at my window. At the books in it. And wrote something down."
Nobody said anything.
"So I packed them," said Edmund Fallow. "That night. Every one. And I put them in a cart, and I walked out of the north gate in the morning with a load of firewood on top, and nobody stopped me." He looked down the road. North. The way they had come. "I've a sister in Haverstock. She'll have me, I think. And if she won't, there's the free valleys, past that. I've heard there's no Ministry in the free valleys."
"Not yet," said Lyra.
Fallow looked at her.
"They've been to Thistle Ford," said Lyra. "Riders. With posters. And a clerk with a book, to count them. And a cart with the blue hand on it." She was quiet. "They're going everywhere."
Edmund Fallow stood very still in the road.
Then he put both hands back on the handles of his cart.
"Then I'll go further," he said. "There's always further. Isn't there?"
Nobody answered him.
He pushed the cart forward. Past them. Leaning into it. The wheels creaked in the ruts. And as he went past Corin, he stopped, for a moment, and looked at him again. At the gray.
"There's a lot of people in the city," he said, "who'd like to see you come back." He looked down at his books. "I'd have liked to see it. I'd have liked to be there."
Then he went on. North. Up the white road, between the ditches, toward Haverstock, pushing everything he owned, getting smaller.
Corin watched him go.
After that they met more.
Not many. One every hour or so. A woman with a baby and a bundle, walking fast, who would not look at them and crossed to the far side of the road. Two young men with a donkey, laden with sacks, who stopped and stared and then went on, whispering. An old man on his own, with nothing at all, not even a coat, who sat down in the snow at the side of the road when he saw the Ashborn spears, and put his hands over his head, and did not move until they had gone past.
All going north. All away from the city.
Nobody was going south but them.
