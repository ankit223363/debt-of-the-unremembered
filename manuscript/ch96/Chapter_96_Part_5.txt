Chapter 96: The Long Way Back, Part 5 of 5: Sef
Sef
Sef was outside the barn when Corin came out of the house again, sitting on the frozen edge of the horse trough with his spear across his knees and his face turned up to the sky.
The clouds had gone. The stars were out over the marsh, hard and white and close, more of them than Corin could ever have counted, and the moon was coming up behind the barn roof, low, yellow, enormous. Sef was looking at it. He had his mouth a little open, like a child.
He looked round when Corin came across the yard. And grinned. All teeth. The way he always did.
"Heron," he said.
"Sef."
Corin sat down on the edge of the trough. Not close. The ice in it was thick and gray, with straw frozen into it.
Sef was young. Corin had never asked how young. Younger than Lyra, he thought. Not much older than Juno. He had a thin brown face, and quick hands, and a line of gray that came down from his hairline over his brow and his nose and stopped just past the tip, like a drop of water that had run down and frozen there. He had got it, Kestrel said, the winter before last, carrying a dead child out of the Glass for its mother. He did not seem to mind it. He did not seem to mind anything. He laughed at Arran's terrible jokes. He went into ditches up to his waist and came out laughing. He walked at the front with Kestrel and looked at everything.
"The city," said Sef. He nodded at the glow over the wall. "Is it big?"
"Very big."
"Bigger than Last Thorn?"
Corin thought about Last Thorn. The camp on the thorn slope, under the Lip, with its hide tents in rows and its fires and its goats with bells. Not so very many people. Perhaps. He had never counted.
"Much bigger," he said. "There are more people in one street than in all of Last Thorn."
Sef looked at the glow for a long time, with his mouth open.
"And they all live in houses?"
"Most of them."
"Stone ones? Like the old woman's town? Like Haverstock?"
"Stone. And brick. And wood, some."
"Do they move them? In the summer?"
Corin looked at him.
"No," he said. "They stay where they are. People live in the same house all their lives. Some of them. And their children after them."
Sef thought about this. He seemed to find it very strange.
"Then how do they see anything?" he said.
Corin did not know how to answer that.
They sat on the edge of the trough and looked at the glow for a while.
"There was a girl," said Sef suddenly. "At Last Thorn. Before. When the iron men came, and the fire." He was not grinning now. "She had a baby. She was running with it. Up the slope. And she fell, and I picked her up. And she held my hand. Like this." He held out his own hand, brown and thin, in the moonlight, and closed it on nothing. Hard. "Very tight. All the way up to the top. And then she let go, and ran on, east, with the others, to the Salt Wells." He opened his hand and looked at it. "I don't know her name. I didn't ask."
Corin did not say anything.
"I think about her," said Sef. "In the night. I think, I should have asked her name." He looked up at Corin. "In your book. Do you have the names of the people? The ones you don't know?"
"Some of them."
"How? If you don't know them?"
"I write what I know," said Corin. "The girl with the baby, who held Sef's hand on the thorn slope. That's a name. Of a kind."
Sef thought about this for a long time.
Then he grinned again. All at once. All teeth.
"Put her in," he said. "Will you? In your book. Like that. The girl who held Sef's hand." He seemed pleased. "Then she's somewhere."
"I'll put her in."
"And put me in too." He said it lightly. As if it were nothing. As if he were asking for a second helping. "Sef. Who went to the big city with the heron. Who lay under the eels." He laughed. "Put that in."
Corin looked at him.
The moon was up over the barn roof now, white, and the light of it lay on Sef's thin young face and the gray line down his nose and his teeth, grinning. Very young. Younger than Juno, perhaps. Corin did not know.
He felt something go through him. Cold. Quick. Like a draft under a door. He did not know what it was.
"I'll put you in," he said.
"Good." Sef stood up off the trough. He picked up his spear. He looked toward the bottom of the yard, toward the little black pool among the reeds, where a thin line of yellow light had just appeared, low down, on the water. "There's the eel man." He grinned. "Kestrel will want me."
He went toward the barn door. Then he stopped, and looked back.
"Heron," he said. "In the big city. With all the houses that don't move. I'll see a great many things. Won't I?"
"Yes," said Corin. "A great many."
Sef grinned, and went in to get Kestrel.
Corin sat on the edge of the frozen horse trough in the moonlight and took out the Ledger and the pencil, and found a page near the back, and wrote, very slowly, in big square capitals, pressing hard, before he could forget:
THE GIRL WITH THE BABY WHO HELD SEF'S HAND ON THE THORN SLOPE.
And under it:
SEF. WHO WENT TO THE BIG CITY WITH THE HERON. WHO LAY UNDER THE EELS.
He looked at it for a while in the moonlight. Then he shut the book.
Outside the Walls
Ned came for the Ashborn at the middle of the night.
Corin heard the punt before he saw it. A soft knock of wood on wood, out in the dark, at the bottom of Uncle Pim's yard, where a drain came in from the marsh and widened into a little black pool with reeds round it. And a man's voice, very low, saying something to the reeds. And then a lantern, shaded, so that only a thin line of yellow came out of it, low down on the water.
He went out to the barn.
Kestrel and Sef were ready. They had been ready, he thought, since dusk. Sitting side by side on a bale of straw just inside the barn door, with their spears upright beside them and their packs on their backs, in the dark, not talking. Waiting.
Kestrel stood up when he came in.
"Heron," she said.
"Kestrel."
She looked at him for a long moment. In the dark of the barn he could hardly see her face. Only the pale gray line over her shaved head, going back from her brow, catching what little light there was.
"The man with the eels," she said, "will take this one and Sef by the water. Under the walls. By a drain. Into the little river that runs into the big one. And then along the big one, under the bridges, at night. To the stairs where they sell fish." She said it carefully, the way she said everything in the southern talk, as if she were laying stones. "And from there, the girl's back ways, to the church. The man with the eels knows them."
"The Fish Stairs."
"The Fish Stairs." She nodded. "This one will be in the hole under the church before you are. Tomorrow, before the light." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "This one will tell them you are coming under the salt. The boy who carves boats will laugh."
"Gideon doesn't laugh."
"Then this one will tell him something else."
Corin did not know what to say.
He thought about the first time he had seen her. At Last Thorn. On the edge of the Glass. A tall woman in a long hide coat and a white fur cape, with a spear, standing at the top of the thorn slope and looking down at him. At his gray hands. They call this one Kestrel. And she had taken him in. Him, and Lyra, and Oren. Into her camp, among her people. And her people had died for it. Some of them. Hask. Arran. And she had walked with him over the Glass and back. Twice.
And now she was going to lie in the bottom of an eel punt under a tarpaulin and go into a city she had never seen, full of gray coats and blue lamps and his face on every wall. For him.
"You don't have to," he said.
Kestrel looked at him.
"This one does not have to do anything," she said. "You have heard this one say it." She paused. "This one chooses."
"Why?"
Kestrel did not answer at once.
She looked past him. Out of the barn door. Toward the south. Toward the wall, and the glow over it, blue and brown, and the thin black needle of the Spire with its red light.
"The old woman," she said. "With the blue hands. She came to us at Last Thorn, a long time ago. Before you. Before this one was a woman. She came across the Glass from the south with a pack on her back and needles in it, and she sat in Old Hask's tent for a whole winter, and they talked. About the old work. About the ink and the skin. About what can be kept, and how." Kestrel's voice was very quiet. "When she went home in the spring, she left Hask a pattern. On his arm. In the old black. So he would not lose the notes."
Corin did not move.
"He did not lose them," said Kestrel. "Not ever. Not even at the end. Even when everything else was gone. He kept the notes." She looked at Corin. "You have them now. In you. His hum. Because of her."
Corin felt it. Down at the bottom of him. Very low. Very slow. Going through the rooms.
"So this one goes," said Kestrel. "For the notes."
She bent down and picked up her spear.
And then she did something she had never done before. She put out her hand. Her long brown hand, with the gray on the backs of the fingers. Not to touch him. She held it out, flat, palm down, over his head. Just above. Not touching his hair. The way you would hold your hand over a candle flame to feel its warmth.
She held it there for a moment.
Then she took it away, and went out of the barn into the dark, toward the little black pool and the thin line of yellow light. Sef went after her. He stopped at the door, and looked back at Corin, and grinned, all teeth, the way he did. And then he was gone too.
Corin stood in the empty barn and listened.
The soft knock of wood on wood. A low voice. A splash. The creak of a pole going into the mud and coming out again. Going away. Getting fainter. Into the reeds. Into the dark. Into nothing.
He did not go back into the house.
He went round behind the midden instead, to the place where they had stood at dusk and looked at the gate. He stood there in the snow in the dark and looked at it again.
The line of carts was gone. The road across the marsh was empty, white, running away toward the wall. At the mouth of the gate there was a brazier, glowing red, a tiny spot of red in the black. And two blue lamps on either side of it. And beyond them, the arch. Black. A mouth.
And the wall, going away on either side, huge and gray and dark, with snow along the top. And over the wall, the glow.
The city.
It lay behind its wall like a fire behind a fireguard. All that light. Blue, from the Ash lamps, a great dome of blue light going up into the sky over the whole city, so that the snow clouds above it were lit from underneath, faintly, the color of a bruise. And under the blue, here and there, yellow. Windows. Gas lamps. Candles. People. More than he could count. More than he could think about.
And the Spire. On its hill. Black against the glow. With its one red light at the very top, burning, like an eye that never shut.
He stood and looked at it all for a long time.
Somewhere in there was a church on top of more steps than he had ever managed to count. Saint Bride's. With a crypt under it, and a brazier, and a boy who whittled boats, and a girl in a boy's coat who had walked all those miles on frozen feet. Safe.
Somewhere in there was a house on Tallow Street, with a purple door at the back and clocks on every wall inside, and a woman with a face like a kind loaf who had let him a room under the roof when he had nothing, and asked him no questions, and put a bowl of soup outside his door on the nights he came home gray and shaking.
Somewhere in there was a house with a stone bird over its door. And under it, in the dark, an old woman with blue hands.
And somewhere in there was a girl.
He did not know where. He did not know what she looked like. He did not know if she knew his name, or remembered that she had ever had a brother, or had ever held his hand on a road in the snow, a long time ago, looking back. He only knew her name.
Elara.
He said it out loud. Very quietly. To the snow. To the dark. To the wall.
"Elara."
Nothing answered.
The hum went on, low, at the bottom of him. Hask's. Going slowly through the rooms. Opening doors. As if it were looking for something too.
He stood behind Uncle Pim's midden in the dark, and looked at the city, and did not burn anything. He wanted to. He could feel it in his hands. He did not.
He was a street away. A wall away. A night away.
He could wait one more night.
He went back into the house, and got the Ledger out of his pack, and the pencil, and sat down at the end of Uncle Pim's long kitchen table, by the last of the red light from the range, and opened it.
Ledger
He wrote slowly.
He always wrote slowly now. He had noticed it on the Glass, and in the loft at Thistle Ford, and he noticed it again tonight, at the end of Uncle Pim's table, with the range ticking and the red light going down. His hand knew the letters. It had always known them. But it did not want to hurry. It made each one as if it were laying a brick. Pressing hard. Big. Square. One. And then the next.
He did not mind. It meant they would stay where he put them.
He turned to a clean page. He wrote the day at the top, the way he always did.
THURSDAY.
He stopped. He looked at it.
Was it Thursday?
He counted back. On his fingers, under the table, where nobody could see, though there was nobody there to see. The sheep pen. The loft. The road. Haverstock. The barn. Here. He counted it twice. It came out the same both times.
It was Friday.
He crossed out THURSDAY. He did it carefully, with one straight line, so he could still read it. Vespera said never to scratch a thing out so hard that you could not see what it had been. You might need to know you were wrong.
He wrote FRIDAY next to it.
Then he sat for a while with the pencil over the page.
Then he wrote.
UNCLE PIM'S FARM. THE SALTMARSH. UNDER THE NORTH WALL.
VESPERA IS TAKEN.
He stopped. He looked at the three words. His hand did not want to go on. He made it.
THE GRAY MAN TOOK HER. KROSS. FROM HER SHOP UNDER THE ARCHES WHERE THE BRICK IS BLUE. SHE SAT IN HER CHAIR AND WAITED FOR THEM SO JUNO COULD GET OUT.
SHE IS IN THE HOUSE WITH THE STONE BIRD OVER ITS DOOR. THE OLD CUSTOMS HOUSE. BY THE CORN BRIDGE. UNDER IT THERE IS A VAULT.
KROSS TOLD BRIN TO TELL ME. IN THE ASHBORN TONGUE.
I DO NOT KNOW WHY.
He looked at that line for a long time. Then he wrote under it:
FIND OUT.
He turned back, a long way, to a page near the front of this Ledger. Ledger Eight. To a page he had written on the Glass, under the Eye, in the command room, by the brazier. FERRAND'S ROD. IT TURNS LEFT. IT IS AT THE FOOT OF THE STAIR. I LEFT IT THERE. MY HANDS SHAKE WHEN I SEE IT. BOTH OF THEM. I DO NOT KNOW WHY. And under it, FIND OUT.
Two FIND OUTS. He had not found out either of them yet.
He turned back to the clean page.
JUNO IS SAFE. KROSS SAID SO. BRIN ASKED HIM. HE SAID ONE WORD.
BRIN IS HURT. A SWORD UNDER THE ARM. HE IS IN A BARN ON THE FEN ROAD WITH ODA. HE MUST LIE STILL THREE DAYS. HE STOOD IN THE WAY AND DID NOT USE THE POINT.
HE SAID SHE WAS BRAVER THAN HIM. AND THAN ME.
HE IS RIGHT.
He stopped.
The range ticked. The red light was very low now. He bent closer to the page to see.
KESTREL AND SEF HAVE GONE BY WATER. IN AN EEL PUNT. TO THE FISH STAIRS AND THE CRYPT.
KESTREL SAYS VESPERA ONCE SAT IN HASK'S TENT FOR A WHOLE WINTER. SHE PUT THE NOTES ON HIS ARM IN INK SO HE WOULD NOT LOSE THEM. HE DID NOT LOSE THEM. I HAVE HIS HUM NOW. BECAUSE OF HER.
KESTREL HELD HER HAND OVER MY HEAD. SHE DID NOT TOUCH ME.
He looked at that. He did not know why he had written it. He did not cross it out.
WE MET A MAN ON THE ROAD. EDMUND FALLOW. HE KEPT A SCHOOL IN THIMBLE LANE. HE IS TAKING HIS BOOKS NORTH IN A CART. HE SAID THEY DREW ME TOO FAT.
EVERYBODY SAYS THAT.
He put the pencil down for a moment. He picked it up again.
A CHILD IN REEDHAM CALLED WILL TUCKETT DREW A TREE IN HIS COPYBOOK. A STRAIGHT TRUNK. ROOTS LIKE FINGERS. A CROWN ON TOP.
IT IS THE SAME TREE AS ON THE SPOON.
UNCLE PIM SAYS ALL THE CHILDREN ROUND HERE DRAW IT. HE SAYS IT IS THE OLD TREE. FROM THE OLD KING'S TIME. HE SAYS IT WAS ON THE BIG GATE ONCE. BEFORE THEY PUT THE EYE UP.
I HAVE THE COPYBOOK. IT PULLED. LIKE THE SPOON.
I DO NOT KNOW WHAT IT MEANS.
I THINK IT MEANS SOMETHING.
He sat back.
He looked at the page. All of it. In the last of the red light. His big square careful capitals, going down the page in rows, like bricks in a wall.
Then he bent forward again, and wrote the last thing. At the bottom. Bigger than the rest.
TOMORROW I GO HOME UNDER THE SALT.
BE QUICK. DO NOT BE STUPID.
THE SECOND ONE MATTERS MORE.
He closed the Ledger. He did up the strap.
He sat for a while at the end of the long table in the dark, with both his hands flat on the cover. The range had gone out. The kitchen was cold. Across the room, on a bench by the wall, Oren was asleep sitting up, with his sword across his knees and his chin on his chest. Garrett was asleep under the table, rolled in a horse blanket, with his red cap over his face. Uncle Pim had gone up a ladder to somewhere under the roof and was snoring, a long slow rumble like a cart on cobbles.
And Lyra.
She was not asleep. He knew without looking. She was sitting on the floor by the dead range with her back against the warm bricks and her knees drawn up, a little more than a hand's width from the end of the table, from him. With the gray linen still tied round her left wrist. The other end of it hanging loose.
She held it up. The loose end. In the dark. Without saying anything.
He held out his wrist.
She tied it. Over the glove. Over the cuff. Not tight.
And they sat there in Uncle Pim's cold dark kitchen, under the north wall of the city, with the linen between them, and waited for the morning, and the salt.
