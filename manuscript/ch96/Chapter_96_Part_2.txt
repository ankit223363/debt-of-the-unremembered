Chapter 96: The Long Way Back, Part 2 of 5: Garrett
Garrett
They heard him before they saw him.
It was getting dark. The sky over the fens had gone from gray to a cold deep blue, with a line of dull orange low down in the west where the sun had gone, and the first stars coming out over the reeds. The snow on the road had gone blue too. The ditches were black. It was very quiet. The kind of quiet that comes over flat country at dusk, when the wind drops and the birds stop and there is nothing for a long way in any direction to make a sound.
And into the quiet, from somewhere ahead, from the south, there came singing.
One voice. A young man's. Loud, and cheerful, and very badly out of tune. Singing the same few lines over and over, about a girl in a red petticoat and a sailor who went away and a cat that ate the wedding cake. Getting nearer. With a creak of wheels under it, and the clop of a small hoof on the frozen road.
Kestrel stopped. She lifted her spear.
And Lyra, beside Corin, made a sound. Half a laugh. Half something else.
"I know that song," she said. "I know that voice."
Corin knew it too.
He had not heard it since the north. Since a night in the hills, by a fire, with a candle and a packet in oilcloth for Gideon at Saint Bride's. Quick and warm. Read it slow. And a young man in a red cap who never stopped talking, who had taken the packet and gone off south in the dark on a sheep path by lantern light, singing that same song all the way over the Lip, to carry it home for them.
The cart came out of the dusk.
It was small. A two-wheeled farm cart, slatted at the sides, with a load in the back under a sack. Pulled by a small sad shaggy brown pony, which plodded along with its head down and its ears flat as if it had heard the song a great many times and was past caring. And on the front of the cart, sitting on a plank with the reins in one hand and the other hand waving in time to the song, there was a thin young man in a long brown coat much too big for him, and a red woolen cap, and a red woolen scarf wound round and round his neck.
He saw them.
He stopped singing in the middle of a word. He pulled on the reins so hard that the pony stopped dead and nearly sat down. And he stared.
Six people in the blue dusk on the fen road. Three of them with spears and gray on their faces.
"Saints," said Garrett. "Saints and all their uncles."
He jumped down off the cart. He came toward them through the snow. Then he stopped. Then he came on again. Then he stopped again, a few paces away, and looked at Corin, and his freckled face did something complicated.
"It's you," he said. "It's really you. I've been looking for you for a day and a half. I've been up and down this road like a dog looking for a bone. I've been asking at every farm. I was going to go all the way to Haverstock. I was going to go all the way to Thistle Ford if I had to, and that old woman with the pipe frightens the life out of me." He took a breath. "You've gone grayer."
"I know."
"Up your face. Up your actual face."
"I know, Garrett."
"It's horrible."
"Thank you."
"I didn't mean—" Garrett stopped. He went red under his freckles. "I meant it's horrible what's happened to you. Not that you look horrible. You look—you look like you, mostly. On the other side." He looked at Lyra. "Hello, miss."
"Hello, Garrett."
"Captain."
Oren nodded.
Garrett looked at the Ashborn. At Kestrel, tall and still with her spear. At Oda. At Sef. He swallowed. Then he made a little bow. A clumsy one. The bow you would give a lady at a fair.
Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. Then she lowered her spear.
"Why were you looking?" said Lyra.
Garrett's face changed.
It went all at once. The way a lamp goes out. All the cheerfulness, all the talk, gone, and underneath it his face was thin and young and very frightened.
"Because of him," he said.
He turned round and went back to the cart. He took hold of the sack that was spread over the load in the back, and lifted the edge of it.
Corin came forward. Lyra came with him. Kestrel was already there.
It was not turnips under the sack.
It was a man.
A very big man. Lying on his side in the bed of the cart, curled up, because he was too long for it, with his knees drawn up nearly to his chin and his boots sticking out over the tail. Wrapped in a horse blanket. With straw packed round him. And his head was bare, and round, and shaved, and on both his cheeks, in the last of the blue light, there was gray.
Brin.
His eyes were shut. His face was the wrong color. Not brown. A kind of yellow-gray, like old candle wax, under the gray of the marks. His breathing was slow and loud and rough, with a catch in it.
And the horse blanket, on his left side, under his arm, was dark. Stiff. Dark all the way through.
Kestrel said something in the Ashborn tongue. Very short. Very low.
Oda pushed past Corin. She did not ask anyone. She climbed up into the cart, onto the straw, beside the big man, and knelt there, and put her hand on his forehead. And then she lifted the edge of the horse blanket. Corin saw her face when she looked under it. It did not change. That was how he knew it was bad.
"I found him at dawn," said Garrett. His voice had gone high and thin. "Yesterday. No. Today. This morning. I don't know. I was coming out of the north gate with the cart, going up to my uncle's for a load, and he was lying in the ditch by the road. Just past the toll. Half in the water. I thought he was dead. I thought he was a dead man somebody had thrown in a ditch. And then he opened his eyes and looked at me." He swallowed. "And he said your name."
Corin did not move.
"He said heron. And then he said Corin. He said it wrong, sort of. Like he'd only heard it said. And then he said the needle." Garrett looked at Corin. "And then he shut his eyes again. So I put him in the cart. I don't know how. I don't know how I lifted him. He's the size of a horse. I put him in and I put the sack over him and I turned round and came north. I didn't go to my uncle's. I just came north. I thought if he wanted you, you must be coming from the north. And I thought—"
He stopped.
"I thought you should know," he said, "whatever it was. Before you got to the gate."
In the cart, Brin opened his eyes.
He did not move his head. Only his eyes. They went from Oda, kneeling over him, to Kestrel. And stayed there. And he said something, in the Ashborn tongue, very low, and Kestrel bent down over the side of the cart to hear.
Then his eyes moved again.
To Corin.
And he said, in the southern talk, slowly, each word a stone he had carried a long way:
"Heron. The gray man. Has the needle."
What Brin Carried
They got him off the road.
There was a barn, a little way back, a long low one of tarred boards with a reed roof, at the end of a track between two ditches, with nobody living near it. Garrett knew it. Garrett knew everything along the fen road. It was his cousin's, he said, or his cousin's wife's father's, or somebody's. It had hay in it, and a door that shut, and no windows.
Oren and Sef lifted Brin out of the cart between them. It took both. Even Oren, who could still lift anything. They carried him into the barn and laid him on the hay, on his right side, the good side, and Oda knelt beside him with her pouches and her knife and a bowl of snow, and sent everybody else away from him with one look.
Corin stood in the doorway of the barn with the dusk behind him and watched her work.
He could not help. He knew that. He could not touch Brin. Not with his gloves off. Not with the gray in him, and the hunger in his hands that never quite went away, that sat at the ends of his fingers like a dog at a door. If he touched a dying man bare-handed he did not know what would happen. He did not want to find out.
So he stood in the doorway. And watched Oda's quick brown hands. And listened to Brin breathe.
It was a long time before Brin could talk.
Oda had cleaned the wound. She had packed it with something from one of her pouches, gray-green, that smelled bitter and sharp, like the paste Vespera had put on Lyra's hand under the Eye. She had bound it with strips from the horse blanket and strips from her own shirt. She had made him drink, a little at a time, snow melted in the bowl in her own hands, held to his mouth. And at last she sat back on her heels and looked at Kestrel, and said something in the Ashborn tongue, and Kestrel nodded.
"The blade went in," said Kestrel, to the rest of them. "Under the arm. Between the ribs. Not deep enough to kill. Not quite." She paused. "He has walked a long way on it. And climbed. And bled. Oda says if he lies still three days, he lives. If he gets up, he dies."
"Then he'll lie still," said Lyra.
"He is Ashborn," said Kestrel. "We will see."
Then Brin talked.
He talked to Kestrel, mostly. In his own tongue. Very low. Slowly. With long stops between the words, where he breathed, and the breath caught. And Kestrel knelt by his head and listened, and every now and then she said something back, short, and he answered. And Corin stood in the doorway and did not understand a word of it, and understood all of it, from their faces.
Then Kestrel stood up.
She came across the barn to the door, where Corin was. Where Lyra had come to stand beside him, a hand's width off. And Oren behind them, and Garrett, twisting his red cap in his hands.
She told them.
She told it the way the Ashborn told things. Plainly. In order. Without putting anything in that had not happened.
The short way over the Glass, Vespera and Brin, fast. The free valleys. Thistle Ford, at dusk, a woman with red arms who fed them in her kitchen. Haverstock. The city. A crypt under a church, with a boy who whittled boats. A girl in a boy's coat, who knew the back ways. The arches by the river, where the brick was blue. An old woman's shop. A list.
A girl in gray, on the road above, at noon, who looked down, and put her hand inside her coat for a whistle, and took it out again empty.
And then the night. The lamps, coming slowly, along the front of the arches, with a smith's sack clanking. And a man in gray, coming down the steps at the vat end. Alone.
"He spoke to Brin," said Kestrel. "In our tongue."
Corin looked at her.
"Not well," said Kestrel. "Like a man who learned it long ago, from someone, and has not used it since. But in our tongue." Her tea-colored eyes were very still. "He said, This one asks you to go. And Brin said, This one stays."
Nobody said anything.
"Then the gray coats came," said Kestrel. "And Brin stood in the way. And did not use the point. And one of them used his." She looked back at Brin, on the hay, with Oda bending over him. "The gray man said, nobody touches him again. And they did not. And Brin climbed the wall."
"And Vespera?" said Lyra.
Kestrel did not answer at once.
"Before Brin climbed," she said, "he asked the gray man two things. In our tongue." She paused. "First he asked, The girl?"
"Juno," said Corin. His voice did not sound like his.
"The girl who knew the back ways. Yes. Brin knew she was there. Behind him. In the hole." Kestrel looked at Corin. "And the gray man said one word."
"What word?"
"Safe," said Kestrel.
Corin put his hand out and found the doorframe of the barn. The rough tarred wood. Through his glove. He held on to it.
"And the second thing?" said Lyra. Very low.
"The second thing," said Kestrel, "Brin did not ask. The gray man said it. When Brin was at the top of the wall, at the parapet, and looked back down." She said it slowly, the way Brin must have said it, the way she had heard it. "Tell the heron. The house with the stone bird over its door."
Nobody understood.
Then Garrett made a sound.
They all looked at him. He had stopped twisting his cap. He was standing very still, behind Oren, with his face gone white under the freckles.
"The Customs House," he said. "The old one. On the river, by the Corn Bridge. It's got an eagle over the river door. A stone one. Big." He swallowed. "Everybody knows it. Nobody goes near it. There's a thing under it. A vault. The Ministry's. They say people go in and—" He stopped.
"And what?" said Corin.
Garrett looked at him.
"And come out different," he said. "Or don't come out at all."
The barn was very quiet.
Corin stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame.
He felt it come up in him. He had known it would. He had felt it coming all the way through Kestrel's telling, the way you feel a storm coming over the fens, a long way off, a darkness on the edge of the sky. And now it was here.
It came up from his hands.
From the ends of his fingers, where the hunger always was. Up his arms. The gray one and the other. Into his chest. Into his throat. Hot. Not like anger. Like hunger. Like the feeling under the Eye, at the end, when he had burned so much that there was nothing left to burn and he had gone on burning anyway, because something had to be done and he was the only one there to do it.
Burn.
It was not a voice. Malakor was gone. There was no voice in his head anymore. It was only him. His own hands. Saying it.
Burn something. Anything. Now. Get up and walk to the city tonight, all night, and go to the house with the stone bird and put your bare hands on its door and burn it down to the ground and every gray coat in it. You can. You've done more. You did more under the Eye. Burn.
He took his hand off the doorframe.
He looked at it.
It was shaking.
"Corin," said Lyra.
He did not look at her. He was looking at his hand. At the glove. At the fingers inside it, shaking, wanting.
"Corin." Closer. Not touching. A hand's width. "Look at me."
He looked at her.
She was standing very close. Closer than she ever stood. In the blue dusk in the barn door, with the snow behind her. Her face was white and set. Her eyes were very dark.
"Brin carried something else," she said. "Didn't he. From her. From Vespera." She did not take her eyes off him. "Kestrel. Didn't he?"
Kestrel was quiet for a moment.
"Yes," she said. "The old woman said it to Brin. On the Glass, at the short way, before they went. If you see the heron before I do, she said, say it again. And Brin has carried it here." She looked at Corin. "Tell him not to be stupid. Tell him to be quick."
Corin stood very still.
"Which one matters more?" said Lyra. Quietly.
He did not answer.
His hand was still shaking.
He made it stop. He did not know how. He held it out in front of him, in the blue light, and looked at it, and thought about a small old woman with blue hands and ragged gray hair, standing in a stable on the Glass with her arms folded, saying Don't be stupid, boy, and Read yourself every morning, and the next one goes in at the mouth. You understand me? Words. And he made it stop.
It stopped.
Not all the way. But enough.
"The second one," he said.
His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat.
"The second one matters more," he said. "She said so. Last time. I asked you which one, at Thistle Ford, and you said the second one. And you were right."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment.
Then she nodded. Once. And stepped back. A hand's width.
"Good," she said.
And behind her, in the hay, Brin opened his eyes again. And looked at Corin across the barn. And said something, very low, in his own tongue. Two words.
Kestrel did not translate. Corin did not ask her to.
He thought he knew what they were.
The Rope
He did not sleep.
The others did. Oda by Brin, with her hand on his chest, so that she would feel it if his breathing changed. Sef curled up in the hay near the door. Garrett in the cart outside, with the pony, under the sack, because he said the pony got lonely. Oren by the door, sitting up, the way he always did, with his sword across his knees. Even Kestrel, Corin thought, though he could not tell. She sat by the wall in the dark with her spear upright beside her and her eyes shut, and she might have been asleep, and she might not.
Corin lay on his back in the hay and looked up at the dark under the reed roof and did not sleep.
His hands would not let him.
They lay on his chest, one on top of the other, in their gloves. He had put them there on purpose. Where he could feel them. And he could feel them. They were not shaking anymore. They were very still. Too still. The way a cat is still, crouched in long grass, watching a bird.
Burn.
It came and went. Like the hum. Like a tide. It came up from his fingers and went up his arms and sat in his chest for a while, hot, and then went back down again, and came again.
It was his own. That was the thing he could not get past. All the way across the Glass, all the long walk south, he had told himself that the hunger was Malakor's. That it was the old man in him, a long lifetime of burning, a long lifetime of wanting. And now the old man was gone. And the hunger was still there. In his own hands. In his own voice.
He had always had it.
He thought perhaps he had been born with it.
He turned his head in the hay.
Lyra was not asleep either.
She was lying on her side, a hand's width away, more, with her face toward him and her eyes open. He could just see them in the dark. Two small gleams. Watching him.
"You're doing it again," she said. Very low. So as not to wake anybody.
"Doing what?"
"Holding your hands like they're going to run off."
He did not answer.
She was quiet for a while.
"Is it bad?" she said.
"Yes."
He had not meant to say it. It came out of him. In the dark it was easier.
"It's mine," he said. "That's what I didn't know. I thought it was his. Malakor's. I thought when he went, it would go with him." He looked at the dark under the roof. "It didn't go. It's mine, Lyra. I'm the one who wants to burn. Not him. Me."
Lyra did not say anything for a long time.
"Everybody wants things," she said at last.
"Not like this."
"No. Not like this." She was quiet. "I wanted to tear the paper off the ford post. At Thistle Ford. With your face on it. I had my hand up. I'd have torn it to pieces and thrown it in the river." She breathed out. "And on the stair under the Eye, I wanted to kill that man. With the stone. I did kill him. I wanted to."
Corin turned his head and looked at her.
"That's not the same," he said.
"No," said Lyra. "It's not. But it's a bit the same." She looked at him across the dark. "You didn't, though. Tonight. In the door. You wanted to and you didn't. You said the second one. You made your hand stop."
"It didn't stop all the way."
"It stopped enough."
He looked back up at the roof.
"And tomorrow?" he said. "And the next day? When we're in the city, and she's in that place, under the stone bird, and I'm a street away?"
Lyra did not answer at once.
Then he heard her move. In the hay. A rustle. And something touched his hand.
Not her hand. Something softer. Cloth.
He looked down.
She had untied the linen from her right hand. The strip she had bound her cut palm with, all the way from the Eye. Long and gray and soft, worn thin. And she had tied one end of it round her own left wrist, over her sleeve. And she was holding the other end out toward him. Across the hand's width.
"Give me your wrist," she said.
He looked at her.
"Your glove. Your wrist. Over the glove." She held the end of the linen out a little further. "I won't touch you. I'll tie it over the cuff. Like this." She showed him, on her own wrist. A loop. A knot. "If you get up in the night, it'll pull. And I'll wake up."
"Lyra—"
"Under the Eye I was your rope," she said. "When you went down into the old man. When you went into the dead woman's head and the gray came up your arm. I held on. I didn't let go." She looked at him. "I'm still your rope. Give me your wrist."
He gave her his wrist.
He held out his left hand, the gray one, in its glove, and she took the end of the linen and wound it round his wrist, over the cuff of the glove, over the cuff of his coat. Twice. And tied it. Not tight. A loop, and a knot. Her fingers did not touch his skin. Not once. There was the glove, and the coat, and the linen, between them. Three layers.
She let go.
They lay in the hay in the dark, a little more than a hand's width apart, with a strip of old gray linen going between them, from her wrist to his. Slack. Lying across the hay in a loose curve. He could feel it. Very light. Almost nothing. Like a thread.
"There," said Lyra.
He did not say anything.
He lay and felt it. The linen. Almost nothing. And he found that his hands, on his chest, the other one and the gray one with the linen round its wrist, had gone still in a different way. Not like a cat in the grass. Like a cat by a fire.
"She said be quick," he said, after a while. Into the dark.
"And not stupid."
"And not stupid." He breathed out. "If I'm quick, I'll be stupid. If I'm not stupid, I won't be quick."
"Then be quick about not being stupid," said Lyra.
Arran's laugh came up out of him. Quiet. Short. Into the hay.
By the door, Oren grunted, and shifted, and was still.
"Garrett knows the gate," said Lyra. "He'll know a way in. Tomorrow we'll get to the city, and we'll get in, and we'll go to the crypt, and Juno will be there. She's safe. He said so. The gray man." She paused. "And Gideon. And Silas. And they'll know things. They always know things. And then we'll know what to do."
"And then?"
"And then we'll do it," said Lyra. "Quick. Not stupid. Together."
He did not answer.
He lay in the dark with the linen round his wrist and listened to her breathe. Slow, and slower. Until she was asleep. And then he lay and listened to the barn. To Brin's rough breath. To the pony shifting outside. To the wind, which had got up a little, moving in the reeds on the roof. To the hum, very low, at the bottom of him, Hask's, going slowly through the rooms of his head, opening doors.
He did not sleep.
But he did not get up, either.
And once, very late, when the burning came up in his hands again and he lifted the left one off his chest without meaning to, he felt the linen go taut. Just a little. A tug. And across the hay, in the dark, Lyra said something in her sleep. Not a word. Just a sound.
He put his hand back down.
The linen went slack.
And after that, he did sleep. A little. Near morning.
