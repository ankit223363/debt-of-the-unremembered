Chapter 65: Breathing the Dead, Part 5 of 5: The Song
The Song
The camp did not go to bed.
Lyra had thought it would. It was very late. The stars had turned a long way round the sky since the sun went down, and the cold was so deep now that the breath froze on the edge of her scarf in little white crystals, and the red heap of the fire gave heat only to the front of you and none at all to the back. But nobody went. The ones who had gone to their tents earlier came out again, wrapped in furs, carrying sleeping children, and sat down round the fire with the others. More wood was brought. Not the great heap, now. Just enough to keep it going. A few branches at a time, laid on carefully, so that the flames came up small and yellow and the red heap underneath glowed.
And people talked. Quietly, in small groups, in their round slow speech. Somebody passed round a skin of something that was not water and smelled of sour milk and fire, and Oren took a long pull at it and coughed and his eyes ran and the people round him laughed and slapped his back. Somebody else brought bread. Lyra recognized one of the rounds. It was pear-shaped and thick at one end. She ate it.
This was part of it too, she understood. You did not take the dead into you and then go to bed alone in the dark. You sat up with them. With each other. Until it was light.
Corin was asleep.
He had fallen asleep sitting up, on his mat, with the cup of tea gone cold in his hands, and Kestrel had taken the cup away and laid him down on his side and put a fur over him, and he had not woken. He lay now curled up in the middle of the empty ring with the fur pulled up to his ears and his crutch beside him, breathing slowly and deeply, like a child. Lyra had moved over and sat with her back almost touching his, so that she could feel his breathing through the fur. She did not know why she wanted to. She did not ask herself.
The twelve had gone back to their families. Except Old Hask. Old Hask sat where he had sat all night, on the twelfth mat, alone now, with his blind face to the ridge, humming. The boy who led him had fallen asleep against his side, and the old man's hand lay on the boy's shaved head, and did not move.
Across the fire from Lyra there was a woman with a little girl in her lap.
Lyra had noticed them before, without noticing. The woman was young, thin, with a gray line that went down past her nose to her upper lip. The girl was small. Four, perhaps. Five. She had the round shaved head and the painted line, and she was wrapped in a fur so big that only her face showed, and she was nearly asleep. Her eyes kept closing and opening again. Her head kept falling against her mother's chest and coming up again with a jerk.
And she was singing.
Not loudly. Under her breath. The way very small children sing to themselves when they are falling asleep, without knowing that they are doing it, so softly that the words were hardly words at all. Lyra would not have heard it if the fire had not dropped just then, and the talk round it had not gone quiet for a moment, the way talk does sometimes, all at once, for no reason.
The little girl's voice came across the fire. High and thin and sleepy, and a little off the note.
Lyra went still.
She knew the tune.
She knew it the way she knew her own name. She knew it from before she knew anything. From a dark room and a warm lap and a voice above her in the dark. From the stairs at Crown Walk, going up to bed, counting. From the dyke road in the snow, humming it flat to keep herself walking. From three notes on a bone flute in the night, and a fourth that went up and hung.
The moon song.
Mother's song.
The little girl was singing the words. Lyra could hear them now. Not all of them. The girl's voice came and went, mumbling, half asleep. But they were the words. The same words. Lyra's lips moved with them, without sound, without her telling them to.
The moon goes walking, over the water... something about a lantern... something about a long road...
They were the words her mother had sung. In the same order. With the same little catch in the middle of the third line where you had to take a breath.
How did a child at the end of the world, in a hide tent by the Glass, know her mother's song?
Lyra did not breathe. She sat with her back against Corin's sleeping back and her hands knotted in her lap and listened.
The song went on. Into the last verse. Lyra knew it was the last verse. She knew what came at the end. She had known it all her life. The last line. Where the song came down, at the very end, soft and low and slow, the way you set down something you have been carrying.
And let her go.
That was how it ended. That was how it had always ended. Her mother had sung it, every night. The moon goes over the water and over the hill and over the long road, and at the end, at the very end, the one who is waiting opens the door and lets her go.
The little girl's voice came to the last line. Wavering. Sleepy. Her eyes were shut now. Her head was on her mother's chest.
She sang:
And she came home.
And stopped. And was asleep.
Lyra sat there.
The fire crackled. Somebody laughed, softly, across the ring, at something somebody else had said. The talk started up again, all round, low and slow. The woman with the little girl in her lap pulled the fur up over the child's head and bent and kissed the top of it, and settled back, and closed her own eyes.
And she came home.
Not and let her go. And she came home.
The same tune. The same words, all the way through, with the same catch for breath in the third line. And then, at the very end, the last line, different.
Lyra looked at the sleeping child across the fire for a long time.
She thought about getting up and going round the fire to the mother and asking. Where did she learn that? Who taught it to her? Do you know it? How does it go?
She did not get up.
She did not know why. She thought, afterward, that it was because she was afraid of the answer. Or afraid that there would be no answer. That the mother would only shrug, the way Arran had shrugged, and say, everybody knows that. It's what you sing when you're falling asleep.
Or perhaps it was only that it was very late, and very cold, and she had cried more today than in the whole of the rest of her life, and she had nothing left to ask with.
She said nothing.
She sat with her back against Corin's sleeping back, in the cold, by the fire, and looked at the child, and said the last line over to herself, very quietly, without any sound at all, the new way. To see what it felt like in her mouth.
And she came home.
It felt like a door opening.
Hask's Tent
Some time in the deep of the night, the camp slept after all.
Not in the tents. Where they sat. One by one, round the fire, the Ashborn lay down on the bare ground in their furs, with their faces to the ridge, and their children between them, and slept. The talk dropped to a murmur and then to nothing. The skin of sour milk went round one last time and was put away. Oren fell asleep sitting up, with his chin on his chest and his arms folded and the goat lying against his legs, and began, after a while, to snore.
Lyra did not sleep.
She could not. She sat with her back to Corin's back and her knees up and watched the fire go down, and the stars turn, and thought about a little girl's sleepy voice singing and she came home. She was so tired that her body felt like it belonged to someone else. But her mind would not stop. It went round and round, like Hask's humming.
Hask.
She looked across the ring.
He was still there. On the twelfth mat. Alone. The boy who led him had woken some time ago and gone, yawning, to his mother. The old man sat with his blind face to the ridge and his hands on his knees and his little beard plaits very still. He was not humming anymore.
And he was the only other one awake. She could tell by the way he sat. Upright. Not the way a sleeping man sits.
She got up. Carefully, so as not to wake Corin. Her legs were stiff with cold and she nearly fell. Then she went round the ring, stepping over the sleeping bodies, to where the old man sat.
She stopped in front of him. She did not know what to say.
"Little one," said Old Hask. Without turning his head.
"How did you know it was me?"
"You walk like somebody who's been taught how to walk." A dry wheeze that was a laugh. "By somebody with a stick. And you smell of flour. Still." He turned his white eyes toward her. "Will you help an old man to his bed? The boy's gone, and these ones are all asleep, and my knees have turned to wood."
She helped him up.
It took a long time. He was very light. Lighter than she expected. Like lifting a bundle of sticks in a coat. But his knees would not bend and his feet would not hold him at first, and she had to put her arm round his thin back and hold him up, and wait, while he got his breath. He smelled of smoke and old skin and the burnt-honey herb. Close to, she could hear his breath in his chest. A small wet rattle, under each breath, like a pebble in a gourd.
"There," he said at last. "There. Now. That way."
His tent was not far. It was small and very old, the hides on it so dark with age and smoke that they were almost black. And it was covered in handprints. Not as many as Kestrel's. But so many that, in the starlight, it looked gray from top to bottom, like a tent made of ash.
Inside, it was dark and warm and close. There was a little fire in a ring of stones, banked down to a red glow under gray ash. A heap of furs. A clay pot. A bone comb. Nothing else.
She let him down onto the furs. Slowly. A little at a time. He lay back with a long breath and closed his white eyes.
She stood there in the dark, not knowing whether to go.
"Sit," said Old Hask. "Sit a while, if you're not sleeping. I'm not sleeping either. It's dull, not sleeping, by yourself."
She sat on the ground beside the furs, by the little red fire.
For a while neither of them said anything. The fire ticked. Outside, very far off, a goat bell clunked, and stopped.
"Tell me what you look like," said Old Hask.
Lyra blinked. "What?"
"I've never seen you. I never see anybody. I've been blind since before your father was born. I've got all their faces in me, all the ones I carry, hundreds of faces, and not one of them's my own looking." He turned his head on the furs toward her. "So tell me. What do you look like, little one?"
Lyra did not know what to say. Nobody had ever asked her that. People told her what she looked like. Ladies at balls. Her governess. Her aunt. Too thin. Too pale. Too sharp in the face. Hair a good color but too fine. Hands too big for a lady.
"I'm..." She stopped. She thought about it. "I'm tall. For a girl. Not as tall as Kestrel. My face is thin. People used to say I had my mother's eyes. My hair's short, it was long but it's short now, it's cut off at my jaw. I've got a red scarf. I've got flour on my face, you said." She stopped. "I don't know. I don't know what else."
Old Hask lay there and listened. Then he laughed.
A small, wheezing, delighted laugh, with the rattle under it.
"Pretty, then," he said. "Good."
"I didn't say pretty."
"No. You didn't. That's how I know." He laughed again, more softly. Then he coughed. The cough went on a while. When it stopped, his breath was shorter.
Lyra sat very still.
"She's in me now," said Old Hask, after a while. "The woman. Her dying. All of it."
"Is it bad?"
"It's dark," said Old Hask simply. "It's very dark. She was very frightened." He was quiet. "She had nobody, little one. That's the worst of it. Not the dying. Everybody does that. But she had nobody. At the end. Nobody holding her hand who wanted to be. Nobody to say her name. Nobody to say goodbye."
The fire ticked.
"Somebody should," said Old Hask. "Don't you think? Somebody should say goodbye to her. Even her."
Lyra did not answer. She could not.
Old Hask did not seem to need her to. He lay on his back on the furs with his white eyes open, looking up at the dark roof of the tent, at nothing. His thin chest went up and down under the hide coat. The rattle was louder now.
And then he spoke. Not to Lyra. Softly, kindly, the way you speak to a frightened child who has woken in the night.
"Goodbye, Vivienne," he said.
The fire ticked.
"Goodbye, little fish girl," said Old Hask. "Goodbye. It's all right now. You can stop being frightened. Somebody's here. Somebody's holding your hand." His own hand moved on the fur, a little, as if he were holding something. "There now. There. You can go home now. It's all right. You can go."
And he smiled.
The same wide toothless smile Lyra had seen in the ring, when he said it was no weight to him. It spread over his gray creased face, slowly, in the red glow of the fire, until his whole face was smiling, every line of it.
Then he breathed out.
A long, slow, easy breath. Like a man settling into a warm bed at the end of a long day.
And did not breathe in.
Lyra waited.
The fire ticked. Outside, the goat bell clunked once, far off.
She waited. She did not count. For the first time in her life she did not count. She only sat there, in the dark, by the little fire, with her hands in her lap, and waited for the next breath.
It did not come.
Old Hask lay on his furs with his white eyes open and the smile on his face. He looked very peaceful. He looked, Lyra thought, as if he were listening to something a long way off. Something he liked.
After a long time she reached out and touched his hand. It was cool. It was not cold yet. Just cool. Like the hand of someone who has been sitting by a window.
She did not cry. She had no crying left. She sat there and held the old man's hand, and the gray light of the dawn began to come in round the edge of the tent flap, and she held it until it was cold.
One of Ours
Kestrel found her there.
Lyra heard the flap move behind her, and the cold come in, and a breath taken sharp. Then nothing. Then Kestrel was kneeling on the other side of the furs, in the gray dawn light, looking down at the old man's face.
She did not say anything for a long time.
Then she reached out and touched his forehead, very gently, with two fingers. And drew them down. Slowly. Down between the white eyebrows, down the long creased nose, to the tip. The line. The whole length of it. As if she were painting it on him one last time.
And she closed his eyes.
"I didn't call anyone," said Lyra. Her voice came out hoarse and strange, as if she had not used it in a year. "I didn't know. I should have called you. He breathed out and there was nobody... There was nobody to breathe him in. I didn't know what to do. I just held his hand." She looked down at it, the old gray hand in hers, cold now. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know."
Kestrel looked at her across the old man's body.
"He knew," she said.
Lyra stared at her.
"He knew," said Kestrel again. Her voice was low and steady, but there was something under it that shook. "He has sat by more deaths than anyone at Last Thorn. He knew what it was, when it came for him. He knew it was coming tonight. He knew it when he took the woman's dying. He knew it was too heavy for one old man. He took it anyway." She looked down at Hask's face. At the smile still on it. "And he waited. Until the whole camp was asleep. Until there was only one awake. One who would sit with him quietly, and not cry, and not shout for us. He wanted it quiet. He wanted it to be just him, and a voice, and a hand." She looked back up at Lyra. "He chose you, little one. Do not be sorry."
Lyra could not speak.
"And he is not gone," said Kestrel. "Not yet. The last breath is a door. But the ones who go through it do not go far, at first. They wait. On the step. Until the sun is up." She looked at the gray light coming in round the flap. "We have a little time. Not much."
She stood up and went to the door of the tent and put her head out and said one word. Not loud. But it went through the sleeping camp like a stone thrown into still water.
They came.
All of them. Out of their furs round the dead fire, in the gray dawn, stiff with cold, with their children by the hand and their babies in their arms. Nobody had to be woken twice. They came across the camp in the half-light without a sound, and stood round the little old black tent, and Kestrel tied back the flap, and they came in. A few at a time. Kneeling round the furs where the old man lay. Close. Close enough to touch. Then those went out, and others came in. Over and over. The whole camp, one ring after another, as the light grew.
And each ring leaned in, and breathed.
Lyra stayed where she was, by his hand. Nobody asked her to move. She watched them come and go. The wide woman from the bread stones. The girl with the gray chin. The children from the path, with their spears left outside, solemn and frightened, breathing in very hard, the way children do when they are told to breathe. Arran, with his face wet, kneeling right by the old man's head, breathing in long and slow with his eyes shut.
And a little gray rose off the old man. Each time. A thin faint mist, off his face and his hands and his chest, like breath on a cold window. And went out into them. A little into each. There was a great deal of it, and there were a great many of them, and so nobody took much.
Then Kestrel went out, and came back, and she had Corin with her.
He was white with tiredness and his eyes were swollen and his hair was full of ash from lying by the fire. He was leaning on his crutch. Oren was behind him. Corin looked at Lyra, kneeling by the furs, and then down at the old man with the smile, and his face went very still.
"He took it," Corin said. Not a question. "Her dying. And it..."
"He was old," said Kestrel. "He was ready. He chose." She put her hand on his shoulder. "Kneel down."
He knelt. It was hard, with the leg. Oren held him by the arm.
Kestrel knelt beside him. "You said yes," she said. "Last night. To the price." Her eyes in the dawn light were dark and wet. "Now you carry one of ours."
Corin looked at her. Then down at Old Hask.
And he leaned in, the way the others had. And breathed in.
Lyra watched. She saw the thin gray rise off the old man's face, a wisp of it, very small, and go into Corin. Only a little. Hardly any. The camp had taken nearly all of it by now. He breathed in, and the wisp went in, and he breathed out, turning his head aside.
That was all.
He knelt there for a moment with his eyes shut. Then he opened them, and looked at Lyra, across the old man, and she could not tell what was in his face.
"What is it?" she whispered. "What did you get?"
He shook his head slowly. "I don't know," he said. "Something small. It's very small." He frowned. "It doesn't feel like taking. It doesn't feel like anything. It just feels... warm."
The sun came up.
They saw it come, through the open flap of the tent, over the hills in the east. A thin gold line, and then a blaze. And the light came across the camp, long and low and gold, and in at the door of the old black tent, and lay across the furs, and across the old man's smiling face.
And Kestrel, kneeling at his head, said quietly, "There. He has gone on."
Nobody wept aloud. But Arran, by the old man's head, put both hands over his face and bent forward until his forehead was on the furs, and stayed there.
Later, that morning, they put the old man's hands in the ash of his own little fire, and pressed them, one by one, onto the hides of every tent in Last Thorn. Every one. Kestrel did it, with the children holding the tent flaps. And Lyra, who had not breathed him and did not carry him, was given his left hand to press on the hide of the tent where she and Corin and Oren had slept, by the north edge of the camp, beside the six or seven old faded prints that were already there.
It was a small hand. Gray with ash. It left a clear mark.
And later still, in the middle of the morning, while they were putting the tent to rights, Lyra heard it.
Corin was sitting on the furs with his back to her, rolling up his coat. He was not thinking about anything. She could tell from the slope of his shoulders. His hands were busy and his mind was somewhere else.
And he was humming.
Under his breath. Low and cracked and tuneless. Up a little and down a little, round and round, like a stream going over the same stones, with no shape to it at all.
She stopped what she was doing.
He did not know he was doing it. She could see that. He went on rolling his coat, and tying it with a thong, and humming, a little, between one breath and the next. And then he stopped humming, and picked up the green book, and opened it, and took out his pencil.
She watched him write.
SUNDAY. HER FATHER WAS BURIED TODAY. SHE STOOD FACING SOUTH. I STOOD WITH HER.
VIVIENNE IS GONE OUT OF ME. HASK TOOK HER DEATH AND DIED.
Then he turned to a clean page, near the back, and sat looking at it for a while with the pencil in his fingers. And then he wrote a heading across the top of it. A heading he had written once before, Lyra knew, in another book, the old one, that was under a mattress in the city now with Juno. And under the heading, one name, in his big leaning capitals.
THOSE I OWE: HASK.
Then he shut the book and put it inside his coat, and began, without knowing it, to hum again.
Lyra did not tell him.
She thought she would tell him one day. Not today.
