Chapter 65: Breathing the Dead, Part 4 of 5: The Death
The Death
It came up out of him slowly.
Not like the gray. The gray had lifted off his skin like mist, light and loose. This came up the way oil comes up through water. Heavy. Thick. Dark. It welled up out of the bare skin at the base of his throat in a slow black bead, and spread, and rose, and hung in the air in front of his chest in a dark trembling cloud the size of two fists. It did not drift. It did not go out in threads toward the twelve. It only hung there, close to him, as if it did not want to leave. Or as if it were too heavy to.
And Lyra saw what was in it.
She did not want to. She tried to look away. She could not.
A room. A small room, in old stone, with a big bed in it, hung with violet curtains. A wig on a wooden stand, by the bed. A cloth on a table, and on the cloth, a necklace of tiny glowing lights. And in the bed, under the violet curtains, a woman.
Not the woman Lyra had seen at the Bourse. Not the tall straight lady with the long pale face and the black hair piled high like a dome, in her violet gown, with her fan. That woman was gone. This was what had been under her. Tiny. Shrunken. So small in the big bed that she was hardly a shape under the covers. Bald. Her head as bare and smooth and spotted as an egg. No teeth. The mouth fallen in. And the gray, gray, gray, up both arms to the elbow, the arms lying on the cover like two dead sticks.
And fear.
That was the black. That was what made it black. Not the room. Not the bed. Not the bald head. The fear.
Lyra felt it. For one breath, she felt it, the way she had felt the cold of the fish girl's hands. It came at her out of the dark cloud and went through her like a knife of ice. A fear so big that there was nothing else. No room for anything else. Not the jars, not the gold, not the children, not the Bourse. Only this. The bed. The dark. The end coming, and nobody there. Nobody who loved her. Only a boy asleep by the door who was there because she had bought him, and a stranger with gray hands, holding her face, pulling.
And under the fear, very small, very far down, a little girl on a wet stone, crying for her mother.
Lyra made a sound. She did not mean to. It came out of her throat, small and broken.
In the ring, the twelve had stopped breathing.
They were sitting very still, all of them, leaning back now, away from the middle. Away from the black. Lyra could see their faces in the haze. They were afraid of it. She could see that. The wide woman from the bread stones had her hand over her mouth. The young man with the beads had gone white under his gray line. The girl who had given up her baby was shaking.
The black cloud hung in front of Corin's chest. Trembling. Waiting.
And Corin's face had changed again.
The lightness was gone out of it. All of it. His face had gone tight and gray and old, and his mouth was open, and his breath was coming fast and shallow, and his eyes were moving under the lids, fast, back and forth, back and forth. His hands on his knees had closed into fists. The black fingertips were digging into his palms.
He was going down. Lyra could see it. He was going down after it. After the black. As if it were his. As if he had to carry it, because nobody else would, because nobody else ever had. Down and down. Holding on.
Her mouth opened. His name was in it.
And Kestrel moved.
She leaned forward off her mat, toward the black. Her long hand came up, open, reaching. Her face was set. Lyra saw it in the firelight. It was the face of a woman who has decided to pick up a hot coal because someone has to, and knows it will burn, and is going to do it anyway.
Her hand was almost at the cloud.
And another hand came down on her wrist.
It was a thin hand. An old hand. Dry and gray and spotted and trembling. It came out of the haze on Kestrel's left and closed on her wrist, gently, and pushed. Down. Slowly. Until her hand was back on her own knee.
Old Hask.
He had not leaned back like the others. He was sitting where he had been all along, on the twelfth mat, with his blind white eyes open and his face turned up a little, toward the cloud, as if he could see it. Perhaps he could. His beard plaits were very still. His hand stayed on Kestrel's wrist, holding it down.
"No, little one," he said.
His cracked old voice was very soft, and very clear, and it carried all through the ring and out into the silent crowd.
"No. Not you. You've got the young ones to carry. You've got all of them." He patted her wrist, twice, the way he had patted Corin's chest. "This one's mine."
"Grandfather," said Kestrel. Her voice cracked. "It's too much. It's all of it. The whole death. Nobody takes a whole death. Not alone."
"I know," said Old Hask. "I know." And he smiled. Lyra saw it. A wide toothless smile in his gray creased face, in the firelight. "I'm old, little one. It's no weight to me."
He let go of her wrist.
And he leaned forward, toward the black cloud, slowly, from his mat. Not far. Just a little. Just enough. And he began to hum.
His hum. The same tuneless round-and-round hum that he had hummed walking down the path last night, and sitting by the fire, and all day, on and off, through the camp. Up a little. Down a little. Round and round, like a stream over stones. Low and cracked and steady.
And he breathed in.
The black cloud moved.
It shivered. It stretched, toward him, in a long dark thread, like the gray threads had stretched toward the twelve. But slowly. Heavily. As if it did not want to go. As if it were being pulled out of something it was stuck to. Out of Corin. Out of the place at the base of his throat. The thread grew longer. Thinner. Darker. It went across the space between them, from Corin's chest to the old man's open mouth.
And went in.
All of it.
Old Hask breathed in, and in, and in, a breath longer than any breath Lyra had ever heard, a breath that seemed to go on forever. And the black went into him. All the room and the violet curtains and the wig on its stand and the bald head and the fear. All of the fear. Into the blind old man on the twelfth mat, humming.
And then the last of it went, and the air in front of Corin's chest was empty.
Old Hask breathed out. Slowly. Turning his head a little to the side, the way the others had. The breath smoked white in the cold, and drifted away into the haze.
And he went on humming.
Round and round. Up a little, and down a little.
As if nothing had happened at all.
Quiet
The ring breathed out.
All of them, all at once, the twelve and the crowd behind them. A long soft sigh that went round the fire like wind going through grass. Somewhere at the back a woman began to cry, quietly, with relief. The wide woman from the bread stones took her hand away from her mouth. The young man with the beads put his face in his hands.
Kestrel did not move. She sat on her mat beside Old Hask with her hands on her knees and her face turned toward him, and she was looking at him with an expression Lyra had not seen on her before. It was not grief. It was not anger. It was the face of someone looking at a thing they know they are going to lose, and have known for a long time, and are trying to see clearly while it is still there.
Old Hask hummed.
And Corin did not open his eyes.
Lyra saw it at once. She had not taken her eyes off him. The black was gone out of him, out of the air in front of his chest. The twelve had leaned back. It was done. It should have been done.
But he was still down.
His face was not tight anymore. It was not afraid. It was worse than afraid. It was empty. Slack. The mouth a little open, the jaw loose. The eyes had stopped moving under the lids. His fists had come open on his knees and his hands were lying there palm up, loose, the gray fingers curled a little, like the hands of a man asleep. Or a man drowned.
And his breathing was slow. Very slow. Slower than the twelve had breathed. In. A long pause. Out. A long pause.
In.
She waited for the out. It did not come. It did not come.
It came.
Kestrel had turned her head. She was looking at Corin now, and her face had changed, and Lyra saw her look across the ring. At the gap between the mats. At the stone where Lyra sat. At Lyra.
She did not say anything. She did not need to.
Lyra's mouth was dry. Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in her teeth.
She thought: say it the way you would say it if you saw him walking off a roof.
She thought: which name?
And then she did not think at all.
"Corin."
It came out of her too quiet. Hardly more than a breath. It went into the white smoke and was lost.
He did not move.
She stood up. She did not mean to. She was standing, on the north side of the ring, in the gap between the mats, with her hands in fists at her sides, and Oren's hand had come down on her shoulder from behind, hard, holding her there, not letting her go in.
She did not try to go in.
"Corin!"
Loud this time. Loud and sharp and high, cracking at the end. The voice she had used to him a hundred times when she was furious with him. When he was being a fool. When he was walking into something he should have walked away from. The voice that said, look at me, you idiot, look at me.
It rang out over the fire and the ring and the silent crowd and off the hides of the tents, and came back, faint, from the ridge.
And Corin's eyes opened.
Not slowly. All at once, wide, the way a man's eyes open when he wakes from a fall in a dream. He drew in a breath, a huge gasping breath that lifted his whole chest. His hands came up off his knees. He looked round. Wildly. At the fire, the haze, the twelve leaning back on their mats, the faces in the crowd. As if he did not know where he was.
Then he looked across the ring.
And found her.
They looked at each other through the smoke. For a long moment neither of them moved. Lyra stood in the gap with Oren's hand on her shoulder and her face wet, she did not know when that had happened, and Corin sat on his mat in the middle of the ring with his coat open and his chest going up and down, and they looked at each other.
And she saw him come back. She saw it in his face. The way you see a lamp being lit in a dark window across a street. Not all at once. A small light first, far back. And then more.
He knew where up was.
He let his breath out.
And then he did a strange thing. He put his head on one side, a little. The way a person does when they are listening for something. A sound in another room. A step on a stair.
He listened for a long time.
"It's quiet," he said.
His voice was hoarse. Wondering. Very small.
"It's quiet," he said again. He put one gray hand flat on his chest, over his heart, over the place where the black had come out. "She's gone. I can't hear her. She was always... there was always a sort of noise. Since the night she died. Like somebody talking in the next room. All the time. And it's stopped." He looked down at his hand. "It's stopped."
Nobody said anything.
He took his hand off his chest. And with the same hand, slowly, clumsily, he began to undo the button at the cuff of his left sleeve. His fingers would not work properly. The black tips fumbled at the button. It took him a long time. Nobody helped him.
The button came undone.
He pushed the sleeve up. Up past the wrist, up past the elbow, bunching the cloth, up and up, until it would go no further and he had to pull the coat half off his shoulder to get it there. In the firelight Lyra saw his arm. The gray of it, smooth and dull, from the black fingertips all the way up. Past the wrist. Past the elbow. Up the long muscle of the upper arm. To the line.
The line was where it had been.
A hand's width below the shoulder. Less a finger. It had not gone back. Not by a hair. The gray was all still there, every bit of it, the same as it had been in the fens and in the valleys and on the Lip.
But it had not gone on, either.
Lyra could see the edge of it. Clear and sharp, against the pale skin of his shoulder above. And she knew, without knowing how she knew, that it had stopped. That it was not creeping anymore. That for the first time since she had known him, the gray was standing still.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
Then his face crumpled.
He put both his gray hands over it and bent forward on his mat until his forehead was almost on his knees, and his shoulders began to shake.
He wept. Not loudly. Almost without sound. The way a man weeps who has not done it for so long that he has forgotten how, and has to learn it again, badly. His whole body shook with it. In the middle of the ring, in front of the twelve and the fire and the whole of Last Thorn, with his coat half off and his gray arm bare, he wept.
Lyra did not know what for. She did not think he knew either. Whether it was because the noise had stopped. Or because of the little girl on the wet stone, gone out of him into twelve strangers. Or because a woman who had sold children had died afraid, and nobody had loved her, and now even the fear was gone and there was nothing left of her in him at all. Or because, for the first time in years, there was room inside him to weep.
Kestrel got up from her mat. She went to him and knelt down beside him and put one long hand on the back of his bent neck, and left it there. She did not say anything.
Old Hask hummed.
And Lyra stood in the gap between the mats with Oren's hand on her shoulder, and did not go in. She had promised. She stood there, and watched him weep, and wept herself, and did not go in.
One Thing Each
Later, much later, when the great fire had burned down to a red heap the size of a cart, and the white smoke had thinned and drifted away south, and half the crowd had gone back to their tents and the other half had sat down on the ground where they stood, the twelve spoke.
Nobody told them to. It seemed to be part of it. Something that was always done.
They were still on their mats, in their ring, though the ring had loosened now, and some of them had turned to face the fire and some had moved closer to each other. Corin was still in the middle. He had stopped weeping. He was sitting with his coat pulled round him again and his knees drawn up, and Kestrel was beside him, and someone had brought him a horn cup of the smoky tea, and he was holding it in both hands and not drinking it.
Lyra had not gone in. But Kestrel had looked across at her, after a while, and nodded, and so she had come round the ring and sat down on the bare earth just outside it, behind Corin, close enough that she could have put out her hand and touched his back. She did not touch him. But she was there. Oren had come and sat down beside her, heavily, and given Corin back his crutch, laying it on the ground beside him without a word.
And the old woman with the gray chin, the first of the twelve, spoke.
"She liked oranges," she said.
Her voice was cracked and slow, in the round Ashborn speech, and Lyra had to listen hard to follow it. The old woman was looking at the fire. Her face was wet.
"She liked oranges," she said again. "More than anything. More than jewels. When she was a little girl, she saw one once, on a ship at the docks, in a sailor's hand. Just one. She had never seen one before. She did not know what it was. She thought it was a ball made of sun." The old woman's mouth moved. It was almost a smile. "She never got that one. The sailor ate it. But she smelled it, when he peeled it. And she thought about that smell for twenty years. And when she was rich, she built a house of glass and filled it with trees, so she could have the smell whenever she wanted."
She was quiet for a moment.
"That's what we carry," she said. "The smell. When he peeled it. That's ours now."
Nobody said anything.
Then the young man with the bone beads in his hair said, without looking up, "She was afraid of deep water."
He was sitting with his arms round his knees.
"She grew up by the harbor," he said. "Every day of her life, she saw it. And she never once went in. Not past her ankles. Because when she was small, there was a boy, a big boy, who held her under. Off the end of the fish quay. For a joke. Not long. Just long enough." He swallowed. "And she never got over it. Even when she was old. Even with all her money. She never went on a boat. She never crossed the river except by the bridge, and on the bridge she never looked down." He was quiet. "She used to dream about it. The green. Looking up at the light through the green, and not being able to get to it."
The fire cracked.
"That's ours," he said. "The green."
The wide woman from the bread stones spoke next. She did not look at anyone. She was looking at her own big hands in her lap.
"She bowed to herself in a mirror once," she said. "As a joke."
Lyra looked up.
"After the ball," said the wide woman. "The one where the old lord bowed to her, and they all laughed, when she was eleven. She went home. To the room where she slept, behind the fish shop, with her sisters. And there was a bit of mirror there, a broken bit, propped on a shelf. And she stood in front of it, in the borrowed dress, with her face still hot. And she bowed to herself. Low. The way the old lord had. All the way down." The wide woman's lips pressed together. "And she laughed. Out loud. All by herself. In the dark. Because it was funny. Because it was so stupid. And then she cried, a bit. And then she went to sleep."
She looked up at last. Across the fire. At Lyra.
"That's ours," she said. "The laugh. In the dark. Before the crying."
Lyra could not look away from her.
One by one, round the ring, they spoke. Each of them, one thing. The one-armed man said she had a scar on her left knee from falling on the fish quay when she was six, and she touched it sometimes, through her silk gowns, when she was nervous, without knowing she did it. The girl who had given her baby to hold said she had once, only once, when she was very young, given a jar back. To a woman who came crying to her door. For nothing. And had never done it again, and had never told anyone, and had thought about it on her last night. A thin old man said she had been very good at sums, so good that she could add up a column of figures faster than any clerk at the Bank, and had been proud of it, and had nobody to tell. A woman with a baby said she had loved a dog, once, a small brown dog, when she was poor, and it had died, and she had never had another. Another said she sang to herself when she counted money, always the same song, and did not know the words.
One thing each. Small things. A smell. A fear. A laugh. A scar. A dog.
And Lyra sat on the bare ground behind Corin's back and listened to them, and felt something happen inside her that she did not want to happen, and could not stop.
She had hated Vivienne.
She had hated her more than she had hated anyone. More than the Archon. More than the Inquisitor. Vivienne, in her violet gown, with her fan and her necklace of little glowing lights, smiling down from the gallery at the Bourse at the people bidding on children's first winters. Vivienne, who had dug a cellar under her house and filled it with beds. Vivienne, who had bought Remy when he was a baby in a basket, and sold the Ash of children in little jars with cards beside them. Lyra had walked through the Bourse on the night of the masks and looked up at her in her gallery and thought, very clearly, I hope you die. I hope you die afraid.
And she had.
She had died afraid. Lyra had seen it, tonight, in the black cloud in front of Corin's chest. She had died in the dark, bald and toothless and tiny, with nobody to love her, as afraid as anyone had ever been. Lyra had got her wish.
And now here were twelve strangers at the end of the world, sitting round a fire, telling her that the woman had liked oranges. Had been afraid of the water. Had bowed to herself in a broken mirror and laughed in the dark. Had loved a small brown dog.
It did not make anything she had done less bad. Not one thing. Not one child. Lyra knew that. She held on to it, hard, the way you hold a rail on a ship in a storm.
But it made her a person.
And Lyra hated that. She hated that it moved her. She hated that her eyes had filled again, sitting there, listening to the wide woman talk about the laugh in the dark. She did not want to feel sorry for Vivienne. She did not want to understand her. She wanted her to stay a monster in a violet gown, up in a gallery, smiling down, so that Lyra could go on hating her cleanly, the way you hate a fire or a flood.
She could not.
The twelve had finished. They sat in silence round the red heap of the fire. Nobody moved for a long time.
Then Corin spoke. Without turning round. Very low, so that only Lyra could hear him.
"The dog was called Biscuit," he said.
Lyra stared at the back of his head.
"I don't know how I know," he said. "I thought it was all gone. It is all gone. But that's still there. Just the name. Like a crumb in a pocket." He was quiet. "Biscuit."
And Lyra put her face in her gloved hands, and laughed, and wept, both at once, and could not have said which was which.
