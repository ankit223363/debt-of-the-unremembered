Chapter 65: Breathing the Dead, Part 1 of 5: Bread
Bread
Lyra Thorne had fought three men in a sewer and held a door against a fire, and she could not, it turned out, make bread.
She found this out in the first hour after sunrise, kneeling on a goat skin by a flat black stone in the middle of Last Thorn, with dough up to her wrists and four Ashborn women laughing at her.
They were not laughing unkindly. That was the strange part. She had been laughed at before, in ballrooms, behind fans, in the soft sharp way that ladies laugh at a girl who has worn the wrong color. This was not like that. This was the way you laugh at a puppy that has fallen over its own feet. Warm. Surprised. A little sorry for it.
She had come out of the tent at first light because she could not lie there anymore. Corin had been asleep at last, on his back, with one arm over his face and the other hand on the green book inside his coat. Oren had been asleep with his hand on his sword. And she had lain between them in the gray light coming through the hides and listened to the hum, and thought about three notes on a bone flute, and a fourth that went up and hung, until she thought she would scream. So she had got up, and pulled on her boots and Silas's black coat and her gloves and the red scarf from Haverstock, and crawled out of the door into the cold.
The camp had been awake already. The Ashborn got up in the dark, it seemed. Smoke was going up from every tent. Goats were being milked, with their heads tied to the tent poles, complaining. Children were running between the tents with buckets. And in the middle of the camp, by the big fire, where they had eaten the night before, a ring of women was kneeling round a row of flat stones set in the edge of the fire, making bread.
One of them had looked up and seen Lyra standing there. A wide brown woman with a gray line that went down past her nose and over her mouth to her chin. She had looked at Lyra for a moment. Then she had moved over on her goat skin and patted the place beside her.
So Lyra had knelt down.
And now here she was.
The bread was simple. Or it looked simple. There was a big wooden bowl of dough, gray-brown and sticky, made of some coarse dark flour and water and salt and a little of something sour from a pot. You took a lump of it the size of an apple. You pressed it flat between your two hands. You turned it and pressed it and turned it and pressed it, quickly, slapping it from one palm to the other, until it was a thin round as wide as a plate. Then you laid it on the hot flat stone at the edge of the fire, and it hissed and bubbled and puffed up in brown blisters, and after a few breaths you flipped it over with your bare fingers, and after a few more breaths you took it off and put it on the pile.
The women did it without looking. They talked to each other while they did it, in their round slow speech, and their hands went slap, slap, slap, turn, slap, and the rounds came out thin and even and perfect, one after another, and went onto the stones, and came off brown and smelling of smoke, and went onto the pile.
Lyra's first one tore in half.
She had taken off her gloves. She had to. You could not do it in gloves. And she had looked at her bare hands for a moment, in the cold morning light, before she put them in the bowl. They were thin hands, with long fingers, and the skin of them was very white, and there were calluses along the inside of the right one from the sword. A lady's hands, that had held a sword. Not a baker's.
She slapped the lump of dough from one palm to the other, the way she had seen them do it, and it stuck to her right hand, and when she pulled it off, it tore. Half of it stayed on her palm in a gray sticky lump. The other half fell on the goat skin.
The wide woman beside her made a small noise in her throat.
Lyra's second one did not tear. It was very thick in the middle and very thin at the edges, like a hat. She laid it on the stone, and it hissed, and she watched it, and she waited, and she waited, and she waited a little longer to be sure, and when she flipped it over the bottom was black.
Not brown. Black. A thick black crust, smoking, that smelled of burnt hair.
One of the younger women across the stones put her hand over her mouth.
Lyra's third one was better. It was round, more or less. It was thin, more or less. She laid it on the stone and watched it very carefully, and when the brown blisters came she flipped it at once, quickly, the way the others did, with her bare fingers.
The stone was very hot.
She snatched her hand back and put her fingers in her mouth, and the bread lay there on the stone, and she could not touch it again, and she watched it go brown, and then dark brown, and then black at the edges, and then the wide woman reached across without a word and flicked it off the stone with one finger and onto the pile.
It lay on top of all the perfect rounds, black round the edge, like a burnt moon.
And that was when they laughed.
All four of them. All at once. Not loud. A soft rolling laugh that went round the ring of them like water round a stone. The wide woman beside her laughed so hard that she had to stop slapping her dough and sit back on her heels and wipe her eyes with her wrist. The young one across the stones laughed into her hand. An old one at the end, with no teeth, laughed a high cackle like a hen.
And Lyra, kneeling there with her fingers in her mouth and her face going hot, found that she was laughing too.
She did not know where it came from. It came up out of her chest the way it had come up last night in the tent, when Oren said another price. Sudden and helpless and nothing to do with her. She laughed until her eyes ran. She laughed until she had to put her floury hand on the ground to hold herself up.
It was such a relief.
That was the thing. She understood it even while she was laughing. It was a relief to be bad at something. Something small. Something that did not matter. For weeks now, for months, everything she had done had mattered. Every word. Every step. Every burn. Every lie she had not told and every truth she had. If she had been bad at any of it, someone would have died. Her father. Corin. Oren. Herself.
But nobody would die because she could not make bread.
The bread would be burnt, that was all. And four women would laugh. And then they would show her how to do it properly.
Which they did.
The wide woman took her hands. Just took them, in her own big rough floury ones, as if Lyra were a child, and put a lump of dough between them, and held her hands round it, and pressed. Slowly. Showing her. Not with the palms. With the heels of the hands, here, and the fingers flat, here, and turn. And press. And turn. Not slapping it, at first. Pressing. The slapping would come later, she said, with her face. The slapping came when your hands knew.
Lyra pressed. And turned. And pressed.
The round came out lopsided. But it did not tear. And when she put it on the stone, the young woman across the stones leaned over and tapped the edge of the stone with a little stick, and said one word Lyra did not know, and nodded at the bread. And Lyra understood that she meant: now. Turn it now. And she turned it, with the tip of the stick the young woman handed her, not with her fingers, and it was brown. Only brown. Brown and blistered and smelling of smoke.
She took it off the stone and held it in her hands, hot. It was not round. It was the shape of a pear, a little. It was thick at one end.
It was bread.
The wide woman looked at it and nodded slowly, very seriously, as if Lyra had brought her something important. Then she took it out of Lyra's hands, and tore it in half, and gave half back, and ate the other half herself.
Lyra ate her half.
It was the best thing she had ever tasted. It was not, really. It was too thick and a little raw in the middle, and it tasted of smoke and sour flour and nothing much else. But she ate every crumb of it, kneeling on the goat skin in the cold sunlight with her hands gray to the wrist with dough, and the women went back to slapping their perfect rounds and talking in their slow voices, and nobody laughed at her anymore.
She made six more before the bowl was empty. Two of them were burnt.
Paying and Giving
She found Kestrel among the goats.
There was a pen for them at the east edge of the camp, a rough ring of thorn branches piled up waist-high, with a gap in it closed by a gate of woven sticks. Inside, the goats milled about in the trampled mud and the frozen dung, black and white and brown, shaggy and complaining, with their breath smoking and their yellow eyes rolling. The smell of them was very strong. Warm and rank and close, like wet wool and old cheese.
Kestrel was in the middle of them with a bag of something over her shoulder. Dried leaves, Lyra thought. Or chopped straw. She was throwing it in handfuls onto the ground, and the goats were pushing and butting each other to get at it, and climbing on each other's backs, and one small black kid had got up onto the top of the thorn fence somehow and was standing there on the spiny branches, very pleased with itself, bleating.
Kestrel did not look up when Lyra came to the gate. But she said, "You have flour on your face."
Lyra put her hand up. There was. A long smear of it, down one cheek.
"I made bread," she said.
"We heard." Kestrel threw another handful. "The whole camp heard."
Lyra felt her face go hot again. Then she saw the corner of Kestrel's mouth, and it was not unkind, and the heat went out of her face.
"Two of them were all right," she said.
"Two is a beginning."
Lyra leaned on the woven gate and watched the goats. They were absurd animals. They fought over every handful as if it were the last food in the world, and then left half of it on the ground and went to fight over the next one. The black kid on the fence had found that it could not get down. It was bleating at its mother, who ignored it.
"You do this every morning?" said Lyra.
"Someone does. Today, us."
"Why you? You're..." Lyra stopped. She did not know the word. "You're the one in charge. Aren't you?"
Kestrel looked at her then. Over the backs of the goats. With her tea-colored eyes.
"Nobody is in charge," she said. "Somebody has to speak to strangers. Somebody has to say yes or no when it matters. That is us, because the old ones asked us to do it, and because we carry more than most." She threw another handful. "But the goats still need feeding. They do not care who is in charge. They only care who has the bag."
Lyra smiled. She could not help it.
They were quiet for a while. The goats ate. The sun was up now, over the hills to the east, low and white and without warmth, and it lay across the camp in long cold stripes between the tents, and lit the smoke going up, and made the gray handprints on the hides look silver. Somewhere a child was singing. Not a tune. Just a child's voice, going up and down, the way children sing to themselves when they are busy.
"Last night," said Lyra at last. "When you were talking about carrying."
"Yes."
"I keep thinking about it." She picked at a loose stick in the woven gate with her gloved finger. "Where I come from... In the city. We don't do that. We don't do anything like that. When somebody dies, they're just gone. Their Ash goes to the Ministry, if they had any left. Or it goes into a jar and somebody sells it. Or it just goes. Into the air. And nobody has it."
Kestrel said nothing. She went on throwing.
"And when we're alive," said Lyra, "we burn. That's what we do. We burn bits of ourselves. To make light, or to make a spell, or to pay for things. And we call it paying." She pulled the stick out of the gate and looked at it. "I've done it. I've paid a lot. My mother's face, some of it. My writing. Things I don't even know about, because once they're gone you can't remember you had them."
The black kid on the fence bleated.
Kestrel put her hand into the bag and found it was empty. She turned it inside out and shook the last bits onto the ground for the goats. Then she folded the bag and tucked it under her arm and came over to the gate, wading through the goats, pushing them out of her way with her knees, and leaned on the other side of the gate, opposite Lyra. Close enough to touch.
"You city people," she said, "burn yourselves, and you call it paying."
Her voice was low and plain.
"And who do you pay? The lamp. The Ministry. The man who sells the jars. Every time you burn, a little of you goes into the fire, and the fire is not yours, and it does not love you, and when the light it made goes out, nobody has what you gave. It is gone. It is gone for nothing." She looked at Lyra. "We give the dead away so they are never lost. We breathe them in, and they stay. In us. In the children. In the children's children. As long as there are Ashborn at Last Thorn, nobody we have carried is ever gone."
She was quiet for a moment.
"One of those," she said, "is a market. One is a family."
Lyra did not say anything.
She could not. She stood with her gloved hands on the woven gate and the stick in her fingers, and looked at Kestrel, and thought of the Bourse.
The Bourse on the night of the masks. The bronze doors and the chessboard floor and the long glass tables under the dome, with the vials laid out on plum velvet like jewels. Little glowing vials. Each one with a card beside it. Lot this. Lot that. A child's first winter. A soldier's last morning. An old woman's wedding day. Laid out under the lamps for people in masks to walk past and look at and bid on, while the music played. And the brass box in the middle of the floor, where you put your card. And the bell at eleven.
A market.
She had walked through it. She had worn a mask and a gown and walked through it, looking at the vials, and she had known what they were. She had always known. Everyone in the city knew. It was how things were. You burned, and someone bottled what was left, and someone else bought it.
And here was this tall gray-lined woman leaning on a goat gate at the end of the world, saying, one of those is a family.
Lyra opened her mouth. She thought there must be something to say. Some answer. That it was different in the city. That there were too many people. That it was not her fault, it was the Ministry, it was the Archon, it was the way things had always been.
But every answer she thought of sounded, even inside her own head, like an excuse.
She shut her mouth again.
Kestrel watched her. She did not look pleased, the way a person looks pleased when they have won an argument. She only looked tired, a little. And sad, a little. As if she had said this before, to other people from the south, and they had not had an answer either, and it had not helped.
"It is not your fault," she said, as if she had heard what Lyra was thinking. "You were born in the market. You did not build it."
"No," said Lyra. "But I bought things there."
Kestrel looked at her for a long moment.
"Yes," she said. "So did we, once. Our grandmothers did. Before the Glass." She straightened up off the gate. "Then the Glass came, and the market could not come up here anymore, because nothing burns up here. And so we had to learn something else." She shifted the empty bag under her arm. "It took a long time. It is still taking a long time. We are not finished learning it."
She opened the gate, and came out, and shut it behind her, and the goats crowded up against it, bleating, because she had been the one with the bag.
The black kid was still on the fence.
Kestrel looked at it. Then she reached up, without hurry, and lifted it down off the thorns in both hands, and held it a moment against her chest, and put it down on the ground inside the pen, by its mother. It shook itself and ran off, pleased, as if it had done it all by itself.
"Go and wash your face," Kestrel said to Lyra. "There is water at the north tent, in a skin. Then go and find the harvester. He is awake." She paused. "And today is a day you will want him near you."
Lyra went still.
"How do you know what today is?"
Kestrel did not answer. But her eyes went, for just a moment, south. Over the camp and the thorns and the long slope of the Lip and the hills beyond. Toward the city.
Then she walked away toward the gray tent, with the goats calling after her.
The Glass by Day
The water in the skin at the north tent was so cold it made her teeth ache. She washed her face in it anyway, and her hands, and the flour came off gray in the water and ran away into the gravel.
Corin was sitting outside their tent on the log where Arran had played the flute in the night. He had the green book open on his knee and the pencil in his hand, and he was not writing. He was looking at the ridge.
He looked up when she came round the side of the tent. And she saw his face change. Not much. A small thing. The way a face changes when it sees someone it was waiting for and did not know it was waiting for.
She did not let herself think about that.
"You're up," she said.
"I've been up a while." He shut the book. "You made bread."
"Does everyone in this camp know?"
"A boy came past with a burnt one. He was showing it to people." The corner of his mouth went up. "He said the lady from the south made it. He seemed very proud of it."
Lyra sat down on the other end of the log. "I made six. Two were burnt."
"Then four weren't."
"Three weren't. One was raw."
He nodded slowly, as if this were serious news. Then he said, "I'd have burned all of them."
"You'd have burned the stone."
"Probably."
She looked at him sideways. He was looking at the ridge again. In the daylight his face was gray with tiredness, and the lines round his eyes were deep, and there was a stubble of beard on his jaw that he had not shaved since the fens. His arms were lying across his knees with the sleeves buttoned down over the gray. His black fingertips were resting on the cover of the book.
"Did you sleep?" she said.
"A little. After."
"After what?"
He did not answer at once. Then he said, "I went up the ridge. In the night. After you went in. Kestrel was up there." He paused. "You should see it. From the top. I think everybody should see it once."
"I saw it from the Lip."
"Not like this," he said.
And as if he had called him, Arran came round the tent.
He was carrying three long strips of something dark over his arm. He had a skin of water on his back and his flute round his neck and he was eating one of the flat round loaves as he walked, tearing pieces off it with his teeth. He stopped in front of them and looked at them, and swallowed, and grinned.
"Kestrel says you're to see it," he said. "In the day. Before tonight." He held out the strips. "Put these on. Up there."
Lyra took one. It was a strip of hide, long and narrow, soft and dark brown, as if it had been smoked. There were two slits cut in it, side by side, each about as long as a finger, very narrow. And there were thongs at the ends, for tying.
"For your eyes," said Arran. He held one up against his own face to show them, over his eyes, with the slits in front of them, and tied it behind his head over his plait. With it on he looked like a bandit in a children's story, or like a man in a mask at a ball. "The Glass is bright, in the day. Brighter than snow. If you look at it long without these, you'll go blind for a day. Maybe two. The children who don't listen go blind every spring." He pulled the strip down round his neck. "Up there. Not yet."
They went up the ridge.
It was easier in the daylight. The gravel was the same, loose and shifting, and Corin's crutch sank into it the same, but Lyra could see where to put her feet, and she could see him, and twice when the crutch slid she put her hand under his good elbow without either of them saying anything about it. Arran went ahead of them, light and quick, and waited at the top by the big black thorn tree.
The tree was even stranger by day. Lyra had seen it from below, black against the sky. Close to, it was enormous, and very old, and dead, she thought, or nearly. There were no leaves on it, and no buds. The bark was gray and cracked and peeling. And every branch, every twig, every thorn on it was bent toward the south. As if a great wind had blown it that way, once, a long time ago, and it had stayed.
"Now," said Arran.
They tied on the strips.
The world went dark and narrow. Two thin bright slits, side by side, and nothing else. Lyra turned her head, and the slits moved, and she saw the trunk of the tree, the gravel, Corin's shoulder, Arran's grinning face with its own dark strip across it. Then she turned toward the north.
And even through the slits it was so bright that she gasped.
The Glass.
It was not black now. It was not green-black either, the way it had been from the Lip. Up here, close, in the full white light of the winter morning, it was every color and no color at all. It shone. It blazed. The light did not lie on top of it the way light lies on snow or water. It came up out of it. From underneath. As if the sun were not in the sky at all but under the ground, very deep, shining up through a floor of dark green glass a mile thick.
It went on forever. Flat. Shining. To the edge of the sky, and the sky above it was white with the light that came up off it, so that there was no line between them. It was like looking into a lamp.
And it was warm.
She felt it on her face. Up here, on the ridge, with the cold morning air all round her, the warmth came off the Glass in a slow steady breath, like the breath from an open oven door. It smelled of something. Burnt sugar. Hot stone. The air over it shook, very slightly, the way air shakes over a road in summer, so that the far distance trembled and swam.
And the hum. Louder by day. Much louder. A deep steady note that she felt in her teeth and in the bones of her face and in the soles of her feet through her boots.
"Saints," she said. It came out in a whisper.
Beside her, Corin did not say anything at all.
"Look there," said Arran. He pointed. "Down. At the bottom of the slope. You see the line?"
She looked down, through the slits. Below the ridge, on the north side, the ground went down in a long slope of scrub and gravel and patches of old snow, about a mile of it, getting lower and flatter as it went. And at the bottom of it, where the scrub stopped, there was a line.
It was perfectly straight. As if someone had drawn it with a rule. On the near side of it, the old snow lay in white patches among the stones. On the far side, nothing. Not one flake. The Glass began, dark and shining, and over the line between the snow and the Glass there was a thin glitter of running water, threads of it, bright as silver wire, where the snow on the edge was melting in the warmth and running out onto the Glass and away.
"The Edge," said Arran. "That's where it starts. You step over that and you're on it. Nothing burns past there." He looked at them, both of them, with his face half hidden by the dark strip. "Nothing comes back the way it went out, either. That's what the old ones say."
Lyra looked at the line for a long time.
She thought of her father. She did not know why. She had not been thinking of him. But she thought of him now, all at once, standing on the ridge with the hot breath of the Glass on her face. Not his face. Not his voice. Just the fact of him. That he was in a box today, in the cold, in the crypt at Saint Clement's. That they were putting him in the vault today, on the Hill, while she stood here, at the end of the world, looking at a line in the snow.
Her throat went tight.
"What time is it?" she said.
Arran looked at her, surprised. "Time?"
"The bells. In the city, the second bell after noon. How long is that from now?"
Arran laughed his bark of a laugh, but softly. "We've got no bells, lady. We've got the sun." He looked up at it, low in the south over the hills, and squinted through his strip. "It's the middle of the morning. You've got a long while yet." Then his face changed, a little. "Why? What's at the second bell?"
Lyra did not answer.
She untied the strip from her eyes and turned her back on the Glass, very deliberately, and looked south.
And Arran, after a moment, did not ask again.
