Chapter 20: The Shadow in Gray, Part 1 of 5: The Circle
The Circle
The trick, Malakor had said once, was not to look like a man walking in a circle.
Corin did not remember being told. But it was in the Ledger, near the front, on a page so old that the pencil had gone soft and gray and smudged at the edges, as if somebody had read it many, many times with a wet thumb. He had read it again this morning, in the crypt, before he left. He had read it twice.
How to see who follows you. (M.)
1. Never look back. A man who looks back is a man who knows. Use windows. Use water. Use the brass on doors.
2. Walk as if you are going somewhere. Not fast. Not slow. A man with nowhere to go walks slowly. A man who is frightened walks fast. Be neither.
3. Turn left. Then left. Then left again. Three lefts make a circle. Anyone who is still behind you after three lefts is not there by chance.
4. Stop at things. A shop. A stall. A notice on a wall. Stop for as long as a man would stop who was really looking. Then go on. Whoever follows must stop too, or pass you. Watch what they do.
5. Never run.
Under that, in a different pencil, darker, newer, somebody had added a sixth line. He thought it was his own hand. He was not sure.
6. If they are good, they will know you have seen them. Do it anyway. Make them choose.
He was doing it now.
The city on a Sunday afternoon was not the same city.
Corin had not known that. Or he had known it and forgotten. On the other six days, the streets around the top of the Cut were loud. Carts and barrows. Hawkers shouting. Dockers going down the hill to the river and clerks going up it to the Ministry, and children running between them, and dogs, and pigeons, and the steady grinding roar of a hundred iron wheels on stone. You could not hear yourself think.
On Sunday it was quiet. The shops were shut. Their wooden shutters were down and barred, every one, all along the wide street, with the shop names painted on them in faded letters. T. GROVE, IRONMONGER. MRS. ABBOTT, BONNETS & TRIMMINGS. FRESH EELS DAILY. The cobbles were empty, except for a few people going slowly along in their best coats, the women with their skirts held up out of the frozen muck, the men with their hats on straight for once, on their way home from church. The bells had stopped. Smoke went straight up from every chimney into the white sky, because there was no wind, and the air smelled of coal fires and roasting meat and the river.
It was easier, on a Sunday, to see who was behind you. There were fewer people to hide among.
It was also easier for them to see you.
He turned left at the corner by the old horse trough, where the wide street met the narrow one that went down toward the tanneries. He did not hurry. He walked with his hands in the pockets of his gray coat and his collar up, the way a man walks who has eaten his Sunday dinner and is going to see his sister on the other side of town.
The narrow street was called Pudding Lane, though there was no pudding in it, only a row of tall thin houses leaning together over the cobbles like old women gossiping. Halfway down it, there was a baker's. Shut, like the rest. But the baker lived over his shop, and his Sunday bread was cooling on the inside sill of the window, behind the glass. Six round brown loaves, with their tops split open in a cross, steaming a little.
Corin stopped and looked at them.
He looked at them for as long as a hungry man would look. Which was not hard. He was a hungry man. He had eaten one of Doctor Silas's hard biscuits at dawn and nothing since, and the smell of the bread came through the cracks round the window frame, warm and brown and yeasty, and his stomach turned over.
And in the dark glass of the window, over the six loaves, he looked at the street behind him.
The tall thin houses. The cobbles. A man in a brown coat, with a little girl on his shoulders, going the other way. A cat on a step.
Nothing gray.
He went on.
At the bottom of Pudding Lane, he turned left again.
This was Tanner's Row. It smelled. Even on a Sunday, even in the frost, it smelled, of old hides and bark and lime and the green stinking water of the tanning pits behind the high walls. Nobody walked on Tanner's Row who did not have to. It was empty from end to end. A long straight cobbled street, with a high blank brick wall on one side and the backs of warehouses on the other, and an open gutter down the middle full of frozen brown water.
A good street, Corin thought. A street where nobody could hide.
He walked down the middle of it. Halfway along, there was a pump. An old iron one, with a long curved handle and a stone trough under it. The trough was frozen solid. On top of the ice, someone had dropped a tin cup, and it had frozen in, upside down, so only the bottom showed, like a little round island.
He stopped beside it. He took his gloves off, slowly, one and then the other, and put them in his pocket. He worked the pump handle. Up and down. Up and down. It squealed. Nothing came out of the spout but a thin, sad, rattling breath of cold air, and a few flakes of rust. Of course nothing came out. It was frozen. A man would know that.
But a man might try anyway. A thirsty man. A man who was not thinking.
He bent his head, as if he were looking at the spout, puzzled. And in the curved iron of the pump, where a thousand hands had polished it smooth and bright, he looked at Tanner's Row.
It was bent in the iron. Long and narrow and curved, like a street seen through the bottom of a bottle. The high wall. The warehouses. The gutter. The far end, where he had come in, from Pudding Lane.
And at the far end, at the corner, standing quite still against the wall in the shadow of a doorway, a shape.
Small. Gray.
It had not come down Tanner's Row. Of course it had not. Nobody could have followed him down that long empty street without being seen. It had stopped at the corner. It was waiting. Watching him from the end of the street, the way you watch a mouse go down a hole, and wait for it to come out the other side.
Good, said Malakor, very quietly. She is good, boy.
"She?"
Look at the shoulders. Look at how she stands. That is not a man.
Corin looked. In the bent bright iron of the pump, the shape was very small. But he thought Malakor was right. Narrow shoulders under the gray cloak. Straight back. Very still. The stillness of someone who had been taught to stand still, by someone who knew what he was doing.
He let go of the pump handle. He shook his head, the way a man does when a thing will not work. He put his gloves back on. And he walked on down Tanner's Row toward the far end, not fast, not slow, with his back to her.
He did not look round.
At the bottom of Tanner's Row, he turned left for the third time.
Three lefts. A circle. He was walking back up the hill now, toward the wide street and the horse trough where he had started, along a lane called Saddler's Walk. It was lined with small houses with green doors and little iron railings in front of them, and some of the doors had wreaths of holly on them already, for Midwinter, though Midwinter was weeks away. A woman was scrubbing her front step. On a Sunday. In the frost. She looked up at him as he went past, and her red hands stopped on the brush, and she looked down again.
Halfway up Saddler's Walk, there was a church.
It was a small one. Saint Ivo's. Old gray stone, with a squat square tower and a clock on the tower that had stopped at twenty past four, years ago, and never been mended. Its doors stood open. The Sunday service was long over, but somebody had left the doors open, and inside, in the dim, Corin could see the rows of dark pews, and the candles still burning on a stand at the side, and the gold of something at the far end, catching the light.
He stopped. He took his hat off. He did not have a hat. He made the movement anyway, his hand going up toward his head, and stopping, and coming down, the way a man does who has forgotten he is not wearing one. And he stood in the church door with his bare head bowed, looking in, the way a man looks into a church when he has not been inside one for a long time and is not sure he will be welcome.
The church doors were old oak, black with age. And on each one, at the height of a man's chest, there was a big brass plate, where the hands of the people going in and out pushed it. Polished by a hundred years of Sundays to a soft, deep, golden shine. Like a mirror made of honey.
In the right-hand plate, Corin watched Saddler's Walk.
The little houses. The green doors. The woman on her step, scrubbing.
And at the bottom of the lane, where it came up from Tanner's Row, a gray shape. Coming round the corner. Walking. Not fast. Not slow. Up the hill, on the other side of the street, under the railings. A long gray cloak, with a hood pulled forward, so that the face was a shadow. Gray gloves. Small neat boots, very clean, with square heels. They made almost no sound on the frosty cobbles.
Three lefts.
She was still behind him.
Not there by chance, thought Corin. And something went through him, very cold and very clear, like the first breath of air through the bars of the grating last night.
She stopped.
Not at him. Not at the church. Halfway up the lane, on the far side, at one of the little houses with the holly wreaths. She stopped at its railings, and looked at the wreath on its door, as if it were a very interesting wreath, the most interesting wreath she had ever seen. Her back was half turned. Her hooded head was bent.
She was doing it too.
Stopping at things. As long as a person would stop who was really looking.
Rule four, thought Corin. And then, because he could not help it, and because it was a strange thing to think, standing bareheaded in a church door on a frozen Sunday with his heart going hard: Somebody taught her the same rules.
Of course somebody did, said Malakor drily. Those rules are older than this city, boy. Older than Kross. Older than the Ministry. Every hunter learns them, sooner or later. And every hunted thing.
Corin did not answer. He did not like the way Malakor said hunter. He liked the way he said hunted even less.
In the bright brass plate, the gray shape had straightened up from the railings. It was walking on. Up the hill. Toward the church. On the far side of the lane, unhurried, with its hooded head turned a little away.
It would pass the church door in about twenty steps.
Corin had a choice. He could see it very clearly, the way you see the two roads at a crossroads. He could walk on, up Saddler's Walk, toward the wide street and the horse trough, with her behind him, the way she had been all afternoon. Take her across the city. To the Bourse, perhaps. To the kitchen door with the chalk eye on it, and Tam behind it. And let her see that, too. And write it in her small black book.
Or.
Rule six, he thought. Make them choose.
He stepped through the church doors, into the dark.
Saint Ivo's was very cold inside. Colder than the street. The cold of old stone that never sees the sun, that holds the winter in it all year round like water in a well. It smelled of candle wax and damp hymnbooks and the dust of a hundred years.
Corin walked down the side of the nave, past the dark pews, toward the candles. He did not hurry. His boots were loud on the stone, and he let them be loud. At the stand of candles, he stopped. There were perhaps twenty of them, burning in rows in their little iron cups, thin and white and wavering. Someone's Sunday prayers. A small wooden box hung on the wall beside the stand, with a slot in the top, and a card nailed above it, in careful old-fashioned writing. For the Remembered. One copper.
He had two coppers. He put one in the slot. He took a new candle from the box of them on the shelf below, and lit it from one of the others, and set it in an empty cup at the end of the row.
He did not know who it was for.
For Wick, perhaps. For Pip. For Felix, on his plank. For a teacher with a bucket and a ladle, and a girl of four in braids. For a soldier who whistled. For himself.
He stood and watched it burn.
And behind him, very softly, in the open doorway, he heard the cold air change.
He did not turn round. He looked at the candle. At its small steady flame. And in the polished brass of the candle stand, behind the rows of little lights, he saw the church door, bright with white daylight. And the dark shape that had just stepped into it, and stopped, with the light behind it, so that it was only a shape. Small and straight. A hood. A cloak.
She stood in the doorway for a long moment, looking down the dark nave at the back of his head.
Then she turned, and went out, and was gone.
Corin let out his breath.
She chose, said Malakor.
"She chose to go."
She chose to let you see her go. A dry pause. That is different, boy. That is very different. Now. Do you want to know where?
Corin looked at the candle a moment longer.
Then he turned, and walked back up the nave, past the dark pews, and out through the open doors into the white afternoon. Saddler's Walk was empty. The woman had gone in from her step. The little houses stood in their row with their holly wreaths and their green doors. And at the top of the lane, just turning the corner into the wide street, very small, going away from him, a gray cloak.
He went after it.
The Bird Market
She did not go far along the wide street.
Corin saw her turn off it, a hundred paces ahead, into the mouth of an arcade between two tall buildings. He knew the place, a little. It was in the Ledger. Corn Arcade. Old market. Covered. Bird sellers on Sundays. He had written it down some morning he did not remember, as a place where a man could buy a cheap hot pie and get out of the rain.
He heard the arcade before he got to it.
Birdsong. Hundreds of birds. Thousands, perhaps. All singing at once, high and quick and sweet and shrill, so many voices that they ran together into one great bright rushing sound, like water over stones. It came out of the mouth of the arcade into the frozen street and filled it. People going past on the cobbles looked up at it and smiled, without knowing they were smiling.
Corin went in.
The Corn Arcade had been built a long time ago, when the city was smaller and richer, for selling grain. It was long and high and narrow, with a roof of iron and glass held up on thin iron pillars, painted green once and now mostly rust. Half the glass panes were cracked and a few were missing, and pigeons sat in the gaps, looking down. The white light of the sky came through the glass and fell on everything below, flat and even, without shadows, so that the whole arcade seemed to float in milk.
And below, on both sides, as far as he could see, were the birds.
They were in cages. Every kind of cage. Big square wooden ones like crates, with wire fronts, stacked one on top of another as high as a man. Small round wire ones, like bells, hanging in rows from hooks on long poles. Tiny wicker ones, no bigger than a fist, tied in bunches like onions. And in every cage, a bird, or two, or ten. Little brown sparrows. Linnets, with red on their breasts. Goldfinches, with their bright yellow bars and their faces painted red and white and black, like actors. Canaries, as yellow as butter. Blackbirds. Thrushes. A row of starlings, all black and green and purple, with white spots like stars, talking to each other in their rattling clicking voices. A magpie in a big cage by itself, black and white, with one bright wicked eye, saying a word over and over that might have been hello and might have been something rude.
The floor was covered with seed husks and feathers and straw, and it crunched under his boots. The air smelled of birds. Of dust and droppings and seed and warm feathers, and under that, from the far end, of roasting chestnuts.
And there were people. Many more than on the street. It was the only place open on a Sunday, the only place warm, and everyone who had nowhere else to go had come here. Fathers with children on their shoulders, pointing. Old men in mufflers, with their hands behind their backs, bending to look into the cages, whistling to the birds through their teeth. Young men in their Sunday coats, showing off to young women. Sellers in long aprons, shouting their prices, holding up cages, whistling, clicking their fingers to make the birds sing.
"Linnet, a linnet, sir, the sweetest singer in the city, four coppers!"
"Goldie, goldie, look at that face, madam, ever see a face like that, six coppers and the cage free!"
Corin stopped just inside the entrance, by a stack of wooden cages full of sparrows, and looked down the arcade.
He could not see her.
For a moment, a sick cold moment, he thought she had gone. Out through some side door, some gap between the stalls, while he was standing in the street with his mouth open listening to the birds. He had been too slow. Too careful. She had chosen, and he had let her choose too far.
Then a group of young men moved, laughing, from in front of a stall halfway down. And there she was.
She was standing very still in the middle of all the moving people. Not looking at the birds. Not looking at anything. Just standing, with her hooded head a little bent, the way someone stands at the edge of a river, deciding whether to cross. The crowd went round her on both sides like water round a stone.
Corin put the sparrows between himself and her, and watched her through the bars.
She did not look back.
She did not need to, he realized after a while. She had other ways. As he watched, she moved to a stall on the left, where a seller had hung up a long row of little round mirrors among the cages, the kind you buy to hang inside a cage so that a lonely bird will think it has a friend. Dozens of them. Bright and round, turning slowly on their strings. She stood in front of them for a long time, as if choosing one. And Corin, behind his sparrows, saw a dozen small round pieces of the arcade turn and flash in them, and knew that one of those pieces was the entrance, and the stack of sparrow cages, and himself.
He did not move.
Rule one, said Malakor quietly. Use windows. Use water. Use the brass on doors. She is using mirrors, boy. In a bird market. Somebody taught her very well.
"Can she see me?"
She can see a stack of sparrows, said Malakor. And a gray coat behind them. In a mirror the size of a coin. A pause. She knows you are here. She has known since the church. The question is not whether she can see you. The question is what she will do about it.
She did nothing about it.
She turned away from the mirrors without buying one, and walked on, slowly, down the arcade. Corin let her go ten paces. Then twenty. Then he came out from behind the sparrows and followed, keeping to the other side, keeping people between them. A fat man with a canary. Two little girls holding hands. A sailor with a parrot on his shoulder, green and red, that bent down and bit his ear every few steps, so that he swore and everyone round him laughed.
Halfway down, she stopped again.
Not at a mirror this time. At a stall.
It was a small stall, poorer than the others. Just a trestle table with a gray blanket over it, and a few cages on it, and an old woman behind it on a stool, in a black shawl, with a face like a wrinkled apple and no teeth. The cages on her table were small and old and mended with string. And the birds in them were small and brown and plain. Not goldfinches. Not canaries. Nobody's favorites. Sparrows, and dunnocks, and a robin with a broken tail. And in the smallest cage of all, on the end of the table, no bigger than a man's two fists put together, a tiny round brown bird with its stubby tail cocked straight up over its back like a little flag.
A wren.
Corin saw her see it.
She went very still. Stiller than before, if that was possible. Her hooded head bent toward the little cage. Her gloved hand came up, slowly, and stopped just short of the wire, and hung there in the air.
The wren looked at her. It hopped on its perch, from one end to the other and back, quick and nervous, the way wrens do. Then it opened its tiny beak and sang.
Corin had not known that something so small could make so much noise. It was a huge song. Loud and fast and bright and ringing, trill after trill, pouring out of the little round body like water out of a broken jug, so loud that people at the next stall turned their heads. It went on and on. It seemed impossible that it would ever stop.
Then it stopped.
The wren sat on its perch with its tail cocked and its tiny black eye shining, and looked at the gray hooded figure in front of it, and waited.
She said something to the old woman. Corin could not hear it, through the birds. The old woman shook her head, and said something back, and held up three fingers. The figure in gray put her hand inside her cloak, and took something out, and laid it on the gray blanket. The old woman looked at it. And then she looked up at the figure in gray, with her toothless mouth open, very surprised.
Corin thought it was silver. He could not be sure. It caught the white light for a moment, very small, on the gray blanket.
The old woman picked it up, quickly, and bit it with her gums, and put it away in her shawl. Then she reached out, and untied the string that held the door of the little cage shut, and pushed the cage across the table.
The figure in gray picked it up.
She did not tuck it under her arm. She did not hold it close. She carried it to the end of the stall, to the edge of the arcade, where one of the green iron pillars went up to the roof, and there was a gap in the glass above, where a pane was missing, and the white sky showed through. She stood under the gap. She held the little cage up, in both her gloved hands, toward the sky.
And she opened the door.
The wren did not move at first. It sat on its perch with its tail cocked, and looked at the open door, and looked at her, and seemed to think about it. A wren thinking, very hard, about a door.
Then it went.
One quick brown flicker, out of the door. Up. Past her hooded head. Up, along the green iron pillar, to the gap in the roof, and through it, and out into the white. Gone. So fast that Corin hardly saw it. Only, a moment later, from somewhere high up on the roof of the arcade, outside, in the cold, he heard it sing again. Loud and fast and bright and ringing, trill after trill, as if it would never stop.
The figure in gray stood with the empty cage in her hands, looking up at the gap in the roof.
Then she put the cage down on the end of the old woman's table, very carefully, as if it were still full. And walked on.
Corin stood by a stall of linnets with his heart doing something strange.
Wren, said Malakor. Not a question.
"Yes."
He had known. He thought he had known since Tanner's Row, since the pump. The narrow shoulders. The straight back. The small neat boots with their square heels. He had read about those boots in the Ledger. Small boots. Narrow. With new square heels. He had read about her stillness, and her care, and her cool clear voice like well water, and the almost-smile. He had read about a small black notebook with something brown pressed in the front of it, in a paper pocket, and a child's writing over it. Keep this. Don't let them take it.
He had not let himself think the name. Not until now. Not until the open cage.
She has just let you see her let a wren go, said Malakor. In front of you. On purpose. Twenty paces away. A dry sound. Kross would be ashamed of her. Or very, very proud. I am not sure which.
"Why would she do that?"
I don't know, said Malakor. Perhaps she wanted to. Perhaps she could not help it. Perhaps it is a message. Perhaps it is a trap. A pause. Most things are all four, with people like her. And people like you.
At the far end of the arcade, the gray cloak went out through the big arched doorway into the street. Into the white afternoon. And turned right. Toward the river.
Corin went after it.
As he passed the old woman's stall, he looked down at the table. At the little empty cage, with its door hanging open on its string. And beside it, where the figure in gray had stood, lying on the gray blanket, something small that the old woman had not noticed.
A feather. Not a wren's. Too big for a wren's. A long gray feather, soft and barred, from some much larger bird.
He did not pick it up. He did not know why it was there, or whether it meant anything at all. Birds dropped feathers. This was a bird market.
But he wrote it down, walking, without slowing. Corn Arcade. She bought a wren, three coppers' worth, paid silver, let it go through the roof. Left the cage. A gray barred feather on the table where she stood.
Then he put the Ledger away, and went out into the cold.
