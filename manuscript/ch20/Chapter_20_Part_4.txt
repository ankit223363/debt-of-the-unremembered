Chapter 20: The Shadow in Gray, Part 4 of 5: Thursday's Report
Thursday's Report
Find her.
Corin sat on the hard wooden chair with his hand on the small black book, and the words went round and round in his head like a moth round a candle.
He did not remember writing them. Of course he did not. Six years ago. A man full of somebody else's ash, in the back room of a pen shop on Quill Row, who did not know his own name. Who had three notes in his throat and no fourth. Who had drawn them on page nine, a little hill going up, and up, and up, and then stopped, because he did not know how it came down.
Somebody else knows the end of it.
And here she was. Sitting across a little square table from him under a frosted skylight, with her hands flat in front of her, in a room full of two thousand people.
"I found you," said Wren Halloway. Very quietly. As if she had heard him think it. "Didn't I. Or you found me. On Needle Street. With a jar in your hand."
"Yes."
"I don't know what it means."
"Neither do I."
She nodded slowly. She looked down at her hands. And then she said, in a different voice, low and quick, as if she had to say it now or never say it at all:
"That's not why I brought you here."
Corin waited.
"I mean—it is. It's part of it. But it's not—" She stopped. She pressed her lips together. Her hands on the table had gone white at the knuckles. "There's something else. Something I have to tell you. And then you can go. And you can hate me. I won't blame you. I'd hate me."
"Tell me."
She did not move for a long moment.
Then she got up. Not slowly this time. Quickly. Like someone getting up to open a window in a room where they cannot breathe. She went to the table under the skylight, the small one where the candle was, and she pulled open a drawer, and she took out a small black book. Newer than the others. The black of the cover was still shiny. There was no label on the spine yet.
She did not bring it back to him. She stood by the table with it in her hands. She opened it near the end. She looked at the page for a long time.
Then she turned it round, and held it out across the room toward him, at arm's length, so that the candlelight fell on it.
He got up from his chair and went and took it.
It was a different kind of page.
Not a name and an address and a list of things taken. A copy. He could see that at once. The neat upright writing was the same, but it was set out differently. Like a letter. Like a report. With a heading at the top, and a date, and lines ruled down the left side for numbers.
To: Insp. S. Kross. From: Insp. W. Halloway. Report of the watch on Harvester C. Vane, Third Basement, Ministry of Records. Thursday.
(Fair copy kept. Original sent up the east tower pipe at the 9th bell.)
And then, in numbered lines, a list. Where he had gone on Thursday. Who he had seen. Corin read it and felt the skin on the back of his neck go cold and tight, one line at a time.
1. 7th bell. Left lodging at No. 14 Tallow Street. Alone. Coat gray. Ledger in left breast.
2. 7th bell and a half. Met the Thorne girl and a man in green livery, large, bearded, scarred (the Thorne captain, Oren Vance) at the corner of Hook Lane.
3. Went with them across Lantern Bridge to the north bank. To the Thorne house on the Hill? Lost them in the snow by the Tide Hall. (Note: Old Tide is running slow. Very slow. Somebody should look at it.)
4. Did not see them again until evening. Saw him go into the Memory Bourse by the kitchen door. With a boy.
5. Asked in the Bell, on Cinder Lane, after dark, as instructed, whether anyone had been talking about the harvester or the dead scribe on Gutter Lane.
Corin's finger stopped on the page.
He read the next line.
6. An old rag-and-bone man, called Wick, was talking about it to anyone who would buy him a gin. He said he found the dead scribe. He said a harvester knelt in the muck and asked him what color. He said he saw a tall lady in the lane before it was light, with a jar, and smelled her flowers, and heard her say something about the tide. He said the harvester would find her. He said it many times. People laughed at him. He lives under the Dye-Works, in the arches.
And under that, one more line. Shorter than the others. As if the hand that wrote it had not wanted to.
7. That is all.
Corin stood under the sloping roof, among the shelves, with the small shiny book in his hands.
He read line six again. And again. He lives under the Dye-Works, in the arches.
He heard Kross's voice. Light and pleasant and a little bored, in a frozen yard, beside a wagon with no horse. An old gentleman who will talk in gin shops. About ladies, and flowers, and things he saw in the dark. And to the two white faces on the box: You have his smell. Don't you. You had it on Thursday.
Thursday.
He looked up.
Wren had not moved. She stood by the little table under the skylight with her arms at her sides and her hands open and empty, the way she had put them flat on the table, so that he could see them. Her face was very white. Whiter than the frost on the glass above her.
"I wrote it down," she said. "Like I write everything down. Like I was told to. Like I've done every day for four years." Her voice was steady. Terribly steady. "I didn't think. I thought—he's an old man. He's harmless. Everyone laughs at him. What could anyone want with an old man who picks paper? I thought Kross would read it and laugh too. I thought it would show him that you were—that the people who talk about you are just—" She stopped.
"Harmless," said Corin.
"Yes."
She swallowed.
"And on Saturday night," she said, "he went to bed early. Kross. He never goes to bed early. He said he had a little errand on the way home and not to wait up. That's what he said to Brack, on the stairs. A little errand. And I heard him. And I didn't think. I didn't think until I was lying in my bed in the east tower at midnight, and I couldn't sleep, and I was going through the day the way I always do, the way he taught me, every person, every word. And I got to Thursday. And line six." Her hands closed into fists at her sides. "And I got up, and I put on my cloak, and I went out over the wall, and I ran. I ran all the way. Over the bridges. I can't run, Harvester. I told you that. I can barely run on stairs. I ran anyway."
"You were on the roof," said Corin.
"I was on the roof." Her voice cracked. Just once. "By the time I got there it was nearly three. It was all over. I knew it was all over before I got there. I could smell them, all the way up the lane. And her. The flowers. And I climbed up onto the roof by the water tank, because I didn't dare go down, and I sat there, and I watched the old woman come out of her door with a candle, and go down the arches, and stop at his door, and look in. And put her hand over her mouth." She was breathing fast now. "And I sat there until it got light. And I wrote it down. Everything. Wick. The arches. The old woman. What she did with her hands. I wrote it all down because that's all I know how to do. That's all I know how to do."
She stopped.
She stood in the candlelight, under the sloping roof, among her two thousand people, with her fists clenched at her sides and her chin up and her face white and wet, and she did not wipe it. She looked at him across the long low room the way a prisoner looks at a judge.
"So that's what I am," she said. "That's the woman who knows the end of your song. I'm the reason he's empty. I wrote him down, and they read it, and they went and knocked on his door."
Corin did not say anything for a long time.
He was thinking of the arches. Of the perfect stacks of paper, white and gray and yellow and brown. Tap, tap. Of a small clean pink face, and combed wet hair with a straight parting, like a boy dressed for church. Of the whiteness inside, smooth and cold in every direction, like the inside of an egg. And the one warm pebble at the bottom of it.
I'm keeping it for the harvester.
He was thinking, too, of the Bell on Cinder Lane, which he had never been inside, and of an old man in rags, standing at the bar with a gin somebody had bought him, telling the story again. A harvester knelt in the muck and asked me what color. Proud of it. Telling it over and over, every night, to anyone who would listen, to people who laughed. And a quiet young woman in a gray cloak, at a table in the corner, writing it down.
And he was thinking of himself. Kneeling in the muck behind a printing house, on a morning he did not remember. Asking an old man what color. Looking him in the eyes. Like I was somebody.
If he had not knelt down. If he had not asked. If he had just taken the jar and written his report and gone home, the way Lucian would have done, the way any other harvester in the Ministry would have done, Wick would have had nothing to tell. Nothing to be proud of. Nothing to say in the Bell, over and over, to anyone who would buy him a gin.
She's not the only one who wrote him down, he thought.
He looked at the small black book in his hands. At line six, in its neat, even, upright hand.
"Did you write the other thing?" he said.
Wren looked at him. She did not understand.
"On the roof," said Corin. "This morning. When you wrote it all down. Did you write the other thing? The thing he kept?"
"I don't—" She stopped. "What thing?"
"You didn't go down," said Corin. "You didn't go in. So you didn't hear him." He held out the book to her. "He kept one thing, Wren. In the moment after the third knock. Before the door opened. He untied the string himself, and he stood behind the door, and he shouted it, so it would be the last thing. So it would stay." His voice was not steady. He did not try to make it steady. "He shouted the words the lady said in the lane. The tide weighs it, and it comes up light. And then he shouted, I'm keeping it for the harvester."
Wren Halloway stood very still.
"And it stayed," said Corin. "It's still there. I went in and I looked. It's the only thing left in him. Like a pebble in a washed-out jar." He held the book out a little further. "He's sitting on a sack in his arch right now, sorting paper, saying it. Over and over. The tide weighs it, and it comes up light. To anyone. To no one. He doesn't know what it means. He doesn't know who he is. But he kept it. For me."
She did not take the book.
"You should write it down," said Corin. "In there. With the rest. So it's not lost." He paused. "So somebody knows it was his."
For a long moment, Wren did not move.
Then she came across the room. Slowly. Between the shelves. Until she was standing in front of him. And she took the small shiny book out of his bandaged hand. Her fingers did not touch his. She was very careful about that.
She went back to the little table under the skylight, and sat down, and took a pen from the drawer, and a bottle of ink, and opened the book at the page after line seven, and dipped the pen.
And wrote.
Corin watched her do it. Her small neat upright hand going across the page, steady now, perfectly steady, in the candlelight. He could not read it from where he stood. He did not need to.
When she had finished, she blotted it. She read it over once. Then, at the bottom, under the new lines, she wrote one more thing, smaller. He saw her hand hesitate over it, and then go on.
He thought he knew what it was. He had seen it at the bottom of a hundred pages in the first book, the one with Ellis Marchbanks and his daughter on the water trough.
She closed the book.
"Thank you," she said, without looking up. Very low.
"Don't," said Corin. And then, because it was what Sergeant Moll had said, in the arches, with his porridge face and his cold pipe, and because it was true: "Don't thank me."
The Leaf
The light in the skylight had turned to gold.
Not bright gold. Pale. The thin, cold, watery gold of a winter afternoon going down, when the sun has come out at last, low and late, just in time to set. It came through the frost on the glass and broke into a hundred small colored sparks in the feathers and ferns of ice. And it fell down into the attic in a slanting square, across the little table, and across Wren's hands, and across the shiny black cover of the book in front of her.
She sat looking at it for a long time.
Corin had gone back to his chair. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his bandaged hand held loosely in his good one, and watched her, and did not say anything. He had learned that this week. From Briggs on his barrel, and Hessa Doon by her pillar, and Sergeant Moll with his cold pipe. That there was a kind of quiet that was not empty. That you could sit in it with someone, and wait, and it was not nothing. It was the opposite of nothing.
At last she looked up.
"Ask me," she said.
"Ask you what?"
"What you came to ask. What you've been wanting to ask since the Third Basement." Her mouth did the almost-smile. Tired. "You wrote it down. Something brown, pressed in the front of her notebook. I read that too."
Corin looked at her.
"Will you show me?" he said.
She did not answer. But she put her hand inside the high neck of her gray dress, at the side, where there must have been a pocket sewn into the lining, close against her body, the way Hesper had sewn pockets into Corin's coat. And she took out another small black book.
This one was not like the others.
It was the same size. The same plain black cover. But it was old. Much older than the ones on the shelves. The black had worn away to brown at the corners, and the spine had cracked and been mended with a strip of black cloth and glue, and then cracked again and been mended again. The pages were swollen and wavy, as if they had been wet once, a long time ago, and dried. There was no label on it. There never had been.
She held it in both hands for a moment, the way Briggs had held his tin.
Then she got up and brought it to him. And this time she did not hold it out at arm's length. She came all the way. She stood beside his chair, in the gold light from the skylight, and she opened it at the front, at the inside of the cover, and held it down where he could see.
There was a pocket. Glued to the inside of the cover. A small flat pocket made of a folded scrap of paper, gone brown with age, with its edges curling. And above it, in faded pencil, in big, round, wobbling letters, the letters of a child just learning to write, pressing hard, going uphill:
Keep this. Don't let them take it.
And in the pocket, just showing at the top, something thin and flat and brown.
Wren put two fingers into the pocket. Very gently. As gently as he had seen anyone touch anything. And she drew it out, and laid it flat on the open page, in the gold light.
It was a leaf.
A small one. No longer than his little finger. Pressed so flat and so long that it had gone thin as paper, and brown as a nut, and you could see the light through it, like a window. Every vein showed. The long one down the middle, and the little ones going off it on each side, like the branches of a tree. Like the lines on the palm of a hand.
And its shape.
Rounded lobes. Five on one side, four on the other, where one had broken off a long time ago. Soft deep curves going in and out along the edges, like waves. Like the edge of a cloud. A shape that everyone knew, even in the city, even people who had never seen one growing. A shape that was carved over the doors of old churches, and stamped on the buttons of old coats, and drawn in children's picture books.
An oak leaf.
Corin did not move.
He sat on the hard wooden chair and looked down at the small brown leaf on the yellowed page, in the gold light, and he did not move or speak or breathe, because if he did any of those things, he thought, he would say what he was thinking. And he did not know yet whether he was allowed to.
He was thinking of a crypt.
Of a green book, bound in cloth, on Lyra's knee by candlelight. A doctor's casebook. W. Of the words in the doctor's careful hand. Brought to me at Crown Walk by H.L. himself, at night, by the garden door. Wrapped in a soldier's coat. Female. Aged about two years. He says she was registered. He says she is Bright. And between two pages, near the back, pressed flat, a leaf. Brown and thin and small, with the light coming through it. And under it, in the doctor's hand, in darker ink, written much later than the rest:
She took this from the garden at Crown Walk on the morning she went, and gave it to me, and said, "For when I come home." I said I would keep it. I have.
And he was thinking of a wall.
A nursery wall in a shuttered house on the Hill, behind a painted-over bell-board. A height chart. Pencil lines climbing up the plaster, year by year, with names beside them. Lyra's. Rowan's. And a third set of lines, older, taller, beginning when the child was two, and stopping at six. With the name scratched out. Gouged away with something sharp, so hard that the plaster had come off. And when Corin had laid a sheet of paper over the scratches and rubbed it with a pencil, a single letter had come up through the gouging, like a face through water.
W.
And beside it, drawn very small in pencil by somebody's careful hand, so small that whoever had scratched out the name had not even seen it—
A leaf.
An oak leaf.
Wren, Malakor had said, in that shuttered room, when Corin folded the rubbing small and put it in his pocket. Just that. Just her name. And Corin had not known what it meant.
He looked up.
Wren Halloway was standing beside his chair in the gold light with the old black book open in her hands, looking down at the leaf. And her face was the face he had seen in the Third Basement, at the very end, when the coolness had gone out of it. Young, and lost, and very far away. Like a girl standing at the end of a long road, looking back at something she cannot see.
"I don't know where it came from," she said. Very low. "That's the honest answer. The only one I've got." She did not look at him. "I've had it as long as I've had the tune. As long as I can remember. Which is the orphan school on the Hill. A long white room with forty beds in it, and a window at the end, and a lady in gray who rang a bell." A pause. "I had this book under my pillow. And the leaf in it. And I couldn't read the writing yet. I couldn't read at all. A bigger girl read it to me. Keep this. Don't let them take it." Her voice thinned. "So I did. I kept it. Through the school. Through everything. Through Kross. He's never seen it. Nobody has. You're the first."
"Who wrote it?"
"I did."
Corin looked at her.
"It's my hand," said Wren. "I've looked at it a thousand times. It's my hand when I was very small. The way I make my K. With the little loop. I still make it like that." She touched the pencil letters with one fingertip, not quite touching. "I wrote it to myself. Before the school. Before I could really write. I must have copied the letters off something. Somebody must have told me what to write and how to write it, and I sat and copied it, letter by letter, pressing hard. To myself. For later." Her finger hovered over the round wobbling K. "Like you. Writing find her in your book, six years ago. For later."
She was quiet.
"I don't know who told me," she said. "I don't know who them is. I don't know what they'd want with a leaf." She looked down at it. "There aren't any oaks in the city. Did you know that? Hardly any. I've looked. I used to walk all over, on my afternoons, when I was new, looking. In the parks. In the churchyards. Elms and limes and plane trees and a few old chestnuts. The Ministry cut the oaks down, years ago. Before I was born. There's a regulation. Nobody knows why." A pause. "I only ever found one. A young one. Not much taller than me. In a private garden on the Hill, behind black railings with gold tips on them, in a square called Crown Walk. The gate was locked. Only the houses have keys." Her voice had gone very quiet. "I stood at the railings for an hour, the first time. I don't know why. I go back sometimes. I stand there. I don't know why I do that, either."
Corin did not say anything.
He could not.
Because he knew where there was an oak. One. A great one. In the middle of a village, in a valley, with a river, and a school with a bell. With its branches spread out over the houses like a hen's wings. With a man in a plain coat standing beside it, a long time ago, lifting his hands. And another man, a big one, twenty years ago, with his back against it, whistling, while the sky went gold.
And he knew a square on the Hill, called Crown Walk, with a locked garden in the middle of it, where a small girl had picked a leaf on the morning she went away, and given it to a doctor, and said she would come home. He had walked past that garden with Lyra. Past Number 3, with its shutters closed. Past Number 11, with its lilies.
And he knew that he could not say any of it.
Not yet. Not here. Not to Kross's student, in Kross's city, with somebody always on the corner and somebody always on the bridge. Not until he knew. Not until he was sure. He had a green book in a crypt, and a rubbing in a pocket, and a leaf on a page, and a feeling under his breastbone like a fist. That was not enough. That was not nearly enough. If he was wrong, it would be the cruelest thing he had ever done. And if he was right—
If he was right, it might be crueler.
Good, said Malakor. Very softly. Hold your tongue, boy. Not yet. Not until you have the whole of it. Half a truth, in this city, is a knife with no handle.
So Corin held his tongue.
Instead, he did something else.
He did not decide to. It came up out of him the way the three notes always came up, by themselves, from the place behind his breastbone. He sat on the hard chair in the gold light with the small brown leaf in front of him, and he opened his mouth a little, and he hummed.
Three notes.
Up. And up. And up.
And stopped. The way they always stopped. At the top. Hanging there. Asking.
Wren's head came round.
She looked at him. For a long moment, the gold light on her pale face and the old black book open in her hands and the leaf lying on the yellowed page between them. And he saw her throat move.
And she answered.
The fourth note. Coming down. Softly, gently, the way a bird comes down onto a branch. Through her nose, with her mouth closed, the way she had hummed it in the Third Basement. The end of the line. The answer to the question.
And as she sang it, something in Corin's coat sang with her.
He felt it before he heard it. Against his ribs. On the right side. In the deep inside pocket, wrapped in its strip of bandage beside the cold silver rod. The jar. Pip's jar. Humming its one low note, the note it had hummed without stopping since the crypt, steady and soft and patient, so constant that he had nearly stopped noticing.
It was the same note.
The same as hers. Exactly. The fourth note. The one that came down. The two sounds met in the air between them, in the candlelight and the gold, and for a moment they were one sound, and the whole long low room full of small black books seemed to hum with it, very faintly, the way a wine glass hums when you run a wet finger round its rim.
The candles shivered. All of them. All at once. Every small flame in the room bent, the same way, toward Corin's chair, as if a breath of wind had gone through the attic. Though no wind had. Though the door was shut and the skylight was frozen and the air was perfectly still.
Then Wren stopped.
She stopped with her mouth a little open, and her eyes wide, staring at his coat. At the right side of it. Where the sound was still coming from. Low and soft and steady. Humming on, by itself, after hers had gone.
"What is that?" she whispered.
Corin put his hand flat on his coat, over the jar.
It was warm. Warmer than it had been all day. Warm as a hand.
"A little girl," he said. "In a crypt. By the river." His voice did not sound like his own. "She drew a gold sky over a valley, with an oak in the middle of it. And when I took the slag out of her, it went into this. And it's been singing that note ever since." He swallowed. "Your note. The one that comes down."
Wren Halloway stared at him.
Then, very slowly, she closed the old black book over the oak leaf. And held it against her chest, in both arms, the way a child holds something it is afraid will be taken away.
"I think you'd better tell me," she said, "what you know."
"I can't," said Corin. "Not yet."
"Why not?"
He looked at her. At the book held tight against her gray dress. At the small round wobbling letters she could not see now, on the inside of the cover. Don't let them take it.
"Because I'm not sure," he said. "And because if I'm wrong, I'll have taken something from you. And you've had enough taken." He paused. "And because if I'm right, you'll want to go and look. And they'll see you looking."
She was quiet for a long moment.
"That's a Kross answer," she said at last.
"I know."
"I hate it."
"I know."
She looked at him. And then, to his great surprise, she laughed. Short and soft and shaky. Not happy. But real.
"All right," she said. "All right, Harvester. Not yet." She held the book tighter. "But you'll tell me. When you're sure. You'll tell me before you tell anyone else."
"I promise."
"Write it down," said Wren. "So you don't forget you promised."
He took out the Ledger. And he wrote it, right there, in the gold light, with her watching. I promised Wren Halloway I will tell her what I know about the oak leaf. FIRST. Before anyone. As soon as I am sure. He underlined it twice.
She read it upside down. Then she nodded, once, and went back to her chair under the skylight, and sat down, and put the old black book away inside the neck of her dress, against her heart.
