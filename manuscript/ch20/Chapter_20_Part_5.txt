Chapter 20: The Shadow in Gray, Part 5 of 5: What Kross Wants
What Kross Wants
The gold went out of the skylight while they sat there.
It went slowly, and then all at once, the way winter light does. One moment the square of frosted glass was a sheet of pale gold, with sparks in it. The next it was gray, and then blue, and the cold came in through it, and the candles seemed to burn brighter, because there was nothing left to fight them.
Wren got up and lit three more. From a box on the shelf. Short stubs, like the others. She held each one to the flame of an old one until it caught, and set it in a saucer, and the smell of mutton fat got stronger.
Then she sat down again, and put her hands flat on the table.
"You've got an hour," she said. "Less. I have to be back in the east tower by the sixth bell, or someone will ask where I've been. And I'll have to say following the harvester, and they'll ask where he went, and I'll have to tell them something." Her mouth tightened. "I'll tell them the bird market and the bridge. That's true. That's safe." A pause. "Ask me what you need to ask me. Quickly."
Corin did not have to think.
"The crates," he said.
Wren looked at him.
"Six of them," said Corin. "Every night. Since July. Out of the slag room under the Lantern Quay. With a closed eye burned into the ends of them and two letters. IV." He watched her face. "I watched them go last night. I watched two of the gray ones carry them up the steps on their shoulders and put them in a wagon with no horse. And Kross was there. Standing in the yard. Eating barley sugar." He paused. "One of the crates broke, on the rail. And there was light. Violet. And voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands." His voice went quiet. "And somebody calling a name."
Wren's face did not change.
But her hands, flat on the table, pressed down a little harder. So that the knuckles went pale.
"Where do they go?" said Corin.
For a long moment she did not answer.
"I don't know," she said at last. "That's true. I swear it is. I don't know where they go." She looked down at her hands. "But I know where they go after where they go."
"What does that mean?"
"It means I've never seen it," said Wren. "Nobody I know has ever seen it. But I've heard him talk about it. Kross. Not to me. To Brack. To others. On the stairs, in the east tower, late at night, when he thinks there's nobody in the record room. I'm good at being nobody in the record room." A flicker of the almost-smile. Gone. "He calls it downstairs. Just that. The crates are going downstairs tonight. Downstairs is hungry. Downstairs wants more. Like it's a person. Like it's a dog in a kennel that has to be fed."
"Downstairs."
"Under the Hill," said Wren. "Under the Ministry. Under everything. Down past the Third Basement, where you work. Down past the vault under the east tower, where I put the jars." She took a breath. "There are stairs, Harvester. Old ones. Older than the Ministry. I've seen the top of them. There's a door at the bottom of the vault, behind the last row of shelves, with a lock on it that nobody has a key to except Kross. And the Archon. And one other person, I think, but I don't know who." Her voice had gone very low. "I've stood by that door on the night watch. And put my ear against it."
"What did you hear?"
Wren was quiet.
"Music," she said.
Corin felt the cold go down his back.
"Not music," said Wren. "Not really. Not a tune. Just—notes. Long ones. Very low. So low you feel them in your teeth more than you hear them. Like somebody very far away, very deep down, holding one key down on a great big organ, and not letting go." She swallowed. "And under it, sometimes, voices. The way you said. Hundreds. Not talking. Not to each other. Just—all at once. Like a choir that's forgotten the words."
Corin's hand had gone to his coat. Not to the rod. To the Ledger.
He did not need to open it. He knew what was there, behind the marbled endpaper at the back, where he had hidden it on Thursday night. A torn corner of old blue paper, from a great sheet. A clockmaker's blueprint. Lines and circles and pipes, very fine, in white ink. Two words, half torn away: Choir organ. And in the corner, in a draftsman's tiny printed capitals:
Sheet 1 of 40.
For installation: Lvl. IV.
There is no Level Four, the Ministry had told him, in its dusty voice, the week before. Everybody knows that. The Deep Archive. It was closed after the war.
"Level Four," said Corin.
Wren's eyes came up fast.
"Where did you hear that?"
"On a crate," said Corin. "Burned into the wood. IV." He did not say and on a scrap of blue paper behind the endpaper of the book over my heart. Not yet. "Is that downstairs?"
Wren did not answer for a while.
"I don't know," she said. "Maybe. Kross never says a number. He just says downstairs." She looked at the candle. "But once, last summer, he came up from the vault late, and he'd been down there, I think, through the door. His coat was wet at the hem. Like he'd been walking in water. And he was humming." She shivered, very slightly. "Kross never hums. I've never heard him hum in thirteen years. And he came up the stairs past the record room humming one long low note, very quietly, to himself. And he saw me. And he stopped. And he smiled. And he said, Wren, dear. Have you ever thought how much nicer the world would be if it were quiet? If everybody, all at once, just—let go?"
The candles burned.
"I said, Let go of what, sir?" said Wren. "And he said, Everything. And he put a barley sugar in my hand and went on up the stairs."
She looked at Corin.
"I've thought about it every night since," she said. "Every single night."
There was a silence.
Corin wrote it down. All of it. Downstairs. Under the Hill. A locked door at the bottom of the vault under the east tower. Kross, the Archon, one other. Organ notes, very low. A choir that has forgotten the words. Kross's coat wet at the hem. Kross humming. "How much nicer the world would be if it were quiet. If everybody, all at once, just let go." "Of what?" "Everything."
He looked at the last word for a long time. Then he drew a box round it. And then another box round that.
"One more thing," said Wren.
He looked up.
She had taken her hands off the table. For the first time since he came in. She was holding them in her lap now, tight together, the way Lyra held hers when she was frightened and did not want anyone to know.
"He knows you were in the coal hole," she said.
Corin did not move.
"Last night," said Wren. "In the yard. Under the grating, behind the water butt." Her voice was flat. "I don't know how. I wasn't there. But he came back to the east tower at two in the morning, after the little errand, and Brack was waiting up for him, and I was in the record room, being nobody. And Brack asked him how it went. And Kross laughed. And he said, Oh, very well. Very tidy. And our forgetful friend came to watch, from a hole in the ground, like a mole. I left him a present." She looked at Corin. "And Brack said, Shall I fetch him, then? And Kross said—"
She stopped.
"What?"
"He said, Not yet. Not yet, Brack. I want to see who he runs to."
The room was very still.
Corin thought of the river road, at a quarter to twelve. The two dark lines in the frost. The three slow knocks. Saint Bride's, with its low black door. Oren's knock, three slow and two quick, that he had not been able to make. Lyra asleep on his shoulder. Doctor Silas, with his tin cup. Pip, under her charcoal hills.
Juno, coming down the crypt steps at dawn, with a scratch on her hand.
The arches. Mother Grue, with her poker. Hessa Doon, with her baby on her hip. Sergeant Moll.
I want to see who he runs to.
He had run to all of them.
"And you," he said. Very low. "You were told to watch who I talk to. Write it down. In your lovely clear hand."
"Yes."
"So you'll write them down."
Wren Halloway looked at him across the little table. Her face was white. Her hands were clenched together in her lap so hard that he could see them shaking.
"I'll write the bird market," she said. "And Lantern Bridge. And he lit a candle for somebody in Saint Ivo's, on Saddler's Walk." Her voice cracked. "And I'll write that I lost you on the north bank, in the dark, at the fourth bell. Because you walk a very good circle, Harvester. Somebody must have taught you." She drew a breath. "That's all I'll write. That's all I'll ever write. About you. From now on."
"And if he asks?"
"Then I'll lie to him." She said it very simply. "It's easier the second time. I've found that out this week."
Corin looked at her for a long moment.
"That's not enough," he said gently. "Is it. He'll have others. Brack. The ones on the corner. The ones on the bridge."
"No," said Wren. "It's not enough." She did not look away. "So go and tell them. Tonight. Everyone you ran to. Tell them to move. Tell them to be somewhere else in the morning. Tell them not to open their doors." Her chin came up. "And tell the girl. The Thorne girl. Tell her that whatever she's planning to do before Monday noon, she'd better do it fast. Because I've seen the papers on Kross's desk. And there's a carriage booked from the Ministry stables for Monday morning, at nine. The address was crossed out, so hard the nib went through. To collect one guest."
"Lord Thorne?"
"I don't know," said Wren. "It didn't say a name. Or where. Just one guest." She looked at him. "Three hours before the lady's carriage from Fallowfield."
Corin sat very still.
Three hours.
Monday noon, the lily letter had said. A Fallowfield carriage will take Lord Thorne. Unless.
But a Ministry carriage was coming at nine.
So it doesn't matter what Lyra brings, he thought. It never mattered. It was never a bargain. It was a clock.
He closed the Ledger. He stood up.
Wren stood up too.
For a moment they stood facing each other across the little square table, in the candlelight, under the dark skylight, among the two thousand people on their shelves. Neither of them said anything.
Then Wren said, "Go down the back way. There's a door at the bottom of the stairs, past Mr. Ambrose's parlor, into the yard. And a gate from the yard into the alley behind. Nobody watches the alley. I've checked." A pause. "Every quarter."
"Thank you."
"Don't," said Wren. And the almost-smile came, very small, at the corner of her chapped mouth. "Somebody told me that, once. Today. In this room."
He went to the door. He put his hand on the latch.
"Harvester," she said.
He stopped. He did not turn round. It seemed to him that this was how it had happened before, in the Third Basement, the other way round. Her at the door. Him behind her. A name.
"Corin," he said. "If you like."
There was a silence behind him.
"Corin," said Wren Halloway. Very quietly. As if she were trying the shape of it in her mouth, to see whether it fit. "Be careful. Please. I've only just found the end of the song." A pause. "I'd hate to lose the beginning."
Two Leaves
The alley behind Mercer Street was black and narrow and full of frozen washing water.
Corin came out into it through a gate in a yard wall, the way Wren had told him, past the bookbinder's coal shed and a row of empty flowerpots and a cat sitting on a water butt, watching him with yellow eyes. Nobody else. The alley ran away in both directions between the backs of houses, with little lit windows high up on either side, and the smell of Sunday suppers, and the sound, from somewhere, of a family singing a hymn round a piano, slightly out of tune.
He did not go back over Lantern Bridge.
He went the long way. East along the alley to its end. Then down a set of steps he did not know, to a little wooden footbridge he had never crossed, that went over the river on tall black legs a long way below the Tide Hall, swaying a little in the wind, with the ice grinding round its legs below. Nobody on it. Nobody on the far side. He crossed it with his collar up and his hands in his pockets and his eyes on every window.
He did a circle at the bottom. Three lefts. Nothing behind him.
He did another. Nothing.
Then he went down into the Cut, through the dusk, toward the chapel with no roof.
He did not knock on the door at all this time.
He went round the side, through the frozen nettles, and found the crack in the wall by the bell tower that Juno had shown him that morning, where the stones had fallen in and you could climb down into the old vestry, and from the vestry into the crypt by a little stair that nobody used. He climbed down. It took a long time with his bad hand. He came out at the bottom into the warm, and the candlelight, and the smell of cabbage and carbolic and sick children, behind a pillar.
Oren had a sword at his throat before his second foot was on the floor.
"It's me," said Corin.
"I can see that," said Oren. The sword did not move. "I could see it was you halfway down the stair. You climb like a cow on a ladder." He looked at Corin's face for a long moment, in the candlelight. Then, slowly, he lowered the blade. "You didn't come by the door."
"No."
"Why not?"
"Because he's watching who I run to," said Corin. "And I ran here."
He told them.
Not all of it. He sat on the bottom step again, his step, with Lyra on the crate in front of him and Oren by the door and Doctor Silas standing by the brazier with his long arms folded and his tired, careful eyes on Corin's face. And Juno, who had woken up at last, cross-legged on the pile of blankets with her chin in her hands. And he told them about the circle, and the bird market, and the bridge, and the house on Mercer Street. About the room of small black books, and the two thousand people in it. About Thursday's report, and line six. About downstairs, and the organ note, and the choir that had forgotten the words.
About I want to see who he runs to.
And about a carriage from the Ministry stables, booked for Monday morning, at nine. To collect one guest.
When he said that, Lyra stood up.
She did not say anything. She stood up off her crate, very fast, and stood quite still in the middle of the crypt with her fists at her sides and her face white, and then she sat down again, slowly, as if her legs had decided for her.
"Nine," she said.
"Nine."
"Three hours before."
"Yes."
"So it was never—" She stopped. Her mouth worked. "The brooch. The portrait. The stone. It was never a—"
"No," said Corin. "I don't think it was. I think it was a clock."
Lyra put her face in her hands.
Nobody said anything. Juno, on her blankets, looked from Lyra to Corin to Oren, with her sharp black eyes very wide. Doctor Silas looked at the floor.
Then Lyra took her hands away. Her face was dry. Quite dry. And very hard.
"Then we stop the clock," she said.
Oren, by the door, made a sound like a man who has been waiting a long time to hear something, and has finally heard it.
They talked for an hour.
Corin wrote it down. Who would go where. Who would tell whom. The children in the crypt could not be moved, Doctor Silas said, not tonight, not in this cold, not without killing half of them. So he would stay. He had stayed through worse. And nobody, he said, has ever yet found it worth their while to knock on the door of a cellar full of fever. Juno would run. Tonight. To Mother Grue, in the arches, and to Hessa Doon on the black barge with the red sail. To Sergeant Moll, at the Watch house, by the back door. To her own Gran, on Tallow Street, to tell her to bolt the purple door and open it to nobody. Not to white, said Corin. Not to gray. Not to anyone who knocks slow.
"And the Warden," said Juno. "The big one. With the boy on the docks."
Corin had not told her about Briggs. He looked at her.
Juno shrugged. "You talk in your sleep," she said. "On the step. All night. Four hundred and twelve, four hundred and twelve. Tallow Row. The green door. Three bolts." She scowled. "I'll go to him too. Tallow Row. The green door with three bolts. I'll knock fast."
And Lyra and Oren.
That was harder. That took longest. They sat with their heads close together, by the brazier, talking low and fast, and Corin wrote down what they said, and then crossed it out, because they changed their minds, and wrote it down again. In the end, he wrote one line, and drew a box round it, and did not cross it out.
Before nine on Monday. Lord Thorne. Whatever it takes.
And under it, smaller, in Lyra's words, because she had said them, quietly, staring into the red coals, and he had thought they should be kept:
She wants me quiet. So we'll shout.
It was later, when the others were busy, that he asked her for the green book.
She was sitting with Pip again, on the edge of the mattress in the corner, under the charcoal hills. She looked up at him when he asked. And then she reached inside her coat, without a word, and took it out, and gave it to him. A doctor's casebook. Bound in green cloth, gone dark at the corners with handling. With a single letter on the front, in faded gold. W.
He took it to the candles on the stone ledge by the stairs, where the light was best. And he opened it near the back, where it always fell open, because there was something between the pages.
The leaf was still there.
Small and thin and brown, pressed flat, with the light coming through it so that every vein showed. Rounded lobes, with soft deep curves going in and out along the edges, like waves. Like the edge of a cloud. And under it, in the doctor's small neat hand, in the darker ink:
She took this from the garden at Crown Walk on the morning she went, and gave it to me, and said, "For when I come home." I said I would keep it. I have.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
He did not have the other leaf. He could not lay them side by side. He would never be able to say, afterward, with his hand on his heart, that they were the same. Leaves were leaves. Every oak tree in the world made a thousand of them every spring, and they all looked alike to a man from the city.
But he had looked at the other one in the gold light for a long time. He had looked at it the way he looked at everything, now, since he had begun to understand that he would lose it. Carefully. Every line. Every curve. So that he could write it down after.
And he had written it down. Walking. In the black alley behind Mercer Street, before he let himself think about anything else.
Her leaf. Small. Shorter than my little finger. Five lobes on the left side, four on the right. One broken off on the right, near the stem, a long time ago. The stem is short and bent at the end, like a hook.
He looked down at the leaf on the doctor's page.
Small. Shorter than his little finger. Five lobes on the left side. Five on the right. Whole. Not one broken.
And its stem, short, and bent at the end, like a hook.
The same kind of hook. The same short bent stem, as if both leaves had been picked by the same small hand, on the same morning, from the same young tree, pulling hard, the way children pull, so that the stems bent.
Two, he thought. She picked two.
One for the doctor. For when I come home.
And one for herself. In a book. Under her pillow. In a long white room with forty beds, and a lady in gray with a bell. Keep this. Don't let them take it.
He could not be sure. He knew he could not be sure. Leaves were leaves.
He closed the green book very gently.
"What is it?" said Lyra.
She had come up beside him without his hearing. She was standing at his shoulder, in the candlelight, looking at the green book in his hands, and then at his face.
"You've found something," she said. "Haven't you. About her. About W."
Corin did not answer at once.
He was thinking of a height chart on a nursery wall. A third set of lines, older and taller than Lyra's, starting at two and stopping at six. A name scratched out so hard that the plaster came off. An empty chair at a Midwinter dinner, for someone who couldn't come. A cup in the nursery cupboard with a scratch on it, put away in the dark. The one I could not keep.
Lyra had been a baby when the lines began. She did not remember. She remembered only the empty chair.
And on the other side of the river, in an attic full of small black books, a young woman with a long nose and a narrow chin and dark straight eyebrows sat by herself in the candlelight, writing two thousand people down so that they would not be lost. Not knowing that somebody, once, had stood her against a wall and drawn a line over her head, every birthday, for four years. And kept her cup.
Tell me before you tell anyone else.
He had written it down. He had promised.
"Yes," he said. "I think I've found something."
"Then tell me."
"I can't."
Lyra's face changed.
"Can't?"
"Not yet." He held out the green book to her. "I promised someone that they'd be the first to know. When I'm sure. And I'm not sure. And it's—" He stopped. "It's theirs to know first. Not mine. Not yours. Theirs."
Lyra did not take the book.
She looked at him for a long moment. He saw the anger come into her face, hot and quick, the way it always did. He saw her open her mouth.
And then he saw her look down at the green cover. At the faded gold letter. W. And at his bandaged hand, holding it out.
And he saw her close her mouth again.
"Is it bad?" she said at last. Very quietly.
"I don't know," said Corin. "I don't think so. I think it might be the opposite of bad." He paused. "I think it might be the best thing I've found all week. If it's true. If I'm right."
Lyra looked at him a moment longer. Then she took the book. She held it against her chest in both arms, the way Wren had held hers.
"When you're sure," she said.
"When I'm sure."
"And then me."
"And then you. Second. I promise."
"Write it down," said Lyra.
He almost laughed. He wrote it down.
I promised Lyra: she will be SECOND.
The bells of the city rang seven.
Five hours to midnight. To Vivienne, and the sister vial, and the six Ledgers in their tide-table coats under a loose board in a pen shop on Quill Row. Fourteen hours to nine o'clock on Monday morning, and a Ministry carriage, and one guest.
Juno had gone. Out through the crack in the vestry wall, up into the dark, light as a cat, with a list of names in her head and her brass key bouncing on its string. Oren was sharpening his sword by the brazier with long slow strokes of a gray stone, shhhk, shhhk, not looking at anyone. Lyra sat with Pip. Doctor Silas went along his rows of mattresses with his bowl and his cloth.
Corin sat on his step and opened the Ledger at a clean page.
He looked at it for a long time.
Then he wrote, at the top, in his clearest hand:
W.
And under it, very small, so small that anyone reading over his shoulder would have needed to bend close to see it:
Wren?
He did not write anything else. He did not draw a box round it, or underline it, or put a mark beside it. He just looked at it. The one letter, and the one word, and the question mark, small and plain on the clean white page.
Then he closed the book, and put it back inside his coat, against his heart. Next to the jar, still humming its one low note. The fourth note. The one that came down.
And he stood up, and went to find his coat collar, and his gloves, and the way out through the crack in the vestry wall. Because it was seven o'clock on a Sunday night, and there was a kitchen door behind the Bourse with an eye chalked on it, and a boy behind it called Tam who knew why a tune could not be taken. And because there were five hours left before midnight.
And because, on the far side of the river, somebody was waiting to see who he would run to next.
Let him watch, thought Corin.
And he climbed up into the dark.
