Chapter 20: The Shadow in Gray, Part 2 of 5: Over the Lantern Bridge
Over the Lantern Bridge
She went down to the river.
Not by the river road. By the streets. Down through the old part of the town, between tall dark houses, along lanes so narrow that the frost on the cobbles had not been touched by a single boot all day. Corin kept far back. Sixty paces. Eighty. Sometimes, at a corner, he lost her altogether, and stood with his heart going hard, and then walked on round the corner and saw the gray cloak again, far ahead, small, going on.
She never hurried. She never looked back. She never stopped again, the way she had at the railings in Saddler's Walk, or at the mirrors in the bird market. She just walked. Steady and neat and quiet, in her small square-heeled boots, down and down toward the water.
And he understood, after a while, that she was letting him.
She was walking the way you walk when you want a dog to follow you home. Not too fast, so that it loses you. Not too slow, so that it catches you up and gets under your feet. Just right. Always just in sight. Always just far enough ahead.
She is taking you somewhere, said Malakor.
"I know."
You do not know where.
"No."
And you are going anyway.
"Yes."
There was a pause.
Good, said Malakor, surprisingly. That is the first sensible thing you have done all day.
"Why is it sensible?"
Because if she wanted you dead, or empty, you would be dead, or empty, already, said Malakor. She has been behind you since noon. She knows where you sleep. She knows who you talk to. She stood in the door of that church and looked at the back of your neck for as long as it takes to say a prayer. A dry sound. Kross's people do not do that for nothing. If she is taking you somewhere, it is because she wants you there. And I would like very much to know why.
Corin did not say anything. But his hand went, without his meaning it to, to the right side of his coat. To the long narrow pocket, and the cold rod in it.
He let it stay there.
The lanes came out at the top of a flight of stone steps.
They were old steps, very wide and very worn, going down in a long slow curve to the river, with an iron rail on each side, black and cold. Each step had a deep hollow in the middle of it, where a hundred years of feet had gone up and down, so that in the frost each hollow held a little pool of white, like a saucer of milk.
At the bottom of the steps was the bridge.
Corin had seen it a hundred times. He had crossed it a hundred times. It was in the Ledger, on the very first page he had ever read, in the light of a dishwater dawn, sitting on the edge of his bed. Saw a busker on Lantern Bridge. But he stopped at the top of the steps, all the same, and looked at it. Because he did not remember it. And because on a frozen Sunday, in this white light, with the river under it full of ice, it was beautiful in a way he did not think he had ever written down.
It was very old. Old gray stone, gone green with damp, and black in the cracks. Seven great arches, one after another, marching out across the river from bank to bank. They were round, and wide, and each one was a little higher than the last toward the middle, and then a little lower again toward the far side, so that the whole bridge made one long, slow, gentle hump, like the back of a sleeping animal. The ice went grinding and turning through the arches underneath, gray and dirty and slow, and piled up against the great stone piers in heaps, and made a sound like a giant chewing.
And along both sides of the bridge, on top of the low stone walls, every twenty paces or so, there were the lamps.
They were not like the lamps of the streets. They were short and squat, made of black iron, and shaped like little houses. Each one had a peaked iron roof, and four small glass windows, one on each side, and a tiny iron chimney on top. Once they had burned oil. Now they burned gemstones, like everything else, and the stones glowed inside them, even in the day, pale and blue and cold. From the top of the steps, the two long rows of them, going away across the river toward the far bank, looked like two rows of tiny houses with their lights on, standing very still in the snow, with nobody inside.
The gray cloak was on the bridge.
She was walking along the left-hand side, by the wall, past the little lamps. Not fast. Not slow. Halfway to the first pier.
Corin went down the steps.
There were people on the bridge. Not many. A carter with an empty wagon and a tired brown horse, going home. Two men in Sunday coats, arguing about something, with their breath going up in clouds. A girl with a basket. A boy with a dog. Corin let them come between himself and the gray cloak, one after another, and walked behind them, using them, the way you use the trees in a wood.
She stopped at the first lamp.
Corin stopped too. Behind the carter's wagon, which had stopped because the horse had decided to look at the river. He stood by the wagon's back wheel with his hands in his pockets, as if he were waiting for the horse, and looked past the wheel at her.
She was standing in front of the first lamp. The little black iron house, on its stone post, at the start of the bridge. And on the stone post, under the lamp, at about the height of a man's chest, there was a plaque.
Corin knew it. It was in the Ledger, on one of the first pages. And he knew, too, without needing the page, that he always looked at it. Every time he crossed. He did not know why.
It was a flat panel of gray stone, set into the post under the lamp, about as big as a book. There were words cut into it. Old words, in tall upright letters with little tails on the ends of them, worn soft by a hundred years of rain. Corin could not read them from where he stood. But he could see where they stopped.
In the middle of the top line, there was a space.
Not a gap between two words. Something else. A long smooth hollow in the stone, where the stone had been chiseled away, carefully, and rubbed flat, and rubbed again, until whatever word had been there was gone. Not hacked out. Not smashed. Smoothed. The way you smooth a bed after someone has slept in it, so that nobody will ever know. It was very neat work. It was the neatest thing on the whole bridge.
She was looking at it.
She stood in front of the plaque with her hooded head a little bent. Then, slowly, she lifted one gloved hand. And she put the tip of one finger into the smooth hollow. Into the place where the word had been. And drew it along, slowly, from one end to the other. The way a child traces a letter in a book, to learn it.
Corin's mouth had gone dry.
He was thinking of a little girl in a crypt, with her empty eyes. Of an old shepherd's voice. The king hath put the sky over us. Of the name that had been cut out of the memory itself, so that even when he drew the slag out of her with his own bare hand, and saw the gold dome and the man in the plain coat lifting his hands in the field, he could only hear the first sound of it. C—. And then nothing. A smooth place. Rubbed flat. As if nothing had ever been there.
The same thing.
The same smooth space. Here, on a bridge, in stone. There, in a child, in Ash.
The figure in gray took her finger out of the hollow. She stood for a moment longer. Then she went on, across the bridge.
The carter's horse decided it had seen enough of the river, and walked on. Corin walked with it.
When he came level with the plaque, he did not stop. He did not even slow down. But he turned his head, as he passed, the way you turn your head to glance at a shop, and read it. He knew the words already. He read them anyway.
LANTERN BRIDGE. BUILT IN THE REIGN OF
Then the smooth place.
RESTORED BY THE GRACE OF THE MINISTRY.
He read it twice, walking. Then he looked away, and walked on beside the wagon, past the little lamp-houses with their cold blue lights.
Somebody had taken the one word that mattered, and smoothed it away, and then written underneath it, in letters just as tall, who had restored the bridge. As if the Ministry had built it. As if there had never been anyone before.
Somebody has a sense of humor, said Malakor. Very low. Or a very great sense of shame.
In the middle of the bridge, at the top of the long slow hump, Corin looked over the wall.
He did not mean to. He had not meant to all afternoon. But his head turned anyway, the way it had turned to the plaque, and he looked down, over the low stone wall, past the little lamp-house, at the river.
At the arch below him.
The great middle arch of Lantern Bridge. The highest of the seven. From up here he could not see under it. Only the curve of its edge, gray-green stone, black with wet. And the ice going in under it, gray, slow, grinding. And on the near bank, at the foot of the first pier, almost hidden, a narrow stone landing, with iron rings in the wall for tying boats, and a short flight of slimy steps going down to it from the river road. Empty. Gray. Half buried in heaped-up ice.
Under the Lantern Bridge, Doctor Aske had written, at the landing on the riverside, at eleven tomorrow night. He says nobody goes there.
And Briggs, on his barrel, in the lantern light: Under one of the big bridges. Down by the water. Where the arches meet the bank. And people would bring him names. And he'd write them on the stones. On the underside of the arch, where the rain doesn't reach.
Corin stood at the top of the bridge with the cold wind coming down the river into his face, and looked at the narrow landing, and the dark underneath of the arch beyond it, where no light went.
He could not see anything. It was too dark under there. Too far.
But he wrote it down. Lantern Bridge. A landing at the foot of the first pier, near bank. Iron rings. Steps from the river road. Under the arch: dark. CAN'T SEE. Come back with a lamp.
When he looked up from the Ledger, the gray cloak was at the far end of the bridge.
She was standing at the top of the steps on the north bank, where they went down to the street. And she had turned round.
She was looking back across the whole length of the bridge. At him.
She did not wave. She did not move at all. She stood there, small and straight and gray, with the two long rows of tiny blue lamp-houses running away from her toward him across the river, and the white sky behind her, and looked at him for as long as it takes to breathe in and out three times.
Then she turned, and went down the steps, and was gone.
Well, said Malakor. She is certainly not pretending anymore.
Corin put the Ledger away and walked on across the bridge, a little faster now, toward the north bank and the steps and whatever was at the bottom of them.
The Bookbinder's House
The north bank was a different city.
Corin felt it the moment he came down the steps at the end of Lantern Bridge. On the south side, in the Ink Quarter, the streets were crooked and narrow and loud, and the houses leaned together like drunks holding each other up, and everything smelled of ink and fish and smoke. Here the streets were straighter. Wider. Cleaner. The houses stood up on their own, tall and narrow and flat-fronted, in long even rows of gray stone and dark brick, with iron railings in front of them and steps going up to their doors. Their windows were bigger. Their doors had brass knockers, polished. Somebody swept the frost off the pavements here.
It was not the High Quarter. Not the great white houses on the Hill, with their gardens and their gates. It was the place in between. Where clerks lived, and schoolmasters, and doctors who were not rich, and widows with a little money put by. Where people had enough, just, and were afraid every day of not having enough.
And it was quiet. Even quieter than the south side. Sunday quiet, with a lid on it. Behind every big clean window, a lace curtain. Behind every lace curtain, a family at its Sunday dinner, being very quiet so as not to disturb the neighbors.
The gray cloak went along it ahead of him, under the bare black trees.
She did not look back again. She did not need to. She had told him what she wanted to tell him, from the top of the steps. Now she just walked, and he followed, sixty paces behind, in plain sight, on the same side of the street. There was no point hiding. They both knew it.
It felt very strange. Like walking home with someone after a dance, a long way behind, without speaking.
She turned into Mercer Street.
It was a small street. Shorter and narrower than the others. Half shops, half houses. The shops were the kind that people on the north bank went to when they did not want the people on the north bank to see them going. A pawnbroker with very clean windows. A shop that mended clocks. A shop that sold second-hand clothes, good ones, pressed and folded, with no prices on them. A shop that cleaned gloves. And halfway down, on the left, a bookbinder's.
It was a narrow house. Three floors and an attic, squeezed in between the glove-cleaner's and a dark brick house with all its shutters closed. The ground floor was the shop. It had one wide window of small square panes, with the shutters down for Sunday, and a sign over it painted in gold letters on black, faded to brown on gray. J. AMBROSE. BOOKBINDER. REPAIRS. GILDING. ACCOUNT BOOKS MADE TO ORDER. Beside the shop door, there was a second door. A narrow one. Plain. Painted dark green, with a brass number on it, and a brass letterbox, and no knocker.
The gray cloak went up the two stone steps to that door. She took something from inside her cloak. A key. She opened the green door, and went in.
And she did not close it.
Corin stopped on the pavement across the street.
He stood by the railings of the dark house with all its shutters closed, and looked at the green door. It stood open. Not wide. About a hand's width. Just enough that you could see it was open. Just enough that you could see, behind it, the bottom of a narrow staircase going up into the dark, with a strip of worn brown carpet on it.
No light. No sound.
Just a door, standing open a hand's width, on a quiet street, on a Sunday afternoon.
Well, said Malakor. There it is.
"There what is?"
The choice. A dry pause. She made hers in the church. This one is yours.
Corin stood by the railings for a long time.
He thought about Wick. About the string on Wick's door. Untied. About the long slow wait after a third knock, and the voice behind the door, loud and proud. I'm keeping it for the harvester.
He thought about the white face at the grating, an inch from his own. About the needles. About the pull, like the tide at his ankles.
He thought about a pale man in a beautiful gray coat setting a tiny folded square of yellow paper on the top bar of an iron grating, without looking down. I do so hate untidiness.
This woman was that man's student. Toby had said it, white to the lips, on Tuesday. She's Kross's. They say he picks them himself. From the orphan schools. And that they'd walk into the river if he told them to.
And she had stood in a cold room in the Third Basement, not two weeks ago, in her white coat, and hummed him four notes. And the fourth one had come down, like a bird coming down onto a branch. And his chest had hurt so much that he could not breathe.
She is either the most dangerous person you have met this week, said Malakor, or the only one you can trust. Possibly both. Possibly on alternate days.
"That doesn't help."
It was not meant to help. It was meant to be true.
Corin took his hand out of his pocket. He looked at it. It was the right one, the one he had burned on the bars. The bandage was gray and dirty. The fingers under it throbbed.
Then he crossed the street.
The hall was dark and narrow and smelled of glue.
Glue, and leather, and paper, and the warm, sweet, dusty smell of old books, coming through the wall from the shop next door. And under it, faint, the smell of a coal fire somewhere, and of boiled potatoes. Somebody's Sunday dinner, a long time over.
Corin pushed the green door shut behind him. Quietly. He did not lock it. He left it on the latch, so that he could get out again quickly, if he had to.
The staircase went up in front of him. Steep and narrow, with a thin wooden rail on the left, polished by hands, and the strip of worn brown carpet held down on each step with a brass rod. Most of the rods were loose. On the right, the wall had been papered once, a long time ago, with a pattern of small brown birds on branches. The paper had faded to the color of weak tea, and was peeling in places, in long curls, and someone had stuck the curls back with paste.
On the left, at the bottom of the stairs, there was a door. Half open. A warm red light came out of it, from a fire. And a sound. A slow, deep, wheezing, whistling sound, like a kettle that is not quite boiling. In and out. In and out.
Corin went to the door and looked in.
It was a small back parlor, crowded with furniture. A horsehair sofa. A table with a red cloth on it and the remains of a Sunday dinner. A glass case on the wall with a stuffed owl in it. Shelves and shelves of books, from the floor to the ceiling, in every color of leather, with gold on their spines. And by the fire, in a big armchair with a high back and wings on the sides, an old man.
He was asleep. He was very small and very bald and very wrinkled, with a fringe of white hair round the back of his head, like a monk's, and a pair of wire spectacles pushed up onto his forehead, and a big white napkin still tucked into his collar. His hands were folded on his round stomach. They were stained, all over, brown and black and gold. Glue, and leather dye, and gold leaf, worked into the skin so deep that it would never come out. A bookbinder's hands.
He was the one making the kettle sound. Snoring. Very peacefully. With his mouth open.
On the arm of his chair, there was a little brass ear trumpet, like a small curved horn.
Deaf, thought Corin. Deaf, and asleep. The same as Puckett. He was beginning to think that the whole city was run by deaf old men who fell asleep after their dinner.
He went back to the stairs.
He went up.
The first floor had two doors. Both shut. Both locked. He tried them, gently. A clerk's rooms, he thought, from the brass name card on one of them. Mr. H. Pettigrew. Away for Sunday. The other had no card.
The second floor had two doors. Both shut. One had a pair of muddy boots outside it, and behind it, someone was playing a flute, very badly, the same five notes over and over, and stopping, and starting again.
The stairs got narrower. Steeper. The brass rods stopped. The carpet stopped. The bird wallpaper stopped, and the walls were just plaster, whitewashed, cracked. The rail stopped too, and there was only a rope, nailed to the wall with big iron staples, to hold on to.
At the top, there was one door.
It was the attic. It must have been. The ceiling over the top of the stairs sloped down sharply on one side, following the roof, so that Corin had to bend his head. The door was small and low and made of plain boards, unpainted, gone gray with age, with a latch instead of a handle.
It was open.
Not wide. A hand's width. The same as the green door below. And through the gap came light. Warm yellow light. Candlelight. Not one candle. Many. It lay across the bare boards at the top of the stairs in a long thin bright stripe, and touched the toes of his boots.
And with the light came a smell.
Not glue. Not leather. Not lilies.
Paper. Fresh paper and old paper and ink. A great deal of it. The smell of a room so full of paper that the air itself had gone dry and soft and papery, like the inside of an old book.
And something else, under it. Very faint. So faint he almost did not catch it. Something sweet, and green, and sharp, like crushed leaves.
He had smelled that before. He could not think where.
Corin stood at the top of the stairs, with his head bent under the sloping ceiling, and the stripe of candlelight across his boots, and his heart going so hard he could hear it.
He did not knock.
It seemed to him, afterward, when he wrote it down, that this was the most important thing he did all day. He thought about it for a long time. About knocking. About three knocks, slow, and a long wait, and a door opening on nothing. About how a knock on a door, in this city, had become a thing to be afraid of.
So he did not knock.
He put his bandaged hand flat on the gray boards of the door, and pushed it, gently, and it swung open on its leather hinges without a sound.
And he went in.
