Chapter 20: The Shadow in Gray, Part 3 of 5: The Room of Small Black Books
The Room of Small Black Books
The first thing he saw was the books.
He could not have helped it. They were everywhere. The attic was long and low and narrow, running the whole depth of the house, from the front to the back, under the slope of the roof. On both sides the ceiling came down at a steep angle, almost to the floor, following the slates. And on both sides, from the floor to as high as a hand could reach, someone had built shelves. Rough ones. Plain pine planks, unpainted, held up on iron brackets screwed into the rafters. They followed the slope of the roof, so that each one was a little shorter than the one below it, like the steps of a stair going up into the dark.
And on every shelf, side by side, packed tight, spine to spine, small black books.
Hundreds of them.
They were all the same. That was what made it so strange. Not a library. Not a collection, the way rich men collect books, with every one a different size and color and shape. These were all the same. Small black notebooks, about the size of a man's hand, with plain black covers, and a black ribbon to mark the place. The kind you could buy for a copper at any stationer's in the city. The kind the Ministry gave its clerks. The kind she had held in her gloved hand in the Third Basement, a few days ago, and opened at the front to show him something brown.
Rows and rows and rows of them. Going away down both sides of the long low room into the candlelight. Each one with a small white paper label pasted on its spine, and on each label, in black ink, in a small, neat, upright hand, a number, and a date.
The candles were everywhere too. On the shelves, between the books, in saucers and jars and old tin lids. On the floor. On the windowsill. On a table at the far end. Twenty, perhaps. Thirty. Short and stubby, most of them, the cheap tallow kind that smoke and smell of mutton fat, burned down to the last inch. Their light was warm and yellow and wavering, and it moved on the black spines of the books, so that the shelves seemed to breathe.
High up in the slope of the roof, there was a window. A small square skylight, set in among the slates, with frost on the glass in feathers and ferns. Through it, the white sky. It was the only daylight in the room.
And under the skylight, at the far end, at a small square table with a candle on it, sitting very straight on a wooden chair with her hands flat on the table in front of her, was Wren Halloway.
She had taken off the gray cloak. It hung on a nail on the wall behind her, by the hood.
Under it, she was not wearing the white coat. He had half expected the white coat. The high collar, the silver thread, the open eye. Instead she wore a plain dark gray dress, high at the neck, with long sleeves and a row of small black buttons down the front, the kind of dress a schoolmistress might wear, or a lady's maid on her day off. Her gloves were off. They lay on the table beside her right hand, neatly, one on top of the other.
Her hands were small and pale and very clean. Long fingers. Short nails. There was a ridge of hard yellow skin on the inside of the middle finger of her right hand, where a pen had rested for years and years.
She had put them flat on the table on purpose. He understood that at once. Where he could see them. Both of them. Nothing in them.
Her hair was the same. Brown, pulled back so tight from her face that it looked painted on, and twisted into a hard knot at the back of her neck, and pinned. Her face was the same. Pale, sharp, careful. The long nose. The narrow chin. The dark straight eyebrows, like two strokes of a pen. But in the candlelight, without the white coat, without the high collar, without the street and the frost and the cool polite voice, it looked younger than he had expected. And more tired. There were dark marks under her eyes, like bruises. Her lips were chapped. She looked as though she had not slept.
She was on the Dye-Works roof at dawn, he thought. In the cold. Writing.
She looked at him across the long low room. At the bandage on his hand. At the coal dust still in the creases of his coat. At his face.
"You didn't knock," she said.
Her voice was the same too. Clear and cool and quiet, like well water. But there was something under it now that had not been there in the Third Basement. Something that shook, very slightly, the way water shakes in a glass when a cart goes by in the street.
"No," said Corin.
"Why not?"
He thought about it.
"I didn't want you to be afraid," he said.
Something happened in her face. It was there and gone so fast that he almost missed it. Like the bird flying past the window, in the Third Basement. Like the wren going out through the roof of the arcade.
"Shut the door," she said. "Please. There's a draft. It makes the candles smoke."
He shut the door.
There was another chair. One. A plain wooden kitchen chair, like hers, standing in the middle of the floor between the shelves, facing the table. About ten paces from her. It had been put there. It had not been there by chance. Its back was to the door.
"Sit down," said Wren. "If you like. Or stand. I don't mind which."
Corin looked at the chair. Then he looked at her.
Then he took the chair by its back and turned it round, slowly, so that it faced the table side on, and he could see the door from it as well as her. And he sat down.
She watched him do it. And the almost-smile came, very faint, at one corner of her chapped mouth.
"Yes," she said. "That's what I'd have done."
"Who taught you?"
"You know who taught me."
"I want to hear you say it."
The almost-smile went away.
"Inquisitor Kross," said Wren Halloway. "Silas Kross. From when I was nine years old." She looked down at her hands on the table. "He taught me the circle. Three lefts. Windows and water and the brass on doors. Never run. He taught me to sit with my back to a wall and my face to a door. He taught me to put my hands where people can see them when I want them to trust me." She lifted her eyes again. "Which is what I'm doing now. In case you were wondering."
"I was."
"I know." She did not move her hands. "He taught me everything I know, Harvester. Everything. How to read a room. How to read a face. How to follow a man across a city without him knowing, and how to follow him across a city so that he does know, and wonders why. How to take a memory out of someone with a needle so cleanly that they never even feel it go." Her voice did not change. "He is the best teacher I have ever had. He is the only teacher I have ever had."
"And you led me here."
"Yes."
"Why?"
She did not answer at once. She looked past him. At the shelves. At the long rows of small black books going away down both sides of the room into the candlelight, with their white labels, their numbers, their dates.
"Because I can't come to you," she said at last. "Not again. Not like I did in the Third Basement. I was lucky that day. Brack was busy. Nobody saw me on the stairs." She paused. "They watch me now. Since Thursday. I don't know who. Somebody. When I go out of the east tower, somebody is always on the corner. When I come home, somebody is always on the bridge." Her mouth tightened. "I'm good, Harvester. But so are they. I can lose them for an afternoon. I can't lose them for a week."
"So you followed me instead."
"I was told to follow you." Her eyes came back to his. "That's the joke. That's the whole joke. He told me to. On Thursday. Watch our forgetful friend, Wren. Watch who he talks to. Write it all down for me in your lovely clear hand." She said it in a different voice. Light and pleasant and a little bored. It was so exactly the voice in the yard last night, the voice behind the white face at the grating, leave it, there's a good fellow, that Corin's skin crawled. "So nobody thinks it's strange when I'm on a roof above you, or in a church door behind you. It's my job. I'm doing my job." Her hands pressed flatter on the table. "And if I happen to lead you, while I'm doing it, somewhere they can't see—"
"They can't see here?"
"Nobody knows about here." She said it very simply. "Nobody in the world. Not Kross. Not Brack. Not anyone at the Ministry. Mr. Ambrose downstairs thinks I'm a governess with a sick mother in the country, and I go to her on weekdays. He's been my landlord for three years. He's never been up the stairs. He can't. His knees." She almost smiled again. "I pay him in advance. In cash. Every quarter. And every quarter I come here a different way. Over a different bridge. On a different day."
She looked round the long low room. At the books.
"This is the only place in the city that's mine," she said. "The only place. And I've just let you into it."
Corin did not say anything for a while.
The candles moved on the black spines of the books. Far below, on the second floor, the bad flute played its five notes, and stopped, and started again. Somewhere out in the city, a bell rang the quarter.
"What are they?" he said at last. "The books."
Wren Halloway looked at him across the table. For a long moment, she did not answer. And he saw that her hands, flat on the table, so carefully still, so carefully where he could see them, had begun, very slightly, to shake.
"They're people," she said.
So They Are Not Lost
She got up from the table.
She did it slowly, so that he could see every movement. She did not reach for anything. She did not go near the cloak on its nail, or the drawer in the table, or anywhere a knife might be. She walked to the nearest shelf on the left, the lowest one, just behind her chair, where the slope of the roof came down almost to the floor, and she bent, and ran her finger along the spines of the small black books, reading the white labels, until she found the one she wanted.
She took it out. She brought it back to the table. She put it down in front of him, in the light of the candle, and sat down again in her chair, and put her hands flat on the table.
"Open it," she said. "Anywhere."
Corin looked at the label on the spine first. No. 1. Third month. Year of the Long Frost.
Four years ago. Nearly five.
He opened it.
The pages were thin and cheap and gone a little yellow at the edges. They were covered in writing. Small, neat, upright writing, in black ink, very even, every line the same distance from the next. The writing of someone who had been taught to write by someone who hated untidiness. It went right to the edges of the pages. No margins. No spaces. Not a word wasted.
At the top of the first page was a heading, underlined twice.
Ellis Marchbanks. Carter. Aged about fifty. 4 Shoe Lane, by the Fish Steps. Wife, Dora. Three daughters, grown. A gray horse called Duchess.
And under it:
Taken: 3rd of the month, the 4th bell of the afternoon, the yard behind the Customs House. By order of Insp. Kross. Witnessed by Insp. Brack. Reason given: "carried a sealed letter he should not have carried and read the address."
What was taken: the address on the letter, and the day he carried it, and the face of the man who gave it to him. ALSO (by mistake, my needle slipped, it was my first) the smell of the stables at Shoe Lane on a wet morning, and the sound of Duchess eating, and the way his youngest daughter used to sit on the edge of the water trough and swing her legs when she was small, and call him "Da" with two a's, "Daa."
I did not mean to take that. I am writing it down here so that it is not lost. Ellis Marchbanks, his youngest daughter, on the water trough, "Daa." Her name was Bessie. She had a blue ribbon. She had a gap in her front teeth. It was a summer evening and the yard smelled of hay and he was very happy.
I am sorry, Mr. Marchbanks.
Corin stopped reading.
He looked up. Wren was not looking at him. She was looking at the candle on the table, at its small steady flame, with her pale careful face quite still.
"Go on," she said. "Read another."
He turned the page.
Liddy Pruitt. Laundress. Aged about thirty. Back room over the Blue Boar, Tanner's Row.
Taken: what she saw from her window on the night of the 9th.
What she saw: a black carriage, no lamps, by the tannery wall. Two men getting out. A third man, in chains, being put in. She knew him. She called his name out of the window. "Danny!" He looked up.
She does not remember any of this now. She will never know why her hands shake when a carriage goes by at night. His name was Danny Fowle. He was a printer's devil at the Inkwell. He looked up.
And the next.
Thomas Harker, called Tom. Boy. Aged about eleven. No home. Sleeps under the Coopers' Arch.
Taken: a face. A tall woman in white who gave him a silver penny to take a note to a door in the High Quarter and wait for the answer.
What else went with it, because he is young and it was all wound together: his mother's voice, saying his name. I could not separate them. I tried. Kross said leave it, he won't miss it, they never miss the ones they're too young to keep.
He will miss it. His mother's voice. She said "Tommy" like a question, going up at the end. She was from the coast.
Corin's hand had stopped on the page.
Like a question. Going up at the end.
He did not say anything. He turned the page. And another. And another. Every page was the same. A name. An address. A trade. A date and a bell and a place. By order of Insp. Kross. What was taken, and why. And then, under that, in the same small neat even hand, all the rest. Everything else that had come out with it, on the needle, the way the roots of a weed come up with the soil still on them. A mother's voice. A dog's name. The taste of a pear. A song sung at a wedding. A brother's laugh. The color of a sky. Written down. Every one. As carefully as if it were the most important thing in the world. And at the bottom of each entry, sometimes, not always, in the same hand, one line.
I am sorry.
He closed the book.
He sat with his hand flat on its black cover, the way he sat with his hand on the Ledger sometimes, at night, when he could not sleep. And he looked down the long low room, at the shelves. At the rows and rows of small black books, going away down both sides into the candlelight, with their white labels.
"How many?" he said.
"Books?"
"People."
Wren was quiet for a moment.
"Two thousand, one hundred and six," she said. "As of Friday."
Corin did not say anything.
"Not all mine," said Wren. "Not all of them. Most. The first four hundred, perhaps, are all mine. Then I started—" She stopped. Started again. "Then I started asking. In the vault. When jars came in from other Inquisitors, from Brack, from the others. I'd be on the vault desk sometimes, on the night watch, signing them in. And I'd read the labels. And I'd write down what was on them. Just the name and the address. And what it said. If it said." She looked at the shelves. "And then I started going to see them."
"The people?"
"Some of them. When I could. When I had an afternoon." Her voice was very even. "I'd find the address. I'd stand across the street. I'd watch them. Sometimes I'd go and buy something from them, if they had a shop or a stall. A cabbage. A pair of laces. And I'd talk to them a little. About the weather. About nothing. And then I'd come home and write down what I'd seen." She paused. "Whether they were all right. Whether they were sad. Whether there was a gap. Sometimes you can see it, in people. A gap. Like a tooth that's been pulled."
Elara's gold tooth, thought Corin suddenly. Capped over an old gap. It never grew in. He did not know why he thought it. He pushed it away.
"Why?" he said.
Wren looked at him.
For a long time, she did not answer. The candles burned. The flute stopped, two floors below, and did not start again. The light from the skylight had begun to change. Going from white to a very pale gold, as the short winter afternoon began, already, to tip over toward evening.
"Because they're not mine," she said at last.
Her voice had gone very quiet.
"Because I take them," she said. "Every week. Every day, some weeks. I put the needle in, and I draw it out, and the thing comes out on the end of it like a thread, all bright and alive and wriggling, and I put it in a jar, and I seal the jar, and I write the label, and I carry it to the vault under the east tower and I put it on a shelf. And it's gone. It's gone out of them. Forever. And they don't even know it's gone. That's the worst part. They don't know. They can't miss it. You can't miss a thing you don't remember having."
She looked down at her hands.
"But somebody has to know," she said. "Somebody has to know it was there. That it was real. That it was theirs. That Ellis Marchbanks had a daughter called Bessie with a blue ribbon who sat on the water trough and said Daa. That a boy called Tommy had a mother from the coast." Her voice cracked, very slightly, on the last word. She pressed her lips together until it stopped. "If nobody knows, then it's like it never happened. Like they never had it at all. Like they were always—"
She stopped.
"Empty," said Corin.
"Yes."
"So you keep it."
"I keep the knowing of it." She lifted her chin. "That's all. I can't keep the thing itself. That goes in the jar. That goes to the vault. That goes to—wherever it goes after that. I don't know where. They don't tell us." A pause. "But I keep the knowing. The name. The address. What it was. So that one day, if anyone ever finds a way to—"
She stopped again. Harder. Like a man walking into a wall in the dark.
Corin knew that stop. He had heard it yesterday, on a barrel at the end of Pike Dock, from a big man in a small green coat. Maybe one day somebody'll come along who knows how to— And then the wall. No. That's daft. Nobody can put it back. Everybody knows that.
"To put it back," he said.
Wren looked at him. Her eyes were very bright in the candlelight.
"Nobody can put it back," she said. "Everybody knows that."
"Yes," said Corin. "Everybody knows that."
They looked at each other across the little table.
He did not tell her about Briggs. About the tin of yellow slips, and the four hundred and twelve tiny jars on their pine shelves in a cellar behind a green door with three bolts. He wanted to. He wanted to very much. It would have been a kind of gift, he thought. To tell her that she was not the only one. That there was a man in this city who had done the same thing she had done, for eight years, every Tuesday, two coppers at a time, for no reason that made any sense, except that he could not bear to let it go.
But he did not tell her.
Because she was Kross's. And because he did not know, yet, which of the four things she was. A message. A trap. Someone who wanted to. Someone who could not help it.
And because Fenn Briggs had said don't let them see you, and Corin had promised.
So instead he said, "It's like my Ledger."
Wren's head came up.
"I write everything down," said Corin. "Because I lose it. All of it. Every night. You know that. Everybody knows that." He put his hand flat on his coat, over the Ledger. "So I write it down, so I'll know in the morning that it happened. That it was real." He looked at the shelves. "You do it for them. What I do for me."
Wren Halloway did not say anything for a long time.
Then she said, very quietly, "I know."
"You know?"
"I've read it." She did not look away. "Your Ledger. Some of it. Not the one you're carrying. The old ones. The six." A pause. "A week ago, a lady we both know sent her maid to a pen shop on Quill Row. At closing time. In a dark cloak, with the hood up. To ask the big man behind the counter for Mister Vane's old books." Her mouth twisted. "And the big man said he'd never heard of you. With your name chalked a foot high on the board behind him. And the lady told Kross, over tea, and laughed about it. And Kross smiled and turned to me and said, Of course he's heard of him, Wren dear. Nobody who'd never heard of him would lie so badly. Pop round, would you? At night. Quietly. Before she does."
She looked down at her hands.
"So I went," she said. "On Friday night. Over the roofs. Through the back window. There's a strongbox under the floor of his back room, under a loose board by the stove. And in it, six books in brown cloth coats, with Harbour Tide Tables pressed on the spines." The almost-smile came and went. "The cloth was still new. It smelled of glue. I sat on the floor by the light of a shuttered lamp and read your Ledgers for two hours. And then I put them back exactly where I found them, in their coats, and the board back over them. And I went home. And I came here."
Corin had gone very cold.
"And I told Kross there was nothing there," said Wren. "I told him Bram must have burned them, or sold them. I told him I'd searched the whole shop, and the strongbox, and found nothing but tide tables." She lifted her eyes to his. "Which was true. In a way." A pause. "It's the first lie I ever told him. In thirteen years."
The candles moved on the black books.
"Why?" said Corin.
"Because on page nine of the first one," said Wren, "in your hand, from six years ago, when you didn't know your own name yet, there's a line of notes. Drawn. Like a little hill. Going up, and up, and up. And then nothing. The pencil just stops." Her voice had gone thin. "And under it, you'd written, I don't know where this comes from. It isn't finished. Somebody else knows the end of it. Find her."
