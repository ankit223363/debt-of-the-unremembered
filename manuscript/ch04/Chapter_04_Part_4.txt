Chapter 4: A Bargain in Bad Light, Part 4 of 5: The Bargain
The Bargain
The rooster finally got it right. Its cry came thin and cracked over the rooftops, and somewhere a dog answered it, and somewhere else a baby began to cry. Hook Lane was waking up.
Inside the shop, nobody moved for a while.
Vespera had lain back on her cot with one arm over her eyes. She was not asleep. Corin could tell by her breathing, which was too even, like someone counting. Lyra sat on the stool with her father's letter folded in her lap. The hidden words had sunk all the way back into the paper now. It was just a letter again. Weather. Roof. Coal.
Corin sat in the chair. His arm ached where Vespera had worked on it. The new line was red and raised, and when he looked at it, the words seemed to swim a little before they settled. You are not Jory Kestle. Under it, all the older lines ran down his arm like the rungs of a ladder. He did not like to look at them for long. It was like reading a list of all the people who had tried to move into his head and been thrown out.
He opened his Ledger on his knee. He turned past the page with the red curtain and the woman humming, past his notes from Pell's body, past the sketch he had made of the gray gloves on the thug at the Inkwell. He came to a clean page.
At the top, out of habit, he wrote the date. Then, under it, he wrote: What I know.
He sat looking at that for a long time.
"You do that a lot," said Lyra.
He looked up. She was watching him again. In the gray light her face looked older than it had at the Inkwell. There were shadows under her eyes, and a small cut on her chin from the fight that he had not noticed before.
"Do what?"
"Write things down. Every time something happens, you take out that book."
"It's my job."
"It's not your job," she said. "Your job is to catch people. Writing things down is something else."
Corin closed the book halfway, then opened it again. "I harvest ash," he said. "From people who've burned too much. Every time I do it, a little of what I take gets into me. Most of it goes into the rod. But some of it sticks. If I don't write down what's mine, I won't know, after a while, what was mine and what was theirs."
He had never said it so plainly to anyone. He was not sure why he was saying it now. Maybe because she had sat in that same room and watched Vespera draw Kettle out of his arm with clear ink. Maybe because she had lost things too, flutes and harps and a whole second language, and she had not cried about any of it.
Lyra looked at the book. Then at his arm. Then back at the book.
"So it's a map," she said. "Of you."
"Something like that."
"What does it say? On that page. What I know."
Corin looked down. Under the heading there was nothing at all.
"That's the trouble," he said.
She almost laughed. It came out as a short breath through her nose. Then she was quiet, and he could see her thinking. She had a way of thinking that showed on her face. Her eyes went a little to the side, and her lips pressed together, and one finger tapped on the folded letter in her lap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
"All right," she said at last. "Then let's fill it."
"What?"
"The page. Let's fill it. Together. You write what you know and I'll say what I know, and we'll see what we've got."
On the cot, Vespera lifted her arm from her eyes and looked at them both. She did not say anything. She only watched, the way an old cat watches two young ones that have not decided yet whether to fight or play.
Corin dipped his pen.
"Pell Dorran," he said, and wrote it. "Grade Three Scribe. First Basement of the Ministry of Records. Found dead on a rubbish heap in Gutter Lane, behind the Inkwell, on Tuesday morning. Emptied. Not burned out. Not like a mage who has spent too much. Emptied, like someone poured him into a cup. And the smell of lilies all over him."
Lyra's finger stopped tapping.
"Lilies," she said.
"Your father's letter said the same."
"Do not trust anyone who smells of lilies. She was there." Lyra said the words as if she were reading them off the inside of her own eyelids. "She wore white then, too."
Corin wrote: Lilies. A woman. White. Was at Oakhaven. Then he drew a line from Lilies up to Pell.
"Pell left a note," he went on. "In his own head. I found it when I harvested him. Red book, the drawer, the Q. That's how I got to the Inkwell. And when I got there, you were already in the drawer."
"Because Old Joss sent for me." Lyra lifted the letter a little, then put it down again. "A girl brought me a note at dusk. She came down off the stable roof as if it were a staircase, bold as a cat. The note was from him. It said a book with my father's seal had been left in his Q drawer by a man who wasn't coming back, and that a Thorne should fetch it before someone worse did. I didn't tell anyone. Not even Rowan." She swallowed. "I didn't know who Pell was. I didn't know he was dead. I just went, because I finally couldn't stand it anymore."
"Couldn't stand what?"
She looked at him as if he were very slow.
"My father," she said, "used to be the cleverest man in Veridia. He sat on the High Council for nineteen years. He could talk for an hour without notes and make the whole Council laugh and then make them cry and then make them vote the way he wanted. He knew the name of every servant in our house and the names of their children. He taught me chess." Her voice was very steady, too steady, the way a glass is steady just before it cracks. "Now he sits in two small rooms over a livery stable on Linden Walk, and he looks out at the yard, and he smiles. He's always smiling. He doesn't know my name. He doesn't know his own. Two years ago he sat down in his study and burned everything at once, in one spell. The doctors say men do that sometimes, when they're tired of carrying themselves. They say it was his choice."
"You don't think it was his choice."
"I think," said Lyra, "that a man who folds his secrets inside himself and hides a copy in a clock and writes letters in sleeping ink is not a man who just gets tired one night and throws himself away."
Corin wrote: Lord Aurelian Thorne. Burned himself out? Or was burned? FOLD. Clock in the long gallery. Twelve stones. Witness K.
"Except the clock isn't in the long gallery," said Lyra.
Corin looked up.
"There is no long gallery. Not for us. We sold the house, the first winter, and everything in it that would fetch a price. The silver. The paintings. The chairs. The clock went with the rest." Her mouth twisted. "My father's old steward handled it. Some pawnbroker in the Ink Quarter, I think, or the Docks. I didn't ask which. I didn't want to know who was buying our grandfather's clock for the price of a winter's coal."
"Then it could be anywhere."
"It could be in some merchant's hall, ticking away, with the truth inside it and nobody knowing." She pressed her lips together. "Or it could be smashed for the brass. Write that too."
Corin wrote: Clock sold. First winter. Pawnbroker? Find it.
He looked at the page. It was filling up now. Names. Lines between names. Questions.
"Oakhaven," he said, and wrote it, and drew a box around it. "Pell said the word once at a harvest feast and then said he made it up. Your father wrote it in the letter. Sixty children. Seven traces."
"And two of them were—" said Lyra.
"And then the stone went out."
"And then the stone went out," she agreed.
They both looked at the page.
"And the stones," said Lyra quietly. "The ones in your pocket."
Corin hesitated. Then he wrote, very small, in the bottom corner: U.W. Three. Sewn shut. From Pell's drawer. He did not write the words that Vespera had not let them say.
On the cot, Vespera grunted. It might have been approval.
Corin set down the pen and looked at what they had made. It was a mess. Arrows going every way. Words crossed out and written again. But it was more than he had known yesterday. And it was more than either of them could have done alone.
"So," said Lyra. "What do you want?"
"What?"
"Out of all this. What do you want? I know what I want. I want to know who did this to my father. And why. And I want to find that clock before anyone else does, because if my father hid the truth inside it, then somebody is going to come looking. Maybe they already have." She tapped the letter. "That's what I want. What about you?"
Corin thought about it. It was strange. Nobody had asked him that in a long time. The Section Chief did not ask. The Section Chief gave orders. Kettle, last night, had not asked. Kettle had only wanted his body. Malakor did not ask. Malakor only wanted to be let out, one day, somehow.
"I want whoever killed Pell," he said at last. "He was a clerk. He sorted files. He hummed while he worked. He never hurt anyone. Somebody poured him out like dishwater and left him on a rubbish heap with flowers on his coat." His hand had closed on the pen without his meaning it to. He made it open. "I want them. And I want to know what Oakhaven was. Because I think—"
He stopped.
"You think what?"
"Nothing."
"Corin."
He looked at the bare place over his heart. He could not see it through his shirt, but he knew it was there. The space he had never let Vespera touch, not once in six years. The one faded word. Elara.
That's taken, he had told her, every time, without knowing why.
"I think it has something to do with me," he said. "I don't know how. I just do."
Lyra did not laugh at him. She did not ask him to explain. She only nodded, slowly, as if that was a perfectly good reason.
"Then here's the trouble," she said. "You're a Ministry man. Some sort of clerk, if I read your badge right."
"Senior Investigator. Harvesting Section."
"Senior Investigator. You can go into the Low Quarter and the Ink Quarter and the docks. You can knock on any door in Tallow Street and they have to let you in. But you can't go up the Hill. Not without a writ from the Council. And the Council isn't going to give you one, not to poke around in House Thorne. Not to poke around anywhere near the people who would know about Oakhaven." She lifted her chin. "I can. I can walk into any house on the Hill. I can sit at their dinners and dance at their balls. I can get you into the Council galleries and half the drawing rooms on the Hill. They still let a Thorne in at the front door, even a poor one. They like to watch."
"And you need me for what?"
"For that." She pointed at the rod in its case at his hip. "I can't harvest. Nobody in my family can. If there's anything left in my father, anything at all, any little bit of ash where his memories used to be, you're the only one who can get it out. And if Pell left more in his head than one note, you're the only one who can read it." She sat back. "You can't get in without me. I can't get anything out without you."
Corin looked at her for a long moment.
She was right. He knew she was right. He had known it, really, since the Inkwell, since the moment he had seen her standing in the dark with the red book held tight against her chest and her chin up, telling Tull and his Black Bank men that they had no business in a Thorne's affairs. He had known it, and he had not wanted to know it, because only a few hours ago a dead boxer called Kettle had tried to take his body for his own, and because there was a line on the rules page of his Ledger, in his own hand, that said Work alone. The Ash is company enough.
She's not Kettle, he told himself.
Nobody's Kettle, until they are, said a small cold voice at the back of his mind. It sounded a little like his own. He pushed it away.
"All right," he said.
Lyra blinked. She had clearly been ready to argue for longer.
"All right?"
"All right. We work together. For now."
"Not so fast."
It was Vespera. She had sat up on her cot. Her gray hair stood out around her head like smoke, and her eyes were sharp and awake in a way that made Corin think she had not been resting at all.
"If you two are going to do this," she said, "in my shop, with my ink on his arm and my lamp burned up on her father's letter, then you'll do it with some rules. Or you'll do it somewhere else."
"What rules?" asked Lyra.
Vespera held up one finger. It was stained blue at the tip, a stain so old it had gone into the skin.
"One. No lies between you. Not big ones. Little ones, fine, everybody tells little ones. But you, boy, don't keep things from her that could get her killed. And you, girl, don't keep things from him that could get him lost."
Corin felt the rod at his hip and thought of the voice inside it. He said nothing. Vespera's eyes rested on him a moment longer than they needed to. He had the feeling that she knew. That she had always known.
She held up a second finger.
"Two. Nobody burns for the other one. Not without asking first. I've seen what happens when people start spending themselves for each other. It starts sweet. It always ends with somebody who doesn't remember why they're there."
Lyra opened her mouth. Closed it. Nodded.
A third finger.
"Three. Those stones stay sewn. Nobody opens that cloth until you know exactly what you're doing and exactly why. And when that day comes, if it comes, you come here first and you tell me."
"Why you?" said Corin.
"Because I'm the only one in this city who'll tell you not to," said Vespera. "And mean it."
She looked from one of them to the other. Then she held up a fourth finger, and her voice went softer.
"Four. If one of you starts to forget the other, the other one has to say so. Out loud. Even if it hurts. Especially if it hurts."
The shop was very quiet.
"That's all," said Vespera. She lay back down and put her arm over her eyes again. "Shake on it, or get out of my shop. I'm too old to watch young people look at each other this long."
Lyra's cheeks went pink. Corin looked at the floor.
Then Lyra stood. She wiped her right hand on her skirt, which was a strange thing for a High Quarter lady to do, and he thought she had probably not done it in years. She held the hand out to him across the worktable.
"Partners," she said.
Corin stood too. His legs were stiff from the chair. He looked at her hand. It was small and pale, with a scratch across the back from the Inkwell and calluses on the fingertips that had once been from harp strings and were now from nothing at all. There was no ink on it. No words. No lines. Not a single mark of anything she had ever been or lost.
Thornes don't wear their secrets on their skin.
He took it. Her grip was firmer than he expected. She shook once, hard, like a merchant closing a deal, and let go.
"Partners," he said.
He sat back down and opened his Ledger to the clean half of the page under What I know, and he wrote one word there, in capitals, and drew a line under it.
PARTNER.
Then, after a moment, he wrote her name beside it. Lyra Thorne. So that whatever happened, whatever he burned or harvested or lost, there would be one page in the world that said he had chosen this.
Lyra leaned over to see. She read the word and the name. She did not say anything. But when she sat back down on her stool, she was almost smiling, and she held her father's letter a little less tightly than before.
