Chapter 4: A Bargain in Bad Light, Part 1 of 5: One Word
Arc 1: The Siphon's Ledger.
One Word
Vespera did not answer at once.
She stood in the middle of her small shop, under the slow silver rain of hanging needles, with her writing-covered arms folded and her plain wide face perfectly still, and looked at Lyra Thorne. The lamp in the window behind her threw her shadow across the floorboards, long and square, and through the colored bottles on the glass shelves, so that the shadow had little spots of red and green and gold in it, like a stained window.
"Because that was the word," she said.
Lyra's hand stayed where it was, flat against the front of her silk coat, over the red book.
"What word?"
"The word I wrote on your father." Vespera unfolded one arm and held up her own wrist, the left one, and turned it so that the inside faced Lyra. There was writing there, as there was writing everywhere on her, small and neat and close. She pointed with one blunt finger to a place just below the heel of the hand, where the skin was thin and the blue veins showed through. "Here. Where nobody would see unless they turned his hand over and looked. One word. Four letters. In ink so fine and so pale you'd think it was a vein."
She lowered her hand.
"FOLD," she said.
The shop was very quiet.
Somewhere at the back, a kettle on the stove had begun to tick as it warmed. The needles turned on their threads in the draft from the door, chiming very faintly, high and silver, like ice in a glass. Outside, down at the quay at the bottom of Hook Lane, the little boats bumped against each other in the brown water, tock, tock.
Lyra did not move. Corin, standing beside her, saw her face. He saw it go white, and then whiter, the way paper goes white when you hold it too close to a candle, just before it catches.
"That's my mother's word," she whispered.
Vespera's sharp dark eyes did not change. But something in the set of her square shoulders did. It loosened, very slightly, the way a knot loosens when you stop pulling on it.
"Is it now," she said quietly.
Twenty Years
"Sit down," said Vespera. "Both of you. Before one of you falls and breaks my good bottles."
She did not wait to see if they obeyed. She turned and went to the stove at the back of the shop, a squat black iron thing with a pipe that went up through the ceiling, and lifted the ticking kettle off the hot plate, and poured water from it into a fat brown teapot. Steam rose. A smell came out of the pot, strong and dark and bitter, with something sweet behind it. Not tea, exactly. Something like tea.
Lyra sat down on a wooden stool by the counter. She sat very straight, with her hands in her lap, the left one pressed flat on top of the right. Corin stood for a moment. Then his legs decided for him, the way Lyra's had on the river steps, and he sat down too.
He sat in the big leather chair.
He had not meant to. He had meant to sit on the floor, or on the other stool. But his body turned, and lowered itself, and the chair took him. It tipped back a little under his weight with a soft creak, exactly as far as he had known it would. His left hand found the arm and gripped it, and his fingers found the place where the leather was cracked and worn through, and fitted into it.
It was the most comfortable thing he had felt in two days.
Vespera came back with three cups. Thick, plain, heavy cups of brown clay, with no handles, hot to hold. She put one into Lyra's hands, and closed Lyra's fingers round it with her own, briefly, the way you would with a child. She put the second on the arm of Corin's chair, beside his gripping hand. She kept the third. She did not sit down. She stood in the middle of the shop with her cup in both hands and looked at Lyra over the top of it, and the steam curled up past her plain wide face.
"It was twenty years ago," she said. "Near enough. Autumn. Late. Long past midnight. I was younger then. I had a shop in the Ink Quarter, a better one than this, on Quill Street, with a sign over the door and a girl to sweep the floor. I'd been an Ink-Binder thirty years. I thought I'd seen everything." She drank. "He came on his own. No carriage. No servant. In an old brown coat that didn't fit him, with the collar turned up and his hat pulled down. As if he thought that would hide what he was."
"What was he?" said Lyra.
"A lord." Vespera snorted softly. "You can put a lord in a brown coat. You can't make him walk like a porter. He came in through my door like he owned the street and then stood in the middle of my floor not knowing where to put his hands." She was quiet for a moment. "And he was frightened. More frightened than anyone I'd ever had in my chair. He'd been somewhere. Seen something. He had it on him, the way you can smell smoke on a man who's been near a fire. He couldn't sit still. He kept looking at the door."
She looked at the door herself, as she said it. At the dark wood, and the faded silver needle painted on it, and the gray dawn growing behind the thick green glass of the window.
"He didn't give me his name," she said. "He didn't need to. He had a ring on his hand. A signet. A rose in thorns. He turned it round so the face was against his palm, but I'd seen it." She drank again. "He said, I want one word. Small. Where nobody will see it. In ink that won't fade. And I said, what word? And he said, fold."
"Just that?"
"Just that. I asked him what it meant. People usually tell you. They want you to understand, so you'll write it properly. He didn't. He said it was something his wife used to say. He said—" Vespera stopped. Her sharp dark eyes went very far away for a moment, as if she were reading something written a long way off. "He said, if I ever forget everything else, I want to remember that I folded something away. So I know to look for it."
Lyra's cup had stopped halfway to her mouth.
"He said that," she said. "Those words."
"Those words." Vespera nodded slowly. "I remember them because I wrote them down. Here." She turned her right arm over, and ran one blunt finger down a line of tiny faded writing near the elbow, among a hundred others. "I write down everyone. Every arm I've ever inked. Every name I've ever put on anybody. On myself, so I won't lose them." Her finger stopped. "Rose in thorns. One word, left wrist. Fold. 'So I know to look for it.' And a date."
Corin leaned forward a little in the leather chair to see. The writing was so small and so faded that it was almost gone, just a pale blue-gray shadow on the brown skin. But he could see it. And the date, at the end.
It was twenty years ago. Autumn. Three days, he thought, counting back from something he did not know he knew, after a column of gray soldiers had marched into a green valley that was not on any map.
He did not know why he thought that.
The Wife
"I didn't know him," said Lyra. Her voice had cracked again. "I mean—I didn't know him like that. When I was a child. I didn't know he could be frightened." She looked down into her cup. "He was always so—" She searched for the word. "Straight. Like a pillar. He stood up very straight in the Council and said what he thought and didn't care who heard. Everyone said so. People were a little afraid of him."
"People who stand very straight," said Vespera, "usually do it because they've got something on their backs they're trying not to drop."
Lyra looked up at her.
"You said fold was your mother's word," said the Ink-Binder. "Tell me."
And Lyra told her.
Not all of it. Not the room with the window over the roses, and the gold light, and the needle and the hoop and the pale clever fingers closing over hers. She could not tell that anymore. She did not have it. She sat on the stool with her cup of not-quite-tea going cold in her hands and told it the way you tell a story you have only heard from someone else. My mother had a saying. Fold it small. When something is too big to carry, you fold it small, and put it somewhere safe, and carry it anyway. She embroidered it. I embroidered it. On everything. I don't know what it means. I never asked. She said it flatly, carefully, like someone reading from a list. And when she reached the part about the needle, she stopped.
"I can't embroider anymore," she said. "I burned it. Tonight. In the Inkwell. To cut a chain."
Vespera did not say anything. She did not say I'm sorry, or how terrible, or any of the things people say. She only looked at Lyra's left hand, lying on top of the right in her lap, for a long moment. Then back at her face.
"And now," she said, "you can't see your mother's face in the room where she taught you."
Lyra went very still.
"How do you know that?"
"Because that's how it works." Vespera's voice was not unkind. It was flat and plain and certain, the way a doctor's voice is certain when she tells you what is wrong. "You think you burn a skill. A thing you can do. Embroidery. As if it's a coat hanging on a peg, and you take it off the peg and throw it on the fire, and the peg's still there and everything else on the wall is fine." She shook her iron-gray head. "It's not like that. A skill isn't a coat. It's a root. It goes down into everything. Into every room where you learned it. Every face that taught you. Every hour you spent getting it right. You pull up the root, girl, and it brings up the earth with it."
She put down her cup on the counter.
"Your mother's face in that room was tangled up in the needle," she said. "So it went with the needle. That's the price nobody tells you, when they teach you to burn. It's never just the thing you meant."
Lyra looked at her for a long time. Her gray eyes were very bright in the lamplight.
Then she reached into the front of her silk coat, and took out the red book, and held it out across the counter.
"He folded something," she said. "In here. I think. Can you read it?"
Vespera looked at the book. At the soft red leather and the rose wrapped in thorns. She did not take it.
"Not yet," she said. "First things first."
And she turned, and looked at Corin, sitting in the leather chair with his split knuckles and his torn gloves and the blue frost round the edges of his eyes.
"You," she said. "Take off your coat."
The Chair
Corin took off his coat.
It was harder than it should have been. His hands did not want to work. The split knuckles had stiffened while he sat, and the fingers had swollen inside the torn gloves, and every time he bent them a hot, deep ache went up his arms to the elbow. He got the coat off one shoulder, and then the other, and then he sat there holding it in his lap, not sure what to do with it.
Vespera took it from him. She did not ask. She took it the way Bram had taken his Ledger that morning, carefully, the way you take something that belongs to someone else. She felt the pockets as she folded it. Left: keys, and a jar with a brass lid that she held up to the lamp and looked at, frowning, at the thick blue-white mist turning in it. Right: the long narrow pocket with the rod. She did not take the rod out. She put her hand flat on the outside of the pocket, over it, and held it there for a moment, with her head on one side, as if she were listening.
"Still in there, are you, old ghost?" she said softly.
Behind Corin's eyes, very faint, a dry voice said: Tell the ink-woman to mind her own business.
Corin did not tell her. Vespera smiled anyway, a small, grim, crooked smile, as if she had heard.
She hung the coat on a hook by the stove, where it began to steam gently in the warmth. Then she came back, and pulled up the other stool, and sat down on it, right in front of the leather chair, so close that her knees almost touched his. And she took his left hand in both of hers.
"Gloves," she said.
She peeled them off. Slowly. One finger at a time. The gray leather was stuck to the split skin of his knuckles with dried blood, and it pulled as it came away, and Corin hissed through his teeth. Vespera did not stop. She did not hurry, either. She worked the leather free with her blunt strong fingers the way you would work a splinter out, steadily, without fuss, and when it was off she dropped the glove on the counter and looked at his hand.
The knuckles were a mess. Split across, all four, and swollen, and purple-black with bruising. There was dried blood in the creases of his fingers, and some of it was not his.
"Kettle," said Vespera. Not a question.
"Yes."
"Thought so. I'd know that right hand anywhere." She turned his hand over, gently, and looked at the palm, and at the wrist. "I told you, the last time. I told you, put that jar back in the box under the bed and leave it there until you really need it." She looked up at him. "Did you really need it?"
Corin thought of Lyra hanging in the air by her throat, with her mended silk boots kicking, and her face going dark.
"Yes," he said.
Vespera looked at him for a long moment with her sharp dark eyes. Then she nodded, once, and did not say anything more about it.
The Map
She made him take off his shirt, too.
He did not want to. He did not know why. There was a feeling in him, somewhere low and old, like the feeling of being a child at a doctor's, and he did not like it. But Vespera only looked at him, and waited, with her hands in her lap. And after a while, he unbuttoned the gray shirt and pulled it off over his head.
Lyra, on her stool by the counter, made a small sound.
Corin looked down at himself.
He had not known. He had not looked at himself that morning, in the gray light of Room 9. He had pulled his boots on and his coat on and gone out. He had not known what was under his shirt.
It was writing.
His arms were covered in it. Both of them. From the wrists, where the little silver-gray bracelet of words ran round the left one, all the way up to the shoulders. Lines of small, neat, careful words, in black and blue and gray and a faint shimmering silver, running round his forearms in rings, and down the insides of his elbows in columns, and across his upper arms in long, slow, curving lines like the lines of a map. Not his own untidy handwriting. Vespera's. Small and square and steady.
He turned his right arm toward the lamp and read some of it.
You are not Barnaby Fell. Barnaby Fell was a carpenter. He died in a fire on Rope Street. You held his last breath. You are not him.
You are not Annis Wake. You do not have a daughter. You do not need to find her. She is safe. Annis knew that at the end.
You are not Old Tobias. You are not afraid of water.
You are not—
There were dozens of them. Name after name. Each one with a line or two after it. You are not. You are not. You are not. Like the rungs of a ladder going up his arm. Like the marks a prisoner scratches on the wall of his cell, one for every day.
And further up, on his shoulders, and across the top of his chest, more. Different. Not names. Things.
You like strong tea with no sugar.
You do not like lilies. You don't know why. Trust it.
When you can't remember which way is up, lie down on the floor. The floor is always down.
Mrs. Marta's stew is not poisoned. It only tastes like it.
Corin read that one twice. Then, for the second time that day, he laughed. A short, cracked, startled sound.
Vespera's mouth twitched at one corner. "You made me write that one," she said. "You'd had a bad Ash. A cook's. You were sure everybody was trying to poison you. You wouldn't eat for three days."
And in the middle of his chest, right over his heart, there was a space.
An empty space. Bare skin. About the size of two open hands laid side by side. All the writing on his chest and his shoulders curved round it, like water curving round a stone in a stream. None of it went in.
Except one word.
It was so faded he could barely see it. Not Vespera's hand. Not his own. A different hand, very old, very blurred, as if it had been written a long time ago in ink that was never meant to last, and had been fading ever since. A single word, small, in the very center of the bare space, over his heart.
Elara.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
"I didn't write that one," said Vespera quietly. "It was there when you first came to me. Six years ago. You were already a harvester then. You came in off the street in the middle of the night with a dead woman's Ash in you, and you didn't know your own name, and you sat in this chair and I took your shirt off to see what I had to work with. And there it was." She looked at the faded word. "I asked you who wrote it. You didn't know. I asked if you wanted me to go over it, make it darker, so it wouldn't fade any more."
"What did I say?"
"You said no." Vespera's voice was very even. "You said, leave it. That one's taken. You wouldn't let me put anything else there either. Not one word. In six years. Every time, you put your hand over it, like this—" She laid her own broad hand flat on her own chest, over her heart. "And said, not there. That's taken."
Corin looked down at the word. At the bare skin around it. At the writing that curved away from it on every side, respectfully, like people stepping back from a grave.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
He did not know her face. He did not know her voice. He did not know if she was alive or dead or where she had gone or when he had lost her.
But he knew that the space was hers. He knew it the way he knew how far back the chair would tip.
He put his hand over it. Flat. Without thinking.
Vespera watched him do it, and did not say anything at all.
The Stain
"Right," said Vespera, after a while, in a different, brisk, practical voice. "Two dead men and a fight. Let's see what we're dealing with."
She got up and went to the little table by the stove and came back with the tray. The black velvet, with its row of needles from smallest to largest. The tiny pots of ink, a dozen of them, with cork stoppers. A folded white cloth. A small dish of something clear that smelled sharply of cloves. And a candle stub in a brass holder, which she lit from the lamp and set on the arm of the chair by his elbow, so that its small warm light fell on his skin.
She picked up his right arm and turned it to the light and looked at it. Not at the writing. Under it. At the skin itself.
"There," she said.
Corin looked.
At first he could not see anything. Then he could. A shadow. A faint grayish-blue tinge, under the skin of his inner arm, like a bruise that has not quite come up yet. It ran from the inside of his wrist to the bend of his elbow, in a thin, wavering line, following the blue vein. And as he looked at it, it seemed to move. Very slightly. Like smoke under ice.
"That's Kettle," said Vespera. "What's left of him. You gave him back his dying, in the jar. Good. That's the main thing. But you drank him too fast and too deep, without the rod to filter him, and he's left a stain." She dipped a cloth in the clove-smelling dish and wiped the inside of his arm, briskly. It stung. "If I leave it, it'll sink. Into you. Down into the root. In a week you'll find yourself standing differently. In a month you'll be picking fights in taverns and not know why. In a year you'll wake up one morning and look in the mirror and see a broken nose that isn't there."
"Can you get it out?"
"Some of it." She picked up a needle. Not one of the small fine ones. A larger one, hollow, with a tiny glass bulb at the end where a thread would go. "Ink takes a stain. That's the first thing they teach you, as an Ink-Binder. A clean ink, with the right crystal in it, will draw a bit of Ash out of the skin the way a poultice draws poison. Not all. Never all. But enough."
She dipped the hollow needle into one of the tiny pots. The ink in it was clear. Almost clear. Like water with a faint sheen of silver on it. She held the needle up to the candle and looked at it. Then she took his arm firmly in her other hand, just above the wrist, and said, without looking up:
"Thorne girl. If you're going to be sick, the bucket's by the door. If you're going to faint, faint toward the counter, not the shelves. Those bottles cost more than your coat."
Lyra did not faint. She sat on her stool by the counter with her cold cup in her hands and watched, white-faced, with her chin up.
Vespera began.
It did not hurt the way Corin had expected. It was a quick, light, stinging buzz, over and over, like being bitten by a very small, very fast, very determined insect. The needle went in and out, in and out, a hundred times a minute, along the line of the grayish shadow under his skin. And with every touch, the clear silver ink went in, and something else came out. He could see it. A tiny bead of gray-blue mist, rising from each pinprick, no bigger than a grain of sand, and drifting up, and fading. Like steam from a cup. Like breath on a cold morning.
And with each tiny bead of mist, something in him eased.
The heaviness in his shoulders. The weight down low in his knees. The tight, waiting, coiled feeling in his fists. The roaring, far back behind the half-shut door. Bead by bead, it went out of him, up through his skin, into the warm air of the little shop, and away.
He sat back in the leather chair and closed his eyes and let it go.
When he opened them again, some time later, the needle had stopped. Vespera was wiping his arm with the clove-smelling cloth. The gray shadow under the skin was gone. Almost gone. There was only a faint, faint line left, like the mark a pencil leaves when it has been rubbed out. And over it, written into his skin in the clear silver ink, so fine that he could only see it when the candlelight caught it, there was a new line of words.
You are not Jory Kestle. You do not have to win. He was surprised at the end.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
"Thank you," he said.
Vespera did not answer. She was cleaning the hollow needle, carefully, in the dish of clove-smelling liquid, with her head bent. And as she bent, and her sleeve fell back a little further from her own left wrist, Corin saw something.
On the inside of her wrist, among the hundred tiny lines of names and dates, there was one line that was different. It was not finished. It began in neat, dark, careful letters, like all the others. And then it stopped. Halfway through a word. As if the needle had been lifted away in the middle of a stroke and never put back.
HOLL—
And nothing after.
Vespera saw him looking. She did not say anything. She pulled her sleeve down over her wrist, slowly, deliberately, and went on cleaning the needle.
But her hands, Corin noticed, were not quite as steady as they had been.
