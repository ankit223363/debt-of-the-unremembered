Chapter 4: A Bargain in Bad Light, Part 5 of 5: The First Page
The First Page
For a while after that, nobody said much.
Vespera dozed, or pretended to. The stove ticked as it cooled. Out in the lane a cart went by, and then another, and somewhere a shutter banged open and a woman shouted down at a boy to bring the milk up before it went sour. The window had gone from gray to the color of weak tea.
Lyra sat on her stool and looked at Corin's Ledger. She did not ask to hold it. She just looked at it, where it lay open on his knee, the way a person looks at a letter on someone else's table.
"Can I see the first page?" she said at last.
Corin's hand moved a little on the cover. It was not quite a no.
"Toby's seen it," he said. "He had to read it to me once. When I got lost. Nobody else."
"Rule one," said Lyra. "No lies between us. Not big ones."
"Not showing you a page isn't a lie."
"No," she agreed. "It's just a door. And you heard her. Some doors open from the other side."
He looked at her for a long moment. She did not smile. She did not push. She only waited, with her hands folded over the place where her father's letter was hidden, and he understood that she would wait like that for as long as it took, and that if he said no, she would not ask again. That was almost worse.
He turned the Ledger over in his hands and opened it at the very beginning. At the inside of the front cover.
The writing there was big and black and untidy, sloping hard to the right, pressed so deep into the cream-colored paper that some of the strokes had cut through. It was the same writing he had copied into the front of every Ledger he had ever owned. The full ones lay in a strongbox under the floor of Bram's shop, six years of him. This was the one he carried now. And every one of them began the same way.
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin.
You were born in Oakhaven.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
You used to fear the dark.
And below that, squeezed in small at the bottom: Turn the page. Then get up. Then eat something. In that order.
Lyra read it. He watched her eyes go along the first line, and then the second.
And stop.
She read the second line again. Then she lifted her head and looked at him, and her face had gone the color of the paper.
"You were born in Oakhaven," she said.
"Yes."
"You knew. All night. When the letter said it. When we wrote it down. You knew it was written in your own book, and you didn't say."
"I said I thought it had something to do with me."
"That's not the same."
"No," Corin admitted. "It's not." He looked down at the page. "I wake up every morning and I don't know anything, Lyra. Not my name. Not my room. Not my own face in the glass. I read this, and it tells me who I am, and I believe it, because it's my hand. But I don't know where Oakhaven is. I'd never heard the word anywhere except in my own writing. Not until Pell. Not until your father." He paused. "I didn't say it because I didn't know what it meant. I still don't."
Lyra was quiet for a long moment.
"Rule one," she said at last, but softer this time.
"Rule one," he agreed. "I'm sorry."
She nodded, once. Then she looked back down at the page, at the third line. You had a younger sister named Elara. And then, without meaning to, he could tell, her eyes went to his chest. To the place under his shirt where the one faded word lay over his heart.
"Elara," she said quietly.
"I don't remember her," said Corin. "Not her face. Not her voice. Nothing. Just the name. And when I read it, a small ache. Here." He pressed two fingers under his ribs. "Like someone pressed a thumb there and took it away."
She looked up at him, and then back down at the page. There was one more thing on the inside of the cover, down in the corner, and he saw her find it, and saw her frown.
It was not a line of writing. It was a drawing. A small one, in the bottom corner, done in the same brown ink. A tree. Just a simple tree, a trunk and a few branches, the kind a child draws. But from the branches hung small shapes on strings. Little ovals with points. Eight or nine of them.
"What are those?" said Lyra. "Leaves?"
"Birds," said Corin. "I think. Wooden birds. Hanging from the tree."
"Why did you draw them?"
"I don't know." He touched the drawing with one fingertip. The ink was dry and hard and did not smudge. "It's in every Ledger. Every one, in the same corner. I must copy it each time I start a new book, but I don't remember doing it. I've never seen a tree like that. But it's my ink and it's my hand." He paused. "Pell's sister wrote him a letter once. About a tree like that. With carved birds. Where their people came from."
Lyra was very quiet.
On the cot, Vespera had taken her arm away from her eyes. She was looking at the ceiling. Her face had no expression at all.
"A memory-tree," Vespera said, to the ceiling. "Old valley custom. When someone dies, the family carves a bird and hangs it up. So the tree remembers them, even if the people don't."
"What valley?" said Corin.
Vespera did not answer that. She only said, "Close the book, boy. You've shown her enough for one night."
Corin closed the book.
Lyra sat back on her stool. She rubbed her thumb slowly along the edge of her skirt, back and forth, as if she were smoothing out a crease that was not there.
"That's fair," she said after a while. "You showed me your first page. I should show you mine."
"You don't have a Ledger."
"No. Thornes don't write things down. We fold them." She almost smiled. "But I have a first page. Everybody does. It's the oldest thing you've got, the thing that's been there so long you forget it's even a memory. You just think it's how the world is."
"What's yours?"
She was quiet for a moment, thinking. Then she began to talk, and her voice was different. Softer. Younger. The voice of someone walking very carefully through a room in the dark.
"The nursery," she said. "At the old house, before we lost it. On the top floor of the east wing. There was a big round window that looked out over the garden, and a rocking horse with a real horsehair tail that Rowan pulled out, a bit at a time, until it was bald. And there was a table. A little table, low down, painted white, with little white chairs. That's where we had our breakfast, when we were small. Rowan and me. Porridge and honey and milk in silver cups."
She stopped. Corin waited.
"There were three chairs," said Lyra.
The stove ticked.
"Three chairs at the table. And three silver cups on the shelf above it. Rowan's had an R on it. Mine had an L. And the third one—" She held up her hand, as if she were holding the cup. "The third one had a letter on it too. But it had been scratched off. Somebody had taken a knife, or a nail, and scratched and scratched until it was just a rough patch. You could feel it with your thumb."
"Who sat in the third chair?"
"Nobody." She lowered her hand. "Nobody ever sat in it. Nurse used to put it back under the table every morning, neat, with its back straight, the way she did with ours. And she used to set out the third cup. Every morning. Empty. And then, at the end of breakfast, she would put it back up on the shelf without anything ever being in it."
"Did you ask her why?"
"Once." Lyra's thumb had stopped moving on her skirt. "I was five, maybe. I asked who the third chair was for. And Nurse dropped the milk jug. It broke all over the floor. White milk everywhere, and the pieces of the jug, and Rowan laughing because he thought it was funny. And Nurse just stood there with her hands in the air, like she'd forgotten what hands were for." She swallowed. "And then she said, There's no third chair, Lady Lyra. There's only two of you. There were only ever two of you. And she cleaned up the milk. And the next morning the third chair was gone. And the third cup was gone. And I never saw them again."
Nobody said anything.
"I forgot about it," said Lyra. "Truly. Not burned it. Just forgot it, the way children forget things. For years and years. I didn't think about it once until tonight." She looked at the iron box on the table, where the burnt-out stone lay. "Until the letter said the one I could not keep."
"And you said there were only ever two of you," said Corin.
"Nurse's words," said Lyra. "I've been saying Nurse's words my whole life. I didn't even know they were hers."
Corin opened the Ledger again. He turned to the page where they had written What I know, with all its arrows and its crossed-out words. He found an empty space near the bottom, beside Lord Thorne's name, and he wrote, small and neat:
Three chairs. Three cups. The third cup's letter scratched off. Nurse dropped the milk. "There were only ever two of you."
He looked up. "What was Nurse's name?"
Lyra opened her mouth to answer.
And stopped.
Corin watched it happen. He had seen it a hundred times, in a hundred faces, on the docks and in the Low Quarter and in the dim back rooms where people went to sell what they could not afford to keep. The little flicker behind the eyes. The reach for something that ought to be there, on the shelf where it had always been. And the hand closing on nothing.
"I don't know," said Lyra.
She said it with great surprise, as if someone had told her that the sky had always been green.
"She looked after us for six years. She taught me my letters. She sang to me when I had the fever. I can see her face. She had a mole here—" Lyra touched the side of her own nose. "—and she smelled of starch and peppermint. I can see her hands. I can hear her voice. But I don't know her name." Lyra's own voice had gone thin. "I didn't burn it. I've never burned anything about Nurse. I'd know. Master Ivo always made me say aloud what I was spending, so I'd know. I never said her name."
"Then somebody else took it," said Vespera from the cot.
Lyra turned to look at her.
"Or she gave it up herself," Vespera went on, still looking at the ceiling. "Some people do that, when they leave a house. A servant who knows too much. She spends her own name on the way out the door, and then nobody in that house can ever call her back, or find her, or ask her what she saw. It's an old trick. A sad one."
"Why would she do that?"
"Because somebody asked her to," said Vespera. "Or because she was afraid."
Lyra sat very still. Then, slowly, she reached across the worktable and put one finger on Corin's Ledger, on the line he had just written. Nurse dropped the milk. She did not smudge it. She just touched it, the way you touch the shoulder of someone who is sleeping, to make sure they are still there.
"Write that I don't know her name," she said. "Please. So I don't forget that I don't know it."
Corin understood exactly. It was the strangest kind of thing to want to keep, and it was the most important. The shape of a hole. The place where something was missing. If you forgot that, then the hole closed over, and you never went looking.
He wrote: Lyra does not know Nurse's name. Nobody took it from her by fire. Find out who did.
And under that, after a moment, because it seemed only fair: Corin does not know his mother's name either.
He had never written that before. He looked at it for a long time. It did not look like much. Eight words in brown ink. But he felt, writing it, as if he had set down something heavy that he had been carrying up a long flight of stairs without knowing it.
Lyra read it upside down. She did not say anything. She did not need to.
Across the room, Vespera had turned her face to the wall. Her shoulders under the blanket were very still. And on her left wrist, half hidden by her sleeve, the four old letters and the unfinished line lay in the weak tea-colored light, the way they had lain for fifty years, waiting for a name that never came.
Morning on Hook Lane
By the time the sun came up properly, Hook Lane was loud.
It was a narrow lane, so narrow that two carts could not pass each other in it, and so crooked that from Vespera's window you could only see about thirty steps of it before it bent away out of sight. The houses on both sides leaned toward each other at the top, like old women whispering over a fence. Their upper floors stuck out further than their lower ones, so that the sky above the lane was only a thin blue ribbon, and the cobbles below stayed wet and dark long after the rest of the city had dried.
Every house had a shop at the bottom. Next to Vespera's was a man who mended clocks and sold secondhand buttons. Across from her was a bakery that was really only an oven and a window and a large, red-faced woman who sold small hard loaves to anyone who had a copper to spare. Further down there was a rag shop, a candle-maker, a cobbler who worked with his door open and sang hymns badly, and a narrow green door with no sign at all that everyone knew was a place where you could sell a memory for the price of a meal and not be asked any questions.
Now, in the early light, the lane was filling up. The baker's window was open, and the smell of bread came across the cobbles in a warm wave, so strong that Corin's stomach turned over and growled. A boy went past with a yoke on his shoulders and two buckets of water hanging from it, slopping at every step. Two women argued about a chicken. A dog with one ear chased a cat with no tail, and the cat ran up a drainpipe and sat on a window ledge, washing its face as if nothing had happened.
And from somewhere across the city, deep and slow, a bell began to ring.
Corin counted without thinking. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.
It stopped at six.
"Rot," he said.
Lyra looked up from her stool. She had fallen half asleep with her head against the wall, and the plaster had left a faint white mark on her cheek. "What?"
"Sixth bell. I have to be at the Ministry by the seventh." He stood up too fast, and the room tipped, and he had to put a hand on the worktable. His arm throbbed. "If I'm not at my desk when the Section Chief does his rounds, he'll want to know why. And if he wants to know why, he'll ask. And if he asks—"
"You'll have to lie."
"I'm not good at it."
"I noticed," said Lyra. But she said it kindly.
On the cot, Vespera made a noise that might have been a laugh or might have been a snore.
Corin pulled his coat straight. He checked his pockets, one by one, the way he did every morning. It was a habit from long before Kettle, from the first year of harvesting, when he had begun to find that he could walk out of a room and not remember what he had brought into it. Left inside pocket: the Ledger. Right inside pocket, buttoned: the black square, warm and heavy and quiet. Hip: the rod in its case, still cold, still silent. Outside right pocket: pen, a stub of pencil, two coppers, a bent nail he did not remember picking up.
He looked at the nail for a moment. Then he put it back. Maybe it was his. Maybe it was not. It was only a nail.
"You go to work," said Lyra. She stood too, and folded her father's letter small and slipped it inside her bodice, where a High Quarter lady would keep a love note. "Act normal. Sit at your desk. Fill in your little forms. And then come to Linden Walk at noon."
"Noon?"
"My father's nurse eats her dinner at the cookshop on the corner at noon, and she's slow about it. Captain Oren walks down to the farrier's at a quarter before, and he won't be back until after one. There's hardly anyone left with us now. Nobody will be upstairs but my father." She said it all very fast, as if she had been planning it for weeks. Maybe she had. "Come to the yard gate on Linden Walk. The one with the stone pineapple on the post. Up the outside stair, over the stable. I'll be there."
"And then what?"
"And then," said Lyra, "you come and meet my father."
She did not say it the way a girl says it to a young man she likes, with a blush and a little joke about how strict her father is. She said it flatly, like a doctor saying and then we'll see how bad it is. Corin understood. She was not asking him to be polite. She was asking him to look at the thing that had happened to her father and tell her what it was.
"Noon," he said.
"Noon."
She went to the door. She stopped with her hand on the latch and turned back, and for a moment she looked unsure, the way she had looked when the stone went out and she said there had only ever been two of them.
"Vespera," she said. "Thank you. For the light. And the tea."
Vespera lifted one hand from the cot and waved it. "Go home, Lady Thorne. Sleep. Eat something. And don't tell anyone where you've been."
"There's no one left to tell."
"Good girl."
Lyra opened the door. The noise of the lane came rushing in, loud and bright and smelling of bread and horses. She pulled her gray cloak up over her hair, so that she was just one more person in a plain gray cloak, and she stepped out into the crowd.
Corin went to the window and watched her go. He saw her walk past the bakery, and the red-faced woman called out something and held up a loaf, and Lyra shook her head and kept walking. He saw her step around the boy with the buckets. He saw her reach the bend in the lane. She did not look back.
And then she was gone.
He stood at the window a little longer than he needed to.
"She'll come to harm," said Vespera from the cot, "that one."
Corin turned. "What?"
"Not because she's foolish. She's not foolish. Because she's brave, and she doesn't know yet what brave costs." Vespera sat up slowly, rubbing her eyes with the heels of her hands. "You'll look after her."
It was not a question.
"We're partners," said Corin. "She'll look after me too."
"Hmm." Vespera looked at him over her hands. "Sit down, boy. You've got time for one more cup. You look like a ghost that's been dug up twice."
He meant to say no. He meant to tell her that the Ministry was a long walk from Hook Lane, all the way across the Ink Quarter and up the hill and up the great black stairs, and that he had to wash his face and find a clean collar first, and that the Section Chief was a man who noticed collars. He meant to say all of that.
Instead, he sat down in the chair.
And the chair was warm, and the stove was warm, and the gray light at the window had turned gold, and somewhere in the lane the cobbler had started singing his bad hymn again, slow and wobbly and comfortable. Corin leaned his head back against the high wooden back of the chair. He closed his eyes for just a moment. Just to rest them. Just while Vespera put the kettle on.
He was asleep before the water boiled.
Vespera stood by the stove and looked at him.
Asleep, he looked younger. He looked like what he was: a young man of twenty-three or twenty-four, too thin, with a scar on his chin and dark hair that needed cutting and a face that was not quite finished yet. His mouth had fallen a little open. One hand lay on his chest, over the bare space where nothing was written but one faded word. The other hand had slipped down to rest on the case at his hip, even in sleep.
She did not wake him.
She took the kettle off the heat before it could whistle. She poured one cup, only one, for herself. She took it to the window and stood there with it, holding it in both hands, the way Lyra had held hers. The steam rose up past her face and fogged the cracked glass for a moment and then cleared.
Outside, the lane went about its business. The baker sold her bread. The dog came back without the cat. The boy with the buckets came back too, with empty buckets now, swinging them and whistling.
Vespera looked down at her own left wrist.
The sleeve of her old wool dress had ridden up a little, and there it was, as it always was, where she had put it fifty years ago with her own needle and her own ink, in the dark of the night, by the light of one candle, with her hand shaking so badly she had almost given up.
Four letters. And a line.
HOLL—
That was all. She had never finished it. She had sat there with the needle in her hand and the fifth letter waiting, and she had not been able to remember what came next. It had been right there. It had been on the tip of her tongue. A name, she was almost sure. A name she had loved. And then she had blinked, and it was gone, and she had sat there until the candle burned out, trying to get it back.
She had never told anyone what it was. She did not know herself.
She touched the letters with the tip of one blue-stained finger. They were old and soft and faded, the way all ink fades in the end. She had inked a thousand names on a thousand arms since then. She had kept other people's lost things alive for half a century. And she could not finish one word on her own skin.
"Holl," she said very softly, to the empty room, to the sleeping boy, to no one. "Holl. Holl—"
Nothing came.
It never did.
She let out a long breath and pulled her sleeve back down over her wrist. She lifted the cup to drink.
And stopped.
Across the lane, in the narrow gap between the bakery and the rag shop, there was a doorway. It was an old doorway, with no door in it anymore, only a dark space and three worn stone steps going down into a cellar that nobody used. Vespera had looked at that doorway every morning for years. Usually there was nothing in it but a broken barrel and a family of rats.
This morning there was someone standing in it.
They stood very still, back in the shadow, where the morning light did not quite reach. If Vespera had not been looking at exactly that spot, at exactly that moment, she would never have seen them at all. Even now she could not make out a face. Only a shape. Tall and narrow. And a coat.
A white coat.
It was long, that coat, almost to the ankles, and it was clean. That was the thing that made her breath stop. Nobody on Hook Lane wore white. Nobody in the whole Ink Quarter wore white. White was for the High Quarter, for weddings and Council days and the gowns of fine ladies. White did not last a day down here. The soot from the chimneys and the mud from the cobbles and the ink from the presses would have made it gray by noon.
But this coat was white. White as new paper. White as a lily.
And whoever was wearing it was looking straight at Vespera's window.
Vespera did not move. She had lived seventy-one years by not being a fool, and she knew better than to move. She stood at the window with her cup halfway to her mouth, and she let her face go blank and tired and bored, the face of an old woman looking out at the street while her tea cooled. She let her eyes drift past the doorway, as if they had not stopped there at all. She looked at the baker's window. She looked at the dog. She looked at the sky.
Then, slowly, she let them drift back.
The doorway was empty.
No shape. No coat. Only the broken barrel, and the three worn steps, and a rat sitting on the top step with its whiskers twitching, looking at the morning as if it owned it.
Vespera stood there for a long time.
She told herself it had been a trick of the light. A sheet hung out to dry somewhere behind the doorway, catching the sun. A ghost of the night, left over in tired eyes. She told herself she was an old woman who had been awake all night burning sleeping ink and talking about things that should not be talked about, and that old women who do that see white coats in doorways.
She did not believe a word of it.
She put her cup down on the windowsill, very carefully, so it did not clink. She went to the door and slid the bolt across. Then she went to the green cupboard and knelt, and from the very back of the bottom shelf, behind the jars of old ink and the boxes of needles and the rolled-up drawings of names, she took out a small knife with a bone handle. She did not open it. She only held it in her lap and sat down on the edge of her cot, facing the door, where she could see both the door and the window and the young man sleeping in the chair.
Corin slept on. His hand had tightened a little on the rod case. Under his coat, over his heart, the bare space rose and fell with his breathing.
Vespera watched him, and watched the door, and watched the window.
"White," she said quietly to herself. "White then, too."
Outside, the cobbler finished his hymn and started it again from the beginning. The bell across the city rang seven times. And Corin Vane, Senior Investigator of the Harvesting Section, who had never once been late to the Ministry, did not hear it.
In the doorway across the lane, the rat on the top step suddenly turned and ran down into the dark, as if something on the stairs below had moved.
But there was no one there to see it.
