Chapter 4: A Bargain in Bad Light, Part 2 of 5: What the Body Forgets
What the Body Forgets
"Now you," said Vespera, and turned on her stool.
Lyra sat up even straighter, if that was possible. She had put her cup down on the counter. Her hands were in her lap again, the left on top of the right.
"I'm fine," she said.
"You've said that four times since you came in my door," said Vespera. "People who are fine don't say it four times." She held out her broad brown hand, palm up. "Throat first."
Lyra hesitated. Then she lifted her chin, and pulled down the high collar of her silk coat.
In the candlelight, the marks on her throat were very dark. Five of them. Four on one side, in a row, and one on the other, larger, where a thumb had pressed. They were purple-black at the center and going yellow-green at the edges already, and the skin around them was swollen and shiny.
Vespera looked at them for a long time without touching. Then she reached out and laid two fingers, very lightly, on the side of Lyra's throat, below the jaw.
"Swallow," she said.
Lyra swallowed. Her face tightened.
"Again."
She swallowed again.
"Say your name."
"Lyra Thorne." It came out as a rasp, like a file on wood.
Vespera nodded slowly, and took her fingers away. "Nothing broken. The pipe's bruised, not crushed. You'll sound like a crow for a week and it'll hurt to eat bread." She got up and went to one of the shelves and came back with a small round pot of something greenish and greasy that smelled of mint and tar. "Rub this on morning and night. Drink warm things. Don't shout." She put the pot in Lyra's hand. "And next time a man the size of a barn picks you up by the neck, kick him somewhere a man the size of a barn doesn't like to be kicked."
"I did," said Lyra. "It didn't help."
Vespera's mouth twitched again.
"Now the hand," she said.
The Gap
Lyra did not move.
She sat on the stool with the little green pot in her right hand and her left hand lying in her lap, palm down, very still. Corin, in the leather chair, saw her look at it. The way you look at a dog you are no longer sure will not bite.
"It works," she said. "I've checked. I can hold the sword. I can make a fist. I can feel everything."
"I know you can," said Vespera. "Give it here anyway."
Lyra gave it.
Vespera took the slim pale hand in both of her broad brown ones, the same way she had taken Corin's, and turned it over, and looked at the palm. She ran her blunt thumb along the base of each finger. She bent each one, gently, at each joint, and watched Lyra's face as she did it. She pressed the pad of each fingertip, one after another, and asked, feel that? and Lyra said yes, and yes, and yes.
Then Vespera reached behind her to the tray on the arm of the chair, and picked up the smallest needle on the black velvet. A fine one. As thin as a hair, with an eye so small that Corin could barely see it. And a twist of plain white thread.
She held them out to Lyra.
"Thread it," she said.
Lyra stared at the needle.
She took it. She took it in her right hand, and the thread in her left, and held them up to the candlelight. And Corin saw her face.
He had seen faces like that, he thought. Somewhere. Through other people's eyes. The face of an old man in a tavern trying to remember the name of the girl he married. The face of a woman standing on a street corner who had come out to buy bread and could not remember which way was home. A face trying to reach for something it knew was there, and finding a wall.
Lyra brought the end of the thread toward the needle. Slowly. Her left hand, holding the thread, moved toward the tiny eye. And stopped. And moved again. And stopped.
It was not shaking. That was the strange thing. The hand was perfectly steady. It simply did not seem to know where to go. It hovered near the needle like a bird hovering over a branch it does not trust, and touched the thread to the side of the needle, and the side of the eye, and the point, and never the eye itself. Again. And again. Not missing by much. Missing by everything.
After a long time, Lyra put the needle and thread down on the counter, very carefully, side by side.
She did not say anything.
"That's the gap," said Vespera quietly. "That's what it looks like. Your hand is fine. Your hand is better than fine. I've seen what you did with that sword in the Inkwell, girl, it's all over you, I can smell the burn on you from here. You've got hands that could carve a name on a grain of rice." She picked up the needle. "But there's a hole in them now, the shape of this. And there always will be."
"Can you—" Lyra's ruined voice cracked. She stopped. Started again. "Can you write it back? Like you write on him? Can you put a line on my arm that says in, through, pull, and—"
"No."
Vespera said it gently. But she said it.
"I can write a fact," she said. "Your name is Lyra. You were born in the High Quarter. You can't embroider anymore. Don't try at parties. I can write a reminder. I can write you used to love this, so you know you did. But I can't write a skill. A skill isn't words. It's not in the head, where words live. It's in the hands, and the eyes, and the back of the neck, and the bottoms of the feet. You can't read your way back into it." She put the needle back on the black velvet, in its place in the row. "If I could, I'd be the richest woman in Vaelen, and the Memory Bourse would be selling my needles instead of other people's childhoods."
Lyra looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once. Short. Sharp. As if she had been expecting it.
"Then don't write anything," she said.
Vespera raised her iron-gray eyebrows.
"On me," said Lyra. "Don't write anything on me. Not a reminder. Not a fact. Nothing." Her chin came up. "Thornes don't wear their secrets on their skin."
The Ink-Binder looked at her. And then, slowly, deliberately, she looked down at her own left wrist, where her sleeve had fallen back again. At the place below the heel of the hand where the skin was thin and the blue veins showed through. Where, twenty years ago, on another Thorne, she had written one word.
She did not say anything.
She did not have to.
Lyra's face went red. Then white. Then she looked away, at the colored bottles in the window, and pressed her lips together until they disappeared.
The Fear
Nobody spoke for a while.
The kettle ticked on the stove. The needles chimed. The candle on the arm of the leather chair burned down a little, and its flame bent and straightened in the draft from the door. Outside, the gray dawn was turning to gold, and Corin could hear the first sounds of Hook Lane waking. A shutter banging open. A woman calling to someone. The creak of a boat being pushed off from the quay.
"How long have you been burning?" said Vespera at last.
Lyra did not look round. "Since I was fourteen."
"Who taught you?"
"A fencing master. My father hired him. Master Ivo. From the south. He taught all the High Quarter children. Sword first, and then the burn, once you were good enough to need it." Her voice was flat. "He said, small things first. Little skills. Things you won't miss. The flute. Knitting. Dancing the gavotte. He said, a duelist should have a hundred small skills, like coins in a purse, so she always has something to spend."
"How many have you spent?"
Lyra was quiet for a long time.
"I don't know," she said finally. "I stopped counting. After my father. After the house went. I had to fight, a great deal, that first year. People thought a Thorne with no money was a Thorne with no teeth." She turned her left hand over in her lap and looked at the palm. "I've spent the flute. And the harp. And painting in watercolors. And riding sidesaddle. And chess, I think, I can't remember the rules anymore, but I don't remember losing them, so maybe I never knew. And the second language my mother taught me, the one from her family. I can't speak a word of it. I can't remember what it was called." She stopped. "And now this."
Vespera did not say anything. She waited.
"I keep a list," said Lyra. "In my head. Of the things I'll never spend. No matter what. The things that are too big." Her voice had dropped very low. "Reading. Walking. Swallowing. Knowing people's faces. I think about it every time I reach. Every single time. I reach in, and I go past all the big things, very carefully, the way you'd walk past a sleeping dog. And I find something small. And I pull it out." She closed her hand. "And every time, I think, what if I reach in and there's nothing small left?"
Her closed hand was trembling now. Just slightly.
"What if the next time," she said, "the only thing in reach is my father's face? Or how to swallow? What if I'm in a fight, and I'm about to die, and I reach, and the only thing left is how to breathe?"
Corin sat very still in the leather chair.
He thought of Pell, on the rubbish heap, reaching past his gran and his sister and his village for the week, the only thing big enough. He thought of a young soldier he had never met, standing in a square under an oak tree hung with wooden birds, reaching for everything, and giving it, and walking away with nothing at all.
He did not know where that second thought had come from.
"You'll know," said Vespera.
Lyra looked at her.
"You'll know," said the Ink-Binder again. "When you reach and there's nothing small left. You'll feel it. Your hand will touch the big things, and stop." She leaned forward on her stool, with her elbows on her knees. "And then you'll have to choose. Spend the big thing and live without it. Or don't, and die with it." She was quiet for a moment. "I've had both kinds in that chair. The ones who spent it, and the ones who didn't. The ones who didn't, I only saw once."
"And the ones who did?"
Vespera looked at Corin. At the bare space on his chest, over his heart, with its one faded word.
"The ones who did," she said, "I see every week."
Sleeping Ink
Vespera cleared the counter.
She did it with one sweep of her arm, the way a woman clears a kitchen table for bread. Pots, cups, the needle and the thread, a ball of string, a pair of scissors, a cat-shaped pincushion with half its stuffing gone, all of it swept to one end in a heap. Then she wiped the dark wood with her apron. Then she held out her hand.
"Now the book," she said.
Lyra gave it to her.
Vespera did not open it at once. She held it in both hands, the way Bram had held Corin's Ledger, the way Lyra herself had held it on the stool in the Inkwell. She turned it over. She ran her thumb along the spine, and the edges of the pages, and the soft red leather of the cover. She lifted it to her face and smelled it, with her eyes shut, a long, slow breath through her nose.
"Calfskin," she said, with her eyes still shut. "Dyed in madder. Bound by Holt, on Quill Row. Twenty years old. I'd know his glue anywhere. He puts horse chestnut in it." She opened her eyes. "And the ink."
She opened the book.
She did not go to the back, the way Lyra had. She went to the first page. Candles, beeswax, two dozen. 3 silver. She bent over it, close, so close that her nose almost touched the paper, and looked at it sideways, along the page, the way you look along a table to see if it is flat.
Corin had got up out of the leather chair. He had pulled his shirt back on, though he had not buttoned it, and he came and stood at the counter beside Lyra, and they both watched.
Vespera tilted the page toward the lamp in the window. The oily sheen slid across the black letters. Green. Violet. Gold. She tilted it back toward the candle. The sheen slid the other way.
"Ah," she said softly.
"What?" said Lyra.
"Two inks." Vespera did not look up. "He's written it twice. The top one's ordinary. Good black iron-gall, the kind you'd buy anywhere in the High Quarter. That's the candles and the flour and the wine. That's just a coat. That's what you see." She touched the page with one blunt fingertip, between two lines, where the faint grooves were. "And under it, here, in the gaps. He's written with something else. Something that went on clear, like water, and pressed into the paper, and dried, and left no mark at all. Except the grooves where the pen went."
"Can you read it?"
"Not like this. Nobody can. It's asleep." Vespera straightened up. "It's what we call sleeping ink."
What Sleeping Ink Is
"There's ink," said Vespera, "and there's ink."
She turned round and reached up to the highest of the glass shelves behind the counter and took down a bottle. A very small one, no bigger than her thumb, with a black wax seal over the cork. She held it up between them. The liquid inside was clear. Perfectly clear. Like water. But when she turned it, slowly, in the lamplight, something moved in it. Faint. Glittering. Like the dust you see turning in a sunbeam.
"Ordinary ink is soot and gum and water," she said. "It sits on top of the paper. It's honest. What you see is what's there." She turned the little bottle. "Binder's ink is something else. It's got crystal in it. Ground fine. Memory-crystal. The same stuff they cut the gemstones from, for the lamps and the Bank. Only a pinch. Only a dusting. But it's enough." She looked at Corin. "That's what's in the silver on your wrist, boy. That's why it glows sometimes. That's why it helps."
Corin looked at the silver-gray letters running round his left wrist. V. made this.
"Memory-crystal wants memories," said Vespera. "It's hungry. That's what it's for. You put a pinch of it in ink, and you write a word on skin, and the crystal holds on to the word. Keeps it. Feeds it a little, from the person, every day. That's why my words don't fade like ordinary tattoos. That's why they work." She put the little bottle down on the counter, very carefully. "And then there's this."
"Sleeping ink," said Lyra.
"Sleeping ink." Vespera tapped the bottle's black seal with one fingernail. "Same crystal. But starved. You grind it, and you wash it, and you wash it, in running water, for a month, until there's nothing left in it at all. No memory. Not a scrap. It's empty. Clear as glass." She looked at the red book. "And then you write with it. And it goes on the page clear, and dries clear, and nobody can see it. Because there's nothing in it to see."
"Then how does anyone read it?"
Vespera looked at her.
"You feed it," she said.
The needles chimed overhead in the draft.
"Empty crystal is hungry crystal," said the Ink-Binder. "Starving. If you hold a page written in sleeping ink near a memory, a real one, burning, the crystal in the ink wakes up and reaches for it. Drinks it. Like a dry sponge in water. And as it drinks, it glows. And then you can read it." She paused. "For as long as the memory lasts."
Corin understood before Lyra did. He felt it go through him, cold.
"You need to burn a memory," he said. "To read it."
"Someone does."
"Whose?"
Vespera did not answer at once. She turned and looked at the window. At the lamp in it, behind the colored bottles. An oil lamp. Yellow. Warm. Then at the stove. Then at the door, and the dawn light, and the street beyond, where the tall black heron-necked lamps of Hook Lane were going out one by one as the sun came up.
Blue lamps. Gemstone lamps. Burning other people's memories, all night, every night, so that the streets of Veridia could be lit.
"That," she said, "is the question, isn't it."
Bad Light
She kept it in a box under the counter.
It was a small, plain, heavy box of black iron, with a lid that fitted so tightly that she had to lever it open with the blade of a knife. Inside, packed in gray wool, there was a lamp. A very small one. A gemstone lamp, the kind poor families in the Lower Docks kept on their kitchen tables, no bigger than a man's fist: a little brass base, a little glass chimney, a little wire cage. And in the cage, a stone.
It was not glowing. It was dark. Pale blue and dark inside, like the three stones in Corin's pocket. Like a lamp waiting to be lit.
"I don't keep them," said Vespera. "I won't have them in my shop. Not burning. I don't like knowing what's in them." She lifted the little lamp out of its box and set it on the counter beside the red book. Her face was set and hard. "But I keep this one. For this. Because sometimes there's no other way."
"What's in it?" said Lyra.
"I don't know. That's the point. I bought it from a lamp-seller at the Market Ring for six coppers. Cheapest stone he had. Small. Low grade. Some poor soul's something. A birthday. A walk by the river. A song they used to sing." Vespera's mouth went thin. "Sold to the Bank for a week's rent, most likely. Cut and polished and sold on for lamps." She looked at the little dark stone in its wire cage. "In my trade, this is what we call bad light. Light you shouldn't have. Light that cost someone something they'll never get back. You use it when you must. And you don't enjoy it."
She looked at Lyra.
"Your father wrote his book in sleeping ink so that nobody could read it by an honest lamp," she said. "Not by a candle. Not by the sun. Only by bad light. Only by burning somebody's memory, right next to the page. So that whoever reads it has to pay for it." She paused. "Or make someone else pay."
Lyra looked at the little lamp for a long time.
"He knew," she said slowly. "He knew that whoever found it would have to do that. He did it on purpose."
"He did."
"Why?"
Vespera shrugged her square shoulders. "So you'd think twice, maybe. So you'd know what kind of thing it was before you read it. So you'd understand." Her sharp dark eyes went to Corin, standing beside Lyra at the counter, with his shirt hanging open over the writing on his chest and the bare space over his heart. "Or maybe because he knew the only people who'd have that kind of light to hand were people like the Ministry. And people like him."
Corin did not say anything.
She's not wrong, said Malakor, very softly, behind his eyes.
He put his hand on his coat, hanging by the stove. On the right pocket. On the rod. He thought about the box under his bed in Room 9. The jars in the box under the bed are NOT empty. He thought of what was in them. Dead men. Dead women. Their last minutes. Their skills. Their deaths. He thought of carrying a jar here, and opening it next to the red book, and burning a dead stranger's last breath to read a lord's secret.
He thought it would be easier than burning a living stranger's birthday from a lamp.
He did not like that he thought that.
"Light it," said Lyra.
Vespera looked at her.
"Light it," said Lyra again. Her ruined voice was very steady. "He wrote it for us. For his children. He wanted us to read it. He knew what it would cost." She pressed her lips together. "I'll pay it. I'll remember that I did. I'll remember there was someone, somewhere, who sold a walk by the river so I could know what my father knew."
She looked at the little dark stone in its cage.
"That's the least I can do," she said. "Remember them. Even if I never know who they were."
Vespera looked at her for a long moment. And something in her plain wide weather-brown face changed. Softened. Just a little. Around the eyes.
"You're his daughter, all right," she said quietly.
Then she took a match from the box on the counter, and struck it, and touched it to the little brass wick at the base of the lamp, where the stone sat in its cage.
The stone woke up.
