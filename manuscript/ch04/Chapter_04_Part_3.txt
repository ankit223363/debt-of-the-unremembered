Chapter 4: A Bargain in Bad Light, Part 3 of 5: The Hidden Pages
The Hidden Pages
It woke slowly, the way a person wakes on a winter morning, not wanting to.
First a faint glow, deep in the heart of the stone. A pale, cloudy blue, like the sky seen through ice. Then brighter. Spreading out from the center to the edges, filling the little wire cage, until the whole stone was alight and humming. A thin, high, faint sound, right at the edge of hearing, like a mosquito in another room. The same sound Corin had heard that morning, standing under a streetlamp on Tallow Street.
The light it gave was cold. Blue-white. Not like the warm yellow lamp in the window, or the candle on the arm of the chair. It fell on the counter and the red book and the three faces bent over them, and it made them all look pale and tired and a little dead.
And there were things in it.
Corin saw them first, because he was used to looking. Shapes, moving in the glow. Faint. Turning slowly inside the burning stone, the way he had seen them turn in the streetlamp. A wooden table, scrubbed white. A window with a red curtain, half drawn. A small girl, five or six, sitting on a stool. And a woman behind her, standing, with a brush in her hand. Brushing the girl's hair. Long, slow strokes, from the crown to the ends. Over and over. The girl's eyes were half closed, like a cat's in the sun.
That was all. That was the whole memory. A woman brushing a child's hair at a kitchen table, one afternoon, a long time ago.
Someone had sold it for a week's rent.
Lyra had seen it too. Corin heard her breath catch. He did not look at her face. He did not need to.
"Quickly," said Vespera. Her voice was rough. "It's a small stone. It won't last."
She slid the red book across the counter until it lay right beside the little lamp, in the full blue-white light. Open. At the first page.
And the page woke up.
Between the Lines
It happened all at once, and it was the strangest thing Corin had ever seen. Or the strangest thing he could remember seeing, which was not saying much.
The black letters of the household accounts did not change. Candles, beeswax, two dozen. 3 silver. They stayed exactly as they were, ordinary and dull and black. But between them, in the spaces between the lines, where the paper had been blank except for the faint grooves of a pen, light came.
Silver light. Thin and bright. It came up out of the grooves in the paper the way water comes up into footprints on a wet beach. It filled them. It ran along them. And as it ran, it made letters. Small, elegant, sloping letters, with long tails on the gs and the ys. Written fast. Pressed hard.
And Corin saw the little blue stone in its cage flicker, as the light went out of it and into the page. Saw the memory inside it, the woman and the brush and the red curtain, waver like a candle flame in a draft.
The page was drinking it.
"Read," said Vespera. "Read it now. Out loud. Before it goes."
Lyra bent over the book. Her lips moved. Then her ruined, scraped, crow's voice came, low and fast, reading the silver words as they burned up out of the paper.
To my children. To Lyra. To Rowan.
And to the one I could not keep.
Lyra stopped.
She stopped as if she had walked into a wall. Her finger, resting on the page beside the silver line, went rigid. She stared at the words.
And to the one I could not keep.
"Read," said Vespera. "Girl. Read."
Lyra read.
The Letter
If you are reading this, I have forgotten you.
I am sorry. I am more sorry than I know how to write. Forgive your father for the handwriting. I am in a hurry, and I am afraid, and my hand will not keep still.
I have done a thing I cannot undo, and I have seen a thing I cannot unsee, and both of them will come for me in the end. When they do, I will burn rather than let them take what I know. I have decided this. I decided it on a hill above a valley, three days ago, with a spyglass in my hand. It is the only way I can think of to keep you safe.
So if you are reading this, it has happened. And I am sitting somewhere, smiling, not knowing your names. Do not grieve for me too much. I chose it. Grieve a little, and then find the clock.
Lyra's voice cracked on the last word. She swallowed, and went on.
Three days ago I was at a place called Oakhaven.
Corin felt the word go through him like a needle.
You will not find it on any map. By the time you read this, it will not be in any book. They will see to that. I was sent there by the Council, to watch, and to write down what I saw, so that it would be done properly. By the book. I wrote it all down. Every word. Every child. And when it was over, I was told to give my report to the Inquisitor-General.
I gave him a report.
It was not the true one.
The silver letters were coming more slowly now. Fainter. In its cage, the little blue stone was dimming. The woman with the brush was fading. Her strokes through the child's hair were growing slower, and slower, and paler, like a painting left out in the rain.
The true one is not in this book, Lyra read. Books can be found. Books can be burned. I have put the truth somewhere else. I have folded it small, the way your mother taught me, and put it in the one place no harvester will ever think to look.
In me.
And I have made one copy, and hidden it where only our blood can open it. In the clock your grandfather built, in the long gallery. Twelve stones. Eleven are ours. The twelfth is mine. The twelfth is the witness.
Witness. K.
Corin saw Lyra's hand begin to shake, very slightly, on the edge of the page.
There were children at Oakhaven. Sixty of them. They were made to put their hands on a glass. Most of them were clear. Seven were traces. And two of them were—
The silver letters flickered.
In its wire cage, the blue stone flickered with them. Went dim. Came back. The woman with the brush was almost gone now. Only her hand was left, faint as smoke, moving through a child's hair that was no longer there.
"No," whispered Lyra. "No, no, not yet—"
The silver words came back. Fainter. Thinner. Further down the page, as if her father had stopped, and crossed something out, and gone on.
I cannot write their names. If this book is found, their names will kill them. Forgive me.
Do not trust anyone who smells of lilies. She was there. She wore white then, too.
Do not trust the Council. Do not trust the Bank. Do not trust anyone who tells you that forgetting is a kindness.
And the man you must fear most is the one who smiles when he—
The stone went out.
Dark
It did not fade. It went out. All at once, the way a candle goes out when you pinch it. One moment there was a small, cold, blue-white glow on the counter, humming faintly. The next there was a small dark stone in a wire cage, gray and dull and dead as a pebble from the river.
And the silver words on the page went with it. They sank back into the grooves of the paper like water draining into sand, and were gone, and there was nothing left between the lines of candles and flour and wine but the faint, blind marks of a pen pressed too hard.
The shop was very quiet.
The oil lamp in the window still burned yellow behind its colored bottles. The candle on the arm of the leather chair still burned. The needles still turned on their threads, chiming. But it felt dark. It felt as though a great deal of light had just gone out of the world, and the little that was left was not enough.
Lyra stood with both hands flat on the counter on either side of the open book, and her head bowed over it, and did not move.
Corin looked at the dead stone in its cage.
The woman with the brush was gone. The red curtain was gone. The kitchen table scrubbed white, and the little girl with her eyes half closed like a cat in the sun. Gone. Burned up, all of it, to make a few dozen silver words shine for a few minutes on the page of a book. Somewhere in Veridia, Corin thought, there was a woman, or a man, who had once had a mother who brushed their hair at a kitchen table on a long slow afternoon. And now did not. And never would again. And did not even know what they had lost.
He did not know who they were. He would never know.
He took out the Ledger, and opened it to the last empty pages, and wrote, small: A woman brushing a little girl's hair. Kitchen table, scrubbed white. Red curtain. One afternoon. Someone sold it for six coppers. Lyra read her father's letter by its light. Remember them.
He showed it to Lyra.
She looked at it for a long time. Then she nodded, once, without lifting her head.
"The one I could not keep," she said, very quietly, to the dark page. "He wrote to Lyra, to Rowan, and the one I could not keep." She looked up, at last, at Vespera, and her gray eyes were enormous. "There are only two of us. There were only ever two of us. Me and Rowan. There was never anyone else."
Vespera did not answer.
"There was never anyone else," said Lyra again.
But she did not sound sure.
The U.W. Stones
For a long time nobody spoke.
The gemstone lamp sat dead in its iron box. The little stone inside had gone from red to gray, and then from gray to a dull brown, like a pebble picked up from a riverbank and left to dry. A thin line of smoke rose from it and curled up toward the low ceiling. It smelled like a hair that had touched a candle.
Vespera closed the lid of the box with one finger. The iron made a soft click.
"That's that," she said. "Whoever she was, she's gone now. The woman and the girl and the red curtain. All of it."
"Not all of it," said Corin.
He held up his Ledger. The ink on the new page was still wet. He had written it fast, in his small square hand, while the light was dying: A woman brushing a girl's hair. Red curtain. The brush has a silver back. The girl is about six. The woman hums a song with no words. She is not Lady Thorne. Nobody knows who she is.
Vespera looked at the page for a long moment. Then she looked at him.
"You wrote down a stranger's memory," she said.
"Someone should remember her."
"It's not yours to keep."
"It's not anybody's to keep now," said Corin. "That's why."
Vespera did not argue. She only shook her head a little, the way a person does when a child says something foolish and true at the same time. She stood, and her knees cracked, and she went to the stove in the corner and put the kettle on.
The room was small, and in the gray hour before dawn it looked even smaller. It had one window, and the window had a crack in it that someone had mended with a strip of paper and paste. The walls were plaster, yellowed by years of pipe smoke and lamp smoke and the steam of a thousand cups of tea. Pinned to the plaster were sheets of paper, dozens of them, each one covered with drawings of words: the shapes of letters, the curls of old scripts, a single name drawn ten different ways. A shelf above the stove held jars of ink in every color, and some colors that did not seem to have a name. One jar was black. One was the blue of a deep bruise. One was clear, like water, and it was the one Vespera had used on Corin's arm.
Lyra sat on a stool by the worktable. She had not moved since the stone went out. Her hands were in her lap, one inside the other, and she was staring at the letter. Her father's letter. The hidden lines had already started to fade again, now that the bad light was gone. The words sank back into the paper the way frost sinks into a window when the sun comes up. In a few minutes there would be nothing left but the plain letter on top, the one about the weather and the roof and the price of coal.
But she had read it. She would not forget it. Not unless she chose to.
"Lady Thorne," said Vespera from the stove, without turning around. "You'll want tea. Everyone wants tea after that."
"I don't want anything."
"You'll want it in a minute. I'll make it anyway."
Corin set down his Ledger and reached into his coat. His fingers found the small cloth bag that had been sitting against his ribs since the Inkwell. It was heavier than it looked. When he set it on the worktable it made a sound like dice in a cup.
He had almost forgotten about it. That frightened him a little. He was a man who wrote things down so that he would not forget, and he had not written this down. He had been too busy with the thugs, and the fire, and Kettle's stain, and the girl with the needle.
He opened the mouth of the bag and tipped it.
Three stones rolled out onto the wood.
They were not like the gem in the lamp. That had been a cheap stone, cloudy and red, the kind sold on market carts to people who wanted to warm their hands. These were different. Each one was about the size of a quail's egg. They were smooth and dark, almost black, but when the lamplight touched them there was a color deep inside, far down, like the glint of a fish at the bottom of a well. The color moved when you turned your head. Green, then gold, then a color like old blood.
And each stone had two letters on its side. Not painted. Pressed into the thin skin of black wax that coated each one, with the point of a pin, by a hand that had pressed hard.
U.W.
"I took these from the Q drawer," said Corin. "At the Inkwell. Before the Bank's men came. They were wrapped in a cloth at the back, behind the files. Lyra had already—"
He stopped, because Vespera had turned around.
She had the kettle in one hand and a cup in the other. She had been about to pour. Now she stood very still, the kettle tipped just a little, and a thin thread of hot water ran out of the spout and splashed onto the floor by her bare feet. She did not seem to feel it.
"Put those away," she said.
Her voice was quiet. It was not the voice she had used all night, the dry, tired, half-joking voice of an old woman who has seen everything twice. It was the voice of someone who has just seen a snake on the path.
"Vespera—"
"Put them away, Corin. Now. Back in the bag."
He did not move fast enough. She set the kettle down on the stove so hard that the lid jumped, and she crossed the room in three steps and stood over the table. She did not touch the stones. Corin saw her hands. They were shaking. They hung at her sides, and the fingers were curled in, as if she were afraid they might reach out on their own.
"Do you know what these are?" Corin asked.
"I know what the letters mean."
"Then tell me."
"U.W." She said it so low that he almost did not hear. Then she shook her head, hard. "No. Don't say it out loud. Don't you say it either, girl. Not the whole of it. Not in here."
Lyra had looked up at last. Her eyes, red at the edges, moved from the stones to the old woman's face. "Why not?"
"Because some words are doors," said Vespera. "And some doors open from the other side."
Inside Corin's coat, against his hip, the rod went cold.
It was not a small cold. It was the cold of a cellar in winter, the cold of a hand that has been dead a long time. It came through the leather case and through his shirt and into the skin of his side, and he hissed and pressed his palm against it without thinking.
Close the bag, said Malakor.
The voice came, as it always did, from somewhere just behind Corin's left ear, a little inside his skull. Most nights it was a sour voice, sly and bored, a voice that liked to say clever things and watch him squirm. Tonight it was not clever. Tonight it was thin and tight, like a rope pulled too far.
Close the bag, boy. Close it and don't look at them again.
You know what they are too, Corin thought.
I know enough.
Then tell me.
No.
It was the first time in all their years together that Malakor had ever refused to tell him something. The old mage's voice had lied to him plenty. It had mocked him and teased him and hidden things behind riddles. But it had never just said no. Not like that. Not like a frightened man shutting a door.
Corin looked down at the stones on the table. The deep color in them moved. Green. Gold. Blood.
He reached out and swept them back into the bag with the side of his hand, careful not to touch them with his fingertips, and he pulled the drawstring tight.
Vespera let out a breath. Her shoulders came down. She went back to the stove and picked up the kettle again, and this time she poured the water into the cups and did not spill any. But her hands were still not quite steady.
"Sit," she said. "Both of you. I'll tell you what I can. Which isn't much."
She brought the cups to the table. The tea was dark and smelled of smoke and something sweet, like dried apple. Lyra wrapped both hands around her cup and held it under her chin, letting the steam touch her face. Corin did the same. He had not known how cold he was until he felt the heat.
Vespera did not drink. She sat on the edge of her cot, across from them, with her cup on her knee.
"When I was a girl," she said, "younger than you, Lady Thorne, I was sent to learn the Binding from an old man in the Ink Quarter. Old Haddon. He had no teeth and he had no wife and he had no name for half the things in his own shop, because he'd burned them all to work the ink. But he had his hands. A Binder keeps her hands, you see. You can burn your mother, your first kiss, the color of your own eyes, but if you burn your hands, you're done."
"I know," said Lyra softly. "You said. A skill is a root."
"So I did." Vespera turned the cup on her knee. "Old Haddon had a rule. There were three things you never inked. You never inked a lie, because a lie on the skin turns the body against itself. You never inked a name that wasn't freely given. And you never, ever, worked a memory that had those two letters on it."
"U.W.," said Corin.
"Yes."
"What did he say they were?"
Vespera was quiet for a while. Outside, a cart went past in the lane. Its wheels rattled on the cobbles and a man swore at a horse, and then it was gone, and the silence came back.
"He said they were memories of a war," she said at last. "A war that never happened."
Lyra frowned. "How can you remember something that never happened?"
"That's what I asked him. I was nine and I thought I was clever." Vespera almost smiled, but not quite. "He hit me on the ear with his spoon. And then he said, It happened, girl. It's only that nobody remembers it. The people who fought it gave it up. All of them. Every soldier, every widow, every child who saw the smoke. They burned it out of the world. And when a thing is burned out of every head at once, it stops being true. It goes into the stones and it waits."
The lamp hissed. Corin found that he was holding his breath.
"Waits for what?" he asked.
"He didn't say. I don't think he knew." Vespera finally lifted her cup and drank, a long swallow, as if the tea were something stronger. "He told me if I ever saw one, I should not touch it with my skin. I should not look into it too long. I should not say the name. I should put it somewhere dark and leave it there. And I should not let it near my ink."
"Why not your ink?"
"Because Binder ink has crystal in it, boy. Memory crystal. You know that much. It's how the ink holds a word in the skin and doesn't let go." She tapped the side of her own neck, where an old line of blue letters ran down under her collar. "Crystal calls to crystal. A memory like that, a big one, a war that a whole country gave up, it would be hungry. If it touched my ink, it might try to climb into the words. And then into the skin. And then—" She stopped.
"And then into the person," Lyra finished.
"Into whoever was nearest," said Vespera. "Yes."
Corin looked at the little bag on the table. It seemed so ordinary. Gray cloth, a frayed string. The kind of bag a market woman uses for coins.
Malakor, he thought. Is she right?
The rod against his side was still cold. For a long moment there was no answer. Then the voice came, very quiet, so quiet that Corin had to hold still to hear it.
She's right that you shouldn't touch them. She's wrong that they're sleeping.
What does that mean?
It means they were in Pell's drawer for a reason, boy. Somebody gave them to him. Or somebody wanted them found with him. Either way, a dead man doesn't keep that kind of thing by accident.
Did you ever see one before? When you were alive?
No answer.
Malakor.
Ask me another night.
And then the cold went out of the rod, slowly, the way cold goes out of a stone after the sun comes up. The voice was gone. Corin knew from long practice that it would not come back for hours. Malakor did that when he was sulking. Or, Corin thought now, when he was afraid.
"Your face went strange," said Lyra. She was watching him over the rim of her cup. "Just now. You looked like you were listening to someone."
"I was thinking."
"You were listening," she said. "My father used to look like that. When he was in his study with the door shut and I came in without knocking. He looked like someone had been talking to him and stopped when I came in."
Corin did not answer that. He did not know how. He had never told anyone about the voice in the rod. Not Mrs. Marta. Not Toby. Not Pell. He was not going to tell a girl he had met last night, however brave she was with a needle.
Vespera saved him. She stood and went to the far wall, where a heavy old cupboard stood, its doors painted green a long time ago and now mostly bare wood. She opened the bottom door and knelt, and Corin heard her moving things: the scrape of a box, the clink of glass, the soft fall of cloth.
When she stood up, she had a square of thick black cloth in her hands, and a needle already threaded with black thread, and a small pouch of leather no bigger than a man's palm.
"Give me the bag," she said. "Don't open it. Just give it."
Corin pushed it across the table. She picked it up by the drawstring, holding it away from her body the way a person holds a dead rat by the tail. She dropped it into the leather pouch. Then she wrapped the pouch in the black cloth, folding it once, twice, three times, until it was a tight square. And then she began to sew.
She sewed fast. Her hands, which had shaken so badly a few minutes ago, were steady now. They knew this work the way a bird knows how to fly. The needle went in and out, in and out, and the black thread pulled tight, and the seam she made was so small and so even that Corin could hardly see it. She sewed all four edges shut. Then she turned the square over and sewed a cross through the middle of it, corner to corner, both ways.
"What's the cross for?" Lyra asked.
"Nothing," said Vespera. "Old Haddon did it. I do it. Maybe it helps. Maybe it doesn't." She bit the thread off with her side teeth. "Mostly it makes me feel better."
She held up the finished square. It was black and flat and plain, about the size of a small book. Nobody who saw it would think twice about it.
"Keep it close," she said, handing it to Corin. "Somewhere dark. Not in your Ledger, and not near your rod. And not near any ink, if you can help it."
"You're not keeping them?"
"In my shop? With forty jars of crystal ink on the shelf?" Vespera laughed, short and harsh. "I've lived seventy-one years by not being a fool, boy. I'm not going to start now."
Corin took the square. It was warm from her hands, but under the warmth, through the cloth and the leather and the gray bag, he thought he could feel something else. Not a cold, exactly. More like a weight that was heavier than it should be. Like holding a cup that is fuller than it looks.
He put it in the inside pocket of his coat, on the right side, away from the rod. Then he buttoned the pocket.
"Can I ask you something?" said Lyra.
Vespera sat back down on her cot. She looked very tired now, more tired than she had all night. The gray light at the window was turning paler. Somewhere a rooster was trying to crow and not quite getting there.
"You can ask," she said.
"The war that never happened. Did my father know about it?"
Vespera looked at her for a long time.
"Your father," she said slowly, "came to my old shop on Quill Street twenty years ago, three days after a place I won't name. He sat in that chair. The one Corin's in now. I brought it with me when I moved. And he asked me to put one word on his wrist so he would know to look for something." She nodded at Lyra's hands. "You read the word."
"FOLD."
"I've thought about that word for twenty years, Lady Thorne. I never knew what he meant by it. I didn't ask. A Binder doesn't ask." She paused. "But I'll tell you this. When he sat in that chair, he had the same look on his face that I had just now, when your friend tipped out those stones. The look of a man who has seen the edge of something very big. And has decided not to step any closer."
Lyra put her cup down. The tea in it had gone cold. She looked at the place inside Corin's coat where the black square was hidden, and then she looked at her own two hands, the hands that could not thread a needle anymore.
"He stepped closer," she said. "In the end. He must have. Or he wouldn't be the way he is now."
Nobody had an answer for that.
Vespera got up one more time and went around the room putting out lamps, one by one, until there was only the gray light from the window and the small red glow from the open door of the stove. In that half-dark, the drawings on the walls looked like a crowd of faces watching.
And Corin, sitting in the chair where Lord Aurelian Thorne had once sat, felt the weight in his pocket, and could not shake the feeling that it was listening too.
