Chapter 88: The Crease, Part 5 of 5: I'm Glad
I'm Glad
And then the hand turned to the old man.
Corin felt it come. He felt the old man feel it come. Not the hand itself, yet. The cold in front of it. Like the cold that comes in front of snow.
The old man did not run.
He could not have run. He was too old, and too tired, and his stick was in the mud of the bank. And he did not try. Corin felt him not try. He stood at the Edge with the empty boy beside him, and the candle flame leaning away south against the wind, and he looked at the gray place where the voice was, and he thought.
Fast. Faster than Corin had ever felt anybody think.
I'm going to die. Now. Here. He'll take all of me, everything I've got, every road and every room and every face, and burn it, and I'll be gone, and nobody will ever know. Nobody will know about the crate. Nobody will know about the blue wagon. Nobody will know the boy's line, or his sister's name, or where she went. He'll have it all, and it'll be gone, and the boy will never get it back.
Unless.
And Corin felt him do it.
He felt the old man reach inside himself, quickly, and take hold of it. All of it. The round room and Corvell's hand. The road in the rain. The crate. The seven faces. The boy sitting up in the straw. Take my sister. The blue wagon going over the hill. The long walk. The footprints. The bunker. The tree with the crown. The C. And the names. Cassian's line. Elara. All of it.
And fold it.
Corin felt him fold it. It was like nothing Corin had ever felt. It was not taking, the way Corin took. It was not burning. It was the other thing. Lyra's mother's thing. Folding. The old man took everything he knew about the boy and the girl and the crate and he folded it, in half, and in half again, and again, and again, faster and faster, smaller and smaller, tighter and tighter, until it was a small hard bright square in the middle of him. And then smaller. The size of a coin. Hard as a stone. Shut so tight that nothing in the world could get its fingers into it.
Not even a long white hand.
And in his coat, against his chest, the black book with the red edges, where he had written it all down, the same night, by the brazier. Corin felt the pages of it fold too. All at once. Snap. Snap. Snap. The same folds, in the paper, as in the man. As if the book and the man were one thing.
The crease.
And then the cold reached him.
And the old man knew that he was dying, and that there was one more thing to do, and no time.
He looked round.
Corin felt him look. Wildly. At the gray evening. At the empty boy. At the candle on its stone. At the grass. For something. Anything. And he saw it.
There was somebody else there.
Not the gentle voice. Somebody behind it. A little way back in the gray, on the good land, by the stone with the candle. A young man. Corin could see him, almost. The old man's eyes could see him. Thin and tall, in a gray coat, with a round black hat. Standing very still. Holding a lamp that was not lit. A young harvester. Corin could tell that, the way you can tell a man's trade from his hands. A young one, new to it. Come here tonight with the gentle voice, to watch. To learn.
He had a rod in his coat pocket.
The old man could feel it. A rod. One of his own. Silver. Twisted to the right. One of the ones from the rack on the wall of the round room, made by the old man's own hands, long ago, and given out to the Ministry's harvesters, and carried. The young man had it in his pocket, the way they all did.
And the old man let go.
Of his body. Of the Edge. Of the cold. Of everything except the small hard folded square in the middle of him. He let go of all of it, all at once, and threw himself, what was left of himself, the folded part, at the young man's coat. At the rod. Into it. The way a drowning man throws himself at a floating spar.
And Corin felt him go into the silver.
Cold. Narrow. Dark. Twisting. Like going down a spiral stair with no light, round and round, smaller and smaller, until there was no room to turn. And then stillness.
And behind him, at the Edge, an old man's body fell down in the grass, with nobody in it.
And the young harvester with the round black hat bent and picked up the empty boy, because the gentle voice told him to. And carried him away, south, into the gray evening. With the rod in his pocket, and the old man in the rod.
Linus Hale.
Corin knew him. He knew the round black hat and the thin tall shape. He knew the voice that would say, years later, in a small room with a window, You were brought to me when you were very young, with nothing.
Linus had brought him. Linus had carried him. All the way back from the Edge.
And the old man had been in Linus's pocket the whole way.
The last fold opened.
And Corin was lying on the stone floor of Sola's command room, with a book on his chest, and Lyra's linen hand round his, and the brazier ticking, and the small square of sky at the top of the shaft going gray.
And the room inside him was almost empty.
He could feel it. Hask's hum, low, in its corner. Arran's coal, warm. And at the back, in the far corner, where the crease had been, there was nothing. The crease was open. It was all open. Laid out flat, every fold, all the way through, like a letter spread out on a table to be read.
And beside it, the old man.
Corin could hardly feel him. He was so thin now. So thin that he was not a shape at all. Not a voice. Just a warmth. A small warmth, in the corner, like the warmth left in a seat when somebody has just got up from it.
Boy, said the warmth.
It was hardly a word. It was a breath. Corin held his own breath to hear it.
I'm here, he said inside.
Good. A pause. So long that Corin thought it was over. Then, very faintly: Did you see?
I saw.
All of it?
All of it.
Good. Another pause. Then you know. Who you are. Who she is. Where to look. The warmth flickered. I'm sorry I couldn't reach her. I'm sorry about the hand. I'm sorry about all of it. I'm sorry about Corvell. I'm sorry about the room and the rods and the jars and the book and the screaming. I'm sorry, boy. I'm so sorry.
I know, said Corin.
No, you don't, said Malakor. But it doesn't matter now.
And then, very quietly, almost gone, a breath going out through a gap in a window:
Your name is Corin Vane.
Corin lay very still.
You were kind, said the old man. When I found you. In the crate. When all the others were quiet. You were the only one awake. And the first thing you did was ask me to take somebody else.
A pause.
You were kind. Nobody steals kindness. Linus said that. He was right.
The warmth was going. Corin could feel it going. Like the last heat out of a stone when the sun goes down.
You were kind, said Malakor. I'm glad.
And then nothing.
Not quiet. Corin knew quiet. He had lived with quiet in the room at the back of his head for long stretches, when the old man was asleep, or sulking, or thinking. That was quiet. This was not that.
This was nothing.
The corner where the warmth had been was empty. Not dark. Not cold. Just empty. Swept. Bare. The way a room is when the last of the furniture has been carried out and the floor swept and the window opened, and it smells of nothing, and the light lies on the boards, and there is no sign anybody ever lived there at all.
The room where Lyra had been, once. And then Vivienne. And then Malakor.
Empty.
Gone.
Corin lay on the stone floor and looked into it, and the old man was not there, and was never going to be there again.
The Far Side
He wept.
He did not know he was going to. He did not decide. It came up out of him the way water comes up out of the ground in a wet field, from somewhere far down, where you cannot see it, and once it started he could not stop it. He lay on the cold stone floor of Sola's command room with the old man's book on his chest and he wept the way he had not wept in all his life. Not for Sola, this morning, on the glass. Not for Arran. Not for Hask. Not for any of the people he had carried and lost. He had wept for them, a little, quietly, the way a man weeps who is trying not to be seen.
This was not quiet.
It shook him. All of him. It came out of his chest in long rough sobs that hurt his throat and his ribs and the inked lines on his ribs, and the book on his chest went up and down with it, and the opened pages rustled. He could not breathe for it. He could not see. The living side of his face was wet and hot and the gray side was wet and he could not feel it, and he did not care.
And Lyra held on.
She did not say anything. She did not call his name now. She knelt by him on the stone with her linen hand round his and held on, and held on, and did not let go, and did not try to stop him. He felt her other hand, after a while, her good one, come and rest on his shoulder. Through his coat. Through his shirt. Not on his skin. Just there. A small weight. Warm.
He wept for the old man.
For the round room and the rack of rods and the hare on the table. For the long years under the ground, not knowing the way out. For Corvell's hand, leading him up the stair. For the road in the rain. For the iron bar and the scream of the nails and the seven quiet faces in the straw. For the six he could not carry. For the long walk across the Glass with a child on his back. For the walk back again, because the child would not stay. For the candle that bent the wrong way. For the moment at the Edge when he knew he was dying and did not run, and did the only thing that was left. For all those long years in a bit of silver in a young man's pocket. In an old man's pocket. In Corin's.
For I'm sorry, boy. I'm so sorry.
For I'm glad.
He wept for the boy, too.
For the thin boy in the crate with somebody still in his eyes. Who sat up. Who did not cry. Who said Take my sister. Who drew a tree with a crown in it by the brazier with his tongue between his teeth, and signed it with the only letter he knew. Who wanted to go back. Who turned his face toward a gentle voice the way a flower turns to the sun, and went empty, at the Edge, in the gray evening, and did not know his name.
And he wept for the girl.
Whom he did not know.
Who could draw the crown with more points.
It went on a long time.
And then, slowly, the way a storm goes, it began to go. The sobs got further apart. Smaller. His breath came back, in pieces, ragged, and then a little steadier. He lay on the stone with his face wet and his chest aching and his eyes shut and Lyra's hand on his shoulder, and breathed.
And under the grief, when it had gone down far enough for him to feel anything else, he felt them.
Hask. In his corner. Humming. The three low notes, very quiet, over and over, the way he had hummed them in life, sitting by a fire, mending a net. Hm. Hm. Hm.
And Arran. A warm coal. A laugh, almost. The fox-bark laugh. Not out loud. Just the shape of it, in the dark, the way you can feel a friend grinning beside you without looking.
They were still there.
The shared dead. The ones the Ashborn had given him, a piece of each, to carry. They had not gone with the old man. They were still in the room at the back of his head, in their corners, small and warm. Hask's hum. Arran's coal.
But the old voice was gone.
Corin lay and listened for it. He could not help it. He listened the way you listen for a clock you have heard ticking all your life, in the night, after it has stopped. And there was nothing. No dry leaf going over on a stone. No Fool. No Boy. No grumbling at the back of his head about the weather or his boots or the way he held a cup.
Nothing.
He opened his eyes.
The square of sky at the top of the shaft had gone gray.
Not black. Gray. And as he looked at it, it went paler. A cold, clean gray, the gray of the very first part of the morning, before the sun has come up over the edge of anything. The stars were gone out of it. And a little light was coming down the shaft. Not much. A thin gray light, falling straight down through the stone, the way it fell every morning, and lying on the glass top of the table above him.
Dawn.
He had been in the crease all night.
He moved. His body did not want to. It was stiff with the cold of the stone and with lying still so long, and his lame leg had gone dead under him, and his gray arm lay on the book like a log. He moved anyway. He took his living hand out of Lyra's linen one, gently, and she let it go. And he put it on the floor and pushed himself up, slowly, until he was sitting.
The book slid off his chest into his lap.
He looked at it.
It lay open, in his lap, at the back. Where the folded pages had been. And the pages were not folded anymore. They lay flat. All of them. Rough gray-brown paper, the cheap straw kind, opened out flat, every fold undone. Corin could see the creases in them, still, the lines where they had been folded, like the lines on an old man's face. But they were open. And there was writing on them. Small. Very small. In the old man's hand. Lines and lines of it, in the old letters. Corin could not read it. He could not read the old letters. But he knew what it said.
He had seen it.
Vespera was still kneeling at his head, by her needle case. She had not used a single needle. He saw that. The needles lay in their loops, all of them, clean. She was looking at him with her old pale eyes, and her face was wet too, and she did not wipe it.
Kestrel had not moved from the blanket. But her spear was standing upright again, in her hand. He did not know when she had picked it up.
She saw him look at her. She did not come closer. But she lifted the spear, a little, and brought the butt of it down on the stone floor, once. A single knock. Hard. It went round the long low room and came back off the scorched walls.
"He spent himself," said Kestrel.
It was the Ashborn way of saying it. Corin had heard it before, on the glass, over Arran. Not he died. Not he is gone. He spent himself. As if a life were a purse of coins, and the only question that mattered at the end was what you had bought with it.
"Yes," said Corin.
"On what?"
Corin looked down at the open book in his lap. At the flat gray pages, with their old creases, like lines on a face.
"On me," he said. "And on a girl in a blue wagon."
Kestrel was quiet a long moment.
Then she knocked the spear on the stone again. Twice, this time. And then a third time, softer. Corin did not know what it meant. He thought it was the knock the Ashborn gave for a good bargain. He thought, from her face, that it was the highest thing she knew how to say.
"Then he bought well," said Kestrel. "We will say his name at the fire. With Arran's. With Hask's." She paused. "What was it? His first name? The one they took?"
Corin opened his mouth.
And found he did not know.
The old man had never told him. In all the months. My first name was taken by the people I worked for, he had said once, in the book. They call me the Grafted. And Corin had seen the whole of the crease, every fold, every road and room, and nowhere in any of it had anybody called the old man by a name of his own.
"Malakor," said Corin at last. "That's all there is. We'll have to say that one."
Kestrel nodded, slowly.
"Then we will say that one," she said.
And Lyra was kneeling beside him, with her bound hand in her lap, and the linen on it was gray and creased from holding his all night, and her face was white with tiredness, and her eyes were red.
"You came back," she said.
Her voice was gone almost to nothing. She had called him all night, he thought. Hour after hour. Until her voice wore out.
"Yes," said Corin.
"Is he—"
"Yes."
She did not say anything. She looked at him a long time. And then she nodded, once, and looked down at her hands.
Corin found his own book.
The Ledger. His. The brown one. It was in his coat, in the inside pocket, where it always was. He took it out. He took out the stub of pencil from the spine. He opened the book on his knee, on top of the old man's black one, to the page after the last one he had written, and he wrote.
Slowly. In his big crooked capitals. With his living hand. The letters coming out wrong, the way they always did, leaning, uneven, like a child's.
THURSDAY. MALAKOR.
He stopped. He looked at the name on the page a long time.
HE GOT ME OUT. HE WAS GLAD.
And under that, after a while:
THOSE I OWE: MALAKOR. ALL OF IT.
He held it out to Lyra.
She took it. She read it. He watched her lips move. And then she gave it back to him, without a word, and he shut it and put it back in his coat.
And then he reached for his pack.
It was by the leg of the table, where he had left it. He pulled it to him across the floor, one-handed, and opened it, and felt inside. Down past the spare shirt and the empty water skin. To the bottom. To the small hard long thing wrapped in a scrap of oiled cloth.
He took it out. He unwrapped it.
The rod.
The silver rod. As long as his hand, with the twist in it, going to the right. The one that had been in Linus's pocket at the Edge, a long time ago, when the old man threw himself into it. And then in Linus's desk, for years. And then in Corin's coat. The one he had carried all the way from the city, wrapped up, because the old man had been in it once, and he had not known what else to do with it.
He held it in his palm.
It was cold. That was all. Cold, and smooth, and a little heavy. It caught the gray light from the shaft and shone, dully, along the twist. There was nothing in it. He could feel that. He did not have to try. When he had held it before, in the city, on the road, there had always been something. A faint warmth. A sense of somebody, very small, a long way down the spiral. Like holding a shell and hearing the sea.
Now it was only silver.
He got up.
It took a long time. He used the edge of the table to pull himself up, and his lame leg screamed, and he did not care. Lyra got up with him. She did not help him. She stood beside him, close, in case.
And he stood at the long table in Sola's command room, in the first gray light of the morning, coming down the shaft, and looked down through the glass at the old map.
At the whole of the north, painted by hand. The Glass in the middle. The Ridges. The Eye, a black ring, with the dome in it, very small. And there, at the bottom, at the south edge, the tree with the crown, and the old letters beside it. Oakhaven. Where he came from. Where the men in gray went up the road with their lamps. And past it, off the edge of the map, where the paint ran out, the way home. Veridia. The city. The Bank and the Ministry and the Spire.
And his sister.
And at the top of the map, at the north edge, past the Eye, the Glass going on and on, gray, to where the painted mountains stood up in a row, small and sharp, like teeth. And past the mountains, nothing. Just the brown vellum, empty. The far side. The land where they burned their dead on high stacks so the smoke went up into the sky. Where the old man was born.
Corin laid the rod down on the glass.
He did not think about it. That was the strange thing, afterward. He did not decide which way to point it. His hand laid it down, on the thick clear glass over the painted map, in the middle of the Eye, by the small black dot of the dome. And when he took his hand away, the rod was pointing north.
Away from Oakhaven. Away from Veridia. Away from the tree with the crown, and the way home, and his sister.
North. Over the rest of the Glass. Toward the painted mountains, and past them, to the empty brown edge of the vellum. To the far side.
He looked at it.
Lyra looked at it.
Neither of them said anything.
And the gray light came down the shaft and lay on the glass and on the map and on the plain silver rod, pointing north. And Corin stood and looked at it and listened, the way he had listened every morning for months, for the dry small voice at the back of his head to tell him what it meant.
And the room inside him was entirely quiet.
