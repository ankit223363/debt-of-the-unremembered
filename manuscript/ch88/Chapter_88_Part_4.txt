Chapter 88: The Crease, Part 4 of 5: Stories by the Brazier
Stories by the Brazier
The folds that came after that were small ones. Quiet ones. Corin felt the old man slow down to open them, the way a man slows down on a good stretch of road, because he knows what is coming after and does not want to get there.
Evenings by the brazier.
That was all they were. Evening after evening, in this room, with the red light coming up from below and the shaft going dark overhead. The old man in the oak seat at the head of the table, with the striped blanket over its back. The boy on the floor, by the brazier, on a folded sack, with his knees drawn up inside his big coat.
Talking.
The boy talked, after a while. Not at first. At first he only answered, when the old man asked him something, and then only with one word, or two. Yes. No. Don't know. But the evenings went by, and the brazier glowed, and the old man did not hurry him, and one night the boy began to talk by himself, without being asked, the way water starts to come out of a spring that has been stopped up with mud.
He talked about home.
Corin listened. He could not help it. He lay on the stone floor of the same room, with a book on his chest, a whole life later, and listened to his own small voice telling an old man about a place he could not remember.
There was a hall. The boy said that first. A long hall made of wood, with a roof that came down low at the sides, almost to the ground, and was covered with turf, so that in the spring there were flowers growing on it. And at the end of the hall there was a pole, and on the pole there was a flag, and on the flag was the tree with the crown in it. Green, on white. Old. Torn at the edge where the wind had got at it.
"We weren't allowed to talk about it," said the boy. "Not to anybody who came. Not to the man who brought the salt, or the man who mended the pots. If anybody asked, it was just a tree. Mother said."
"Just a tree," said the old man.
"Yes."
There were apple trees. A lot of them, on the hill behind the hall, in rows. In the autumn everybody picked, even the little ones, and the apples went into great baskets and then into a press, and the juice came out brown and sweet and ran down a wooden trough into barrels. The boy had been allowed to turn the handle of the press, once. It had been very hard. His sister had helped.
There was a mill, by the stream, with a wheel that went round and round all day and made a sound like somebody knocking slowly on wood.
There was a mother. The boy did not say much about her. Corin felt the old man notice that. The boy said Mother said, sometimes, and Mother made, and once, Mother sang the one about the fox. But when the old man asked him, gently, what she looked like, the boy went quiet and looked at the brazier and did not answer, and after a while the old man stopped asking.
Corin knew why. He knew it from the inside, the way you know your own body. The boy did not answer because he could not. The men with the lamps had been to Oakhaven before the crate. They had gone from house to house. And the boy's mother had been in one of the houses, and the boy had been in it with her, and somebody had put a hand on the boy's face too, that night, not long enough, not all the way. Long enough to take his mother's face. Not long enough to take the rest.
That was why he was awake, in the crate.
That was why there was somebody still in his eyes.
They had not finished.
And there was a sister.
The boy talked about her more than anything. Corin heard it. Every evening, sooner or later, it came back to her, the way a dog comes back to the door.
She was little. Much littler than him. She only came up to his chest, and she wore her hair in two braids that she would not let anybody but Mother do. But she could run nearly as fast as he could, and she climbed the big apple tree at the top of the hill, the one nobody else would climb, and he had to go up after her and get her down. She could draw. She could draw anything. She drew on everything, on slates and on the walls of the hall with burnt sticks and in the dust of the yard with her finger, and Mother scolded her, and she went on drawing. She drew the tree with the crown better than anybody. She did the crown with more points.
And she could write.
That was the thing the boy kept coming back to. She was little, but she could write her whole name. All of it. She had learned her letters before he had, quicker than anybody, the way she did everything. And his. She had been teaching him. In the dust of the yard, with a stick. Every evening, before supper. She did the letters and he copied them, under hers. He could do the first letter of his own name. The curve, like a moon on its back. He had got that. The rest was hard.
"She was going to show me the rest," said the boy. "After the apples."
The old man did not say anything.
"After the apples were in," said the boy. "She said. When there was more time."
And then the men came, with the lamps. Before the apples were in.
The boy did not talk about that. Not once. Not on any evening. Corin felt the old man wait for it, and it did not come.
And on some evenings the old man talked instead.
Not about himself, much. He was not a man who talked about himself. But the boy asked questions, the way children ask, one after another, without stopping, and some of them the old man answered.
"Where did you come from?" the boy asked, one night. "Before. Before the round room."
The old man was quiet a long time.
"A long way off," he said at last. "North. Over the rest of this Glass. And over mountains. High ones. With snow on them all the year round, even in summer. And down the other side, to a hot dry country, with red earth, and a city on a river, all made of yellow stone."
"Is it nice?"
"It was," said the old man. "I don't know what it is now. It was a long time ago." He looked at the brazier. "They burn their dead there. Did you know that? They don't put them in the ground, the way you do. They build a great stack of wood, high, as high as a house, and they lay the dead person on the top, and they set it alight at sunset. And the smoke goes up. Straight up, into the sky. And everyone stands round and watches it go."
The boy thought about this.
"Why?" he said.
"So that nothing is left behind," said the old man. "So that the dead don't stay. In the ground, or in the house, or in anybody's head. They go up, all of them, all at once, into the sky, and they're free." He was quiet a moment. "That's what my mother told me, anyway. When I was your size."
"Did you want to go up?" said the boy. "When you die?"
The old man laughed. The dry leaf, going over. Younger, in the memory. Louder.
"I've done too many things to be let go so easily," he said. "I think I'll have to stay a while. In somebody's pocket, probably. Making a nuisance of myself."
The boy did not understand that. Corin saw him not understand it. He saw him frown, and look at the old man, and decide it was a joke, and smile a little, uncertainly, the way children smile at jokes they do not get.
But Corin understood.
And lying on the floor of the same room, a whole life later, with the old man going thin as smoke in the corner of his head, he felt something tighten in his chest, and stay tight.
You knew, he said inside. Even then. You knew you'd end up in a pocket.
The old man did not answer. He was too thin to answer. He only went on opening the folds, one by one, slowly, toward the one he did not want to get to.
And in the memory, by the brazier, the boy pulled his knees up inside his big coat and put his chin on them and looked at the red glow, and said, very quietly, the way you say a thing you have been thinking about for a long time:
"I want to go back."
The Page
Somewhere a long way off, somebody gasped.
Corin heard it. Not in the memory. Outside it. Above it. The way you hear a sound in the real world when you are deep asleep and dreaming, and it comes down into the dream and does not fit there. A sharp small sound. A breath pulled in, quick, through the teeth.
Lyra.
And then her voice. Not calling his name. Something else.
"Vespera. Vespera, look. The pages."
Corin could not look. He was in the memory, by the brazier, watching a small boy touch a crooked C with a black finger. He could not move his eyes. But he could feel. And he felt, on his chest, on his breastbone, where the old man's book lay, something move.
The pages.
He felt them stir. Under his gray hand. The small hard bright squares of folded paper, that had been shut so tight for so long that fingers slid off them like water off glass. He felt them move against his palm, through the numb gray skin, the way you feel something move in a pocket. A small, slow stirring. Like leaves beginning to uncurl in the spring.
They were opening.
The ones in the book. At the same time as the ones in him. Fold by fold. Click. Click. He felt them go. A soft dry sound, very small, coming up off his chest. Paper unfolding. Paper that had not been unfolded since—
In a long, long time.
"They're coming open," said Lyra. Her voice was strange. Very quiet. Shaking. "All of them. By themselves. Look. Look at them."
"I see them," said Vespera.
"Can you read it?"
"My eyes are no good for that small. You read it, girl."
A pause.
And then Lyra's voice again. Closer. As if she had leaned over him. Over the book. And her linen hand was still round his. He felt it tighten.
"It's his writing," said Lyra. "The old man's. Small. Very small. Smaller than the rest of the book. As if he had to fit a lot on a little paper." She stopped. "It's in the old letters. Hold on. Let me—"
And she read.
Corin heard her read it. From far off, down through the memory. Her voice coming down to him the way a voice comes down a well. Slow. Careful. Each word laid down on its own.
"THE BOY IS A BRIGHT," Lyra read.
Corin lay very still.
In the memory, the small boy by the brazier looked up at the old man in the oak seat. And the old man looked back at him.
"HIS LINE IS CASSIAN'S LINE," read Lyra.
Her voice caught on it. Corin heard it catch, the way a sleeve catches on a nail. And then go on.
"HE IS THE HEIR."
Nobody said anything.
Corin heard the brazier tick. He heard Kestrel, at the blanket, let out a long breath, very slowly, the way the Ashborn let out their breath when they had been holding it a long time. He heard Vespera's needle case creak on the stone as she leaned on it.
And he heard Lyra breathe in again.
"There's more," she said. "Under it. A little gap, and then more. In a different ink. Darker. As if he wrote it after. Another night."
"Read it," said Vespera.
And Lyra read:
"AND THE GIRL WAS KEPT."
A pause.
"HER NAME IS ELARA."
And in the memory, by the brazier, in the red light, the small boy held up his drawing of the tree with the crown in it, and said to the old man: "My sister can draw it better."
And Corin understood.
It did not come all at once. It came the way water comes into a dry ditch. A little at the front, and then more, and then all of it, filling up every crack. He lay on the stone floor with the book on his chest and Lyra's voice in his ears and the boy in front of his eyes, and it came into him, slowly, until he was full of it.
A Bright.
He had heard the word. He had seen it painted on a crate. In white. Running in the rain. OAKHAVEN. BRIGHT. He had seen the children who were Brights, packed in the straw, with their quiet faces. The ones with the gift in them, whatever the gift was, the thing that made the men in gray come up the road with their lamps.
He was one.
Cassian's line. He knew that name too. Everyone knew it. The old kings. The kings before the Council. The ones whose faces had been melted smooth on the old walls, whose names were scratched out of the books. Eight of them. The last one taken, and emptied, and gone.
His line.
He was the heir.
He did not feel like an heir. He lay on the cold stone floor with half his face gray and his left arm dead and his leg lame and the inside of his head swept bare, and he did not feel like anything at all. He felt like a man who has been told that the house he has lived in all his life has a room in it he never knew was there. And that the room is full of things. And that they are all his. And he has no idea what any of them are for.
But it was not that.
It was not the heir. It was not the king. It was not the line.
It was the girl.
The girl was kept.
She had been in the blue wagon. The light going over the top of the hill in the rain. The old man had not been able to reach her. And she had gone on, in the blue wagon, to wherever the blue wagon was going. Somewhere south. Back. To the city. To the Vault, maybe. To the men in gray.
And they had kept her.
Not emptied her, and thrown her away, the way they threw away the others. Kept her. For something.
And her name was Elara.
Elara.
Corin said it inside himself. Carefully. The way he said his own name, every morning, to make sure it was still there.
Elara.
He did not know her. He did not know her face. He did not know her voice. He did not know if she was tall, or short, or kind, or cruel, or alive, or dead. He did not know anything about her except one thing. That she could draw the tree with the crown in it better than he could. That she did the crown with more points.
And that she had been going to show him how to write the rest of his name.
He felt his face go wet again. Both sides. The living side, he could feel. The gray side, he could not. But he thought it was wet too.
And Lyra's hand, in its linen, held on to his so tight it hurt.
"Corin," said Lyra.
Not reading now. Calling.
"Corin. Corin Vane. Corin."
The Edge
He heard her. He held on to it. But the memory had him, and it would not let go yet, and the old man was still opening it, fold by fold, and there was more.
There was the boy, saying it again. The next night, and the next, and the next.
Corin saw the last of those nights. The brazier lower. The boy older, a little, thinner, his hair longer. Standing in front of the oak seat at the head of the table with his small fists shut at his sides.
"I want to go back," said the boy.
The old man looked at him.
"I want to go back," said the boy again. "For my sister. She's in the blue wagon. You said you couldn't reach her. But we could go back now. We could find out where it went."
"It went to the city," said Malakor. "To the men in gray. You can't go there. They'll take you too."
"Then I'll get taken," said the boy. "And I'll be where she is."
The old man did not answer for a long time.
And Corin, inside him, felt what he felt. Felt him look at the small boy with the shut fists, and know that he was right, and know that he was wrong, and know that it did not matter, because the boy would go. With him or without him. Tonight or another night. The boy would walk out of this room and up the iron steps and across the black glass and the gray, all the way back to the Edge, by himself, on his small feet, in his boots stuffed with straw. And die on the way. Or not die, and get to the city, and be taken.
Unless somebody went with him.
"All right," said Malakor.
The boy stared at him.
"All right," said the old man again. Tired. So tired. "We'll go back. Together. As far as the Edge, anyway. And then we'll see."
And Corin saw them go.
Fold. Fold. The iron steps, going up. The black glass. The shore. The short way. The Ridges. The long gray flat, going south, day after day. The old man slower than before. Much slower. Leaning on a stick, now. The boy walking ahead of him, and stopping, and waiting, and walking ahead again. Small. Stubborn. Not looking back.
And near the Edge, where the glass was warm and soft, their footprints, going down into it.
Going the other way, this time. South. Back toward the good land. A line of small crooked prints, close together, from boots that were too big. And beside them, the old man's, longer, slower, with a round hole beside every second print, where the stick went down.
The glass kept those too.
And then the Edge.
The clean line. The grass on the far side of it. Dead bracken. Stones. A few thin trees. It was evening. The sky was low and gray and there was a thin cold wind coming up off the good land, the first wind in weeks, and it smelled of wet earth and rot and green things. The boy stopped at the line and breathed it in. Corin felt the old man watch him do it.
And on the far side of the line, in the grass, there was a candle.
One candle. Standing on a flat stone, by itself, in the open, with no lantern round it. Lit. Its small yellow flame standing straight up in the gray evening.
The wind came up off the good land.
And the candle flame did not bend with it.
Corin saw that. He felt the old man see it. The wind was coming from the south, from the good land, toward them, toward the Glass. It moved the dead bracken. It moved the boy's hair. It should have pushed the candle flame north, toward them, the way wind pushes a flame.
The flame bent the other way.
Away from them. South. Against the wind. As if something on that side, out in the gray evening, beyond the stone, were pulling at it. Drawing it. The way a draft draws a flame toward a crack under a window.
The old man stopped breathing.
Corin felt him stop. Felt his whole old body go still, and cold, the way it had gone cold in the round room when Corvell came in with the paper. Colder. He knew what it was. He knew what the flame meant. Corin did not. But he felt the old man know.
No, thought Malakor. No. Not here. Not now. Not with the boy.
And a voice said:
"There you are."
It came from the other side of the line.
From the gray evening, beyond the stone and the candle, where the bracken was. Corin could not see who said it. There was nobody there. Or there was somebody, and the evening was too gray, and the light was too low, and his eyes, the old man's eyes, would not settle on them. They kept sliding off. The way fingers slide off a folded page.
But the voice was there.
It was a gentle voice.
That was the worst of it. Corin had heard about it. From the old man himself, on a bad night, in a whisper. Him with the gentle voice. And he had thought, when he heard it, that it was a strange thing to call a man who did what that man did. He had thought it must mean something else.
It did not mean anything else.
It was gentle. Kind, almost. Warm and low and patient, like the voice of somebody's favorite uncle, calling them in from the garden at dusk because tea was ready. There you are. As if the boy had been out playing too long, and somebody had been a little worried, and was not cross, only glad to see him.
The boy turned toward it.
He did not mean to. Corin saw that. He saw the boy's face turn, the way a flower turns to the sun, without deciding to. His big dark eyes went wide and soft. His small shut fists came open.
"Come here, child," said the gentle voice. "You must be tired. You've come such a long way."
And a hand came out of the gray.
A long hand. Thin. White. Very white, whiter than skin should be, the white of a candle, or of a bone. With long thin fingers, held out, palm up, open. Reaching across the line. Over the clean edge where the Glass stopped and the grass began. Toward the boy.
"No," said Malakor.
Out loud. Hoarse. Corin heard it come out of the old man's mouth, felt it tear his throat.
"No. Not him. Take me. Take what I've got. I've got a great deal. More than he has. More than any of them. Take me instead."
The gentle voice laughed.
Softly. Fondly. The way you laugh at a dog.
"Oh, old man," it said. "I'll have you too. In a moment. But you're not what I came for."
And the long white hand touched the boy's face.
Just touched it. Laid itself along his cheek, very gently, the way you touch the face of a sleeping child. And Corin felt it.
He felt it through the old man, who was watching. And he felt it through himself, lying on the floor of Sola's command room, a whole life later, with a book on his chest. He felt it the way you feel a thing that happened to you, not to somebody else. In his own face. On the left side.
Cold.
The boy went empty.
Corin watched it happen. He watched the boy's big dark eyes, that had had somebody in them, in the crate, in the rain, when all the others were quiet. He watched the somebody go out of them. Not fast. Not like a candle blown out. Slowly. Like water going out of a basin through a crack in the bottom. Down and down. Until there was nothing in the eyes at all. Until they were the eyes of the quiet ones in the crate.
The boy stood at the Edge with the white hand on his face and his own small hands open at his sides, and he did not know who he was, or where he was, or why. He did not know there had been a sister. He did not know there had been a blue wagon, or a crate, or an old man, or a long walk across the Glass, or a tree with a crown in it drawn in charcoal by a brazier.
He did not know his name.
And Corin, lying on the stone floor—
Lying on the stone floor, a whole life later, with Lyra's hand round his, understood at last why he had come to Linus with nothing.
This was the nothing.
This was where it had come from.
