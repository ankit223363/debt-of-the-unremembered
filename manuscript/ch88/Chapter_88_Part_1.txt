Chapter 88: The Crease, Part 1 of 5: The Hand
The Hand
"I'm the hand, boy," Malakor said. "I folded it with me, so I pay with me. It's simple. Don't make it difficult."
Corin did not answer him.
He was standing at the long table in Sola's command room with his living hand flat on the glass top of it, and the old man's voice was so small in his head that he had to stand very still to hear it at all. Like a voice in the next house, through a wall. Like a voice at the bottom of a well.
The room was dark except for the brazier.
It was a long, low room at the far end of the corridor, behind a gray army blanket that somebody, long ago, had nailed up across the way in for a curtain. The blanket hung in folds, thin and dusty and eaten by moths at the bottom edge. Past it the room went back a long way into the dark under the glass. The walls were the same fused stone as the rest of the bunker, gray and smooth and a little shiny, as if they had been melted and let cool. Here and there on them were round black patches, the size of a cartwheel, where something had burned against the stone once and left its shadow. Nobody knew what. Sola had known. Sola was gone.
In the middle of the roof there was a shaft. A square hole going up through the stone, as wide as a man's two arms, all the way to the top of the dome and out. By day a gray light came down it and lay on the table in a long bar. Now, at midnight, nothing came down it at all except a little cold air, and when Corin looked up he could see, at the very top, a small square of sky with three stars in it.
The table took up most of the room.
It was huge. Longer than a cart, and wide enough for a man to lie down across it. It stood on iron legs as thick as Corin's arm, bolted to the floor. And its top was glass. A single sheet of clear glass as thick as his thumb, laid over a great map painted on old vellum. The vellum had gone brown and soft with the years, and the paint on it had faded, but you could still see it. The whole of the north, painted by hand. The Glass in the middle, gray. The Ridges. The Eye, a black ring. The shore. And down at the bottom, at the edge, where the Glass ran out and the good land began, a small painted tree with a crown in its branches, and a name beside it in old letters, that Lyra had read out to him on the first day.
Oakhaven.
The iron seats stood round the table, pushed back. Most of them. One lay on its side on the floor where it had fallen, a long time ago, and nobody had picked it up. At the head of the table was the only one that was not iron. A heavy seat of dark oak, with arms, and over the back of it a horse blanket, striped red and brown and yellow, faded almost to gray. Nobody sat in that one. Nobody had said so. Nobody did.
The brazier stood near the table, on three iron feet. It was full of the black stuff the Ashborn had found in the store room, the hard dark lumps that burned slow and hot without much smoke. It glowed red. The light from it came up from below and lay on everything in the room the wrong way round, so that faces had their shadows above them instead of below, and the iron legs of the table threw long black bars up the walls.
The old man's journal lay on the glass of the map, in the red light, by Corin's hand.
It was open.
It lay open at the back, where the folded pages were. Rough gray-brown paper, the cheap kind made of straw, that you could see the little bits of straw in if you held it to the light. Folded, and folded again, and again, down and down into small hard shining squares. The ones at the front were the size of Corin's palm. The ones at the back were smaller. The very last ones were as small as coins. Hard and smooth and bright, almost, the way a stone is bright in a stream. When Corin had tried to open them, on the first day, his fingers had slid off them as if they were glass.
That was the crease.
In the book. And in the old man.
Corin could feel it now. Inside himself. In the room inside him, the one at the back of his head where he kept the people he carried. It had been a crowded room once. Lyra had been in it, a long time ago, before he gave her back. And then Vivienne. And then Hask, and his share of Arran, and Sola, and the old man. All of them at once, some days, so that it was like living in a house full of relatives at a wake.
It was not crowded now.
Sola had gone out of it this morning, through his hands, to save Tansy. She had taken the rest of the room with her. The calm. The strength. The lame leg was his again. Hask was a hum, very low, curled up small in a corner. Arran was a warm coal, a laugh he could not quite hear.
And the old man was at the back.
Very small. Very thin. Hardly there at all. Sitting, Corin thought, if a voice could sit, in the far corner of the empty room. And beside him, in the corner, the crease. Corin could feel it there the way you feel a stone in your shoe. Small. Hard. Square. Folded down and down so tight that nothing could get into it.
Oakhaven, the old man had said, on their first night in the bunker, when he first showed it to them. That's the crease. I folded it in the book. And I folded it in me.
And tonight, on the stair, in the dark, going down to look at the journal one more time before he slept, Corin had heard him say something else.
It's time, boy. Tonight. I won't last another day. I can feel myself going thin. If we don't open it now, it goes with me, and nobody ever opens it.
And Corin had stood on the iron steps in the dark with the journal under his arm and listened, and then gone up to the top, to where Oren sat on watch in the way in, and told him.
Now they were all here.
Lyra stood on the other side of the table, across the glass from him, with her bound hand held against her chest and her face red on one side from the brazier and dark on the other. Vespera sat on one of the iron seats, pulled close to the brazier, with her old inked arms folded and her needle case on her knee, in its roll of soft leather. Kestrel stood by the blanket at the way in, with her spear upright beside her, the way she always stood. Looking at nothing. Listening to everything.
Oren was on the stair. Outside, at the top of the corridor. He had come down with Corin from the watch, as far as the bottom of the steps, and then stopped there, and sat. He never sat down in this room, not once, not even on the first night. He stood, or he stayed out. Tonight he stayed out. Corin did not know why. Something to do with the oak seat at the head of the table, he thought. Something Oren could not say.
And up above, at the way in to the dome, under the stars, the silent man sat facing south, and Tansy slept against his leg. Corin could not see them. But he knew they were there. Somebody always had to be at the top.
"Tell them," said Lyra. "What he told you."
Corin looked at her.
"He says he's the hand," he said. "The only one. He says that's the rule. You pay with the hand that would unfold it." He stopped. "He folded it. So it's his hand. So he pays."
"Pays what?" said Kestrel, from the way in.
Corin did not answer.
He did not have to. He saw Lyra's face. He saw it understand, before he could say it. The red side of it and the dark side of it, both at once, going still.
"All of him," said Lyra.
"Yes," said Corin.
And in the back of his head, in the far corner of the empty room, very small and very tired and very fond, the old man said: There. That wasn't so hard, was it?
Three Times
"No," said Corin.
He said it out loud. To the room. To the old man in the back of his head. To all of them.
Nobody answered him. Lyra did not move. Vespera did not look up from the brazier. Kestrel, at the blanket, did not even turn her head. They were waiting, he thought. Waiting for the old man to answer, and for him to tell them what the old man said.
The old man did not answer at once.
Corin felt him there, in the corner of the empty room. Felt him sigh. It was a strange thing, to feel a man sigh inside your own head. Like a draft under a shut window. A small cold breath of air going through you, and then gone.
Boy, said Malakor.
"No," said Corin again. "There has to be another way. You said yourself you didn't know how to open it. On the first night. You said you didn't know yet. So there might be another way. We could look. Lyra could look. It's her mother's art. The folding. She knows more about it than any of us." He turned to her. "Doesn't she? Lyra. Tell him."
Lyra looked at him across the glass.
She did not say anything for a long time. And while she said nothing, Corin watched her face, red on one side, dark on the other, and saw that she was not looking for another way. She was looking at him. At the gray side of his face. As if she were sorry for something.
"Fold it small," said Lyra quietly. "That's the first thing my mother taught. The thing everybody knows. Fold it small, and it keeps." She paused. "And the second thing. The thing only the folders know. You pay with the hand that would unfold it. That's the rule, Corin. It's not a rule somebody made. It's just how it is. Like water going downhill."
"Then find a different hand."
"There isn't one." Her voice did not change. "Whoever folds a thing is the only one who can open it again. Anybody else who tries, it just gets tighter. That's the point. That's why you fold it. So nobody can get in." She looked down at the journal on the glass, at the small hard bright squares of paper. "He made sure of it. He folded it so tight that not even the man with the gentle voice could get in. And the only way out is the way it went in. Through him."
"Through him," said Corin.
"Yes."
"And it costs all of him."
Lyra did not answer that.
And in the back of his head, the old man said: She's a clever girl. Her mother's girl. Listen to her.
"No," said Corin.
The third time.
And this time the old man laughed.
It was not much of a laugh. It was hardly there. A dry small sound, like a leaf going over on a stone. But Corin had heard it before. He had heard it a great many times, in the long months since the oak-leaf key. In rooms under the city. On the road north. On the glass. It was the laugh the old man laughed when Corin was being slow.
Fool, said Malakor.
And he said it the way he always said it. Not cruel. Fond. The way an old man says it to a dog that has brought him the wrong stick.
You fool. Listen to me, boy. Listen properly. I'm going to say it once, and I haven't got the breath for twice.
Corin listened.
He shut his eyes. He did not mean to. They shut by themselves, the way they shut when you are trying very hard to hear something far off. He stood at the long table with his living hand flat on the glass and his eyes shut and the red light of the brazier on his lids, and listened.
I'm old, said Malakor. Older than you know. Older than I like to say. I was born a long way from here, in a city of pyres, before your grandfather's grandfather was thought of. I came north when I was young and clever and stupid. I did terrible things. I did them very well. And I have been dead, boy, for a long time now. Since the night by the Edge. You know that.
I know, said Corin, inside.
Since that night, I've been in a rod. A bit of silver in a young man's pocket. And then in an old man's pocket. And then in yours. And then in your head. And in all that time, what have I done? A pause. Talked. That's all. Talked, and watched, and been carried about like a coat. The leaf went over on the stone again. A coat that's too small. I've told you that.
Corin did not answer.
I'm very tired, boy, said Malakor. I'm so thin now I can see through myself. I won't last another day. Not after this morning. Sola took the room with her when she went, and what's left of me is holding on to the back of it with my fingernails. One more day, and I'll be gone anyway. And the crease goes with me. Oakhaven goes with me. All of it. Every face. Shut up tight forever where nobody can ever get at it.
And your sister, said the old man. More quietly. She goes with it too.
Corin opened his eyes.
He did not know he had done it until he saw the red light on the glass in front of him, and the small bright squares of the journal, and Lyra's face across the table, watching him.
Sister, he said inside. What sister?
Open it, said Malakor. And you'll see.
Corin stood very still.
He had not known. He had not known there was a sister. He had not known anything. Not his own first name, not his mother's, not the village he was born in, not his own face as a child. All of it gone, long ago, before he could remember. Before Linus. Before anything. Linus had written it, in the letter he left: You were brought to me when you were very young, with nothing.
With nothing.
And there was a sister.
Let me do something, said Malakor. Very softly now. So softly that Corin had to hold his breath to hear. For once. In all this long, long time. Let me spend myself on something. Something better than what I spent myself on before.
Corin did not say anything.
Let me spend myself on something, for once, said the old man again. Please, boy.
And it was the please that did it.
Corin had never heard him say it before. Not once. Not in all the months. Not in the rooms under the city or on the road or on the glass. The old man gave orders. He grumbled. He laughed his small dry laugh and called Corin a fool. He did not ask.
He was asking.
Corin looked down at the journal on the glass. At the folded squares. At his own living hand, lying beside them, brown and young and scarred. And the gray hand, hanging at his side, which he could not feel.
He looked up at Lyra.
"He says please," said Corin.
Lyra did not say anything. But he saw her eyes go wet in the red light, and stay wet, and she did not wipe them.
"He's never said please," said Corin.
"I know," said Lyra.
Corin looked at the journal a long time.
And then he said, out loud, to the room, and to the old man, and to himself: "All right."
The Stair
Before he lay down, he went out to Oren.
He did not know why. Nobody asked him to. He only knew, standing at the long table with the old man's book in front of him and the old man's please still in his ears, that he could not lie down on the stone and go into the crease without going out to Oren first.
So he went.
Out past Kestrel, who stepped aside from the blanket without a word. Through the blanket. Into the corridor. It was dark there, and narrow, and the walls were the same fused gray stone, close on both sides, so that his crutch knocked on one wall and his shoulder brushed the other. The corridor went back toward the stair, past the black openings of the sleeping room and the store room, and the air in it was cold and smelled of iron and of the cistern, wet stone and ferns.
At the end of it, where the iron steps came down, there was a little light. Starlight, from the way in, far up at the top of the stair, coming down and down the iron steps and lying pale on the last few of them.
Oren was sitting on the bottom step.
He had his sword across his knees. Of course he did. Corin heard it before he saw it. The soft long hiss of the stone going down the blade, in the dark. Sssss. And a pause. Sssss.
It stopped when Corin came.
Oren looked up. His big face was gray in the starlight, the beard and the broken nose and the scar through the eyebrow, all gray, like a face carved in old wood.
"Lad," he said.
"Oren."
"Is it now?"
"Yes."
Oren nodded slowly. He looked at the sword on his knees, and at the stone in his hand, and put the stone away, in his pocket, carefully.
"You'll be lying down," he said. "In there. On the floor. With the old man's book."
"Yes."
"And going in."
"Yes."
"Like you went in to the girl's father. That time. With her calling you."
Corin looked at him.
"You remember that?"
"I remember her voice," said Oren. "After. It was gone to nothing. She couldn't talk for two days. I remember that." He paused. "I don't remember much else. But I remember her voice."
They were quiet.
Corin stood at the bottom of the iron stair, leaning on his crutch, and looked down at the big man on the step. And he found he did not know what he had come out to say. He had thought he knew. He had thought there was something. Now he was here, and there was nothing in his mouth at all.
Oren seemed to understand.
He did not ask. He sat on the bottom step with the sword across his knees and looked up at Corin and waited, the way Lyra had waited for him on the stair that afternoon. Not hurrying. Just there.
"He's going," said Corin at last. "The old man. When he opens it. He goes with it. All of him. That's the price."
Oren did not say anything.
"He's been with me a long time," said Corin. "Since the city. Since the key. He's been there every day. In my head. Grumbling." He stopped. "I didn't like him, at first. He was cruel. He'd done cruel things, and he was proud of being good at them. He said so." He stopped again. "And then he wasn't. Or he was, but he was other things too. And now he's going."
"Aye," said Oren.
"And I don't know how to be without him."
It came out before he could stop it. He heard it come out, into the dark corridor, and go away down it, and not come back.
Oren looked at him a long time.
Then he got up.
Slowly. With one hand on the iron rail and the other holding the sword. His back cracked. He stood up on the bottom step, so that he was a little taller than Corin, and he looked down at him.
"I've lost a thing today," said Oren. "You know that. You wrote it. I don't know how to be without it either." He looked at the sword in his hand. "I sat on that stair all afternoon trying to work out how. And I'll tell you what I worked out, lad, for what it's worth. It's not much."
Corin waited.
"You do the next thing," said Oren. "That's all. You told me that yourself. This afternoon. You said you read your book and you do the next thing, and if you don't know what the next thing is, you ask the lass." A corner of his mouth went up, under the beard. "It's good sense. I've been doing it all evening. It works."
Corin did not say anything.
"So go in," said Oren. "That's the next thing. Go in, and let the old man do what he wants to do. He's a grown man. Older than both of us put together, by the sound of it. If he wants to spend himself, it's his to spend." He paused. "And when you come out, there'll be another next thing. And another. And the lass will be there. And so will I."
He turned.
He put the sword up, carefully, by the grip, and laid it over his shoulder, across his back, and held it there with one hand. And with the other hand on the iron rail, he went up three steps. Four. And stopped, and turned round, and stood there, on the stair, in the starlight, facing down the corridor toward the gray blanket at the far end.
Feet apart. Knees soft. Weight low.
Standing.
"I'll be here," said Oren. "In the way in to the corridor. Nobody comes down this stair till you're out. Not the man in gray, if he comes back. Not anybody."
"Nobody's going to come," said Corin.
"No," said Oren. "I know. But I'll be here anyway."
Corin looked up at him. At the big man standing on the iron stair with a sword he could not use over his shoulder, in the starlight, in the way.
And he understood why he had come out.
Not to say anything. Not to be told anything. Just to see this. Just to know, before he lay down on the stone and went into the dark, that there was a man standing at the top of the corridor who would not let anything by.
"Thank you," said Corin.
"Go on, lad," said Oren. "The old man's waiting."
And Corin turned, and went back down the dark corridor, step and crutch and step, toward the blanket and the red light behind it. And he did not look back. He did not need to. He could feel Oren there, behind him, the whole way. The way you feel a wall at your back.
