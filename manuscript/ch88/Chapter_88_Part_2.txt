Chapter 88: The Crease, Part 2 of 5: The Rope
The Rope
They got ready the way people get ready for a birth, or a death. Quietly. Without much talk. Each of them doing a small thing with their hands, so that their hands would have something to do.
Vespera got up off her iron seat and dragged it back out of the way, scraping, across the stone floor. She knelt down on the floor at the end of the table, by the brazier, and unrolled her needle case on the stone beside her. The soft leather fell open. Inside, in a row of little loops, were the needles. Long and fine and dark, some of them bone and some of them steel, and the little stoppered pots of ink, black and blue and a brown that was almost red. She laid them out the way a cook lays out knives. Each one straight. Each one in its place. Then she sat back on her heels and looked at Corin and said nothing.
Kestrel did not move from the blanket. But she took her spear and laid it down, flat, along the floor at her feet. Corin had never seen her put her spear down before. Not once, in all the days. She stood with her hands empty at her sides and watched him.
And Lyra came round the table.
She came round the end of it, past the oak seat with the striped blanket over its back, and stood beside him. Close. Closer than she had stood to him in days. Since the Eye. Since the dark under the dome, on the first night, when he had reached for her without thinking and felt, for one breath, the last thing she had of her mother come loose in her like a tooth.
She did not touch him.
"You know what I have to do," she said.
"Yes."
"I'll be your rope. The way I was with my father." She held up her right hand. The bound one. The white linen Vespera had wound round it on the glass that afternoon, tight round the palm and between the thumb and the finger and round the wrist, with a red line showing through it now across the middle. "You'll go in, and I'll hold on, and I'll call you. All the time. Your name. Your other name. Until you come back up."
"In the Eye," said Corin.
"I know."
"If I touch your skin—"
"I know." She did not look away. "That's why it's this hand."
He looked at it. The linen. Thick. Wound round and round.
"There's cloth between," said Lyra. "All the way round. Vespera did it well. You won't touch me. Not my skin. You'll be holding linen with my hand inside it." She paused. "It's the best I can do, Corin. It'll have to be enough."
He looked at her face.
And he thought about the last time. With her father. He had gone down into Aurelian's last night for hours, and she had held on to him, and called him, the whole last hour without stopping, Corin, Corin, Corin, until her voice was gone to a whisper. He did not remember it well. It was in the Ledger. He had read it many mornings since. SHE CALLED ME THE WHOLE LAST HOUR. THAT IS HOW I GOT BACK.
"It'll be enough," said Corin.
He lay down.
On the floor, at the side of the table, between the iron legs and the brazier. The stone was cold under his back. Colder than he had thought. It came up through his coat and his shirt and into the inked lines on his ribs, Vespera's lines, the grounding ones, so that he could feel them, almost, like threads of cold drawn across his skin.
Lyra knelt down by him. On his right side. His living side.
Vespera knelt by his head.
And Lyra reached up onto the glass of the table and took the old man's journal and laid it on his chest.
It was heavy. Heavier than it looked. It lay on his breastbone like a stone, open at the back, with the folded squares of gray paper sticking up out of it in their small hard bright stacks. He could feel the edges of them through his shirt. He could feel the weight of the book go down into him with every breath and come up again.
He put his gray hand on it.
He did not decide to. The gray hand moved, by itself, the way it had moved on the glass that morning. It came up from the floor at his side, slowly, heavy and stiff and numb, and settled on the book, flat, over the folded pages. He could not feel it. He could only see it, if he tipped his head and looked down. Gray. Black at the fingertips. Lying on the old man's book like a hand of stone laid on a grave.
And then Lyra took his living hand in her bound one.
He felt the linen. Rough. Warm from her. Wound tight. And under the linen, inside it, he could feel the shape of her hand. The bones of her fingers. The heat of her palm. The beat of her blood, very faint, through the cloth. She closed her hand on his. Carefully. Her fingers wrapped in white going round his brown ones and holding.
No skin. Not anywhere. He checked. He could not help it. He lay on the cold stone and felt along the edge of her hand with his, and there was linen everywhere. Linen between them all the way round.
It was the closest they had been since the Eye.
"All right?" said Lyra.
"Yes."
Vespera leaned over him. Upside down, from where he lay. He saw her old face hanging over his, lit red from below by the brazier, with the deep lines in it running the wrong way. She put her thumb on the corner of his mouth. The left corner. The gray one.
"Say it," said Vespera.
"Corin."
"The other one."
"Corin Vane."
"Good. Keep it there." She took her thumb away. "I'll be here. If you start to go, I'll know. I'll see it in your face before she feels it in your hand. And I'll ink you back." She touched the needle case beside her. "I've done it before. I can do it again."
"Thank you," said Corin.
"Don't thank me," said Vespera. "Come back. That's thanks."
And she sat back on her heels, out of his sight.
Corin lay on the floor of Sola's command room and looked up.
Above him was the underside of the table. The thick glass of it, and through the glass, from below, he could see the back of the old vellum map, brown and blotched, and the faint shapes of the painted land showing through it the wrong way round. The Glass. The Ridges. The Eye. And at the edge, where the map ran out, a dark small blot that he knew was the tree with the crown. Oakhaven. Seen from underneath.
And past the edge of the table, the roof. And the shaft going up through it. And at the top of the shaft, the small square of sky, with the three stars in it.
One of them had moved. There were two now, and a third just at the edge, going.
He looked inside.
Into the empty room at the back of his head. Swept and bare and quiet. Hask's hum, low, in one corner. Arran's coal, warm, in another. And in the far corner, the old man. So thin now that Corin could hardly see him, the way you can hardly see a man standing in front of a window with the sun behind him. Just a shape. A thinness in the light.
And beside him, the crease.
Small and square and hard and folded, sitting in the corner of the room like a box nobody has ever been able to open.
Ready? said Malakor.
Corin breathed.
In. Out. He felt the book go up and down on his chest. He felt Lyra's hand round his, through the linen. He heard the brazier tick and settle. He heard Kestrel, at the blanket, not breathing at all.
"Go on, then, old man," said Corin. Out loud.
And in the corner of the empty room, very small and very far off, he heard the old man laugh. The dry leaf, going over on the stone. For the last time, he thought. Or nearly the last.
That's better, said Malakor.
And reached out a hand Corin could not see, and laid it on the crease, and began to open it.
The Oak-Leaf Room
It opened the way a fist opens.
Slowly. One finger at a time. Corin felt it. In the corner of the empty room at the back of his head, the small hard square of the crease began to loosen. A corner came up. Then another. The way a folded letter, left on a warm windowsill, begins to open by itself, one fold and then the next, as the paper remembers what shape it used to be.
And as each fold came open, the old man got thinner.
Corin felt that too. He felt it the way you feel a candle burning down in a dark room: not the flame, but the dark getting a little bigger round it. Each fold that opened, a little more of Malakor went into opening it. Went out of him. Was spent. Like a coin put down on a counter that you will not get back.
And then the last fold of the first square came open.
And Corin was somewhere else.
He was standing in a room.
A round room. Gray. Under the ground. He knew it was under the ground at once, from the air. It was the air of a place that has never had the sun in it. Cool and still and a little damp, and smelling of stone, and old paper, and lamp oil, and something else, sharp and bitter, under everything. Like the smell of a jar that has had something burned in it.
The walls were round. All the way round. No corners anywhere. Smooth gray stone, with the marks of the tools still in it, long and curved, where somebody had cut the room out of the rock a very long time ago. There was one lamp, on a hook, burning low. There was a cot against the wall, narrow, with a gray blanket on it, pulled straight and tucked in tight, the way a man tucks a blanket who has made the same bed every morning for years and years.
And on the far wall, a rack.
A wooden rack, dark with age, with pegs in it, row on row. And on the pegs, rods. Silver rods. Each one as long as a man's hand, with a twist in it, like a screw. Rows and rows of them, catching the lamplight. Some of them were bright and new. Some were dull. Some were black with age. They all twisted the same way. To the right.
Corin knew this room.
He had never been in it. He had stood outside it, in the dark under the Ministry, with the oak-leaf key in his hand. He had read about it, in the old man's book. And he had heard about it, all the long months, in the old man's grumbling voice. My room. Before they built their great gray house on top of it. Before any of them.
He knew it.
And on the table in the middle of it, under the lamp, there was a stain.
An old ink stain, soaked into the wood. Dark blue, gone almost black. It had spread out a long time ago, when somebody knocked over a pot, and dried, and never come out. And it was the shape of a hare. Corin saw it at once. A hare, running. The long ears laid back. The back legs stretched out behind. The front legs reaching.
The hare-shaped stain.
And at the table, sitting, with his hands flat on the wood on either side of the hare, was an old man.
Corin had never seen him.
In all the months, he had never once seen his face. He had heard his voice. He had felt him move about in the room at the back of his head. He had felt him laugh, and sigh, and grumble, and sleep. But he had never seen him. Malakor had never let him see.
He saw him now.
He was a thin man. Very thin. Tall, Corin thought, though it was hard to tell with him sitting. With long arms, and long hands, brown and spotted, with big knuckles, lying on the table. His hair was white. All of it. Long, and thin, and tied at the back of his neck with a strip of black cloth. His face was long and brown and lined, deeply, all over, like the bark of an old tree. A big nose, bent at the top as if it had been broken once. A mouth that turned down at the corners, the mouth of a man who has spent his life being displeased with things.
And his eyes.
His eyes were dark. Almost black. And they were looking at nothing. They were looking at the hare on the table, and not seeing it.
This was a memory. Corin knew that. He was not really here. He was lying on the stone floor of Sola's command room with a book on his chest and Lyra's linen hand round his. And this was the old man's memory, opening, one fold at a time. A night long ago. The night the crease began.
There was a knock.
The old man did not look up.
The knock came again, and then the way in to the round room opened. Corin turned to look. There was a way in, in the curved wall, that he had not seen before. A thick wooden one, dark, carved all over with oak leaves. It swung inward. And a young man came through it, quickly, with a lamp in his hand and a paper in the other.
A clerk. Corin could see that at once. Thin and pale, with ink on his fingers and a gray coat too big for him, and his hair falling into his eyes. Young. Hardly more than a boy. Out of breath, as if he had run down a great many stairs.
"Master," said the young clerk.
The old man did not look up.
"Master Malakor. The Council." The clerk held out the paper. His hand was shaking. "They sat tonight. Late. They've written it off. Oakhaven. All of it. The whole village." He swallowed. "It says fever. Lost to fever. They've voted the money already. For the fever."
The old man looked up.
And Corin felt it. Not saw it. Felt it, the way you feel it in a memory. The old man's heart, inside him, going cold. Not fast. Not loud. Just cold, all at once, the way a pond goes cold when the sun goes behind a cloud.
There is no fever, the old man thought.
Corin heard him think it. Clearly. In the old voice, but younger, and harder.
There is no fever in Oakhaven. There was never any fever. I know what there is in Oakhaven. I know who went up there last week with the lamps.
He stood up.
"Corvell," he said.
The young clerk jumped.
"Fetch my coat," said Malakor. "And my book. The black one. From the shelf."
The clerk did not move.
"Master," he said. "Master, you can't. You don't—" He stopped. His thin face went red, and then white. "You don't know the way. You gave it away. The way out. You haven't been out of this room since—since before I was born."
The old man looked at him.
"I know," said Malakor.
And Corin felt it in him. The hole. The place at the back of the old man where the way out of this room used to be. A hole like the holes in Oren. Clean-edged and old. He had given it away a long, long time ago, to keep a promise, and he had sat in this round room ever since, and made rods, and written his book, and not once gone up the stairs, because he did not know where the stairs were.
"I know," said Malakor again. "So you'll have to lead me, boy. Won't you?"
The young clerk stared at him.
And then he went and got the coat. And the book. A big book. Black leather. With red edges that were not faded yet, but bright, like new paint.
And the old man put on the coat, and took the book under his arm, and held out his hand.
And Corvell, the young clerk with ink on his fingers, who was kind and careful and too soft for this work, took the old man's hand in his. Like a child. Like leading a child across a road. And led him out of the round room, through the oak-leaf way, and up the dark stairs he could not remember, one at a time, into the night.
The Crate
The next fold opened onto rain.
Corin felt it before he saw it. Cold. On his face. On the backs of his hands. Heavy, steady rain, the kind that comes down straight with no wind behind it and goes on all night. He could hear it everywhere. On the road. On the grass at the side of the road. On the hood of a coat that was not his, pulled down low over a face that was not his. On leaves somewhere, a great many leaves, in the dark.
He was walking.
The old man was walking, and Corin was inside him, walking too. Along a road. North. He knew it was north. He did not know how. The road went up a long slope between dark hedges, and it was mud, all of it, deep soft mud that sucked at the old man's boots at every step and let them go with a wet sound. There was no moon. There were no stars. There was only the rain, and the dark, and far ahead up the slope, small and yellow and swinging, a light.
A lamp. On a cart.
The old man was following it.
Corin did not know how long he had been following it. Days, maybe. The memory did not say. It only had this one night in it, this one stretch of road. The rain and the mud and the swinging light ahead, and the old man's breath going in and out, short and hard, the breath of a man too old to be walking this far in this weather. And his legs aching. And the black book under his coat, held against his chest to keep it dry.
Corvell was not with him.
Corin felt that, too. The old man had sent him back. Somewhere, a long way behind, at the city gate, or the first crossroads. Go home, boy. You've done enough. You've led me out. I can find my own way from here. And the young clerk had stood in the road with his lamp and not wanted to go. And gone.
The cart had stopped.
The old man saw it stop, ahead, and stopped himself. He stood in the dark at the side of the road, by the hedge, with the rain running off the hood of his coat, and watched.
The lamp stopped swinging. It hung on its hook at the back of the cart, still, and lit a small round yellow world in the rain. Corin could see the back of the cart in it. A big flat cart, a farm cart, with high wooden sides. Two horses, standing with their heads down in the rain. And on the seat at the front, two shapes. Men. In gray. With an oilcloth pulled over the pair of them, shining wet in the lamplight, like the back of a seal.
They did not move.
The old man waited. A long time. Long enough for the rain to soak through the shoulders of his coat. And the men under the oilcloth did not move, and did not move, and then one of them made a sound, a long low rattling sound, and Corin understood that they were asleep.
The old man went forward.
Slowly. In the mud. Keeping to the side of the road, by the hedge, where the grass was. Until he was at the back of the cart, in the yellow light of the lamp.
There was a crate on the cart.
One big crate. Long. Wooden. Made of rough planks nailed together, not well. Taller than the sides of the cart, so that the lid of it stuck up above them. And on the side of it, painted in white, in big rough letters, words. The rain had made the paint run in long white streaks down the planks, but you could still read them.
Corin could still read them.
OAKHAVEN. BRIGHT.
And under it, smaller:
TO THE VAULT.
The old man looked at the words a long time.
And then he took something out of his coat. Corin felt his hand go in, and close on it. A short iron bar. Flat at one end. The kind a man uses to open crates. He had brought it with him. He had known what he would find.
He climbed up onto the back of the cart.
It was hard. He was old. His knees hurt. The wood of the cart was wet and slippery, and the mud on his boots made it worse. But he got up, in the end, and knelt on the boards at the back of the cart in the rain, and set the flat end of the iron bar under the edge of the lid.
And pushed.
The nails came up with a long thin scream. Corin heard it. He felt the old man's heart stop at the sound. He felt him freeze, on his knees, with the bar in his hands, and look at the two shapes under the oilcloth at the front of the cart.
They did not move.
One of them rattled again, in his sleep.
The old man pushed the lid up, and over, and looked in.
Faces.
That was all Corin could see, at first. Faces. In the dark of the crate, under the lid, in the yellow light of the lamp coming in sideways. Small faces. Pale. Close together. Looking up.
Children.
Seven of them. Corin counted. He could not help it. He counted the way Lyra counted. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven. Seven small faces, packed into a crate on a farm cart in the rain, on straw. Lying close together on their sides, under sacks, the way puppies lie in a basket. Their hair was wet with the rain that had come in through the gaps in the planks. Their faces were white and still.
And they were quiet.
That was the worst thing. Corin felt the old man feel it. They were so quiet. Nobody was crying. Nobody was calling for anybody. They lay on the straw in the crate and looked up at the old man with the iron bar, and their faces did not change, and they did not make a sound. As if crying were a thing they had not been taught. Or a thing that had been taken.
The old man knelt on the wet boards and looked at them.
And Corin felt him think.
Seven.
Seven, and I am old, and I am alone, and I am on foot. And the Glass is ten days' walk from here. And I can carry one.
One.
He could not take them all. Corin felt him know it. He felt him look at it from every side, quickly, the way a man looks at a fire for a way through. Seven children. Two horses. Two sleeping men. Could he take the cart? No. He could not drive a cart. He had never learned. And the men would wake. Could he wake the children and tell them to run? Where? Into the dark, in the rain, in the mud, seven small quiet children with nothing in their heads? They would not run. They did not know how. They did not know anything.
One.
He could carry one.
He looked at the faces. One by one. In the lamplight. And Corin looked at them with him, and did not know any of them, and wanted to look away and could not.
And then one of them moved.
At the end of the crate. Nearest the old man. A boy. Not the biggest. Not the smallest. A thin boy with dark hair, plastered flat and wet to his head, and a thin face, and big dark eyes. He was lying on his side like the others, under a sack. But his eyes were moving. They were looking at the old man. Not the way the others looked. The others looked at nothing. This boy was looking at him.
He was awake.
Really awake. Corin could see it. There was somebody in there. Behind the eyes. Somebody who had not been emptied out. Not all the way. Not yet.
The boy sat up.
He did it slowly, pushing the wet sack off himself. The other children did not move. He sat up in the crate, among them, with the straw sticking to his clothes and the rain running down his face, and he looked at the old man.
He did not cry.
He did not ask who the old man was, or what he wanted, or where they were going. He looked at the iron bar, and at the old man's face, under the hood, and Corin saw him understand. All at once. The way children understand things sometimes, faster than grown people. That the old man had come to take somebody. And that he could only take one.
And the boy said:
"Take my sister."
His voice was small and rough, like a voice that has not been used for days.
"She's in the blue wagon," said the boy. "Take her instead."
The old man did not move.
"She's in the blue wagon," said the boy again. And he turned his head and looked past the old man. Up the road. Into the rain.
And the old man turned and looked too.
And Corin, inside him, looked.
Far up the road. Far ahead. At the top of the long slope, where the hedges ended and the dark began. A light. Another lamp. Small, and yellow, and swinging. On another cart. Going away.
Very far away. So far that it was only a speck. A spark in the rain.
The blue wagon.
The old man watched it. Corin felt him watch it. Felt him measure it. The distance. The mud. His own old legs. The horses that pulled it, fresh, and the men who drove it, awake. And he knew. Corin felt him know it, all at once, cold and plain. He could not catch it. He would never catch it. It was already gone.
The light went over the top of the hill, and was gone.
And the old man turned back to the crate.
To the boy. Sitting up in the straw among the quiet ones. Looking at him with his big dark eyes, with somebody still in them.
"I can't," said Malakor. "I'm sorry. I can't reach her."
The boy looked at him.
And the old man reached down into the crate, in the rain, with his long brown spotted hands, and lifted out the boy.
He was light. So light. Like a bundle of sticks. Corin felt the weight of him come up out of the straw and into the old man's arms. Felt the cold wet thinness of him through his clothes, and his heart going, quick and small, like a bird's. Felt him not struggle. Felt him not cry.
Felt him turn his head, over the old man's shoulder, and look back up the road. At the place on the top of the hill where the light had gone.
And Corin, lying on the stone floor of Sola's command room, a long way off, with a book on his chest, felt his own face go wet.
He knew who the boy was.
He had known since the boy sat up.
