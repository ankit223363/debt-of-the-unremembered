Chapter 88: The Crease, Part 3 of 5: The Ditch
The Ditch
The old man did not put the lid back.
Corin felt him think about it. Felt him look down at the six small faces left in the straw, quiet, looking up at nothing. And at the iron bar in his hand. And at the lid, lying crooked across the top of the crate where he had pushed it. He could put it back. He could push the nails down into their holes again with the heel of his hand. The men under the oilcloth might not notice, in the morning, in the rain, that the crate was one child lighter. They might drive on to wherever they were going and never know.
He did not put it back.
He pulled the sacks up over the six, a little higher, to keep the rain off their faces. That was all. He did it quickly, with one hand, the other arm round the boy. And then he climbed down off the back of the cart, slowly, his old knees cracking, the boy held against his chest. And stood in the mud.
And one of the men under the oilcloth woke up.
Corin felt it before the old man saw it. A change in the dark. The rattling breath stopping. A long silence, the silence of a man lying awake trying to work out what woke him. And then the oilcloth moved. Lifted, at one corner. A shape sat up under it.
The old man ran.
Not far. He could not run far. He was old and the mud was deep and the boy, light as he was, was a weight in his arms. He ran three steps, four, to the side of the road. To the hedge. And there was a ditch there, at the foot of the hedge, the way there is always a ditch at the foot of a hedge on a country road. Deep. Full of black water and dead leaves and the rain.
He went down into it.
Corin felt the cold. The water came up over the old man's boots, over his knees, to his waist. Icy. Thick with mud. Stinking of rot. And the old man went down into it with the boy held up against his chest, out of the water, and crouched there, in the black ditch, under the hedge, with the thorns of it catching in his hood and the rain coming down on both of them.
And lay still.
Up on the road, the man in gray had got down from the cart.
Corin could hear him. Boots in the mud. Slow. Heavy. A man who has just woken up and is not sure yet whether he has heard anything or dreamed it. The boots went round the side of the cart. To the back. Stopped.
The lamp moved.
The man had taken it down from its hook. Corin saw the light of it swing, through the gaps in the hedge, yellow, over the wet road, over the back of the cart, over the crate with its lid pushed crooked.
The boots did not move for a long time.
Then a voice. Low. Rough with sleep.
"Dell."
Nothing.
"Dell. Get up. The lid's off."
The oilcloth rustled. A groan. Another voice, thicker, further off.
"What?"
"The lid. On the crate. It's been pushed off."
"Wind."
"There's no wind."
A long pause. Then the second man, getting down. Slower. Grumbling. Two pairs of boots now, in the mud, at the back of the cart. And the lamp held up high, so that its light went out wider, over the road and the hedge and the ditch.
The old man did not breathe.
Corin felt him not breathe. Felt him crouch lower in the black water, so that it came up over his chest, and his chin, and the rain ran down his face into his mouth. Felt him hold the boy up, out of it, against his shoulder, with both arms. Felt the boy's heart going against his neck, quick and small, like a bird's.
The boy did not move.
The boy did not make a sound.
"Count them," said the first man, up on the road.
The lamp went down, into the crate. Corin heard the straw rustle. Heard the man counting under his breath, the way men count who do not count often. Slowly. Moving his lips.
"Six."
A pause.
"Count again."
"Six. I can count, Dell. There's six."
"There was seven."
"Was there?"
"There was seven on the list. Seven out of the big house. I wrote it myself."
Another pause. Longer.
"Well," said the second man. "There's six now."
The lamp came up out of the crate. And swung. Out, over the road. Over the mud. Over the hedge. Slowly. Up and down the hedge, along it, the yellow light going in through the gaps between the branches, catching the rain, catching the wet leaves, catching the black shine of the ditch water at the foot of it.
It stopped on the ditch.
The old man's heart stopped with it. Corin felt it stop.
The light lay on the black water a hand's width from his face. He could see the rain dimpling the water in it. He could see the dead leaves floating. He could see his own breath, if he had breathed.
The light moved on.
Along the ditch. Away. Up the hedge. Back to the road.
"Fox," said the second man.
"A fox didn't take a child out of a crate."
"No. But a fox might have knocked the lid. And one of them might have climbed out. And wandered off. They do that, sometimes, the empty ones. Walk off. You've seen it." A pause. "It'll be in a field somewhere by morning. Lying down. It won't get far."
"It'll be on the list."
"Then we'll change the list."
A long silence.
And then the first man laughed. A short, tired, ugly laugh.
"Seven out of the big house," he said. "And we lose one. On a night like this." The boots squelched. "Put the lid back, then. And give me the bottle."
The old man stayed in the ditch.
He stayed while they put the lid back, and he heard the nails go in with the heel of a boot, one, two, three. He stayed while they climbed back up onto the seat and pulled the oilcloth over themselves. He stayed while the horses were woken, and the reins slapped, and the cart creaked, and moved, and went on up the road, with its lamp swinging, slowly, into the rain. Toward the top of the hill. Where the blue wagon had gone.
He stayed until the light was gone over the top of the hill and the sound of the wheels was gone and there was nothing left but the rain.
Then he climbed out.
It was the hardest thing Corin had ever felt anybody do. Harder than the crate. The old man's legs had gone dead in the cold water. His hands had gone dead. He could not feel the boy in his arms. He got up out of the ditch on his knees and his elbows, in the mud, with the boy held against his chest, and lay on the grass at the side of the road in the rain for a long time, shaking, before he could stand.
And the boy lay on his chest and looked at him.
In the dark. Corin could not see the boy's face. But he could feel him looking. Feel the small cold weight of him lying on the old man's chest, and the face turned up, and the eyes open.
"Seven out of the big house," said the boy.
It was the second thing he had said.
The old man did not answer.
"That's us," said the boy. "The big house. That's where we live. Me and my sister." He was quiet a moment. "They wrote it on a list."
"Yes," said the old man.
"Is she on the list too?"
The old man lay in the rain and did not answer for a long time.
"I expect so," he said at last.
And the boy put his head down on the old man's wet coat, and was quiet, and did not cry. And after a while the old man got up, and took him up, and turned his back on the road and the hill and the place where the lights had gone, and went off into the fields. Into the dark. North.
The Glass
The folds came faster after that.
Corin felt them going. One after another, in the corner of the empty room at the back of his head. Click. Click. Click. Like the pages of a book turning by themselves in a wind. And each one that opened showed him a little more, and each one took a little more of the old man with it, and the old man did not stop.
He saw a road in the dark, going north, with the rain stopped and the mud gone hard and cold. An old man walking on it with a child on his back. The child's arms round his neck. The child's head on his shoulder, asleep, or pretending.
He saw a village at dawn, small and gray, with smoke going straight up from the chimneys, and the old man going round the edge of it, through the wet fields, so that nobody would see.
He saw a barn, and straw, and the old man lying in the straw with his eyes open, all day, listening, with the child asleep against his side. And at dusk, the road again.
And then he saw the Edge.
He knew it at once. He had stood there himself, not so long ago, coming the other way. The place where the good land stopped and the Glass began. A clean line, as if somebody had drawn it with a rule. On one side, grass, and dead bracken, and stones, and the last thin trees. On the other side, nothing. Gray. Flat. Shining a little, under the sky, the way the sea shines on a dull day. Going away north forever.
The old man stood at the line with the boy beside him.
Not on his back now. Standing. Holding his hand. A thin small boy with dark hair, in clothes too big for him that the old man had got somewhere, from somebody's washing line, perhaps. A coat that came down past his knees. Sleeves turned back and back. Boots stuffed with straw so they would stay on.
The boy looked at the Glass.
"Is that where we're going?" he said.
It was nearly the first thing he had said since the night in the ditch. Corin heard it, in the old man's ears. A small rough voice. Careful. As if each word cost something, and the boy was not sure he could pay.
"Yes," said Malakor.
"Why?"
"Because they won't follow us there," said Malakor. "Not for a long time. Not on foot. And there's a place, out there, where we can hide. A place I know. Somebody I knew built it." He was quiet a moment. "She's dead now. But the place is still there."
The boy looked at the Glass a long time.
"What about my sister?" he said.
The old man did not answer.
And they stepped over the line.
Corin felt it. The change. Through the old man's boots, and up his legs. The ground was not ground anymore. It was hard. Smooth. Warm, even in the cold morning, the way a stone is warm that has been lying all day in the sun. And under the warmth, so low that you felt it in your teeth before you heard it, the hum.
The old man walked. The boy walked beside him.
And Corin saw their footprints.
He saw them because the old man looked back, once, at the end of the first day. Stopped, and turned, and looked back the way they had come. And there they were. On the gray glass behind them. Two lines of footprints. The old man's, long and flat, deep at the heel. And beside them, the boy's. Small. Close together. A little crooked, because the boots were too big and the straw had shifted.
They had sunk into the glass.
Not much. A finger's depth. But they were there. Where the glass was warm and soft near the Edge, their weight had pressed into it, the way a foot presses into wet sand. And the glass had kept the shape. And as Corin watched, through the old man's eyes, the edges of the prints went smooth and shiny in the low sun, and hardened, and set.
The glass would keep them. He knew that. Without knowing how. The glass kept everything.
The old man looked at the small prints a long time.
Then he turned, and took the boy's hand again, and walked on.
And the days went by. Corin saw them going, fold by fold. Click. Click. The old man and the boy, walking. By night, mostly. Resting by day under a coat stretched over two sticks. The old man getting slower. The boy getting thinner. Water from a skin, a little at a time. Bread, harder and harder. The hum in their teeth. The white sky. The stars.
The Ridges. Black and broken, going up out of the flat glass like the backs of huge drowned beasts.
The short way.
And the Eye.
Corin saw it open out in front of them, black and clear and flat, with the drowned valley far down under it, and the dome sitting in the middle. He saw the old man stand on the gray stony shore and look at it. And he felt what the old man felt. Not glad. Not afraid. Tired. So tired. And something else, under the tiredness, that Corin did not have a name for. A kind of sorrow, old and worn smooth, like a stone carried a long time in a pocket.
Sola, the old man thought. I've come back. I'm sorry. I've brought you a child.
And they went across the black glass, and in at the way in, and down the iron steps, into the dark.
Into this room.
Corin saw it. Sola's command room. The long table. The glass top. The map under it. The iron seats. The oak one at the head, with the striped blanket over its back, brighter then, the red and the brown and the yellow not faded yet. The shaft in the roof. The brazier, standing on its three iron feet. All of it the same. Many years younger, and the same.
And the old man lit the brazier, and they stayed.
Weeks. Corin felt them go by. A long time. Long enough for the boy to stop being afraid of the dark under the glass. Long enough for him to learn the way to the cistern and let the bucket down himself, the way Tansy had this afternoon. Long enough for the old man to begin, very slowly, to sleep at night.
And one evening, by the brazier, the boy drew.
Corin saw it.
He saw it so clearly that it hurt. The boy, sitting on the floor of this room, by the brazier, cross-legged, in his too-big coat with the sleeves turned back. With a scrap of paper on the stone in front of him. Rough gray-brown paper, the cheap straw kind, torn out of the back of the old man's black book. And a stub of charcoal from the brazier, black, in his small fist.
And he was drawing a tree.
A round tree, with a thick trunk. The kind of tree a child draws. With a lot of branches going out, and leaves done as little loops, round and round. And in the middle of the branches, in among the leaves, a crown. A small crown, with points on it. Sitting in the tree the way a bird sits in a nest.
The boy drew it slowly. Carefully. With his tongue between his teeth.
The old man watched him from the oak seat at the head of the table. He did not say anything. Corin felt him not say anything. Felt him watch the small hand go round and round with the charcoal, and the tree come up out of the paper.
And when it was done, the boy looked at it.
And then, at the bottom, in the corner, he made a mark.
A letter. One letter. Big and crooked. A curve, like a moon on its back, open on one side.
A C.
Corin knew it.
He had seen it. On the first night in the bunker, when they opened the iron box under the map table. The book, and the drawing. A child's drawing of a tree with a crown in it, on rough gray paper, gone soft with age. And in the corner, a crooked C. And he had looked at the C a long time, and then written in the Ledger, in his own crooked capitals:
THE C IS MY C.
He had not known how he knew. He had only known.
Now he knew how.
He was watching himself draw it.
He was lying on the stone floor of this room, with a book on his chest, with Lyra's linen hand round his, a whole life later. And he was watching a small boy, on the same stone floor, by the same brazier, draw a tree with a crown in it and sign it with a C.
And the boy was him.
Corin had no face for the boy. He had never had a face for himself as a child. It had all gone, long ago, before Linus. He had looked in the mirror every morning for years and not known what he had looked like at five, or eight, or any age at all.
He had a face now.
Thin. Pale. Big dark eyes. Dark hair, grown out a little since the crate, falling into the eyes. A small serious mouth. A tongue between the teeth.
Him.
"What is it?" said the old man, from the oak seat. Gently.
The boy held up the drawing.
"It's ours," he said. "It's on the flag. Over the hall. At home." He looked at it. "My sister can draw it better. She does the crown with more points."
The old man looked at the drawing a long time.
"And the C?" he said.
The boy looked at it too.
"That's me," he said. "That's my name. The start of it." He touched the crooked letter with one black finger, carefully, as if it might come off. "I can do the start. I can't do the rest yet. She was going to show me."
