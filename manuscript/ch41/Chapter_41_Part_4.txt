Chapter 41: The Drummer's Brother, Part 4 of 5: Kross's Regards
Kross's Regards
Harlan turned to his horse.
He gathered up the dropped reins in his left hand, and put his left boot in the stirrup, and his right hand on the back of the saddle. And then he stopped there, half turned away, with his face toward the gray horse's shoulder and his back to the quay. So that nobody on the quay could see his mouth.
And he spoke. Not to Oren. To the horse. Low and quick, the way a man talks to a horse to settle it.
"Listen to me, because I'll say this once."
Oren did not move.
"A man came to Highmoor a week ago," said Harlan, to the horse's shoulder. "To the barracks. Tall. Gray coat. Pleasant face. He sat in my office for an hour and drank my brandy and asked me about the Commonwealth Foot. About my years as a drum boy. Very interested. Very kind. He asked if I remembered the calls. I said I did. He asked me to beat a few for him, on my desk, with my fingers. For old times' sake." A pause. The horse blew. "I beat him halt, and advance, and form on the colors. And he smiled. And then he tapped one for me."
Oren's mouth had gone dry.
"Four. Two. Nine. Three," said Harlan. "On my desk. With one finger. Watching my hands." He was quiet for a moment. "I didn't answer him, Oren. I've had twenty years' practice not answering. My hands didn't move. I made sure of it."
"And then?"
"And then he thanked me for the brandy, and gave me my orders for Midwinter. In person. Signed with his own hand and countersigned by the Archon's office." Harlan's voice was flat. "Every lock, every canal, every barge. And a line at the bottom, in his own writing, that isn't in the copy on the Watch house boards. Particular attention to loads of hay. An old man, gray-haired, who does not speak. Alive, if possible."
The gray horse stamped. Harlan put his hand on its neck.
"He told me something else," he said. "As he was leaving. At the door. With his hand on it. He said, I gave your brother your regards, Commander. In the autumn. At the Thorne gate. I thought you'd want me to." Harlan's voice did something then. Went very slightly rough, and then smooth again. "I never sent you any regards, Oren. Not in six years. Not one word. I want you to know that. Whatever that man said to you at the gate, I never sent it."
Oren stood very still in the snow.
Captain Vance. Your brother sends his regards. At the Thorne gate, in the autumn, with Corin on the roof. He had carried those words for weeks. He had turned them over at night, the way you turn a coin you have found in the street, wondering whose it was. He had thought, a little, in the bottom of himself, where he did not look often: perhaps he means it.
"He's using us," said Harlan. "Both of us. Like two sticks on a drum. He tapped that call at me, and he tapped it at your sergeant, and I'd wager every silver button on this coat he's tapped it at others. He's looking for whoever answers. And now he's found you, standing in front of my horse in the snow on the Lantern Bridge, tapping it into a wall in front of half the city." His voice was very low and very hard. "You fool. You great fool. You've just told him everything he wanted to know."
"Mrs. Marta said ask in the open," said Oren. "Not in a room."
"Mrs. Marta," said Harlan, "was right. If you'd asked me in a room, there'd be a room with you in it by tonight, and a man with a glass." A pause. "In the open, he has to be careful. In the open, he has to be pleasant. He likes to be pleasant. It costs him something to stop."
He swung up onto the horse.
He did it in one movement, smooth and quick, the way he did everything. And then he was up, on the tall gray horse, with his back like a rod and the reins in his black-gloved hands, looking down at Oren with their mother's face, cold and pale and closed. The face for the quay. The face for the Hill. As if none of the last minute had happened.
But he did not ride on. Not yet.
"My orders," he said, in the voice for the drums, so that the whole quay could hear him, "are to hold every lock on every canal in and out of this city, from tomorrow at first light, and to search every load that passes, in the name of the Archon. I will carry out my orders to the letter."
The words went out over the silent quay.
Then, lower. Not to the quay. To Oren. A voice just loud enough to carry the three feet between them.
"My orders say every lock. They do not say in what order."
Oren looked up at him.
"I'll begin at Saltgate," said Harlan. "In the south. At the sea locks. Where the salt barges come in. A great many barges come in at Saltgate, at Midwinter. Wine and salt and fish and coal. Every one to be turned out on the bank. Every barrel opened." He looked down at Oren. "And then the Fish Locks. And then the Tanners' Locks. And the Mill Cut, and the Coldwater, and the weir. One at a time. Very carefully. My men are new to the city. They don't know the canals. It will take them a long while." A pause. "It's a long way from Saltgate to the Long Pond, brother. Four days, at least. If my men are careful. And I'm going to tell them to be very careful."
The gray horse blew a white breath into the cold.
"Four days," said Oren.
"Four days. Then I'll reach the Long Pond. And then I'll go on north, past it, to wherever the hay barges go." Harlan's face did not move. "Because those are my orders, and I carry out my orders. I have done for twenty years. I'm not you, Oren. I don't throw everything away for a house."
He lifted the reins.
"Four days," he said. "Take the old man somewhere I can't follow. Further than the hay goes. And then leave him there, and leave the Thornes, and the girl, and whatever it is you're all doing. Before they cost you the rest of it." His voice dropped, very low. "Before they cost you the last of what you've got. You've only got so much left to burn, brother. I can see it on you. You can't even say spoon."
Oren stared up at him.
He had not said it. He had not said spoon once, on the quay, or the bridge. He had said the spoons about the kitchen table, because Juno had lent him the word. But Harlan had heard something in the way he said it. A little catch. A little stop, before it, the way a man stops before a step in the dark.
His brother had heard that.
After six years.
"Harlan," said Oren.
But Harlan had already turned the horse's head. He touched it with his heels. The gray horse walked on, past Oren, into the lane of trodden snow along Lantern Quay. And he lifted his black-gloved hand, without looking round, and made a small sharp movement with it. Two fingers. Down.
The drum boys' sticks came down.
The drums spoke.
And behind them, on the Lantern Bridge, four hundred men lifted their feet from the frozen stone all together, and brought them down, boom, and the bridge answered, and the black column moved again. Off the bridge. Onto the quay. Past the lamppost where Oren stood. Line after line after line. So close that he could have touched them. Pale young faces under flat black caps with the white eye on them, looking at nothing. Boots polished so hard that the snow would not stick. And on each left hip, under the long black coat, the small square shape, like a box.
Oren stood with his back to the lamppost and let them go past.
He did not look at the faces. He looked at the backs of the drum boys, going away along the quay toward the Hill, small and straight and black against the white. He watched the eight of them until they were past the foot of the Bank steps. Until they were past the lions with snow on their backs. Until they turned the corner at the end of the quay and were gone, and only the sound of them was left, coming back off the white front of the Bank.
He listened to them, and could not understand a single word.
And he thought: one of them is ten years old. One of them is younger than the boy on the tailboard was.
The carts came last. The six long black wagons, with their bundles of pale poles and their black iron hooks swaying over the tailboards, catching the light. They went past him slowly, grinding in the snow. He watched them go, all six, until the last one turned the corner.
Then Lantern Quay was empty, except for the people. And the trodden snow down the middle of it, gray and black with four hundred pairs of footprints. And the snow along the parapet of the bridge beside him, under the first blue lamp, where there were six small round holes, down to the stone, in the shape of a call that nobody had finished.
Bought Stones
The crowd did not go away at once.
Crowds never do. Oren had stood at enough gates on enough feast days to know that. When the thing they have come to see is over, a crowd stands for a while, the way water stands in a ditch after the rain has stopped. Not sure what to do with itself. Then it begins to move, slowly, at the edges. Then all at once.
He stood by the lamppost and let it move round him.
People went past him on both sides, going home, going to work, going back down the river steps or up the side streets toward the Hill. Talking now, in low voices, the way people talk after a funeral. He heard pieces of it. —four hundred, Jem said— and —did you see the hooks, did you see them— and —who was that, that stood in front of him, who was that big old man— and —his brother, Nan said, the commander's brother, I heard him say it—
Nobody stopped. Nobody looked at him for long. A woman with a basket on her arm looked at his face as she went by and then looked away quickly, the way you look away from a man with a bad sore.
Oren did not move.
He was thinking about his brother's hand.
Not the hand that had come down on his, flat and still, in the snow on the parapet. The other one. The right hand, hanging down by the hilt of the long sword, where nobody could see it but Oren. The forefinger, twitching against the black cloth. One. And the double.
I've had twenty years' practice not answering, Harlan had said. My hands didn't move. I made sure of it.
He had made sure of it for Kross. At Highmoor, in his office, with the brandy. He had kept his hands still.
He had not kept them still for Oren.
Oren stood in the cold and turned that over, the way he had turned over your brother sends his regards. Slowly. Looking at both sides of it.
And then he thought of something else.
He thought of what Harlan was. Not the commander. The burner. Every burner in the city had a way, a kind of magic that was his, and everybody on the Hill knew what Commander Vance's way was. Oren had heard it said in the servants' hall at Crown Walk, years ago, with a kind of shudder. The commander burns words, like his brother. But not his own.
Not his own.
Harlan bought stones. Memory stones, from the Bank, from the Bourse, from anyone who would sell. Other men's words, other men's days, drawn out of them and pressed into stone. And when he needed a wall, or a fire, or a word that would knock a man down, he burned those. Somebody else's mother's voice. Somebody else's first kiss. Somebody else's knowledge of how to read. He held the stone in his black-gloved hand and burned it, and it went up like dry paper, and none of it was his.
It cost a great deal of money. The Ministry paid. That was the price of being Commander of the Enforcer Battalion. You never had to burn yourself.
You never had to forget.
Oren stood very still by the lamppost.
There were no wagons, Harlan had said, in the voice for the drums. As if he had burned it. As if it had gone, the way Moll's valley had gone, on a glass at the Bank one winter for a month's pay.
But Harlan had never burned anything of his own in his life. Not since he was a boy. Not since the march.
He remembered.
He remembered all of it. The valley. The gentleman in the gray cloak with the long white hands, bending down, tapping a call on a drum. The blue wagons coming down out of the hills in the dark. The children under the canvas, crying. The tailboard, and the sticks, and the call. All night. Witness. Hold. Fold. Home. The ford, near dawn, and the wheel going into the hole, and the wagon going over slow as a falling tree, and the screaming. And whatever came after. Wherever they had gone.
And a little girl with two braids, who said Cor, look.
He had carried all of it for twenty years. Every day. Never burning any of it. Buying other men's memories to burn instead, so that he would never have to lose his own.
Oren did not know why. He did not know if it was because his brother could not bear to forget, or because he did not dare to. He did not know if it was a kind of faithfulness, or a kind of punishment. Or both. He only knew that it was there, behind the long pale face and the gray eyes, in the dark house at the back, where a door had opened a crack three times on the Lantern Bridge and closed again.
And that Kross knew it too.
He's looking for whoever answers.
Oren lifted his head.
The quay had nearly emptied. The last of the crowd was going away in twos and threes along the trodden lane and down the river steps. The Watch constables were going back to their corners. The woman with the pies was packing up her tray. The light had come up properly now, a hard white winter light, with the sun a pale disc behind thin cloud low over the river to the east, and the snow on the quay was too bright to look at.
And at the foot of the white steps of the Bank, by the lion on the left, a carriage was standing.
It had not been there before. Oren was sure of it. He had looked at the Bank steps before the drums came, and they had been empty except for people. He had not seen it arrive. It must have come along the quay behind the last of the carts, in the noise, and stopped there, and waited.
It was black. Plain and black and well kept, with two black horses standing quite still in the cold, and a driver up on the box in a black coat, with his collar up. There was no crest on the door. No badge. No color of any kind. Just black, polished, with the snow-light lying on it in a long white gleam.
Oren had seen it before. In the autumn. Outside the Thorne gate. Standing in Linden Walk with its door open and a tall man stepping down out of it.
The window of the carriage was down.
And in the window, looking out, a face.
It was a long way off. Forty paces, across the quay. But Oren had good eyes, and the light was very clear. A long, pleasant, middle-aged face, in a plain gray hat. Gray hair at the temples. Pale eyes. Washed-out, like a sky after rain. Looking straight across the white quay at him. Not smiling, exactly. Not quite. But nearly. The way a man nearly smiles when he sees an old friend across a room and has not decided yet whether to go over.
Oren did not move.
The face in the window did not move either.
Then a hand came up into the window, beside it. A gray-gloved hand. It rested on the sill. And the forefinger lifted, very slowly, and came down on the polished black wood of the window frame.
Once.
And then twice, quick. The double.
Heard.
The hand went away. The window went up. The driver lifted his whip, and the two black horses leaned into their collars, and the black carriage with no crest rolled away along Lantern Quay toward the Hill, slow and smooth, in the tracks of the battalion, and turned the corner, and was gone.
The Window
Oren stood by the lamppost for a long time after the carriage had gone.
He was not thinking. He knew that. There is a kind of standing still that a guard learns, at a gate, in the middle of the night, when he has heard something in the dark and does not yet know what it was. You do not think. You do not move. You let the world come to you, and you wait for it to tell you which way it is going to jump.
The world did not jump.
The quay was empty now, or nearly. A sweep's boy was scraping at the trodden snow with a shovel, for no reason Oren could see. Two constables stood talking at the corner, with their staves under their arms. A gull came down out of the white sky and landed on the parapet of the bridge, a little way along, and walked about on the snow there, leaving little forked marks, and looked at Oren with one yellow eye.
Oren looked down at the snow on the parapet beside him.
The six holes. Four, and two. Round and black and very small, going down through the white to the gray stone. Already the edges of them were softening, as the white light warmed the snow. In an hour they would be gone. Nobody would know they had ever been there.
He brushed them away with his hand.
Then, because he had looked at it before and a guard looks again at what he has looked at before, he turned his head and looked up at the Bank.
The white front, glowing. The pillars. The rows of tall dark windows, with snow on their sills. Third floor. River end.
The three tall windows.
The light in the middle one had gone out. He could see that at once. The window was dark now, like the others. The glass full of white sky.
But there was someone standing at it.
Oren went very still.
It was hard to see. The glass was old, and thick, and the sky was in it. But there was a shape behind the glass. Standing quite close to it. A figure. Not tall. Slight. Upright. With its hands together in front of it, at the waist. He could see the hands, because they were white. Very white. Whiter than a face, against the dark of the room behind. Gloves.
And above the white hands, a coat. Dark. Darker than the room. Green, perhaps. A dark, deep green, the color of holly leaves in winter.
And above the coat, a face.
He could not see the face. Not properly. Only a paleness in the glass. A small pale oval, under dark hair pinned up close. Turned down. Looking down. Not at the quay, where the battalion had gone. Not at the bridge.
At him.
Oren felt it the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck in a crowd. He had felt Kross's eyes like that, from the carriage. These were different. They did not have that almost-smile in them. They did not have anything in them that he could feel. They were just looking. Steadily. The way you look at a sum on a page that has come out wrong, and you do not yet know why.
He looked back up at the window.
He did not know who it was. He did not know anyone who worked in the Bank. He had never been inside it in his life. He only knew that his brother, riding over the Lantern Bridge at the head of four hundred men, with his back like a rod and his eyes straight ahead, had lifted those eyes once, for the space of one beat of the drums, to this window.
To the middle one of three.
The pale oval in the glass did not move.
Then one of the white hands came up. Slowly. And touched the glass. Flat. The palm against it, the fingers spread, white on the dark. As if the person behind it were steadying themselves. Or as if they were touching something on the other side of the glass that they could not reach.
Then the hand went down.
And the shape turned away from the window, and went back into the dark of the room, and was gone. And there was only the glass, full of white sky.
Oren let out his breath.
He found that his hands were in fists, in the pockets of Terrence's coat. He made them open.
The Fourth Desk, he thought. He did not know where the words came from. Somewhere in the back of his head. Something Corin had said once, perhaps, in Vespera's back room, in the autumn, about the Bank. About a woman. She sits at the Fourth Desk. On the third floor. Over the river.
He did not know if it was the same window. He did not know if it was the same woman.
He turned his back on the Bank and went down the Lantern Bridge steps to the river.
He did not go back to the arches the short way. He did not go back the way he had come. He had seen the black carriage at the foot of the Bank steps. He had seen the gray-gloved finger on the window frame. Heard. And he had walked behind Corin Vane through the city in the autumn, many times, and watched him go home, and learned from it.
So he went the long way.
Down the river steps and along the bank, west, the wrong way, away from the dye-works. Under one bridge and then another. Up a flight of steps into a fish market, full of people and cold and noise and the smell of guts, where he went in at one end and out at another, and turned his coat inside out in a doorway so that the fur collar was hidden and the lining showed, brown instead of black. Up a hill. Down an alley. Into a church, through the nave, past an old woman lighting a candle, out through the vestry door into a yard. Over a wall. Into the Ink Quarter.
In the Ink Quarter, among the tangled streets of printers and stationers and binders, with their shop signs hanging over the lanes so low that he had to duck under them, he stopped in a doorway and waited.
He had seen a boy.
A thin boy in a gray cap, about twelve years old, with his hands in his pockets. Twice. Once at the fish market, by the eel tanks. Once at the bottom of the hill. Walking. Not looking at Oren. Never once looking at Oren. Looking at shop windows, at dogs, at the sky. A boy with nowhere to go.
Oren waited in the doorway of a bookbinder's, in the smell of glue and leather, for a long time.
The boy in the gray cap came round the corner.
He was walking slowly, with his hands in his pockets, looking at the signs over the shops. He passed the bookbinder's doorway without turning his head. He went on down the lane. At the end, he stopped, and looked about him, at the three ways the lane went, as if he were lost. He looked for a long time.
Then he took his right hand out of his pocket, and something white with it. A small folded square of paper. He looked at it. He put it back.
And he went away down the left-hand lane, quickly, with his head down.
Oren did not follow him. He went out of the doorway and the other way, up the right-hand lane, and through a printer's yard, and over another wall, and down to the river again a long way east, under the bridges. And there, in the dark between the great stone piers, among the sleepers round their fires, he stood for a quarter bell more, with his back to a pier, and watched the way he had come.
Nobody came.
Then he went on, east, along the black river, toward the place where the water turned blue.
