Chapter 41: The Drummer's Brother, Part 3 of 5: The Battalion
The Battalion
They came out of Quill Row onto the far end of the bridge like a black river pouring into a white one.
First the drums. Eight drum boys, in two rows of four, in black coats with silver buttons and tall black hats with a silver badge in front. Boys. The oldest might have been fifteen. The youngest could not have been more than ten. They marched with their backs as straight as rods and their chins up and their eyes on nothing, and their sticks went up and down in front of them, all together, so fast that they blurred, and the drums on their hips spoke. The sound came across the river and filled the whole quay, the whole morning, and hit the white front of the Bank and came back.
Behind them, alone, a man on a horse.
And behind him, the battalion.
Oren had seen soldiers before. He had been one, in a way, for thirty years. He had stood at the Thorne gate in a red coat with the house badge on it. He had seen the Watch drill, and the Archon's guard on parade days, in their white and gold. He had seen the old Commonwealth regiments march down the Hill once, when he was a boy, with their colors flying, before the colors were put away.
He had never seen anything like this.
They were all in black. Black coats, long, to the knee, buttoned to the throat. Black trousers. Black boots, polished so hard that the snow did not stick to them. Black gloves. Black caps, flat, with a short peak, and on each cap a small badge of white metal in the shape of an open eye. Their faces under the caps were pale in the dawn light, and all of them were young, and all of them were clean-shaven, and none of them looked at anything.
They came in lines of eight. Line after line after line. Out of Quill Row and onto the bridge and across it, between the blue lamps, between the people standing two and three deep along the walls. In step. So perfectly in step that their feet struck the frozen stone as one foot, one huge black foot, and the bridge itself seemed to answer it, a deep dull boom in the stone under Oren's boots, every step.
They did not carry pikes. They carried nothing in their hands at all. Their hands hung at their sides in their black gloves, swinging a little, together. But on each man's left hip, under the long coat, there was a shape. A small square shape, like a box. And on each man's right hip, the hilt of a short sword.
And behind the lines, at the back, came the carts.
Oren saw them come onto the far end of the bridge. Six of them. Long low black wagons, each pulled by two black horses, each with a driver in black on the box. They were open. And in the bed of each one, stacked in bundles and lashed down with rope, there were poles. Long poles of pale wood, as thick as a man's wrist, so long that they stuck out over the tailboard of the wagon by half their length, swaying a little with every turn of the wheels. And on the end of every pole, catching the dawn light, a hook of black iron. Curved like a shepherd's crook, but sharp. Barbed on the inside of the curve, so that what it went into would not come out again easily.
Twelve-foot hooks. For searching hay.
Along the walls of the bridge, the people looked at the carts and then looked away.
Oren looked at the man on the horse.
He had known, of course. He had known since the hay, since Moll's face in the gold light. He had known since he was twenty-six years old, sitting across a scrubbed kitchen table from a thin silent boy with two spoons. But knowing a thing and seeing it are not the same. And he found, standing by the lamppost at the end of the Lantern Bridge in another man's coat, that he had not truly believed it until now.
The man on the horse was his brother.
He rode a tall gray horse, very pale, almost white, with a black saddle and a black bridle and no ornament on either. He rode it the way the drum boys marched. Straight. Perfectly straight. His back did not move with the horse at all; the horse moved under him, and he stayed still on top of it, like a post driven into the saddle. Like a rod. Like a boy sitting on a tailboard all night in the dark.
He was in black, like the rest. The same long coat, the same gloves, the same flat cap with the white eye on it. But on his shoulders there was silver. Two plates of it, flat and plain, and a silver cord looped from the left shoulder across his chest. And at his hip, not a short sword but a long one, with a plain black hilt.
He was taller than Oren.
Oren had always known that too. Harlan had grown, the year after he came home. He had shot up like a weed, all at once, the way some boys do, and passed Oren by the time he was fifteen, and gone on. He was taller than Oren, and leaner, and longer in the leg. On the tall gray horse, he looked like something cut out of black paper and stood up against the white.
His face was bare. No beard. No scar. Oren looked for one, and there was none. Not a mark on it anywhere. A long, lean, pale face, with high cheekbones and a long straight nose and a thin mouth. Their mother's face. Oren had forgotten that. He had not forgotten it the way he had forgotten spoon. He had just not thought about it, for six years, and now here it was, coming across the Lantern Bridge on a gray horse. Their mother's face, made long and hard and cold, with all the laughter gone out of it. The eyes in it were gray. Oren's own color. They looked straight ahead, over the heads of the drum boys, at the quay.
He was thirty-one years old. He looked older. Not in his face. In the way he held it.
Oren stood by the lamppost and watched him come.
The drum boys reached the middle of the bridge. The great black column came on behind them, line after line, booming on the stone. The people along the walls stood very still between the blue lamps. Somewhere a baby began to cry and was hushed. On the far side of the river, in the windows of Quill Row, the faces watched.
And the man on the gray horse rode on, straight-backed, with his eyes on the quay.
Then, as the drums came down off the last arch of the bridge and onto the quay, and the man on the horse came after them, past the second lamppost from the end, something happened.
It was very small. Oren would not have seen it if he had not been watching the face so closely, the way a guard watches a face. The way he had watched faces at the Thorne gate for twenty-two years, looking for the first small sign of a hand going to a knife.
Harlan Vance lifted his eyes.
Not his head. Only his eyes. For a moment. Up, and to the left. Away from the quay and the drums and the lane of trodden snow. Up the white front of the Bank, past the pillars and the rows of dark windows. To the third floor. To the river end. To three tall windows side by side, with a single warm light still burning behind the middle one.
He looked at them for perhaps the space of one beat of the drums.
Then his eyes came down again, and were on the quay, straight ahead, as if they had never moved. And his face had not changed at all.
Oren turned his head, very slowly, and looked up at the Bank.
The three tall windows. The light in the middle one. Nothing else. Snow on the sills. The glass dark at the edges, gold in the middle. No shape. No face.
He looked back at his brother.
The drums came off the bridge onto the quay. The first drum boy passed so close that Oren could have touched his shoulder. A thin boy with a pale cold face and red ears, perhaps twelve years old, with his sticks blurring and his eyes on nothing. Then the second. Then the gray horse.
The gray horse came off the end of the Lantern Bridge, past the first lamppost, where Oren stood.
And Oren took his hands out of the pockets of old Terrence's coat, and stepped out into the lane.
Brothers
The gray horse stopped.
It did not shy. It did not throw its head. It was too well trained for that. It simply stopped, all four feet planted in the trodden snow, with Oren standing in front of it so close that he could feel its breath on his face, hot and grassy, coming out of its nostrils in two long white plumes.
The man on its back did not move.
Behind him, the drums went on for three more beats. Four. And then the drum boy at the head of the left-hand line saw that the horse had stopped, and his sticks stopped too, in the air, and the boy behind him saw it, and stopped, and the stopping went back down the lines of drums like a ripple down a rope. And the drums were silent.
And behind the drums, the battalion.
Oren heard it stop. He did not look round. He did not need to. He heard four hundred pairs of boots come down on the frozen stone of the Lantern Bridge, together, one last time, boom, and then not lift again. He heard a voice somewhere back along the bridge shout one word, and another voice further back shout it again, and another, going away over the river. And then silence.
The whole of Lantern Quay was silent.
The people along the edge of the quay, two and three deep. The people along the walls of the bridge, between the blue lamps. The people on the white steps of the Bank, and in the windows of Quill Row across the water, and on the river steps. Half the city, Mrs. Marta had said. Oren could feel them all. He could feel their eyes on his back, like the heat of a fire.
Nobody made a sound.
A Watch constable was coming toward him from the side of the lane. Fast. With his stave up. Oren heard his boots in the snow. He did not turn his head.
He looked up at his brother.
Harlan Vance looked down at him from the back of the tall gray horse.
For a long moment, his face did not change. It stayed exactly as it had been on the bridge. Long and pale and hard, with the thin mouth closed and the gray eyes cold. Looking down at a big old man in a shabby coat with a bald fur collar, who had stepped out of the crowd into the path of his horse. A beggar. A drunk. A fool.
Then the eyes moved.
They went over Oren's face. Slowly. The way Moll's had, on the barge. The scar, from the left temple across the cheek to the corner of the mouth. The short gray beard. The jaw. The shoulders, too wide for the coat. The hands, scarred, hanging at his sides.
And Oren saw the moment when his brother knew him.
It was nothing. Nothing that anyone else on the quay could have seen. Not a flinch. Not a word. Only something at the back of the gray eyes. A door, a long way back in a dark house, opening a crack and closing again. So quick that if Oren had blinked he would have missed it.
The Watch constable reached him.
"You! Out of the lane! Out of the—"
"Leave him."
Harlan did not raise his voice. He did not need to. It was a voice that had been taught to carry over drums, and it carried now, flat and cold and quite quiet, across the silent quay. The constable stopped dead, with his stave in the air.
"Sir—"
"I said leave him." Harlan did not look at the constable. His eyes were on Oren. "Go back to your post."
The constable went back to his post.
And the brothers looked at each other. In the middle of the lane of trodden snow at the end of the Lantern Bridge, at dawn, with four hundred men and half the city watching them.
"Oren," said Harlan.
It was the first word he had said to him in six years.
Oren had thought about this. On the barge, on the coal cart, in the cellars under Tallow Street. What he would say. What his brother would say. He had thought of a great many things. He had not thought it would be just his name, said flat and quiet in that cold carrying voice. He had not thought it would go into him like that. Like a knife going in between two ribs, so thin and so quick that for a moment you do not feel it.
"Harlan," he said.
His own voice came out rough. Too loud. He had not used it all night.
The gray horse blew a long breath out of its nostrils. White. It hung between them in the cold air and slowly went to nothing.
"You're in my way," said Harlan.
"I know."
"There are four hundred men behind me on that bridge. And six carts. And the Archon's order to be at the Ministry barracks by the seventh bell." Harlan's face did not move. "You have about as long as it takes that horse to get tired of standing still. He's not patient. Neither am I."
"I won't need long."
"Then say it."
Oren looked up at him.
At the long pale face that was their mother's. At the silver on the shoulders, and the silver cord across the chest. At the white eye on the black cap. At the black-gloved hands, resting on the pommel of the saddle, one over the other, quite still.
I'm going to ask him, he had said to Corin, in the fourth arch, at the fifth bell. About a wagon.
He found that he could not, yet. Not first. There was something else that had to come first. Something that had been waiting six years.
"You were right," said Oren.
Harlan's eyes narrowed.
"At Mother's funeral," said Oren. "In the kitchen. After. When you put your gloves on." He kept his voice steady. "You said I'd thrown the name away. For a house that was falling down. You said the Thornes would cost me everything." He paused. "You were right. They have. They've cost me nearly everything."
Harlan said nothing.
"I've burned a great deal for them," said Oren. "Words. Things I knew. On the Saltmarsh I burned the calls. And the other thing. The thing you beat them on. I can't say it any more."
And there it was again. The door, at the back of the eyes. Opening a crack. This time Oren was ready for it, and he saw it plainly. Saw something come up into his brother's face from very deep down. Not anger. Not pity. Something he did not have a name for. It came up, and stood at the window, and looked out at him.
And went away again.
"The drums," said Harlan.
"All of them. The whole winter. The kitchen table." Oren's throat was tight. "I can see your hands. I can see the spoons. I can see Mother throwing them out of the window into the snow, and you laughing. I can't remember one call. Not one. If you beat halt at me now, Harlan, I'd stand there like a post and not know what it was."
The gray horse shifted its feet in the snow.
Harlan did not look at the horse. He was looking at Oren. And for a long moment he did not say anything at all.
"Then you're a fool," he said at last.
It was said very quietly. Not loud enough to carry. For the first time, it was not a voice for the drums.
"You always were. You're the biggest fool I ever knew." Harlan's black-gloved hands had closed on the pommel of the saddle. "You burned the drums. For a lord who doesn't know his own name. For a girl who—" He stopped. "And now you've come out of a crowd in a dead man's coat to stand in front of my horse, in front of my battalion, in front of the whole of the Hill, on the morning I march in. To tell me I was right." His mouth went thin. "I didn't need you to tell me I was right, Oren. I knew I was right. I knew it six years ago."
"I know you did," said Oren. "That's not why I came."
"Then why?"
Oren took a breath.
He turned his head, and looked at the wall of the bridge, beside him. At the low stone parapet, under the first blue lamp. White with new snow along the top, smooth and unmarked, a hand's breadth deep.
He put his right hand on it.
He felt the snow go cold and wet under his palm. And under the snow, the stone. Old, and hard, and very cold.
"Because of this," said Oren.
And he lifted his forefinger, and brought it down.

Four.
He did it the way Lord Thorne did it. Plain. Four flat taps, with the tip of one finger, into the snow on top of the parapet. Each one made a small round hole in the white, down to the stone. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
He could not do it the way Moll did it, with the shape inside it. Tap-tap. Tap. Tap. He did not know the shape any more. He only knew the number.
Two.
Two more holes in the snow. Tap. Tap.
He did not look at his hand. He looked at his brother's face.
And he saw it happen.
Harlan did not move. He sat on the tall gray horse with his back like a rod and his black-gloved hands on the pommel of the saddle. His face did not change. It did not go white, the way Moll's had gone white on the top of the hay. It did not go anything. It stayed exactly the same. Long and pale and cold.
But his eyes went to Oren's hand.
They had never once, since the horse stopped, left Oren's face. Now they left it. They went down, slowly, to the wall of the bridge, to the snow on the parapet, to the big scarred hand lying in it, and the forefinger lifting for the third time.
Nine.
Tap.
Tap.
And Harlan moved.
He moved so fast that Oren did not see him do it. One moment he was on the horse. The next he was down off it, on the near side, between the horse and the wall, with the reins dropped and his boots in the snow. So close that Oren could smell him. Leather, and horse, and cold, and something sharp and clean like gun oil. And his right hand, in its black glove, had come down on top of Oren's hand on the parapet.
Not hard. That was the strange thing. Not a blow. It came down flat and heavy and still, the whole length of it, over Oren's hand, the way you would put your hand over a candle flame to put it out without burning yourself. Pressing it into the snow. Holding it.
The forefinger was pinned. It could not lift. It could not come down a third time, or a fourth, or a ninth.
Along the walls of the bridge, the people watched. Oren heard a small sound go along them, like wind going through dry grass. Somebody whispered. Somebody else hushed them.
Harlan's face was very close now. Their mother's face. Oren could see the fine lines at the corners of the gray eyes, that had not been there six years ago. He could see that his brother had cut himself shaving that morning, a tiny cut under the jaw, with a dot of dried blood on it. He could see a small white scar after all, very small, in the left eyebrow, where the hair did not grow. He had not known about that scar. It was new to him. Some time in six years, someone had hit his little brother in the face.
"Don't," said Harlan.
He said it so quietly that nobody but Oren could have heard it. His lips hardly moved.
"Don't do that. Not here. Not anywhere." The hand on Oren's hand pressed down. "Never do that in this city. Do you understand me, Oren? Never."
"Why?"
Harlan did not answer.
"Who taught it to you?" said Oren. He kept his own voice as low as his brother's. "Moll says a gentleman. In a gray cloak, with the hood up. At the top of a valley. In the dark. He says he had long thin white hands."
The gloved hand on his did not move. But Oren felt something in it. Through the leather, through the snow. A small hard tremor, like a horse's skin twitching where a fly has landed.
"Who's Moll?" said Harlan.
"A sergeant. Rope Street Watch. He was a corporal then. On your march. Guarding the last wagon." Oren watched his brother's eyes. "The one you were sitting on. On the tailboard. With your legs hanging down. Tapping. All night. To the children in the back."
Harlan's face did not change.
But he blinked. Once. Slowly. Like a man with grit in his eye.
"The wagon went over at a ford," said Oren, "near dawn. Moll was under it. It broke his leg. He says you jumped clear. He says a surgeon's mate told him the little boy with the sticks was walking back with the rear guard, and not to worry." Oren swallowed. "He says you were the bravest man on the march. He says you never stopped tapping once. Not once. All night."
The gray horse shifted behind Harlan and blew. Harlan did not look round.
"Where did the wagons go, Harlan?"
Nothing.
"Where did they take the children?"
Nothing. The gloved hand, pressing down. The tremor in it.
"There was a girl," said Oren. "On your wagon. Maybe. Small. Six years old. With two braids." He did not look away from his brother's face. "She used to say Cor, look. When she found something. Cor, look at this. Like she wanted somebody to see."
And that was the one.
Oren saw it go in. He saw it the way you see a stone go into still water. Not the stone. The rings. They went out across his brother's face from somewhere very deep, so faint that no one but Oren would ever have seen them. The fine lines at the corners of the eyes went deeper. The thin mouth went thinner. And the gray eyes, very close to his own, went for one moment somewhere else. Not up at the Bank. Not at the bridge. Somewhere far away and long ago, in the dark, on a road. Looking down. Looking down, the way a boy on a tailboard looks down into the back of a wagon.
Then they came back.
And Harlan Vance took his hand off Oren's hand, and stepped back, and stood up straight in the snow, and his voice when he spoke was the voice for the drums again. Flat and cold and carrying. Pitched to reach the whole of the quay.
"There were no wagons."
The words went out over Lantern Quay and came back off the white front of the Bank.
"You are confused, Captain Vance," said Harlan. "You have been ill. Everyone on the Hill knows it. You have burned too much in the service of a fallen house, and you have lost your memory of the drums, and now you are trying to find it again in the gutters, from drunk old sergeants who were never on any march I was on." He did not take his eyes off Oren's. "I was a drum boy in the Commonwealth Foot for two years, when I was a child. I marched on the border. I came home. There was no valley. There were no wagons. There were no children."
The quay was silent.
"I am going to get back on my horse," said Harlan, "and ride to the Ministry barracks. And you are going to step out of my way. And if you ever stand in front of my horse again, in front of my men, and say those words in this city, I will have to arrest you, brother." A pause. "I would rather not."
Oren looked at him.
He looked at the long pale face, and the cold carrying voice, and the gray eyes. And he did not believe one word of it. Not because of the face, which had not moved. Not because of the voice, which had not shaken. Because of the hand.
Harlan's right hand. Hanging at his side now, in its black glove. Down by the hilt of his long sword. Where nobody on the quay could see it but Oren.
The forefinger was moving.
Very slightly. Against the black cloth of his coat. So slightly that it was hardly moving at all. A twitch. And another. And a pause. And another. The way a man's finger moves when he is asleep and dreaming of something he does with his hands.
Oren counted.
One. And then two, quick. A double.
Then still.
He did not know what it meant. He had burned it. But he had seen Moll do it, on the top of a haystack, into the gold light, with his arm stretched down toward an old man's face.
Heard.
