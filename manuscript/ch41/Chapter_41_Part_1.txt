Chapter 41: The Drummer's Brother, Part 1 of 5: Tallow Street at Midnight
Tallow Street at Midnight
The city came up out of the snow like a ship out of fog.
Oren saw it through the crack between the sacks. First, a glow. Low down on the black sky ahead of them, faint and dirty yellow, the color of an old bruise. The light of ten thousand lamps and fires and forges, coming up off the city and lying on the underside of the clouds. Then, as the cart came over the last long rise of the north road and started down, the shapes. Roofs. Chimneys. The black stub of a tower. The great bulk of the Ministry on its hill, with a few high windows still lit. And somewhere in the middle of it all, far off, a round dark shape against the glow, like the back of a beetle. The green dome of the Bourse.
And the snow, coming down over all of it. Big soft flakes, slow and silent, falling out of the dark and turning gold for a moment as they passed through the glow, and then gray, and then gone.
The drums had stopped an hour ago. Somewhere back on the high road, the battalion had halted for the night. Oren had heard the last roll of it come across the fields and stop, the way a man stops speaking in the middle of a sentence. And then nothing. Only the snow, and the creak of the cart, and the horse.
They came to the north gate a little before midnight.
It was not really a gate any more. There had been a wall round Veridia once, long ago, before the war that nobody remembered, and the north gate had been a great arch in it with doors of oak and iron. The wall was gone now. Pulled down, stone by stone, for houses. But the arch was still there, standing by itself in the middle of the road with nothing on either side of it, black with soot, like a door with no house. And under it, a Watch post. A little wooden hut with a stove pipe, and a red lamp, and a bar across the road.
Oren felt the cart stop. He heard the hut door open.
"Abner." A young voice. Bored. Cold. "Saints, you're late. I thought you'd gone in the canal."
"Snow," said Abner, round his pipe.
"Is it snowing?" The young voice was sour. "I'd not noticed." A lantern swung. Oren saw the yellow light go across the crack between the sacks, and away. "What've you got?"
"What I always have."
"Coal."
"Coal."
"Going back into the city? You took coal out this morning."
A pause.
"Farmer at Brindle didn't want it all," said Abner. "Said his barn's full. Said he'll take the rest Monday week." Another pause. "I'm not tipping best house coal in a ditch for you, Constable Tull. If you want to climb up and count the sacks, climb up. I'll wait."
Oren lay very still under the coal, with his sword along his body and his hand on the hilt. Beside him, Moll did not breathe.
The lantern swung again. Oren heard boots crunch in the snow, round to the back of the cart. He saw the light come close to the crack. So close that he could see the snowflakes in it, falling, and the black wool of a constable's glove, holding the lantern up. He shut his eyes, so that they would not shine.
"Saints," said the young voice, from very near. "It stinks."
"It's coal," said Abner.
The light went away. The boots crunched back round to the front. The bar went up with a creak and a thump.
"Go on, then," said the constable. "And Abner. They're saying the battalion comes in by Lantern Bridge in the morning. First light. There'll be no carts on the quay till they're past. Not even coal."
"I'll keep off the quay," said Abner.
The cart rolled.
They went in under the black arch, and the snow went with them. Down the north road into the city. Past the first houses, dark and shut. Past a tavern with one lit window and a man asleep in its doorway with snow on his boots. Down a long hill, with the cart's brake shrieking. Into streets Oren knew.
He lay in the coal and named them to himself as they went. Tanner's Hill. Bellows Lane. The top of Tallow Street. He had walked these streets for twenty-two years, behind Lord Thorne's carriage and in front of it, by day and by night. He knew the cobbles by the feel of them under the wheels. He knew where the drains were. He knew which corners had a lamp and which had none.
The cart turned into Tallow Street and stopped.
"Number sixteen," said Abner, very low, from the box. "Out you get. Cellar flap's open. Mind the step."
Oren pushed the sacks off himself and sat up.
The cold hit him in the face like a slap. Tallow Street lay white and silent under the snow, from end to end, with not a single footprint in it but the two black lines of Abner's wheels. The houses on both sides were tall and narrow and dark, leaning together over the street as if they were telling each other secrets. A single lamp burned at the far end, by the pump, with a white cap of snow on it. Its light lay on the snow in a soft round pool and did not reach anything else.
Beside the cart, at the foot of the wall of number sixteen, a square iron flap stood open on its hinge. A black hole in the snow. A smell came up out of it, of coal and damp and cold stone.
Oren got Moll out.
It was easier than at Brindle, because Moll could slide off the tailboard onto the snow and sit there, and swear, and get his breath. Then Oren took him under the arms and lowered him down through the flap, feet first, the bad leg out stiff, and somebody below took his feet. A woman's voice said I've got him. I've got you, Moll. Come on. Come on, you great lump. And Moll went down into the dark.
Oren turned back to the cart.
Abner Cole was sitting on the box with the reins in his black hands and his pipe in his teeth, looking straight ahead up the white street. The snow was settling on his hat and on his shoulders and on the back of his big brown horse, and he did not brush it off.
"Thank you," said Oren.
Abner did not look round.
"Hessa Doon," he said. "Twelve year ago. My boy fell off the Saltgate lock wall in a flood. She went in after him. On a rope." He took the pipe out of his mouth and looked at it. "Hessa Doon's lot is my lot."
He put the pipe back. He clicked his tongue. The horse leaned into the shafts, and the coal cart rolled away up Tallow Street through the snow, slowly, leaving its two black lines behind it. At the corner by the pump it turned, and was gone, and the lines began at once to fill up white.
Oren stood alone in the snow for a moment.
He looked up at the sky. At the soft gold-gray of the clouds, and the flakes coming down out of them, endlessly, like ash. He thought of the canal north of Brindle. The long straight black line of the water, between the willows. A small yellow lantern, swinging. Hessa at the bow, with her pole, breaking the ice. In this snow. In the dark. And somewhere behind them, or in front of them, at a bend, a red light that flashed three times.
He thought of an old man in a box in the middle of the hay, tapping.
Hold.
He turned round and went down through the iron flap into the cellar of number sixteen, and pulled it shut over his head, and the snow went on falling on Tallow Street, and covered everything.
Mrs. Marta's Kitchen
The cellars under Tallow Street went on for a long way.
Oren had not known that. He had never been under Tallow Street. He followed the small square shape of Mrs. Marta's back and the small yellow bead of her candle through a low brick passage that smelled of coal, and then through a doorway with no door, and then through another cellar full of old barrels, and then down two steps and up three and round a corner, bent nearly double, with his sword scraping the wall and Moll's breath sawing behind him.
"Mind the beam," said Mrs. Marta, without looking round.
Oren hit the beam.
"I said mind it," said Mrs. Marta.
At the end of the last passage there was a door. A real one, this time, low and narrow and made of old gray planks, with a wooden latch. Mrs. Marta lifted the latch and went through it, and Oren came after her, and found himself standing in a cellar he knew by its smell before he knew it by anything else. Apples. Onions in a string. Coal. And bread. Old bread and new bread, the smell of a house where bread has been baked every day for thirty-four years, so that it has got into the bricks.
Number fourteen. Mrs. Marta's.
She took them up the cellar stairs into the kitchen.
She did not light the lamp. She lit two candles, and put them on the floor, under the edge of the big scrubbed table, so that their light lay low across the flagstones and did not reach the window. The shutters on the window were closed and barred. There was a blanket hung over them as well, nailed at the top.
"He's on the front step," said Mrs. Marta. She said it quite calmly, the way you would say it's raining. "The Inquisition's man. Sitting in the doorway of the chandler's across the street, in a brown coat, with his collar up. He's been there since Thursday. Day and night. They change him at the sixth bell. This one's the night one. He's called Fenwick. I gave him a hot potato on Saturday through the letter box, because it was so cold, and he said thank you, missus very politely, and then he wrote it down."
She put the kettle on the range. The fire in it was banked low, red under gray ash. She raked it, gently, so that it would not roar, and put on two small shovels of coal.
"Sit," she said.
They sat.
Oren sat at the end of the scrubbed table on a stool that was too small for him, with his sword across his knees. Moll sat in the chair by the range, Mrs. Marta's own chair, with the patched cushion on it and the knitted blanket over the back, with his bad leg out on the stool in front of him and his face gray and slack with weariness. And Mrs. Marta stood.
Oren looked at her in the low light.
He had seen her before. Once or twice. On the steps of the Watch house, and in the yard. A small, round, upright old woman, seventy-one years old, with white hair pinned up hard under a black cap and a face like a winter apple, red and creased. She had on a brown wool dress with an apron over it, and her mother's old gray shawl round her shoulders, crossed and tied behind. Her hands were big and red and rough, a baker's hands. She held them folded in front of her apron, one over the other, very still.
She was looking at Moll.
"Well?" she said.
Moll looked up at her.
"You went to the Inquisition at the tenth bell," said Mrs. Marta, "in your good coat, and you didn't come back. And at the second bell Abner Cole came to my cellar door from sixteen and said your sergeant's gone north under my coal, missus, and he says don't wait up." Her voice did not rise. "And I've been sitting in this kitchen in the dark for nine hours, Ebenezer Moll, with a man on my step writing down my potatoes, not knowing if they'd collected you. So I'll ask you again. Well?"
Moll opened his mouth. Then he shut it again.
Then he told her.
He told it all. The Inquisition, and the tall pleasant man in the plain gray coat, and the finger on the table. Four, two, nine, three. And his own finger under the table, coming down once. The papers folded small and put away. Do bring your memory with you. The coal cart. The battalion. The order on the Watch house board. The barge. The old man in the box in the hay, looking up through a square of gold light.
And the wagons. All of it. The valley and the blue wagons and the drummer boy on the tailboard, and the ford, and the wheel going into the hole, and the bone breaking. Corporal, there were no wagons.
He told it in a low flat voice, without stopping, with his eyes on the red glow of the range. Mrs. Marta did not interrupt once. She stood with her hands folded in front of her apron and listened. Only her face moved. Oren watched it. It went tight round the mouth when Moll told the part about the finger. And when he told the part about the wagons, and the children crying under the canvas in the dark, it did something else. It went very still and very old, all at once, as if the years had come down on it like snow.
When Moll finished, nobody spoke.
The kettle began to sing on the range. Mrs. Marta took it off before it could whistle.
She made the tea. Strong and black, with a lot of sugar, in three thick brown mugs with chips in the rims. She gave one to Moll, and one to Oren, and kept one for herself, and held it in both her big red hands without drinking it.
"Children," she said at last. "In wagons. Twenty years ago."
"Nearly," said Moll.
"And you never said."
"I didn't know, Marta. I swear to you. I'd burned it. The name, and the faces, and the road. I went to the Bank and sat on a glass. I didn't know." His voice cracked. "Not till my finger started."
Mrs. Marta looked at him for a long time.
Then she reached out with one big red hand and put it on top of his head. On his thin gray hair. Just for a moment. The way you put your hand on a child's head when it has told you the truth about something bad it did.
"No," she said. "I don't suppose you did."
She took her hand away. She turned to Oren.
"And you," she said. "Captain. You've left the old lord with Hessa Doon on a canal in the snow, and come to the city to find your brother." It was not a question. "Who's coming over Lantern Bridge in the morning with four hundred men."
"Yes."
"To ask him about a wagon."
"Yes."
She looked at him over the rim of the chipped mug.
"My Terrence was a night porter at the Ministry," she said. "Thirty-one years. On the Third Door. He came home one morning eight years ago with his thumb going round and round, rubbing a key that wasn't there, and he didn't know me." She paused. "He'd asked somebody a question, Captain. That's all. I never found out what. But he asked somebody on the Hill a question, one night, about something he'd seen. And they answered him."
Oren said nothing.
"I'm not telling you not to go," said Mrs. Marta. "You'll go. I can see it on you. You've got the same face my Terrence had, the night before. I'm telling you to ask your question in the open. On the bridge. In front of four hundred men and half the city. Not in a room. Never in a room with a door that shuts." She set down her mug. "Things happen in rooms."
"I'll remember," said Oren.
"See you do."
She turned back to the range. She did not seem to know what to do with her hands, now that the mug was gone. She picked up a cloth and wiped the clean table with it.
"The boy," said Oren. "Corin. Moll said he's at the arches. Hurt."
"Fourth arch. At Mother Grue's. Under the dye-works." Mrs. Marta did not stop wiping. "Nell came at the fifth bell. A little thing with hair like a bird's nest. She'd run all the way from the arches in the dark, and she was crying so hard I had to give her bread before she could talk. His leg's gone bad. He went into the Bourse cellars last night, on the bad leg, and came out on his elbows. And there's a doctor." She wiped harder. "And there was something about a man with peppermints. Nell didn't know what it meant. She just said it over and over. He saw the peppermint man. He saw the peppermint man's face."
"I'm going there now," said Oren.
Mrs. Marta stopped wiping.
"Now? Tonight? In this?"
"He needs to know about the barn." Oren stood up, and remembered the ceiling was higher here than on the barge, and stood up straight. "Where Hessa's taken the old lord. And about the battalion, and the locks. And about Moll." He looked at the sergeant, slumped in the chair by the range with his eyes half shut. "And I need to tell him before the bridge. In case."
He did not say in case of what. Mrs. Marta did not ask.
"Where's the barn?" said Moll, from the chair, without opening his eyes.
Oren looked at him.
"No," said Mrs. Marta sharply, before Oren could speak. "Don't tell him. Don't tell me either. Don't say it in this house." She looked at Moll. "He's going back up forty-one steps on Thursday, Captain. With his memory. And they'll put a glass on him, and they'll take what they want, and whatever's in his head on Thursday morning will be in Kross's book by Thursday night." Her voice shook, very slightly, and steadied. "So you tell the boy. Only the boy. With your mouth, in his ear. And the boy keeps it the way he keeps everything. And nobody else in this city knows where Hessa Doon has gone."
The kitchen was very quiet.
Moll opened his eyes and looked at Mrs. Marta for a long time. Then he nodded, slowly, and shut them again.
"She's right," he said. "Don't tell me, Captain. I'd only lose it to them."
Oren buckled his sword belt tighter.
"How do I get out?" he said. "Without Fenwick."
Mrs. Marta almost smiled.
"The same way you came in," she said. "Back through the cellars to sixteen, and out of Abner's yard into Cinder Row. Then down the Steps to the river. Then along under the bridges, east, till you see the blue water in the Mill Cut. Then up by the dye-works." She went to a hook by the cellar door and took down a coat. An old man's coat, long and dark and patched, with a fur collar gone bald. "And put this on over yours. You look like a soldier. Terrence's coat makes everybody look like somebody's grandfather."
Oren put it on. It was too small for him at the shoulders, and too short in the arms. It smelled of pipe tobacco and bread.
"There," said Mrs. Marta. "Somebody's grandfather."
She looked up at him, in the low candlelight, under the edge of the table. And for a moment her face was not calm at all.
"Bring him back, Captain," she said. "Whatever your brother says. Bring yourself back. That boy at the arches can't lose anybody else this week. He hasn't got the room."
