Chapter 41: The Drummer's Brother, Part 2 of 5: The Fourth Arch
The Fourth Arch
It was the fifth bell before he got there.
The way Mrs. Marta had told him was the right way, and a long one. Back through the cellars, bent double, with the candle she gave him. Out through a coal merchant's yard where a chained dog lifted its head and looked at him and put it down again. Down the Steps to the river, a long crooked flight of slimy stone with a rusted iron rail, where the snow had not settled because the river breathed on it. Along the bank, under the bridges.
Under the bridges was another city. Oren had always known it was there. He had never walked in it. A city of arches and pillars and dripping dark, where the snow did not fall and the frost did not reach, and the river ran past black and quick and loud on one side. People lived there. He saw their fires, small and red, in the hollows under the great stone spans. He saw shapes round the fires, wrapped in blankets and sacks. He smelled smoke, and fish, and wet dog. Nobody spoke to him. Nobody looked up. A man in an old coat with a fur collar going past in the dark at the fourth bell was not anything anybody under the bridges wanted to look at.
Then, at last, ahead of him, the water changed color.
Even in the dark he could see it. The Mill Cut came down into the river from the left, out of the dye-works, and the water in it was blue. Not a little blue. Blue like ink, like the blue in a church window, glowing faintly even at night as if it had a light in it. It ran out into the black river in a long smoking stripe, steaming a little in the cold, and the snow that fell on it vanished at once.
Above it, black against the gold-gray sky, the chimneys of the dye-works.
And under the dye-works, the arches.
He found the gap in the wall where Mrs. Marta had said it would be. He went through it. Down a short slope of rubble and snow. And there in front of him was a row of great brick mouths in the dark, side by side, going back into the hill under the dye-works. Six of them. Seven. Old storehouse vaults, Hessa had told him once, from the days when the dye-works stored its wool down here. Each one hung across with sacking or old sailcloth or a door made of planks. Each one with a thread of firelight round the edges.
A small dark shape stood up from beside the nearest one.
"Who's that?"
A girl's voice. Fierce and frightened. Oren stopped.
"A friend of Corin Vane's," he said. "From the barge."
The shape came closer. It was a girl of fourteen or so, small for it, in a shawl and a man's boots, with a mass of tangled hair standing up round her head like a bird's nest. She had a stick in her hand. A real one. A thick one. She held it like a club.
"What's your name?"
"Oren."
She looked up at him for a long moment. All the way up.
"He said you'd be big," she said at last. And lowered the stick. "I'm Nell. I do the door. Mother Grue says anybody who comes at night, I hit them first and ask after." She paused. "I'm not hitting you. You're too big. It wouldn't do anything."
"Thank you," said Oren.
"He's in the fourth," said Nell. "He's awake. He's been awake all night. He won't sleep. The doctor said he's to sleep and he won't." Her voice wobbled. "I think he's waiting for something."
She took him along the row of arches in the snow and held back the sacking of the fourth one for him.
He went in.
It was warm. That was the first thing, after the cold of the river. Warm and close and smoky, with a low red fire at the far end in a brick hearth, and a smell of coal and boiled cabbage and many people asleep. And a smell under that, sharp and clean, that Oren knew from the barracks infirmary. Vinegar. And under the vinegar, the smell of a wound.
The arch was long, and low, and round-roofed, like the inside of a great brick barrel. Its walls were black with old smoke and wet in patches where the hill came through. There was a long crack in the roof with a dead weed in it. All along the floor, in the red light, there were sleeping people. Heaps of sacking and shawls, with a foot sticking out here and a hand there.
And near the fire, on a pallet of sacking stuffed with straw, a man lay on his back with one leg out straight on a folded sack.
Oren went down the arch to him, stepping over the sleepers.
Corin's eyes were open. They were looking at the crack in the roof. They came round to Oren as he got near, and Oren saw them see him, and not know him, for a moment. And then know him.
"Oren," said Corin.
His voice was a croak. His face in the firelight was thin and gray and wet with sweat, and there was coal dust still in the lines round his eyes, and a cut on his cheek. His hands lay on the blanket on top of him. They were gray to past the wrist, the Ash gray that went up past his elbows now, and wrapped round the knuckles in rags, and the rags had black and red on them.
Oren knelt down beside the pallet.
Beside it, on the floor, a boy was asleep on a sack, curled on his side with his knees up and one arm in a sling. A thin fair boy with a head like a bird's. He had his good hand inside his coat, over his chest. He was breathing slow and even.
On the other side of the fire, Lyra was asleep sitting up, on an upturned bucket, with her back against the brick and a gray army blanket round her shoulders and her head tipped sideways. Her hair was short. Hacked short, ragged, like a boy's. Oren had not known that. He looked at it for a long moment. He did not say anything. He did not wake her.
"You came," said Corin. "From the barge. How—"
"Under the coal. With Moll." Oren kept his voice low. "Listen. There's a great deal. I'll tell it short."
He told it short.
The battalion. The order on the Watch house board. Every lock, from tomorrow, every load turned out with hooks. Hessa taking the pin out from under her bunk. Moll on the top of the hay, and the old man's face looking up through the square of gold, and the word that had come out of the sergeant's mouth. Witness. What the four numbers meant. Witness. Hold. Fold. Home. The wagons in the valley. The children under the canvas. The drum boy on the tailboard of the last one, tapping all night so that they would stop crying.
And Lord Thorne's voice. The drummer. Find—
Corin did not move while he told it.
He lay on the pallet and looked at Oren, and as Oren went on, his face went slowly from gray to white, the way Moll's had. And when Oren came to the drummer boy on the tailboard, his gray ragged hands closed on the blanket.
"A blue wagon," said Corin.
Oren stopped.
"A blue farm wagon," said Corin. His voice had gone very strange. Very thin. As if it were coming from a long way off. "With canvas over the top. Painted blue, the way the hill farmers paint them. And a boy on the tailboard, at the back, with his legs hanging down. Ten or eleven. With a back as straight as a stick. No scar." He swallowed. "Tapping. With two sticks. On the edge of the board."
Oren stared at him.
"How do you—"
"I saw it," said Corin. "In the fever. On Saturday night. When the leg went bad." His eyes were on Oren's face, wide and black. "Lyra wrote it in the Ledger, because I couldn't. I've read it every morning since. A drummer boy on a blue wagon full of children. Tapping a call. And a small hand. A child's hand. Sticky. Holding mine. And then pulling away from me. Toward the wagon."
He stopped. His breath was coming fast.
"And a voice," he said. "A girl's voice. Small. Saying Corin, where are they going?"
The fire hissed.
Oren knelt by the pallet and could not say anything at all.
"I thought it was the fever," said Corin. "I thought it was a dream. I thought it was the leg." His gray hand came up off the blanket and gripped Oren's sleeve, through Terrence's old coat. Hard. The fingers shook. "Oren. Who is he? Moll's drummer. Does Moll know who he is?"
Oren looked down at the hand on his sleeve.
"My brother," he said.
Corin's hand went still.
"Harlan," said Oren. "He came home from a march in the north, twenty years ago, when he was eleven. He wouldn't say where. And then he taught me the calls. All of them. One winter, at the kitchen table." He took a breath. "He's coming over the Lantern Bridge at first light. At the head of four hundred men. I'm going to ask him."
Corin looked at him for a long time.
Then his hand let go of the sleeve. It went to the front of his own shirt, fumbling, and found the place where the Ledger was, inside his coat, on the left, against his heart. And pressed flat on it.
"Ask him," said Corin. His voice was hardly there. "Ask him if he remembers a little girl. With two braids. On the last wagon. Who used to say Cor, look." He shut his eyes. "Cor, look. Cor, look at this. Like that. Like she'd found something. Ask him if she was on his wagon. And where it went."
Oren did not ask who the girl was.
He did not need to. He had read the first page of Corin's Ledger once, in Vespera's back room, when Corin was asleep and the book had fallen open. You have a sister. Her name is Elara.
"I'll ask," he said.
Then he bent down, very close, until his mouth was at Corin's ear. And told him about the barn. Very low. In a whisper. The way Mrs. Marta had said. Past Withy Reach. The peat cuttings. The old turf barns. The third one, with the broken roof. There's a cut runs up to the door. He said it twice. He felt Corin's head move against his face. A small nod.
"Don't write it," said Oren. "Marta says. Don't write it in the book."
Corin opened his eyes.
"I'll write it," he said. "I have to. I'll lose it otherwise. You know I will." A ghost of something moved at the corner of his mouth. "But I'll write it so that only I can read it."
He held out his hand. Oren found the stub of pencil on the edge of the pallet and put it in his fingers. And held the Ledger open for him, the way Lyra did, on his knee, in the firelight.
Corin wrote. It took a long time. The letters were large and shaking, and went downhill.
The old lord is where Hessa owes the goat. Third one. Broken roof.
He looked at it. Then he gave the pencil back.
"Big Abel," said Oren. "The Tanners' Ditch."
"No," said Corin. "That's who she owes. Not where." His eyes were closing. "Nobody will know where but me. And nobody will know what it means but me. And if they take it out of me, they'll only get a goat."
He was asleep before Oren had shut the book.
Oren put it back inside his coat for him, against his heart. Then he sat back on his heels by the pallet, in the red firelight, and looked at the man asleep on the sacking, and the boy asleep on the floor, and the girl with the hacked hair asleep on the bucket.
He looked at them until the gray came round the edges of the sacking at the mouth of the arch, and the first cart went over the bridge above, and somewhere across the river a bell began to ring the sixth hour.
Then he got up, and went out, and walked down to the river in the snow, toward the Lantern Bridge.
Lantern Quay at Dawn
The snow had stopped by the time he reached the quay.
It had stopped the way snow sometimes does just before the light comes, all at once, as if somebody had turned off a tap. The last flakes came down slow and big and few, out of a sky that was going from black to the color of wet slate, and then there were none. And the city lay under it, white and still and silent, as if it were holding its breath.
Oren came up the Lantern Bridge steps from the river with Terrence's coat buttoned to the chin and the fur collar up round his ears, and his sword under it, along his leg, where the coat hid it.
The steps were wide and shallow and very old. Gray stone, worn into hollows in the middle by a thousand years of feet, with an iron rail on one side that had been painted black once and was now mostly rust. The snow lay in the hollows like milk in a saucer. At the top of them, where they came out onto the quay, there was a lamppost. A short thick post of black iron, with a lamp on top shaped like a little house, with a pointed roof and four glass windows. The glass was blue. Behind it, a flame was still burning, low, from the night. It turned the snow round the foot of the post a soft cold blue, like the shadow of a cloud.
Oren knew that lamp. He had walked past it a thousand times. He knew the plaque on the side of the post too, though he had never stopped to read it.
He stopped now and read it.
LANTERN BRIDGE. BUILT IN THE REIGN OF—
And then a smooth place in the iron, where a word had been. Ground off, so carefully that you could not see a single scratch. Only the smoothness, a little brighter than the iron round it, where the snow had not stuck.
—. RESTORED BY THE GRACE OF THE MINISTRY.
He looked at the smooth place for a long moment. Then he turned and looked at the bridge.
It went out across the river from where he stood, long and wide and gray, on seven great arches. Wide enough for two carts to pass and people on both sides. Along both its walls, at every twenty paces, stood more of the little house-shaped lamps on their black posts, with their blue glass windows, going away across the water in two straight lines, glowing. Fourteen of them. Twenty. He did not count. The blue light lay on the new snow along the top of the walls and on the white road between them, and made the whole bridge look like something in a dream. Like a road made of ice going out over black water to nowhere.
On the far side, the north bank. Quill Row, and the stationers' shops with their shutters still down. And the great white bulk of the buildings along the quay.
And on this side, the south bank, behind him and to his left, Lantern Quay.
Oren turned and looked at it.
He had never liked Lantern Quay. Nobody who worked on the Hill liked it. It was too wide and too clean and too empty, a great long stretch of pale stone paving along the river, with stone benches that nobody sat on and stone bollards that no boat ever tied up to. It had been made for looking at. And what it had been made for looking at stood on the far side of it, facing the river, filling the whole south end of the quay from the bridge to the next street.
The Bank.
The Oblivion Bank of Veridia. White stone, four floors high, with tall narrow windows in long rows, and a great flight of white steps going up to a great pair of doors. Pillars. A pediment on top, with figures carved in it that the snow had picked out in white on their heads and shoulders and outstretched arms. Lions on plinths at the foot of the steps, with snow on their backs. The whole front of it was lit from below, even now, at dawn, by lamps in iron baskets set into the paving, so that it glowed against the gray sky like a thing made of ice. Oren had heard Corin say once that the Bank never closed. That there was always somebody in it, somewhere, awake. Counting.
He looked up at the windows. Row after row of them, dark and blank, with snow on their ledges.
On the third floor, near the river end, there were three windows taller than the others. Three long narrow windows side by side, with a light behind them. A single light, warm and yellow, in the middle one. As if someone had sat up all night at a desk.
Oren did not know what was behind those windows. He looked at them because they were lit, and the others were not, and a guard looks at what is lit.
Then he looked away.
The quay was filling up.
It had been nearly empty when he came up the steps. A few early carts, being turned back by the Watch at the corners, with their drivers swearing. A woman with a tray of hot pies. A sweep and his boy, with their brushes over their shoulders. But in the time it took him to read the plaque and look at the Bank, people had begun to come. Out of the side streets and down from the Hill and up from the river steps, in ones and twos and families, wrapped up against the cold, with their breath going up white. Standing along the edge of the quay, and along the walls of the bridge on both sides, between the blue lamps. Not talking much. Stamping their feet. Waiting.
They had come to see the battalion.
Oren understood that slowly, the way you understand that a field you are crossing is full of people sitting very still in the long grass. They had come the way people come to see a hanging. Not because they wanted to. Because they had been told it was happening, and it would be worse not to have seen.
There were Watch constables along the quay, in their blue coats, every forty paces, with their staves. Keeping a lane clear down the middle of the bridge and across the quay, between the people. A wide white lane of trodden snow, going from the far end of the bridge, where the north road came in, past the foot of the Bank steps, and away along the quay toward the Hill.
Oren went and stood at the foot of the first lamppost on the south end of the bridge, by the top of the steps. Where the lane came off the bridge onto the quay. Where anybody coming over the bridge at the head of a column would have to pass within an arm's length of him.
He stood with his back to the lamppost and his hands in the pockets of Terrence's coat, and the hilt of his sword against his hip under it, and waited.
Nobody looked at him. A big old man in a shabby coat with a bald fur collar, standing by a lamppost. Somebody's grandfather.
Ask your question in the open, Mrs. Marta had said. On the bridge. In front of four hundred men and half the city. Never in a room.
He could not see four hundred men yet. But he could see half the city. More of them every minute. Along the walls of the bridge, between the blue lamps, two and three deep. Along the edge of the quay. On the steps of the Bank. Up in the windows of the houses on Quill Row, across the water, where the shutters had begun to open, one by one, with faces in them.
The gray of the sky went paler. Then a little pink, far in the east, down the river, low over the roofs. The blue flames in the house-shaped lamps began to look thin and weak, the way lamps do when the day comes.
Then, very far off, from the north bank, beyond Quill Row, where the north road came down into the city, there came a sound.
A single drum.
One roll. Long and hard and rattling, like hail on a roof. Rising. And then stopping, all at once.
The whole quay went quiet.
Oren felt it go. Like a wind dropping. Every voice. Every stamp of a boot. The woman with the pies, halfway through calling out the price of them, stopped with her mouth open. Along the walls of the bridge the people stood still between the blue lamps, and turned their heads to the north, all together, the way a field of corn turns when the wind comes across it.
And into that silence, from beyond Quill Row, the drums began.
Not one. Many. Rolling, all together, so that the sound came across the river and hit the front of the Bank and came back off it doubled. And then the roll broke into beats. Beats with a shape. A pattern, going over and over. And under the beats, very faint at first and then louder, another sound. A sound like a great slow heartbeat. Like the floor of the world being struck with a mallet, again and again.
Feet.
Four hundred pairs of feet, in step, on frozen stone.
Oren stood by the lamppost and listened to the drums.
He knew they were talking. He could hear that they were talking. The way you can hear, through a wall, that the people in the next room are speaking, and are angry, or glad, or afraid. He could hear the shape of the words. He could hear where one stopped and the next began.
He could not understand one of them.
And somewhere at the front of them, at the head of that sound, coming down toward the river through the white streets, was the boy who had taught him how.
