Chapter 41: The Drummer's Brother, Part 5 of 5: The Fish Stairs
The Fish Stairs
He passed the Fish Stairs on the way.
He had not meant to. He had not known they were on the way. He only knew the river from the top, from the bridges and the quays, the way a man from the Hill knows it. From underneath, among the piers and the fires, it was a different river, and he went along it the way you go along a road in a strange country, by the light and the smell and the sound.
The smell told him first. Fish. Not the clean salt smell of the fish market, with its ice and its eel tanks. An older smell, deeper, soaked into the stone. A thousand years of fish, landed and gutted and sold on the same steps, by the same families, in the same baskets. And under it, the river mud. And smoke.
Then the sound. Voices. Children's voices. High and quick and many, the way children's voices are in a playground.
He came round the foot of a great stone pier, and there they were.
The Fish Stairs went up from the river to the street above in one long wide flight, very old, very worn, with a landing halfway where the fishwives had their stalls in summer. Now, in winter, the stalls were shut and the boats were drawn up on the mud below, upside down, with snow on their bellies. The steps were black and wet where the river had breathed on them, and white at the top, where it had not. A gull stood on every third step.
And on the steps, children.
Thirty. Forty. He did not count. Small ones, mostly. Six, seven, eight years old. In rags and shawls and men's coats cut down, and boots tied on with string, and some with no boots at all, with their feet wrapped in sacking. Playing. Sitting on the steps in rows and talking. Throwing stones at the gulls and missing. Two little girls at the bottom, by the water, playing a clapping game, with their red hands smacking together in the cold. One, two, the river's blue.
Oren stopped at the foot of the pier and looked at them.
He did not know why. He had a great deal to do. He had a man to see at the arches, and a message to carry, and a long way still to go. He stood and looked at the children on the Fish Stairs.
Then he saw the board.
It was on the wall at the top of the steps, on the street, beside the iron gate where the fishwives came down. A notice board, wooden, with a little roof over it to keep off the rain. The kind every parish had, for the Watch and the Ministry to post their orders on. There were papers pinned to it. Old ones, torn and gray. And one new one. White. Very white, against the gray, in the hard winter light.
Oren went up the steps.
The children made way for him, the way children make way for a big man, without looking up, the way water goes round a rock. A small boy by the landing stopped throwing his stone and stared at the hilt of the sword under Terrence's coat. Oren went past him.
At the top, by the gate, he stood and read the notice.
It was printed. On good paper. With a seal at the top, pressed into the paper itself, not in wax. A round seal. A rim. An open hand, palm out. And above the hand, a small lamp.
THE MINISTRY. OFFICE OF PUBLIC HEALTH.
FOR THE CARE OF THE CITY.
NOTICE TO THE RIVER PARISHES.
The Office is aware of the sad increase in the River Fever among the children of the parish this winter. The Office is doing all in its power. The tonic for the River Fever will be given out FREE, as before, at the head of the Fish Stairs, on FRIDAY MORNING, at the eighth bell, by an officer of the Office in person. All children of the parish are to attend. Parents are asked to see that they do.
In this season of Midwinter, the Office wishes to remind the parish that it has not forgotten them.
By order. A. Pallister. Under-Secretary.
Oren read it twice.
He did not know who A. Pallister was. He did not know what the tonic was. He had not been in the fourth arch on Monday when the doctor broke the blue seal. He knew nothing about any of it, except what Mrs. Marta had said in her kitchen in the dark, when Nell had come at the fifth bell crying. Something about a man with peppermints. He saw the peppermint man. He saw the peppermint man's face.
He looked at the seal. At the open hand. At the lamp.
Has not forgotten them.
Behind him, on the steps, the two little girls by the water had started their clapping game again. One, two, the river's blue. Three, four, a sweet for more.
Oren turned round and looked at them.
He looked at them for a long moment. At their red hands. At their thin faces. At the small boy by the landing with his stone, still staring at Oren's sword. At all of them, on the old black steps, in the hard white light.
Then he turned back to the notice, and took hold of the corner of it, and tore it off the board.
It came away with a long dry rip. He folded it in half, and in half again, and put it inside Terrence's coat, against his chest, next to the oilcloth packet with the cream paper in it.
He did not know why he had done it. It was a stupid thing to do. There would be another one there by tomorrow. There were probably a hundred of them, on every parish board along the river.
But he did not think the boy at the arches would mind seeing it.
He went back down the Fish Stairs, past the children, and along the river, east.
Four Days
Corin was awake when he got back. Sitting up, this time, with his back against the brick wall of the arch and a rolled sack behind his shoulders, and the bad leg out straight in front of him on its folded sacks. His face was still gray. But his eyes were clear.
"The doctor came," he said, before Oren could ask. "At the ninth bell. He looked at it. He says the red road hasn't gone any further. He says it hasn't gone back, either. He says if I move he'll know." A thin smile. "He drew a line on my leg in ink. Where the red stops. So he can tell."
Oren looked. There was a line. Black ink, across the inside of the thigh, a hand's width above the knee, where the red ended.
Lyra was sitting on her bucket by the fire with the Ledger on her knee. She looked up at Oren when he came in, and he saw her see his face, and put the Ledger down.
"Sit," she said. "Before you fall."
He sat. On the floor, by Corin's pallet, with his back to the brick. He had not known how tired he was until he sat down. It came up through him from the cold floor like water.
Gideon was sitting cross-legged on the other side of the pallet, with his arm in its sling and his good hand inside his coat. He looked at Oren with his pale calm eyes.
"You've been to the bridge," he said. "Nell said. To see the soldiers."
"Yes."
"Was your brother there?"
Oren looked at the boy. At the thin white face, and the pale eyes, and the bird's head too big for its neck. A boy who could not feel anything, asking the plainest question in the world.
"Yes," he said. "He was there."
And then he told them.
All of it. The black column coming over the Lantern Bridge, and the drums, and the hooks. The gray horse stopping. Oren. The snow on the parapet, and the four and the two, and the gloved hand coming down flat over his, pressing it into the snow. Never do that in this city. The wagon. The girl with two braids. The door at the back of his brother's eyes, opening. There were no wagons. And the finger, by the sword hilt, against the black cloth. One, and the double.
And then the other things. Kross at Highmoor, with the brandy. The line at the bottom of the orders. An old man, gray-haired, who does not speak. Alive, if possible. The regards that had never been sent.
And Saltgate. And the Fish Locks, and the Tanners' Locks, and the Mill Cut, and the Coldwater, and the weir. One at a time. Very carefully.
"Four days," said Oren. "He said four days. To the Long Pond. If his men are careful. And then on north, past it, to wherever the hay barges go."
Corin was very still against the wall.
"Four days," he said. "From tomorrow."
"Yes."
"Wednesday. Thursday. Friday. Saturday." Corin's lips moved as he counted. Oren saw him count on his gray fingers, against the blanket. Four. Easily inside nine. "Saturday night, the Long Pond. Sunday, past it. Brindle. Withy Reach." He stopped. "Sunday."
Nobody said anything.
"And Midwinter's Monday," said Lyra, very quietly. "The Masquerade. The day after."
Corin looked at her.
Then he held out his hand for the Ledger. She brought it to him and held it open on his knee, and gave him the pencil, and he wrote. Slowly. In big shaking letters, going downhill.
Harlan Vance (Oren's brother) will search the locks from Saltgate north. Four days to the Long Pond. Saturday. Then north. Kross's orders: an old man in hay, who does not speak. ALIVE IF POSSIBLE.
Harlan says there were no wagons. His finger said HEARD.
He looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote one more line under it.
Go further than the hay goes.
"Hessa has to know," he said. "Before Saturday. She has to move him again. Further. Somewhere the battalion won't go."
"Ellis," said Oren. "The carter. He comes down to the city on Tuesdays with straw from the high farms. He'll be at the Bridewell stables tonight. He goes back north tomorrow, by the north road, past Withy Reach." He rubbed his face with his scarred hand. "He's got a note of mine in his coat. For Moll. That I gave him yesterday morning at Brindle, before Moll came. I'll take it back off him. And I'll give him a message for Hessa by mouth. No paper."
"Tonight?"
"Tonight."
Corin looked at him. At the gray in his face, Oren supposed, and the snow still on Terrence's coat, and the way he was sitting against the brick.
"You haven't slept," said Corin.
"No."
"Since Sunday."
"I'll sleep at Bridewell. In the straw. I'm good at straw now."
The ghost of a smile went across Corin's face, and went away.
Then he said, in a different voice: "Was there anything else? On the quay?"
Oren had been going to tell it. He had been going to tell it last, when the rest was done. He did not know why he had left it till last. Perhaps because it was the only thing he did not understand.
"A carriage," he said. "Black. No crest. At the foot of the Bank steps. After. With the window down." He met Corin's eyes. "Kross. He tapped on the window frame. One, and the double. At me."
Corin did not move.
"And a window," said Oren. "In the Bank. Third floor. At the river end. Three tall windows. Harlan looked up at it, once, riding over the bridge. Just his eyes. And afterwards, when everyone had gone, I looked up at it too." He hesitated. "There was somebody in it. A woman, I think. Small. In a dark green coat. With white gloves. Looking down at me. She put her hand flat on the glass. And then she went away."
The arch was very quiet.
Oren saw Corin's face change. He saw it go white, the way it had gone white at the fifth bell, at the blue wagon. He saw the gray hands close on the blanket.
And he saw Lyra see it.
She was standing by the pallet, still holding the Ledger open on Corin's knee. She was looking at Corin's face. And Oren watched her look at it, and understand that there was something there that she did not know. Something that had been there for a long time.
"Corin," said Lyra. "Who is she?"
Corin did not answer.
He was looking at Lyra. Oren could see him looking. The way he had looked at the Ledger that first morning in Vespera's back room, when it fell open. The way you look at a door you have been standing in front of for a long time and know you have to open.
"Rule one," he said at last.
His voice was very low.
Lyra went still.
"I said it to you on the wain," said Corin. "On Friday night. When you couldn't say C. T. out loud. I said not yet. When you can." He swallowed. "I should have said it to myself. I've had something I couldn't say out loud since Thursday. Since before. Since the sewers." He looked at his own gray hand on the blanket. "I'm going to say it now. Because I can't not, any more. Because Oren's brother looked up at a window."
He put out his hand. Lyra did not move. Then she shut the Ledger, slowly, and put it into his hand. And he opened it again. Not at the back. At the front. At the first page, that he read every morning, that she had read to him at every bell on Monday while he slept.
He held it out to her, open.
"Read it," he said. "The fourth line."
Lyra looked down at the page.
Oren did not need to look. He had read it once, in Vespera's back room. He knew what the fourth line said.
You have a sister. Her name is Elara. She works at the Fourth Desk. She has a gold tooth. You have not spoken to her. You think she may know who you are.
He watched Lyra read it. He watched her read it twice. Then he watched her look up from it, at Corin's face, and he saw that she had read that line before, on Monday, at every bell, to a man asleep. Aloud. Without knowing what it meant. Thinking it was only a fact, like his name and his age and the name of the Bank. And now it was not.
"The Auditor," said Lyra. "The one with the gold tooth. Who audits the Bourse at Midwinter. Who the Duke warned me about." Her voice was very steady. "That's your sister."
"Yes."
"And the girl in your fever. On the blue wagon. With the sticky hand. Corin, where are they going?"
"Yes."
"How long have you known?"
"Since the sewers. Since before. Since the night in the Bank, in the autumn, when our hands touched on her glass and it went white." Corin did not look away from her. "I didn't know for sure. I'm still not sure. I think so. I think so more every day."
Lyra closed the Ledger.
She did it gently. She put it back into his hands. And then she went back to her bucket by the fire, and sat down on it, and looked at the fire. For a long time. With her face turned away.
Oren did not know where to look. He looked at his own hands.
"You should have told me," said Lyra at last, to the fire.
"Yes."
"On the barge. On Thursday. When you told me about Ro." Her voice shook and steadied. "You sat there and told me about my brother, and how he'd read my letter and was writing back, and you were so kind about it. And all the time you had a sister of your own, a few streets away, and you didn't say."
"Yes."
"Why?"
Corin was quiet for a long moment.
"Because if I said it out loud," he said, "it would be true. And then I'd have to do something about it. And I didn't know what." He looked at the book in his hands. "And because you had enough. You had your father in the hay, and your mother's memory going to the Duke, and Ro. I didn't want to give you one more thing to carry."
Lyra turned round on the bucket and looked at him.
Her face was wet. She did not wipe it.
"That's not your choice," she said. "What I carry. That's mine. That's the whole point of rule one, Corin. That's the only point of it."
Corin looked at her for a long time.
"Yes," he said. "I know. I'm sorry."
And Lyra nodded once. As if that were enough. As if that were the only thing she had wanted. And turned back to the fire.
Nobody said anything else for a long while.
Oren sat on the cold floor with his back to the brick and his eyes closing, and thought about a small pale shape behind a window, with a white hand flat on the glass. And a boy on a tailboard, looking down into the back of a wagon. And his brother's eyes, going up, for one beat of the drums.
Cor, look.
He was asleep before he knew it, sitting up, with his sword across his knees. The way a guard sleeps.
A Visitor with Ink on His Cuff
He woke because somebody had said a name.
He did not know whose. He came up out of sleep the way a guard does, all at once, with his hand already on his sword and his eyes already open, and for a moment he did not know where he was. Brick. A low round roof. A red fire. The smell of cabbage and vinegar.
The fourth arch. Mother Grue's.
The light at the mouth of the arch had gone from white to the thin yellow of early afternoon. He had slept perhaps two hours, sitting up against the wall. His neck hurt. His legs had gone stiff. Terrence's coat had slid off one shoulder.
Corin was asleep on his pallet, with his head turned to the wall. Gideon was not there. Lyra was not on her bucket.
She was standing at the mouth of the arch, by the sacking. With her back very straight. And beside her, holding the sacking aside with one small brown hand, stood Mother Grue.
Oren had met Mother Grue once, on the night of the raid, in the autumn, before the sewers. He remembered her the way you remember a small hard stone in your boot. A tiny old woman, no bigger than a child of ten, brown and wrinkled as a walnut, in a great many shawls of different colors, one on top of another, with a man's hat on top of it all. Her eyes were black and very bright. She was looking out through the gap in the sacking at something outside, in the snow. And her face had the look of a woman who is deciding whether to set a dog on somebody.
Oren got up.
He got up quietly. He came down the arch between the sleepers, and stood behind Lyra's shoulder, and looked out over her head through the gap.
There was a young man standing in the snow.
He stood at the foot of the rubble slope, below the arches, where the gap in the wall came through from the river. Quite still. With his hands at his sides. He had come through the gap and down the slope and then stopped, at the bottom, a long way short of the first arch, the way a man stops at the edge of somebody else's yard. Waiting to be asked in.
He was not tall. He was slight, and neat, and young. Twenty, perhaps. Twenty-two. With dark hair cut short and brushed flat, and a narrow pale clever face, and dark eyes. He wore gray. A gray coat, very good, cut close, buttoned to the throat. Gray trousers. Gray gloves, very fine. A gray hat, which he had taken off, and was holding against his chest in both hands, so that the snow-light fell on his bare head.
There was nothing on him that was not gray, except one thing.
On the cuff of his right sleeve, where it came down over the wrist of the gray glove, there was a mark. A small dark stain. Blue-black, like a bruise. Ink. As if he had been writing very fast, and his sleeve had gone through the wet line.
It was the only untidy thing about him. Oren's eyes went to it at once, the way they would go to a loose thread on a guard's coat at inspection.
"Mister Castellan," said Lyra.
She said it very quietly, over her shoulder, without turning her head. For Oren. Not for the young man.
"Vivienne's ward," she said. "Her eyes. Corin met him. In the autumn. In the slag room at the Bank. And at the Bourse." A pause. "He watched us climb over the roofs from Niles's yard."
Oren said nothing. He looked at the young man in the snow. He looked at the gap in the wall behind him, and the river beyond it. At the rubble slope, and the arches on either side. He looked for other men. For the big man who smelled of beer and onions. For Gorse.
There was nobody. Only the young man. Alone. With his hat against his chest.
"He asked for Mister Vane," said Mother Grue. Her voice was like a gate creaking. "By name. Polite as a parson." She did not take her eyes off the young man. "I told him there's no Mister Vane here. I told him there's no Mister anybody here. I told him this is a place for people with no names, and he could take his nice gray hat and go and look for Mister Vane in the river."
"And?" said Oren.
"And he gave me this."
She held up her other hand. Between two small brown fingers there was a card. A small white card, very stiff and very plain, with something written on it in black ink.
Lyra took it. She read it. She gave it to Oren.
The writing was small and neat and very even, like the writing of a clerk who has written a great many ledgers. Only one line.
R. Castellan. To see Mr. C. Vane, on a matter of business. He may set any terms he likes for the meeting. I will wait.
And under it, smaller, as if it had been added afterwards, in the same neat hand, but faster, so that the letters leaned:
About Tam.
Oren looked at it for a long time.
He did not know who Tam was. He looked at Lyra.
Lyra had gone pale.
"Tam Hollin," she said. "Vivienne's man. The quiet one. He died. Here. In the fifth arch, in the autumn, after the raid on the Bourse. Mother Grue sat with him." She swallowed. "Corin harvested his Ash. When it was over. He saw—" She stopped. "He saw something in it. A man. Behind Vivienne. He wrote it in the Ledger."
Mother Grue's hand on the sacking had closed into a small brown fist.
"He was a good lad, Tam," she said. "In the end. For all he worked for her." She looked at the young man in the snow with her bright black eyes. "And that one knew him. I saw that one come to the edge of my wall in the autumn, the day after, and stand there in the snow, just like that, with his hat in his hands. He stood there an hour. He never came down. And then he went away."
Oren looked at the young man.
The young man had not moved. He stood at the foot of the slope with his hat against his chest and the snow-light on his dark head, and looked up at the arches. At the fourth one. At the gap in the sacking, where three faces were looking out at him. He could not have seen them clearly, against the dark. But he looked at the gap as if he could.
He did not smile. He did not call out. He did not take a step.
He only lifted his right hand from the brim of his hat, slowly, the hand with the ink on the cuff, and held it out a little from his side, palm open. Empty. The way a man shows a dog that he has nothing in his hand.
And waited.
Behind Oren, on the pallet by the fire, there was a movement. A rustle of sacking. A hiss of breath, as a man moves a leg he should not move.
"Who is it?" said Corin.
His voice was thick with sleep.
Lyra turned round.
"Remy Castellan," she said. "In the snow. On his own. He wants to talk to you. He says you can set any terms you like." She held up the card. "He says it's about Tam."
There was a silence.
Oren looked back over his shoulder at Corin. He saw him lying on the pallet, propped on one elbow, with his gray face turned toward the mouth of the arch. With the ink line across his thigh, a hand's breadth above the knee, where the doctor had drawn it. If I move, he'll know.
He saw Corin look at the line.
Then he saw him look at the card in Lyra's hand.
And Oren knew, the way you know a man you have stood beside at a gate, what Corin was going to say before he said it.
"Bring him in," said Corin. "Oren. Bring him in. And stand where he can see your sword."
