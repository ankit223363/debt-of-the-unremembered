Chapter 71: The First Night, Part 1 of 5: Waking in the Heap
Waking in the Heap
Lyra woke because someone's elbow was in her back.
It was a hard, bony, pointed elbow, and it was right in the soft place under her shoulder blade, and it had been there, she thought, for some time. Long enough to make an ache. She did not know whose it was. She did not know, for a moment, where she was. Only that it was dark, and close, and that she was lying on her side on something hard, with something heavy over her, and that the air she was breathing was warm and thick and smelled of goat.
Then the elbow moved, and dug in deeper, and somebody behind her muttered a word in the round Ashborn speech, crossly, in their sleep.
Oda.
It came back all at once. The Glass. The heap. The furs over them all, and the heat stones in the middle, and ten people lying back to back and front to front in the dark like puppies in a basket. Some time in the night, without waking, the heap had shifted, the way heaps do. Oda had been on the far side of Corin. Now she was behind Lyra, curled up tight with her knees in the backs of Lyra's legs and her elbow in Lyra's back and her face pressed into the fur. And Corin was in front.
Lyra lay still.
She was cold. Not all of her. The parts of her that touched other people were warm. Her back, where Oda was. Her front, where Corin was. Her feet, which were down among the heat stones in their felt wrappings. But her face was cold. Her ear, the one on top, was cold. And the tip of her nose was so cold that it hurt, a small sharp ache, like a pinch.
The furs had slipped. That was it. There was a gap, up by her face, where the edge of a hide had pulled away from the edge of the felt, and the night was coming in through it.
And the night out here was cold in a way she had not known nights could be.
She had thought she knew cold. She had walked north for a week from the city, through snow, through the fens with the ice in the reeds, over the drove road with the wind coming at them sideways off the hills. She had slept in a hay loft with frost on the inside of the window. But that cold had been wet. It had been the cold of snow and mist and dripping eaves, a cold you could see and feel and smell. This was different. This was dry. It had no smell at all. It came in through the gap by her face as thin and clean as a knife, and it went straight through the skin to the bone.
It was, she thought, about the fourth hour after midnight. She did not know how she knew. The stars, perhaps. She could see a few, through the gap. They had turned a long way since she lay down.
She should wake the others. Kestrel had said the fourth hour. They had to walk to dawn.
She did not move.
In a moment, she thought. One moment more.
She was looking at Corin.
She had not meant to. He was there, that was all. A hand's width from her face, in the dark, with his back half turned to her and his head on his arm. She could see the shape of his ear, and the line of his jaw, and the edge of his cheek. Not well. It was very dark under the furs. But there was a little light.
The light was coming from his hand.
His left hand was out. Out through the gap in the furs, on the far side of him, where he had pushed it in the night. Lying flat on the Glass, palm down, in the cold. She could see it, past his shoulder. Gray, against the black. And under it, under the Glass, so close that it lit his fingers from beneath and laid a faint gray-blue shine across the backs of them, the small soft light.
It was still there.
She had seen it come. She had been awake, then, for a little while, in the middle of the night, with her back against his. She had felt him go still in a way that was not sleep. And she had lifted her head, just a little, and looked past him, and seen the light come up out of the deep black and settle under his hand like a bird settling on a branch. And she had heard Kestrel, on the far side of the heap, say They don't do that. And she had lain down again very quietly and pretended to be asleep, because she did not know what else to do.
The light was still there. Hours later. Under his hand. Breathing. Brighter. Fainter.
And Corin was asleep.
Really asleep, now. She could tell from his breathing. Slow and deep and even, with a small catch at the top of each breath, like a man going up a stair. His hand had not moved. It lay on the Glass where he had put it, with the light under it, and he slept.
She looked at his face.
She let herself. Just this once. In the dark, with nobody to see. She did not often let herself. When he was awake there was always something. His eyes on her, not knowing her. Or his eyes not on her, looking past her, trying to remember something. Or his eyes on his own gray hands, frightened. She had learned not to look at his face for long when he was awake, because whatever she saw in it, it hurt.
But he was asleep.
And asleep, he looked younger.
Much younger. The lines had gone out of his forehead. The tight place between his eyebrows, the place that was always pulled together as if he were reading small writing, had smoothed out. His mouth was a little open. His hair had fallen across his eyes, dark, with gray in it at the side that had not been there when she first knew him. She remembered when she first knew him. He did not. But she did. And in the dark, asleep, with the gray hair hidden and the lines gone, he looked like that again. Like the man in the room over Mrs. Marta's, who used to argue with an old man in his head over supper and go red to the ears.
He looked, she thought, like somebody's boy.
She did not know where that thought came from. It came, and she let it go.
She watched him breathe.
In. The small catch at the top. Out.
She thought about putting out her hand and moving the hair off his eyes. She did not. She kept her hands where they were, folded against her chest, over the hard little shape of the bone heron under her coat.
Oda's elbow dug in again.
And from somewhere on the far side of the heap, under the furs, Kestrel's voice said, low and quite wide awake, "Now."
The Light Under His Hand
The heap came apart slowly, the way heaps do, with groans.
Someone pulled the top hide back, and the cold came in all at once over all of them like a bucket of water, and everybody swore. Arran in the round speech. Oren by the Saints. Vespera in a word Lyra had not heard since the docks. Brin did not swear, but he sucked in his breath through his teeth and sat up very carefully, holding his strapped arm against his chest.
Lyra sat up too, with the fur still round her shoulders. Her whole back was stiff. Her hip, where she had lain on it, ached as if someone had kicked it. She rubbed it and looked round.
The stars were still out. All of them. Above and below. But in the east, very low, there was the faintest thinning of the black, so faint you could only see it if you did not look at it. Not dawn. Not yet. Only the first promise of one.
And beside her, Corin was waking up.
She watched him do it. He did not come up all at once, the way Oren did, like a soldier at a bugle. He came up slowly, from a long way down, the way a diver comes up out of deep water. His breathing changed first. Then his face. The smooth young look went out of it, bit by bit, and the lines came back, and the tight place between the eyebrows. Then his eyes opened.
He lay there for a moment, looking at nothing. At the furs. At the dark.
Then he looked down at his hand.
It was still out on the Glass, past the edge of the felt. Still flat. And the light was still under it.
Lyra saw him see it. Saw his face go very still.
He did not move his hand.
Around them the others were getting up, and rolling the furs, and stamping, and blowing on their fingers, and somebody was digging in a pack for bread. Nobody was looking at Corin. Only Lyra.
And Kestrel.
Lyra had not seen her come round. But she was there, standing at the edge of the felt just beyond Corin's hand, tall and dark against the stars, with her white cape round her shoulders and her spear in her fist. Looking down. At the hand. At the light.
She did not say anything.
Corin lifted his hand.
Slowly. Off the Glass. A finger's width. Two.
And the light came up with it.
Lyra saw it. She would not have believed it if she had not. The light rose. Up through the black, the last little way, until it was right at the top of the Glass, pressed against the underside of it, the way a fish presses its mouth to the top of the water when you hold a crumb over it. Following his palm. Reaching.
Corin's hand stopped. Stayed where it was, a hand's breadth above the Glass, in the cold air.
The light stayed where it was too. Just under the top. Right under his palm. Breathing. Brighter. Fainter.
For a long moment, nothing moved.
Then Corin turned his hand over. Palm up. Empty. And drew it back, and closed it, and held it against his chest.
And the light sank.
Not fast. Not the way a stone sinks. The way a leaf sinks in a still pond in autumn, rocking a little from side to side as it goes. Down. Down through the black. Getting smaller, and fainter, and further. Until it was only one more small pale blur among all the others drifting far down in the deep cold under the Glass. And then Lyra could not tell which one it was.
Corin let out his breath.
Kestrel was still looking at the place where the light had been. Her face did not move. But Lyra saw her hand go up to the gray line on her face, and touch it, forehead to nose. Not the quick neat touch of a greeting. Slowly. Pressing. The way you might touch a charm. The way Lyra had touched the bone heron in the night.
Then Kestrel turned away, and went to her pack, and began to tie it up. As if nothing had happened.
But it had. And the others had seen. Lyra knew it when she looked round.
Not all of them. Oren had not; he was over by the heat stones, wrapping them, with his back turned. Vespera had not. But Arran had. Arran was standing very still with a roll of felt in his arms, looking at the place on the Glass where Corin's hand had been. And Marrit had. The old woman was sitting on her heels with her pole across her knees, and her lips were moving. Saying something under her breath. Over and over. Lyra did not know the words. But she knew the sound of them. It was the sound her old nurse had made, when Lyra was small and there was thunder in the night. A thing you say to keep the dark off.
And Oda had seen. Oda, with her sharp brown face and her quick black eyes, standing with her spear. She was looking at Corin. Not at his hand. At him. With her mouth pressed thin.
Nobody said anything about it.
They ate standing up. Bread. A little fat. A strip of dried goat, which was like chewing a boot. Then Kestrel came round with the water skin, and they each had a mouthful, and she watched them swallow.
Corin came and stood by Lyra, chewing.
"You saw," he said. Not a question.
"Yes."
"Did I—" He stopped. Swallowed his bread. "Was I doing that? Was I calling it? I wasn't trying to. I didn't feel anything. I just put my hand down."
"I don't know," said Lyra.
She did not. That was the truth. She did not know anything about Ash-ghosts, or the Glass, or what Corin was, or what he had been before the gray coats got to him. She only knew that a light had come up out of the dark and followed his hand like a dog follows the hand that feeds it, and that Kestrel had touched her face as if she were afraid.
"Whatever it was," she said, "it went back."
"Yes."
"When you let it."
Corin looked at her.
She did not know why she had said that. It had come out of her the way things did sometimes, before she had thought about them. But when she heard it, she thought it was right. He had turned his hand over. Empty. And the light had gone.
As if it had been waiting for him to say it could.
Blue Fire
Brin showed her the fire while the others were tying their packs.
She had not asked him to. She had not said anything to him at all, since the Edge, except thank you, once, when he passed her a water skin. He was a big slow quiet man, as big as Oren and broader, with a round shaved head and the gray spread over both his cheeks like frost on a window. He did not speak much of the southern tongue. A few words, from trading. He did not seem to need many.
She was sitting on the rolled felt with her hands pushed up her sleeves, trying to get some feeling back into her fingers. And he came and sat down on his heels in front of her with his strapped arm held against his chest, and put out his good hand, closed. And opened it.
In it was a twig.
A thorn twig. As long as her hand, dark and dry and crooked, with three or four long pale thorns sticking out of it. From the thorn slope, she thought. From the bushes that all leaned away from the Glass.
And a small flat piece of gray stone, and a curved bit of old iron with a hole in one end, worn bright along the edge. A striker.
Brin looked at her. Then he nodded at the twig. And at her. As if to say, watch.
She watched.
He held the twig between his knees, with the thorny end up. He could not use both hands, so he held the stone in his bad hand, the one in the sling, pinched in the fingers that stuck out of the end of the strapping. And he struck the iron down across it, once, hard, with his good hand.
Sparks.
They jumped out, bright orange, a little shower of them, the way sparks always do. Lyra saw them fall on the dry end of the twig. She saw one catch, and glow.
And go blue.
She leaned forward.
The spark had not gone out. It had caught. There was a flame on the end of the twig now. A real flame, small, no bigger than the flame of a cheap candle. It stood up off the dry wood and moved, and flickered, and leaned in the small dry wind the way flames lean.
But it was blue.
Not the blue at the bottom of a candle flame, that you see if you look close. All of it. Every bit. A pale clear cold blue, like the blue of the sky in the very early morning, before the sun. It had no yellow in it. No orange. No red. It gave almost no light. She could see it clearly against the dark, but it did not light Brin's face, or his hand, or the twig. It was as if somebody had cut a small flame-shaped piece out of a winter sky and stuck it on the end of a stick.
And there was no smoke.
She stared at it.
Brin held it out to her. Toward her face. Close. Closer than you would ever hold a flame to somebody's face.
She did not flinch. She did not know why not. Perhaps because there was nothing to flinch from. She could not feel it. It was a hand's width from her cheek, and she could not feel it at all. No warmth on her skin. Nothing.
Brin moved the twig down. Toward her hands. And nodded.
She did not want to. Every part of her that had ever been a child, and been told don't touch that, it's hot, did not want to.
She put out one finger. And put it into the flame.
It was cool.
Not cold. Cool. Like putting your finger into water. Clean running water, in a stone basin, in summer. She could feel it, a little, round her fingertip. A faint softness. A faint movement, like the flicker of the flame against the skin. But no heat. No pain. No smell of burning skin.
She took her finger out and looked at it. It was not even red.
She put her whole hand in.
Brin laughed. A low, slow, surprised sound, like a big dog grumbling. And held the twig still while she held her hand in the blue fire, palm down, and turned it over, and back, and the cool soft flame licked between her fingers and over her knuckles and round her wrist, and did nothing at all.
"Nothing burn here," said Brin.
He said it slowly, in the southern tongue, picking each word, the way you pick stones to cross a stream.
"Nothing burn here. Not even—" He nodded at the twig. Looked for the word. Found it. "Fire."
Lyra looked at the twig. The flame had burned down into it a little way, the length of her fingernail. And the wood where it had been was not black. It was not ash. It was gray, and smooth, and hard, like stone.
"It's turning the wood to stone," she said.
Brin did not understand her. But he looked at the gray end of the twig and nodded, as if he did. Then he licked his finger and thumb and pinched the blue flame out. Just like that. Between his finger and his thumb. The way you would pinch out a candle, if candles did not burn.
He gave her the twig.
She held it. It was light. It was warm, a little, from his hand. The burned end was cold, and smooth, and gray.
"Thank you," she said.
Brin nodded, and got up, slowly, and went back to his pack.
Lyra sat with the twig in her hand and looked at the gray end of it.
She had been afraid of fire for weeks. Since the dome. Since Corin held it up and the gold came down and she watched his arms go gray to the shoulder. Since Last Thorn, two nights ago, when the tents went up all round her and she smelled the hide burning and heard the goats screaming, and gave away her dancing to keep three children alive. She had dreamed of fire. She had smelled it in her hair for two days, and the smell would not wash out.
And here, fire was cool as water. Here, fire could not hurt you.
She did not know whether that made her feel safer, or more afraid.
She put the twig in her pocket, next to the bone heron, and stood up.
