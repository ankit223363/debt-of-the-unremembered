Chapter 71: The First Night, Part 5 of 5: One Thing
One Thing
She dropped back to Corin. She did not tell him what Kestrel had said. She did not know how.
He was going steadily on his crutch, a little behind the others now, with his face set and gray with sweat under the hide. Pat. Step-step. Pat. She fell in beside him on his bad side and matched her slide to his step, and for a while neither of them spoke.
Then he said, without looking at her, "Tell me one thing."
"What?"
"One thing. About you. Anything." He kept his eyes on the Glass in front of the crutch. "I've been thinking. I lost all of you at the dome. Everything. I've got your voice and that's all. And every day since, I've been getting bits back. Not the old bits. New ones. You hit a constable with a milk jug. You break bread like somebody who's done it before. You slept." He took a breath. "I want more. I want one a day, at least. Out here, where nothing can be taken. So that whatever I get on the Glass, I keep."
Lyra walked.
She thought about it. About what one thing to give him. It was harder than it sounded. There were so many. And most of them hurt, or were too big, or were about her father, or her mother, or things he had been there for and did not remember. She did not want to give him one of those. Not out here, with his face gray and his breath short.
She put her hand inside her coat and took out the bone heron.
It was warm from her skin. Small and yellowed and smooth, standing on its one leg, with its long neck curved down. The loop of leather round her neck pulled tight as she held it out.
"When I was small," she said, "there were herons at the bottom of our garden. On Crown Walk. The garden went down to the river, and there was a wall, and a gate in the wall, and steps going down to the water. And in the mornings, early, there was always a heron standing on the bottom step. Just standing. On one leg. Looking at the water."
Corin was listening. She could tell by the way his head had turned, a little, toward her.
"I used to get up early to see it," said Lyra. "Before anyone else. I'd go down the garden in my nightgown, in bare feet, with the grass wet. And sit on the wall. And watch it. And it would watch the water. For a long, long time. Not moving at all. And then all of a sudden—" She jabbed with the heron in her fingers, quick, down. "And it would have a fish. And it would swallow it. And go back to standing."
She looked at the charm.
"I used to try to stand like it," she said. "On the wall. On one leg. To see how long I could do it. My mother caught me once and I thought she'd be cross. But she just stood on one leg too. In her good shoes. On the garden path. And we stood there, the two of us, like a pair of fools, until the heron flew away." She put the charm back inside her coat. "That's where the dancing started, I think. Standing on one leg on the garden wall."
Corin did not say anything for a while.
Then he stopped. Leaned his weight on the crutch. Pulled his pack round, one-handed, and got out the green book and the pencil stub. And wrote, there, standing on the Glass in the hot sun with the others going on ahead.
She watched the letters come. Square and black.
SHE STOOD ON ONE LEG ON A GARDEN WALL TO WATCH A HERON. HER MOTHER DID IT TOO. IN GOOD SHOES.
He looked at it. Then he added a line underneath.
THAT'S WHERE THE DANCING STARTED.
He closed the book and put it away. And looked at her.
"The girl at the Edge," he said. "Who gave you that. She said you dance like one."
"Yes."
"She was right, then." He settled the crutch under his arm. "Even if you can't anymore. She saw it. Whatever it was, it's still in how you stand."
Lyra did not answer. She could not.
They walked on, a little behind the others, side by side. And after a while, she found that she was holding the heron again, through the cloth of her coat. Pressing it flat against her chest. The way you press your hand over a place that hurts, or a place that is glad, and cannot tell which.
Two Hours Behind
The ticking came back with the heat.
Lyra did not hear it at first. She felt the others hear it. That was how it went, out here. You were walking, and the sun was white, and the air was beginning to shake over the swells, and the felt on your feet was hot and wet. And then the person in front of you changed the way they walked. Just a little. Their shoulders came up. Their head turned, half round, and then back. And you knew.
It was Sef, in front of her, whose shoulders came up first. Then Brin's. Then Oren stopped.
He stopped on the side of a long low swell, and turned round, and stood with his head on one side.
Lyra had seen him do it before. On the road north. In the fens, at night, with the mist on the water. He would stop, and turn his head, and stand very still, like a big old dog listening at a door. And he would stay like that for a long time. And then he would say something like three men, on foot, a mile back, one of them lame, and he would be right.
She stopped too. They all did.
And in the silence, she heard it.
Not loud. Not even quite a sound. A tiny, hard, even tapping, very far off, coming to them on the hot still air from the south. Like someone tapping a fingernail on a window, three rooms away.
Tick. Tick. Tick.
Steady. Patient. Not hurrying. Not slowing.
Oren listened for a long time. His eyes were shut, under the eye hide. She could see his lips moving a little, inside his short gray beard. Counting. Not numbers. Something else. The way soldiers count the space between a flash and the bang that comes after.
Then he opened his eyes.
"Two hours behind," he said. "Maybe less."
Nobody said anything.
"They've had the morning," said Oren. "They started when the sun warmed the top. They don't stop. They don't rest. They don't drink. They walk in a straight line, and they don't have to go round anything." He did not look at Kestrel. But Lyra knew he was thinking of the humming lump, and the long wide curve they had walked round it. "We lost a bit there. We'll lose more at noon, when we lie down and they don't."
"And at night they freeze," said Kestrel.
"And at night they freeze," Oren agreed. "And we gain it back. And a little more." He rubbed his face. "It's a race. That's all. A race where they run by day and we run by night, and every night we have to get further than they get every day."
"Can we?" said Vespera.
Oren looked at her.
"So far," he said.
He turned round to the north again. And began to walk.
And they all walked faster.
Nobody told them to. Kestrel did not say anything. Oren did not say anything. But Lyra felt it happen, all along the line. Every step a little longer. Every step a little quicker. Sef in front of her, who had been dragging his feet since the dawn, picked them up. Marrit, slow and heavy, leaned harder on her pole and went faster with it. Oda, on her bad feet, did not limp at all, though Lyra knew from the set of her back that every step must hurt.
And Corin.
Corin, on his crutch, with his stiff knee that would never bend again. He went faster too. Pat. Step-step. Pat. Step-step. His face, under the hide, was set and gray with sweat. Lyra could hear his breath, short and hard. He did not ask anyone to slow down. He did not look back.
She went up beside him, on his bad side. And did not say anything. And did not take his arm, because he needed both, now, for the crutch and the balance. She just walked there. Close. So that if he went over, she would be there.
She listened to the ticking.
She could not stop listening, now that she had heard it. It was always there, under everything. Under the scuff of the felt and the pat of the crutch and the small hot wind that had got up, a little, from the west. Tick. Tick. Tick. She found that her feet were walking to it. She made them stop. They started again. Tick. Step. Tick. Step.
Three of them. She had counted them at the Edge, in the dark, on the night Last Thorn burned. Three voices, ticking. Three tall iron men with clockwork insides, coming down the long slope after them through the rotten snow.
And somewhere behind those three, the other two, wherever they were. And somewhere behind them, a man on a horse, with his hat in his hand.
She thought about the iron hand coming down on the children in the burning camp. The way it had not been angry. Not cruel. Not anything. Only doing what the word said. Pushing people out of the way like thorns.
It has only ever wanted him, Kestrel had said.
She looked at Corin, beside her, going on his crutch across the shining Glass with the sweat running down from under his eye hide.
She thought: then it will have to come through me.
She did not say it. She put her hand on the hilt of her sword, the way Oren did. Not gripping. Just resting. And walked.
Tick.
Step.
Tick.
Noon
Kestrel stopped them in a trough again, when the sun stood straight overhead and Lyra's shadow had shrunk to a dark puddle round her feet.
The Ashborn put up the shades. Lyra helped this time. She had watched them do it the day before, and she wanted to have something in her hands. Arran showed her how the bone poles fitted together, three pieces each, the end of one sliding into the hollow end of the next with a small dry click. How the sand bags went. How you leaned the pole into the bag, not straight, but tipped a little toward the middle of the shade, so that the hide pulling on it would push it down into the sand and not out.
He was quiet. He did not tell stories. He did not take out his flute. When she got a pole wrong and it slid out of the bag and fell with a clatter on the Glass, he did not laugh. He picked it up and gave it back to her and showed her again.
"It's all right," she said to him. "About the lump. Nothing happened."
Arran looked at her.
"Something happened," he said. "It woke. For a moment. It sang back." He tied off a corner of the hide. "And now it knows that someone came by who knew the tune. And it will lie there, under the Glass, and wonder who. For a long time." He pulled the knot tight. "That is a cruel thing to do to anyone. Even a lump."
Lyra did not know what to say to that.
The shades went up. Three of them. They crawled in under them and lay down on the felt, in the hot gold light that came through the hides, and the glare went away.
Kestrel came round with the water.
Not four mouthfuls today. One.
She did not explain. She went from person to person on her knees under the low hide, with the skin in her hands, and held it to each of their mouths, and let them take one swallow. And then took it away. Firmly. With her hand on the neck of the skin, so they could not take more.
When she came to Lyra, Lyra swallowed, and it was warm and tasted of hide, and it was gone almost before it touched her throat. Her mouth was as dry afterward as before. Drier.
"One?" said Oren, when she came to him.
"One."
"Yesterday it was four."
"Yesterday we were close to the Edge." Kestrel held the skin to his mouth. "Today we are not. Tomorrow we will be further. Each of us has three skins. Each skin must last two days. There is water at the Eye, Old Hask said, under the ground, if it has not gone bad since his time. Between here and there, nothing." She waited while he swallowed. "So. One."
Oren swallowed. He did not argue.
Lyra lay on her back on the felt and looked up at the glowing gold hide.
She was so thirsty.
She had not known that thirst could be like this. She had been thirsty before. After dancing. After a long ride in summer. You were thirsty, and you drank, and it went away. This did not go away. It sat in her mouth and her throat and behind her eyes. Her lips were cracked. Her tongue felt too big. When she swallowed, there was nothing to swallow, and her throat clicked.
And it was only the second day.
She made herself think of something else.
She thought of Oren laughing on the Glass in the morning, with his sword in his hand. Of the Ashborn clapping. Of the slide, slide, turn from the hips, and her blade coming up to take his and hold it, for one moment.
She would do it again after this rest. He had said so. And at every rest after.
She thought of the heron charm under her coat. She put her hand on it. It was warm from her body.
She thought of her mother's handkerchiefs. Hold it up to the light. Look.
And of Vespera's mouth closing like a seam pulled tight.
She turned her head on the felt. Under the next shade, through the gap between the hides, she could see Vespera. Lying on her side with her back to them all, curled small, with her blue hands tucked under her cheek. She did not look asleep. She looked like someone pretending.
Under the same shade, Corin was asleep. Truly asleep, this time. On his back, with one arm over his eyes, and the crutch lying beside him, and his stiff leg out straight. His mouth was a little open.
She watched his chest go up and down.
She thought about what Kestrel had said. Because it knew him. And: He says he has never been here.
She thought about the small footprints, going south, that Corin had found the morning before and said were nothing. She had seen his face. She had seen him put his boot beside them. She had seen the angle.
She had not said anything. Nobody had.
She closed her eyes.
The heat came up through the felt. The light came down through the hide. And under all of it, faint, steady, patient, too far to hear unless you were listening for it, and she was always listening for it now, the ticking.
She did not sleep.
She lay with her eyes closed and her hand on the heron and her dry mouth, and thought about water, and about her mother, and about a small child walking barefoot across the Glass toward the south, alone, with the Glass going soft under its feet.
The Water
Lyra saw it because she was not asleep.
Kestrel had gone round all three shades with the water skin. One mouthful each. Lyra had counted, without meaning to, lying on her back and listening. Oren. Vespera. Corin, woken for it and then asleep again at once. Oda. Brin. Marrit. Arran. Herself. Sef.
Kestrel had come to Sef last. He was at the far end of the last shade, lying on his side, facing north, the way the Ashborn always lay. He had not said a word since dawn. Not to anyone. He had walked with his head down and his thin shoulders up and his eyes on the Glass, and when Arran had told the story of the goat that married a Kesh-Amaran goat, and even Brin had laughed, Sef had not.
He was young. Lyra had not understood how young until now, seeing him lying there in the gold light under the hide, with his knees drawn up. The gray line on his face was very short. It went from his forehead down over the bridge of his nose and stopped just past the tip. Arran's went further. Kestrel's went all the way over her head. The more you carried, Lyra had learned, the further the gray went.
Sef had not carried many.
One, she thought. His sister's man. Two winters ago. And now he was out here, a long way from his sister and her baby, with you'd better still in his ears.
Kestrel held the skin to his mouth, and he drank his one swallow. And she took the skin away.
And then she sat back on her heels, and looked at him.
And lifted the skin again. To her own mouth.
Lyra watched her. Kestrel had not had hers yet. She always went last. She tipped the skin up to her lips.
And did not drink.
Lyra saw it quite clearly. Saw Kestrel's throat not move. Saw the water come up to the neck of the skin and stop there, against her closed lips, and go back down. Kestrel held the skin there for exactly as long as it takes to swallow. Then she lowered it. And wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist, the way you do after you drink.
And then she held the skin out to Sef again.
"Here," she said. In the round speech. Something short. Lyra did not know the words. But she knew the voice. It was the voice her old nurse had used when Lyra would not eat her supper. Brisk. Not to be argued with. Here. Go on. There's a bit left in the bottom, it'll only go warm.
Sef looked at the skin. Then at Kestrel.
He said something. A question.
Kestrel answered. Short again. And shrugged, and looked away, out north from under the edge of the shade. As if it did not matter at all.
Sef took the skin. He drank. One more swallow. Kestrel's swallow.
Kestrel took it back, and tied the neck, and put it in her pack. And lay down at the end of the shade, near Sef, on her back, with her spear beside her and her arm over her eyes.
She did not look at anyone.
Lyra lay very still.
She thought nobody else had seen. Arran was asleep, his long body curled up like a dog's. Brin was asleep. Oda was lying with her eyes shut and her jaw clenched, holding her bad foot. Marrit was facing away, toward the north, with her old gray head on her arm.
Nobody had seen but Lyra.
Kestrel had given away her water. Out here, where water was life. Where one mouthful at noon was all there was. She had given her whole share to the youngest, and pretended she had drunk it, and lied to him about it with a shrug. And now she was lying there with her arm over her eyes in the heat and her mouth as dry as Lyra's. Drier. She had walked further than any of them today. She had stood on the top of the swells in the sun, listening. She had walked round the lump.
And she had given her water to Sef.
Because he was young, and quiet, and far from home, and had not laughed at the story of the goat.
Lyra thought: giving works.
That was what Vespera had said. On the Glass you cannot take, and you cannot burn. But giving works. A laugh is a kind of giving.
She had not thought, when Vespera said it, that it meant this. She had thought it was magic. Some rule of the Glass, like the blue fire, like the lights.
But perhaps it was not magic at all. Perhaps it was only this. A woman giving her water to a boy, out here, where nobody could see. Where nothing could be taken from you, so whatever left your hands, you had to give.
She thought about her own one swallow. Already gone. Already dry in her mouth.
She thought about the night before, and the taste of salt that would not burn, and how glad she had been. How safe. Nothing can be taken from me here. She had sat on the Glass and cried with relief, because for the first time in years, she would arrive somewhere with everything she had.
And here was Kestrel. Who had arrived here with nothing. Who carried a whole crowded bed of the dead. And who was still finding things to give away.
Lyra felt her face go hot again, under the hide. Not the soft hot of the morning, with Corin and the book. A different kind. The kind you feel when you see something good, and know you would not have thought to do it.
She lay there for a long time.
Then she reached into her coat, very quietly, and felt for the twig. Brin's thorn twig, with the end turned to smooth gray stone by the blue fire. She took it out and held it.
She could not burn. She could not take.
But she could give.
She did not know yet what she would give, or to whom. But she lay there in the heat, with the twig in her hand and her eyes on Kestrel's still shape at the end of the shade, and thought about it.
She was still thinking about it when the shouting started.
People in the Trench
It was Sef shouting.
Lyra had not seen him go. She had been looking at Kestrel, and at the twig in her hand, and thinking. And at some point Sef must have got up from his place at the end of the last shade, quietly, the way he did everything. And gone out into the glare.
She thought she could guess why. He had felt the extra water go down his throat, and had lain there with it, and had not been able to bear it. You did not say thank you to Kestrel. She would only shrug and look away. So he had got up to do something useful instead. To go up to the top of the next swell north, in the noon sun, and look at the way ahead, so that Kestrel would not have to do it herself when the rest was over.
It came from the north. From up the long slope of the swell ahead of them, beyond the edge of the shades, out in the white shaking glare. A man's voice, high and cracked, calling something in the round Ashborn speech. Over and over. The same few words.
Kestrel was up before Lyra had even turned her head.
She came out from under the end of the shade in one movement, on her feet, with her spear in her hand and the eye hide already pulled down. Arran was a breath behind her. Then Oda, on her bad feet, with her spear. Then Brin, holding his arm.
Lyra crawled out after them. The glare hit her like a slap. She dragged the eye hide down over her eyes, and the world went to two thin burning lines, and in the lines she saw him.
Sef. Coming down the swell toward them. Running.
Not the way the Ashborn walked, smooth and careful, sliding their felt. Running the way you run when you have forgotten where you are. His feet going out from under him on the smooth hot Glass, and catching, and going again. He fell. She saw him fall, full length, on the black, and she thought of the noon heat and Kestrel's words, cooked like meat, and her stomach turned over. But he was up again at once, scrambling, his hands slapping the hot Glass and jerking away from it, and running again. Down the slope toward them. Still shouting.
Kestrel ran to meet him.
She caught him at the bottom of the swell, by both arms, and held him up. He was gasping. Lyra could hear it from where she stood. Great dragging sobbing breaths, as if he had run a mile instead of a few hundred steps. His palms were red. His knees, where he had fallen, were red through the torn leggings.
And his face.
Lyra saw his face as he came up into the shade of Kestrel's body.
It was white. Not pale. White. White as chalk, white as paper, all the color gone out of it, so that the short gray line from his forehead to the tip of his nose stood out on it as dark as a burn. His mouth was open. His eyes, under the edge of the eye hide where it had slipped, were very wide.
He was not hurt. Or not badly. That was not why.
He had seen something.
Kestrel was saying something to him. Low. Fast. Holding his arms. Sef was answering in gasps. A word. Then another. In the round speech. Lyra could not follow it. But she saw Kestrel's face change as she listened. Saw it go very still.
The others had come out of the shades now. All of them. Oren with his sword half drawn. Vespera, small and squinting, with her hand on her needle roll. Marrit with her pole. And Corin, last, levering himself up on his crutch, slowly, with his stiff leg, and coming to stand beside Lyra.
"What is it?" said Oren. "Are they ahead of us? Has something got round us?"
Kestrel did not answer him. She was still listening to Sef.
Then Sef turned his head. And looked past Kestrel, at the four from the south. At Oren, with his sword. At Lyra. At Corin.
And he said it again, in the southern tongue. Slowly. Carefully. The way Brin had said nothing burn here. Picking each word, like a stone to step on. As if he wanted to be sure they understood. As if he needed someone else to have heard it, so that it would not be only his.
"The Trench," said Sef.
He swallowed. His dry throat clicked.
"The Trench. And there are people standing in it."
