Chapter 71: The First Night, Part 3 of 5: No Dreams
No Dreams
She told him at the first rest, when the sun was a hand's width up and the Glass was beginning to warm.
They had stopped on the side of a swell, all ten of them, sitting in a loose ring on their rolled felt with their backs to the sun and their eye hides pushed up on their foreheads. Not long. Only long enough for a mouthful of water and a strip of dried goat, and for Kestrel to put her hand flat on the Glass and listen.
Corin was sitting with his bad leg out straight in front of him and the crutch across his lap, rubbing the knee with the heel of his gray hand. Slowly. Round and round. The knee that would not bend anymore, that Silas had set straight for good. He did it without seeming to know he was doing it, the way some people chew their lip.
Lyra sat down beside him.
"I didn't dream," she said.
He looked at her.
"Last night. In the heap." She did not know why she was telling him. She had not meant to. It was just there, in her mouth, and it came out. "I didn't dream. I went to sleep and then I woke up with Oda's elbow in my back and there was nothing in between. Nothing at all. Like a door shutting and opening."
"Is that unusual?"
"Yes."
She looked down at her hands.
"I've dreamed every night," she said, "since my father went quiet. Since he stopped talking. Before he died. Before any of it." She turned her gloves over. "Not always bad dreams. Just—dreams. Every night. Him, mostly. In the house on Crown Walk, the old house, before it was shut up. Sitting in his chair by the fire with a book. Or in the garden. Or on the stairs. And he'd look up. And he'd open his mouth to say something. And I'd wake up."
Corin did not say anything.
"Every night," said Lyra. "Since the autumn. And then on the barge, and in the crypt, and on the road. And after he—after Thursday. Every night. I'd see him look up, and open his mouth. And I never once heard what he was going to say."
She stopped.
"And last night I didn't dream at all," she said. "Not about him. Not about anything. I just slept."
She did not say the rest of it. That she had woken, and lain there with the cold on her face and Oda's elbow in her back, and not known for a long moment what was different. And then known. And felt, under the cold, something loosen in her chest that had been tight since the autumn. The way a knot loosens when you have been pulling at it for so long that you forgot it could.
She did not say that she had felt, for that moment, rested. Really rested. For the first time in months.
She did not say that she had felt guilty about it at once.
Corin was quiet for a while. Then he reached round behind him and pulled his pack across, and undid the strap, and took out the green book.
Ledger VIII. Her mother's family book. With the gold tree on the cover, worn almost away, and the cracked spine, and the pages at the front full of her mother's people's names in faded brown ink. And then the pages after, in Corin's square black capitals, pressed hard into the paper. Day after day. Everything he could not trust himself to keep.
He found the page. He took the stub of pencil out of the spine. And he wrote.
She watched him do it. She could still read. She could not write, not one letter, not since her father's hand. But she could read. And she watched the letters come out of the pencil one by one, square and black and careful.
THURSDAY. ON THE GLASS.
He stopped. Thought. Wrote again.
SHE SLEPT.
That was all. Two words. He looked at them for a moment. Then he put the pencil back in the spine, and closed the book.
"Why did you write that?" said Lyra.
He looked surprised.
"Because it's important," he said.
"It's not important. It's nothing. It's sleeping."
"You haven't slept since the autumn. You just said." He put the book back in his pack. "And now you have. That's important. That's one of the most important things that's happened." He did up the strap. "If I forget everything else, I want to know that. That we got onto the Glass, and you slept."
Lyra looked at him.
She could feel it happening. The warmth coming up in her face. Something going soft and foolish in her chest. It was such a small thing. Two words, in pencil, in a book. She slept. It was the kind of thing you would write about a child, or a sick person, or a dog.
And it pleased her so much that she could have hit him.
She was annoyed. Really annoyed. With him, for writing it, and for saying that's important in that plain flat voice as if it were obvious. And with herself, for minding. For being pleased. For sitting there with her face going hot like a girl at her first ball because a man who did not remember her had written two words about her in a book.
"You're very annoying," she said.
"So you've said."
"When?"
"At Thistle Ford. In the loft." He frowned. "I think. I wrote it down. She says I'm very annoying. So you must have."
"Well. You are."
"All right."
She got up. She did not know where she was going. Away. Anywhere. To the other side of the ring, to sit by Vespera, who would say something sharp and make her feel normal.
She went three steps. Then she stopped, and turned round.
"Corin."
He looked up.
"Did you dream?"
He thought about it. She could see him think about it. Looking back into the night, the way you look into a dark room.
"No," he said slowly. "No. I don't think I did." He looked down at his gray hand, the left one. At the palm. "I don't think anybody dreams out here."
"Is that good?"
"I don't know."
She went and sat down by Vespera. Vespera did not say anything sharp. She gave Lyra a sideways look, out of her small black eyes, and then looked away again. And moved her pack, a little, so there was more room.
It was worse than something sharp.
Lyra sat with her knees up and her arms round them, and looked out at the Glass. And her face stayed hot for a long time.
Footing
Oren saved her. He did not know he was doing it. Or perhaps he did. With Oren it was hard to tell.
He got up from his place in the ring, slowly, with a grunt, and stretched until his back cracked. And then he drew his sword.
Everybody looked up. The Ashborn too. Oda's hand went to her spear.
Oren did not seem to notice. He held the sword up in front of his face and looked along the blade in the low gold light. It was an old plain soldier's sword, long and straight, with a leather grip gone black with sweat and a notch out of the edge near the guard, from Last Thorn. A black stain ran down one side of the blade that would not wipe off. Iron oil, from the inside of an Eraser.
He turned it in his hand, once. Then he looked at Lyra.
"Up," he said.
"What?"
"Up. Sword out. You've been falling over like a newborn calf since the Edge. Two nights I've picked you up. You'll do it in a fight, and I won't be there to pick you up, and you'll be dead." He swung the sword round, easy, and set the point on the Glass. "So up. We'll find out what your feet know."
"They don't know anything," said Lyra. "That's the point. I gave it away."
"You gave the dancing," said Oren. "Not the feet. The feet are still on the ends of your legs. I can see them." He tipped his head. "Up."
She got up.
She did not want to. Her face was still hot from Corin and the book. But it was easier, she found, to be angry with Oren than with herself. She stood up and drew her sword.
It felt the same in her hand. That was something. The grip. The weight. The long slim blade with the black stain from Last Thorn dried along its edge. Her hand knew it. Her arm knew it. She had not given those.
She set her feet.
And they went wrong at once. She felt it. They went into the old stance, the one she had stood in ten thousand times, light and up on the balls, one in front of the other, ready to dance. The stance the dancing had built. And without the dancing to hold it up, it was just a shape. An empty shape. Her feet were in it, but they did not know why. They did not know what to do next.
And under them the felt was sliding.
She had not noticed it so much, walking. But standing still, in a stance, she could feel it. The felt on the smooth Glass was not like a boot on stone. It did not grip. It sat on the Glass like a cloth on a polished table, and the smallest push sent it sliding.
Oren came at her.
Not fast. Not hard. A plain cut from high on the right, slow as a lesson. She knew it. She knew exactly what to do. Step left and in, take it on the flat, turn the wrist, and—
She stepped. Her front foot slid. A finger's width, no more. But it was enough. Her weight went wrong, and her blade came up late, and Oren's took hers just above the guard and turned it, almost lazily, and her sword went out of her hand and slid away across the Glass with a long ringing scrape.
She stood there with her hand empty.
"One," said Oren.
She went and got it.
She came back and set her feet again. Lighter this time. More carefully. Up on the balls.
He came at her. The same cut. She went to step. And her back foot went out from under her, sideways, and she staggered, and he flicked the sword out of her hand again. Not even hard. Like knocking a cup off a table.
"Two," said Oren.
She went and got it. Her face was burning now. Not the soft burn from before. The other kind.
She could hear the Ashborn behind her. Not laughing. Not yet. But watching. Arran said something low to Brin.
She came back. Set her feet.
"No," said Oren.
She looked at him.
"Not like that. That's how you used to stand. Up on your toes, like a girl at a ball. That's the dancing." He shook his head. "It's gone. Leave it. You stand like that out here, you'll go over every time." He lifted his own front foot a little, and set it down flat. "Flat. Like this. Whole foot. Heels down. Knees bent more. More. Like you're sitting on a stool that isn't there."
She bent her knees.
"More."
She bent them more. It felt ugly. It felt like the way the men on the docks stood when they were loading a cart.
"Good. Now. Small steps. Little ones. Don't lift your feet. Slide them. Like you're on a floor somebody's just polished and you don't want to leave marks." He slid his own foot forward on the Glass, a hand's width, and brought the back one after it. "Like that. And you don't fight from your feet anymore. Your feet are just for standing. You fight from here." He put the flat of his free hand on his own hip. "From the hips. Turn here. Not there. Everything comes from here."
She tried it. Slide. Slide. It felt like walking in mud.
Oren came at her again.
The same cut. She did not step. She slid, a hand's width, and turned from the hips, and her blade came up and took his on the flat, and held it.
For a moment.
Then he twisted his wrist, quick as a snake, and his blade went round hers and under and up, and her sword went flying for the third time.
But it was not the same. She knew it was not. She had held it, for a moment. Her feet had not slid. She had not fallen.
"Three," said Oren.
And he laughed.
She had never heard him laugh like that. Not the short bark he used when something was grimly funny. A real laugh. Big and deep, from the belly, rolling out over the Glass. He stood there with his old sword in his hand and his scarred face split open in a grin, and laughed, and his gray eyes were wet with it.
"Saints," he said. "Saints, girl, you held it. Did you feel that? You held it."
And behind her the Ashborn began to clap.
Not all at once. Brin first, slow, his good hand slapping against his thigh because he could not use the other. Then Arran, with his long hands. Then Sef. Then Marrit, very solemn, with her pole held in the crook of her arm. Even Oda. Even Kestrel. Clapping, out here, in the middle of nothing, for a girl who had dropped her sword three times.
Lyra went and picked it up.
She stood with it in her hand, and looked at Oren, and found that she was grinning too. She did not know when it had started.
"Again," she said.
"At noon," said Oren. "And every rest after. Till you can do it in your sleep." He sheathed his sword. His face was still bright. "Saints, it's good to be just a swordsman again. No walls. No burning. No words going out of my head every time I lift a hand. Just a sword, and a bit of floor, and somebody to knock about." He looked out over the Glass. "I'd forgotten."
He went back to his place in the ring and sat down, still smiling.
And Lyra understood, watching him, why he had done it. Not just for her feet.
Out here, with nothing to burn, he had nothing left to lose. For the first time in years, every time he drew his sword, it would cost him nothing at all.
She had never seen him so happy.
L
There was grit on the Glass at the next rest.
Not much. A thin skin of it, in the bottom of a long shallow dip between two swells, where the wind had carried it and dropped it. Fine dark grit, almost black, like ground pepper. Sand, Kestrel said, blown in from the far side over years and years. Or ash. Nobody knew. It lay on the smooth Glass in long soft ripples, like the ripples the tide leaves on a beach, and when you walked through it your felt left marks.
They stopped there because the sun was getting high and Kestrel wanted to listen again. And Lyra sat down on the edge of the grit, a little way from the others, with her sword across her knees and her legs aching from Oren's slides.
She looked at the grit for a long time.
Then she took off her right glove.
She did not decide to. Her hand did it. She watched it happen. She pulled the glove off finger by finger and laid it on her knee, and looked at her bare hand, pale and chapped, with the nails broken short and a long healing scratch across the back from Last Thorn.
And she put out one finger and touched the grit.
It was soft. Softer than she expected. Cool, and very fine, and a little warm underneath where the Glass came through. Her finger left a dent in it.
She drew the finger down. A line. Straight down, toward her.
Or not straight.
She looked at it. It was not a straight line. It wandered. It went down, and leaned a little to the right, and then came back, and then wobbled at the bottom where she had stopped. Like a line drawn by a child. Or by someone very old, whose hand shook.
She had meant to make it straight. She had thought about straight. She had pictured it, in her head, a clean straight line. And her hand had heard her, and nodded, and done this.
She drew the second stroke. The foot. Out to the right, from the bottom of the first.
It went out. Then it curled up. Then it went down again, and ran into the first line in a little tangle, and stopped.
She looked at what she had made.
It was not an L. It was not anything. It was a wandering scratch in the grit, with a tangle at the bottom, that could have been made by a bird walking.
L. For Lyra. The first letter of her own name. The first letter her mother had ever taught her, in the window seat of the house on Crown Walk, with the rain on the glass and her mother's hand closed round hers on the pencil. Down, and along. Down, and along. There. That's you.
She had written it a thousand times. Ten thousand. On letters and lists and the flyleaves of books. At the bottom of every letter to Rowan, every month, for years. L.
And she had given it away. All of it. Every letter. To fold a square of paper small enough to fit into her dying father's hand.
She had known that. She had known it every day since. She had known it in the crypt at Saint Bride's, the night after, when she picked up Gideon's blue chalk without thinking and her hand made marks like a spider's legs on the stone. She had known it at Thistle Ford, when Granny Abbe held out the stub of a pencil for her to put her name in the hall book, and she had to shake her head and let Corin do it. She knew it.
But she had thought, somehow, that out here it might be different.
Out here, where nothing could be taken. Where the Glass held everything and gave nothing back. She had thought, without ever quite thinking it, that if nothing could be taken here, then perhaps nothing had been. That if she tried, here, her hand might remember.
It did not remember.
The Glass kept what you had. It did not give back what you had already lost.
She sat looking at the scratch.
And beside her, someone sat down.
She did not look round. She knew who it was by the crutch, set down on the Glass, and the stiff bad leg going out straight in front of him, and the small grunt.
Corin did not say anything. He took his left glove off. And put out one gray finger, with its black tip, and drew in the grit beside her scratch.
Down. And along.
An L. Square and clean and black against the dark grit. The same square careful capital he used in the Ledger. Exactly the right shape. Not beautiful. But right.
He did not look at her. He looked at the L. Then he put his glove back on.
Lyra looked at it.
She looked at it for a long time. The straight down. The straight along. The neat square corner where they met.
Then she put out her finger again, beside his. And tried to copy it.
Down.
Her finger went down. And wandered. And leaned.
She stopped. Lifted it. Tried again, beside it. Slowly. Very slowly, watching his L the whole time, the way you watch a stepping stone when you jump.
Down.
It leaned. It wandered. It went where it wanted.
Along.
It curled up.
She stopped.
There were three marks in the grit now. His clean L. And two wandering scratches beside it, that looked like nothing at all.
She could feel her throat getting tight. She could feel her eyes. She thought of what she had said to him at noon yesterday. Because I'll cry, and I've only got four mouthfuls of water. And she thought, I am not going to cry. Not about this. Not about a letter in the dirt.
She did not cry.
She put her palm flat on the grit, and wiped.
All three. His and both of hers. One sweep of her bare hand, and they were gone, and there was only smooth dark grit with the marks of her fingers in it, and no letters at all.
She looked round quickly. Nobody had seen. Oren had his back to them. The Ashborn were sitting in a knot round Kestrel, who had her hand on the Glass. Vespera was doing something to Oda's feet.
Nobody had seen but Corin.
She pulled her glove back on.
"It's all right," said Corin. Very low.
"It isn't."
"No," he agreed. "But I can write it for you. Whenever you want. Anything. You only have to say it, and I'll put it down." He looked at the smooth grit where the letters had been. "That's what the book's for. Isn't it? For the things we can't keep ourselves."
She did not answer.
After a moment, he got up. Slowly, with the crutch. And went back to the others.
Lyra sat by the smooth grit with her gloved hands in her lap.
And she thought: he can write my name. He can write it ten thousand times. But he does not remember why it matters to me. And I can remember why it matters, every bit of it, the window seat and the rain and my mother's hand. But I cannot write it.
Between the two of us, she thought, we make one person who can write my name.
She did not know whether that was funny or not.
She got up, and went to see if Vespera needed help with Oda's feet.
