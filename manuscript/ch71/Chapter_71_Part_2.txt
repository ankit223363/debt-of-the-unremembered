Chapter 71: The First Night, Part 2 of 5: Trying to Burn
Trying to Burn
She tried it before they set off. She did not tell anyone.
She went a little way from the others, while they were still rolling the last of the felt and Kestrel was counting the heat stones back into the packs. Not far. Twenty steps, out onto the black, with the stars all round her. Far enough that nobody would see her face. And she stood there, with her back to them, and her hands at her sides.
She was frightened.
She told herself she was not. She told herself it was only the cold, making her shake. But she knew. She had been frightened of this since the Edge. Since Kestrel said, at the very start, by the fire in the tent of hands, On the Glass nothing can be taken, and nothing can be burned. She had heard it, and nodded, and not believed it. Not really. Not in her body. Because her body knew how to burn. Her body had been burning, a little at a time, for years.
She knew the way it went.
You picked a thing. A small thing first. Always a small thing first, to wake it up. Then you found it in yourself. Not in your head, exactly. Lower down. In the place where you kept the things your hands knew. And you held it. And you let it go. You let it go into the moment, the way you let go of a breath into cold air. And it went out of you. And the moment took it. And gave you something back.
Speed. Or strength. Or a light. Or the edge of a blade cutting through a thing it had no right to cut through.
And afterward there was a hole where the thing had been. And you learned to walk round the hole.
She had done it so many times. Her handwriting, in the end, all of it, to fold the square into her father's hand. Before that, the little skills, one by one. Things her hands had known how to make. And two nights ago, the dancing. All of it, all at once, at the bottom of the burning camp, with an Eraser's iron hand coming down on three children and no time.
She had not had time to be afraid then.
She had time now.
She picked the smallest thing she could think of. The taste of salt. Not salt itself. Not what it was, or where it came from, or what you did with it. Just the taste. The sharp clean bite of it on the tongue. The thing she would miss least. She would still know salt. She would only not taste it. She thought she could live with that.
And she reached for it.
She found it at once. There it was. Low down, in the place where her hands kept their knowing. The taste of salt. Bread with a crust of it. The sea wind at the docks. Tears, when you cried hard enough that they ran into your mouth. She held it. Very carefully. The way you hold a coin you are about to drop into a well.
She did not want much for it. A spark. A little light. Enough to see her own hand by. That was all.
She let it go.
And nothing happened.
Not nothing as in a small thing. Not nothing as in a failure, a misfire, a spark that would not catch, the way it sometimes was when you were tired or hurt. Nothing at all. It was like pushing on a door and finding that the door was painted on a wall. Her whole self leaned forward, the way it always did, to give the thing away. And there was nowhere for it to go. The moment would not take it. The air would not take it. The Glass under her feet would not take it. It was like holding out a coin to a shopkeeper and having them look through you as if you were not there.
She stood there, leaning, holding out the taste of salt to nothing.
And it came back.
She felt it come back. Slide back down into her, into the low place, where it had been. Settle. Like a cat that had been put out of the door and walked straight back in through the window.
She licked her lips. They were dry and cracked from the cold, and a little salty, from sweat.
She could taste it.
Her legs went.
She did not fall. She sat down. Hard, on the cold Glass, with her knees up and her hands flat on the black either side of her, and her breath coming out of her in short white puffs. She was shaking. Not with the cold. With something else. Her whole body was shaking the way a body shakes after it has been running for a long time, or after a near thing with a cart in the street. Her teeth were chattering. Her hands were trembling against the Glass.
She could not stop it.
She did not try.
She sat there and shook, and after a while she found that she was crying, a little, silently, the tears running down cold on her cold face and into the corners of her mouth.
Salt.
She could taste them.
And under the shaking, under the crying, slowly, there came up in her a feeling she did not have a name for. It was so big. It was like the Glass. It went all the way out to the edge of the stars.
It took her a long time to know what it was.
It was relief.
Nothing could be taken from her here. Not by anyone. Not by Kross, not by the men in gray, not by the iron things ticking behind them on the Edge. Not by herself. Not even if she wanted it to be. Not even if a child was dying in front of her and she would have given anything, anything, to stop it.
She could not give anything. Not here.
She would get to the other side of the Glass with everything she had now. Every skill she had left. Every memory. The taste of salt, and the smell of bread, and her father's voice, and the way the hay smelled in the loft at Thistle Ford. All of it. Safe. Locked up in her like money in a strongbox, where nobody could get at it, not even her.
She had not known how tired she was. Of giving. Of losing. Of reaching into herself every time there was danger and wondering what would be left after.
She was so tired.
She sat on the Glass and cried, quietly, and tasted the salt, and let herself be glad.
Then she wiped her face with her sleeve. And got up. And went back to the others.
Corin looked at her as she came. He was leaning on his crutch by the rolled felt, with his pack on.
He did not ask. She was grateful for that.
But he moved over, a little, so that there was room for her to stand beside him. And she stood there. And her shoulder touched his arm. And she let it.
The Second Cairn
They walked again in the last of the dark, with the cold biting at their faces and the stars going faint in the east.
Lyra walked behind Corin. She had found, the night before, that it was easier there. She could watch his crutch go down, and his bad leg come after it, stiff at the knee, and his good leg after that, and she could set her own feet in the gaps without thinking. Boot. Crutch. Boot. The felt on the crutch foot made a soft dull pat on the Glass, like a hand on a door. She found herself walking to it. Pat. Step. Step. Pat.
Her feet did not know the Glass.
They did not know anything, now. Not the way they used to. Before Last Thorn, before she gave the dancing, her feet had known every floor she had ever walked on. Stone and wood and turf and cobbles. The tilt of a deck. The give of a rope bridge. She had never thought about it. That was what dancing was, she understood now that it was gone: not the steps, not the music, but the knowing. The way your feet talked to the ground and the ground talked back.
The ground did not talk to her now. She put her feet on it, and it was there, and that was all. Like walking in someone else's boots. She had fallen twice on the first night, without warning, just going over sideways on flat Glass with nothing to trip on, and Oren had picked her up both times without a word.
She watched her feet. Left. Right. Pat.
It was Marrit who found the cairn again. The old woman stopped at the front of the line and lifted her pole, the way she had the night before, and pointed. A little left. And there it was, far off, a small dark lump against the faint gray of the coming dawn. A hole in the shine.
It was smaller than the first one. Not much higher than Lyra's knee. And older, she thought, when they came up to it. Some of the stones on top had fallen, and lay round the foot of it on the Glass, and the finger stone on the top was leaning, not quite straight, as if it had been knocked.
Kestrel set it straight. Very carefully, with both hands, the way you would straighten a picture on the wall of somebody else's house. She checked it against the stars. Moved it a hair. Checked again. Then she stood back.
The Ashborn put their stones on. Each one, and a name. Marrit last.
And Lyra stood with her hand in her coat pocket, round the stone.
She had forgotten it was there. Or not forgotten. She had not thought about it. It had been in her pocket since the Lip, since the boy Davin gave it to her on the hill in the snow, with his dog Flint sitting on his feet. For luck, he had said. A small gray stone, smooth and round as an egg, with a ring of white running round the middle of it like a belt. My granda found it on the top. They come from the middle.
She took it out and looked at it.
In the gray light it looked like the stone on the first cairn. The one Marrit had put on, for luck, for her son. Gray, with a white ring round it.
They come from the middle.
She looked north. Toward the middle. Toward wherever the middle was.
"What's that?" said Arran. He had come up beside her, quiet on his felt feet. He was looking at the stone in her hand.
"A boy gave it to me. At the Lip. He said his grandfather found it on the top of the hill. He said they come from the middle of the Glass."
Arran held out his hand. She gave it to him. He turned it over in his long fingers, and held it up to the gray light, and ran his thumb round the white ring.
"Belt stone," he said. "Yes. They come from the middle. Nobody knows how. You find them all round the Edge, and on the hills, and on the Lip. Never out here." He gave it back. "The old ones say the walkers who come back from the middle bring them. One each. And put them down, when they are safe home, where they first see the valleys again." He shrugged. "The old ones say a lot of things."
Lyra looked at the stone.
Somebody had carried this. Out from the middle of the Glass. All the way to the Lip. And put it down, there, on the top of the hill in the snow, where they first saw the valleys.
When? Who?
She thought about putting it on the cairn. That was what the stones were for. To give back. To say a name.
She did not know whose name to say.
She closed her hand on it, and put it back in her pocket.
"Not this one," she said. "Not yet."
Arran looked at her. Then he nodded, as if that were a perfectly good answer, and went back to his place in the line.
Corin was looking at her too. From the other side of the cairn, leaning on his crutch. She did not know how long he had been looking.
"I'll know when," she said to him. "I think."
He did not ask what she meant. He nodded, the way Arran had. And looked north.
And then the dawn came up, twice, the way it had the morning before. The blue and the gray and the green and the gold. And the steam lifting off the Glass in sheets, pink, peeling. And the light coming up into their faces from under them, so that they all looked, for a moment, like people standing over a great fire.
Lyra tied the eye hide on. The world went to two thin bright lines.
And they walked on, north, past the second cairn, into the day.
Arran's Stories
Arran told stories as they walked. He told them the way other men whistle. Without seeming to think about it. Without seeming to care whether anyone was listening.
But everyone was listening.
"There was a woman called Sola," he said. He was walking beside Lyra now, on her left, with his long thin legs taking one step for every two of hers and his plait swinging behind him. "You know her. Everybody knows her. The Sun-Burner. Your Oren's great-great-something. She was very bad-tempered."
"She was not," said Oren, from behind.
"She was. Old Hask said so. Old Hask's grandmother met her. She said Sola Vance had a voice like a crow with a sore throat and she shouted at everybody, all the time, even her horse. Especially her horse." Arran grinned. His burned eyebrows were growing back as two dark smudges, and they went up when he grinned, so that he looked very pleased with himself. "And one day she was very, very angry. More angry than anyone has ever been. So angry that she opened her mouth to shout, and what came out was the sun."
He waved his long arm out over the Glass. Out at the great flat shining floor going away to the edge of the sky, every color of smoke in the morning light.
"And that," he said, "is why you should never make a Vance angry."
Oren snorted. But Lyra saw, out of the corner of her eye, through the slits of the hide, that he had his hand on the hilt of his sword. Not gripping it. Just resting there. The way a man rests his hand on the head of a dog he is fond of.
"That's not the Sun Road," said Oda, from in front. It was the first thing Lyra had heard her say in the southern tongue. Her voice was short and dry. "Tell it right."
"I'm getting to the Sun Road." Arran looked hurt. "You can't have the Sun Road without the shouting. The shouting is the best part."
"Tell it right."
Arran sighed.
"When Sola shouted the sun," he said, in a different voice, a little lower, a little slower, the voice of someone saying a thing the way it has always been said, "it went out from her in every direction. Like a ring in water when you drop a stone. And everything it touched, it melted. And in the middle, where she stood, there was the Eye. And from the Eye, going out, the sun ran over the ground in a road. One road. Straight. Going south. The way her army had come. The way they would go home, if any of them were left to go home."
He was quiet for a few steps.
"And none of them were left. So the road is empty. But it is still there. If you walk on the Sun Road, you walk straight to the Eye, and nothing on the Glass can make you lose your way. Not dark. Not storm. Not the lost ones." He shrugged. "That is the story."
"Is it true?" said Lyra.
"We are not on it," said Arran. "So who knows?"
"Why aren't we on it?"
"Because the Sun Road goes through the Trench." He grinned again. But it was a smaller grin. "And nobody goes through the Trench if they can go round."
"What's the Trench?"
Arran did not answer at once. He took his bone flute out of the front of his coat and put it to his lips, and blew one soft note, and took it away.
"The Trench," he said, "is where Sola's soldiers dug in. At the end. Before she shouted. A great long ditch in the ground, across the way the gray men came, with a wall of earth in front and stakes in the wall. And the soldiers stood in it, all along it, with their pikes and their bows and their faces to the south, waiting for the gray men to come." He put the flute back. "And then she shouted. And the sun came. And the earth melted, and the stakes melted, and the Glass came over everything."
"And the soldiers?"
"They're still there," said Arran cheerfully. "Standing guard. Old Hask said they will stand guard until somebody tells them the war is over. And nobody has, because nobody knows the words." He looked at her sideways through his slits. "Hask said they are very polite. They don't say anything. They only watch you go by."
Lyra did not know if he was joking.
She looked at him. He was smiling. He was always smiling. But his voice, when he said standing guard, had gone flat for a moment. The way a voice goes flat on a thing it does not want to think about.
"And the Eye?" said Vespera, from behind. She had been listening all along. "Tell her about the Eye, boy. Go on. You've done the shouting. Do the Eye."
Arran laughed.
"The Eye is easy," he said. "The Eye is where the dead are quiet and the living are loud."
Nobody said anything.
"That's all," said Arran. "That's the whole of it. That's all anyone says about the Eye. You ask Old Hask, what's at the Eye, grandfather? And he says, the dead are quiet there, little one, and the living are loud. And you say, what does that mean? And he says, go and see. And you say, I don't want to go and see. And he says, good. Then you are not stupid." He spread his long hands. "So I am not stupid. And I am going anyway. Which makes me stupid. So the story is true."
He laughed again, the short bark. Brin, ahead, laughed too, slow and low, as if he had understood. Perhaps he had.
Lyra did not laugh.
She was thinking about what he had said. The dead are quiet there. And the living are loud.
She thought about Corin. About the old man in his head, who had gone quiet at the first step onto the Glass and had not said a word since. About the lights under the Glass, drifting, silent. About her own burning, that would not burn.
The dead were quiet here, too. All of them. On the whole Glass.
But at the Eye, the living were loud.
Loud how? she thought. Loud like what?
She did not ask.
Arran had started on another story. Something about a goat that had walked across the Glass on its own, in Hask's grandfather's time, and come out on the far side, and been so pleased with itself that it walked back again, and then a third time, and on the third time it had married a Kesh-Amaran goat and refused to come home. It was very silly. Brin laughed at it until he had to hold his strapped shoulder.
Lyra walked, and listened, and watched Corin's crutch go down on the Glass in front of her. Pat. Step. Step.
They were all jokes, the way Arran told them. Every one.
She did not think any of them were jokes at all.
Sola's Ground
Oren dropped back after the stories. Lyra felt him come. A big slow shape on her right, matching his stride to hers, the way he had on the road north for a week, and in the city before that, and on every road she could remember since she was a girl and her father first put her in his keeping.
He did not say anything for a long time.
She let him not say it. She knew Oren. If you asked him what was wrong, he told you nothing was. If you waited, sometimes he told you.
The sun was up properly now, a hand and a half above the rim of the world, and the Glass had begun to shine. Not the soft gold of the dawn. A hard white shine, coming up off the black in every direction, so that even through the slits of the hide Lyra had to keep her eyes half shut. The steam was gone. The air was clear and dry and very still, and she could see a long way. Swell after swell, going away north. Each one a little paler than the one before, until the farthest were only a shimmer at the edge of the sky.
"Bad-tempered," said Oren at last.
Lyra waited.
"Shouted at her horse." He shook his head slowly. "I'd have liked to see that. The smith at Haverstock had its shoe. Hanging over the forge. Big as a dinner plate." He held up his hands, a little apart, to show her. "I'd believe she shouted at a horse with feet like that."
Lyra smiled under the hide.
She remembered Haverstock. Five days ago, though it felt like a year. The round hill with the rings of houses going up it, and the squat church, and the inn with the sun and the pike on its sign. And the cross in the square. The great square pillar on its three steps, with the rings of names cut round it, hundreds of them, everyone who had marched from those valleys and not come home.
She had read him his family. Off the north side, the second ring. Standing in the snow with her gloved finger under each name. And he had said every one after her, slowly, in his deep rough voice, like a soldier answering a roll. Because he could not read them himself. Not anymore. He had burned his reading, a long time ago, to hold a shop door shut in the city with a wall of light.
Twenty-three names. And a smooth place, between two of them, where a name had been chiseled away. And at the end, in bigger letters than all the rest: SOLA VANCE. GENERAL. SHE GAVE HER WORDS.
"I keep thinking," said Oren.
He stopped. Started again.
"I keep thinking I'm walking on her," he said. "That's all. It's daft. But I keep thinking it." He looked down at the Glass under his boots. Brown-black, here, with one of the long rust-red threads running away under the surface. "This is what she made. All of this. Every step. And I'm walking on it. And it doesn't know me. It doesn't know anybody."
Lyra did not say anything.
"My grandfather never said where he came from," said Oren. "Never once. Came south to the city as a young man, before the war. Married. Had my father. Swore by Sola's teeth when he dropped a hammer on his foot, and never told us what it meant. We thought it was just a thing old men said." He shook his head. "And all the time he was Aldric's boy. From the top house at Haverstock. And his father's sister was the General."
He looked out at the Glass. The great white shining floor, going away to the edge of everything.
"He knew what she did," said Oren. "He must have heard. Up there, everybody knows. And he never said. Not one word. Not even when he was dying." He walked a few steps. "I used to think he was ashamed of where he came from. Poor folk. Hill folk. Now I think he just didn't know how to say it. My aunt did this."
The ticking was not audible yet. The Glass was still warming. But Lyra thought about it anyway. About the lights under the Glass in the night. The drifting ones, that nobody had breathed in.
All of that was Sola, too.
"She saved people," said Lyra. "Didn't she? Arran said. Her army was dug in, and the gray men were coming. And she stopped them."
"Aye," said Oren. "She stopped them." He walked. "She stopped everybody."
They did not say anything for a while.
Then Oren said, very low, "The smooth place."
Lyra looked at him.
"On the cross," said Oren. "Between Aldric and Mira. The youngest brother. The one who sold the march road to the gray men the night before." He was not looking at her. He was looking straight ahead, at Kestrel's white cape, very far in front. "The widow said he lived. Went south, after. Had children. They've got his name, she said. Whatever they call themselves now."
Lyra remembered.
And she remembered, too, something much older. Kross, in the city, a long time ago now. Saying to Oren, in his cool soft voice: Captain Vance. Your brother sends his regards.
She did not say it. She did not need to. She could see in Oren's face that he was thinking it too.
"I never had a brother," said Oren. "That I know of. I'd know. You'd know a thing like that." He rubbed his face with his big hand, under the hide. "But there's more than one kind of brother. A man can mean a lot of things by it."
"Oren—"
"It's all right." He dropped his hand. "It's all right, girl. I'm not going soft. I'm just walking. And thinking. It's a long walk. A man thinks."
He walked on a few steps.
"You can't take anything out here," he said. "Kestrel says. Nothing comes off you. Nothing goes in." He looked down at the Glass again. At the rust-red thread under his boot. "So whatever I've got left, I've got. All the way across. That's something." A pause. "And whatever I've given before, that's still gone. The Glass won't give it back."
"No," said Lyra quietly. "It won't."
"Here," said Oren suddenly.
He had stopped. He was bending down. Lyra stopped with him, and saw him put his big hand flat on the Glass. Just for a moment. The way Kestrel did when she was listening. But he was not listening. She could see that. He was only touching it. The way you would touch the stone on a grave.
Then he straightened up.
"There," he said. To nobody. Or to somebody. "Now you know one of Aldric's came."
He walked on.
Lyra stood for a moment, looking at the place on the Glass where his hand had been. There was no mark. The Glass did not take marks, Kestrel said. Not anymore. Not from anyone.
Then she slid her feet over the Glass, small and careful, the way the Ashborn walked, and went after him.
