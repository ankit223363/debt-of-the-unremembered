Chapter 71: The First Night, Part 4 of 5: Vespera and the Stitching
Vespera and the Stitching
She found Vespera kneeling in the grit by Oda's feet, with her needle roll open on the Glass beside her and her blue hands working.
Oda's heels were worse. The blister on the side of her right foot, the one Vespera had lanced the afternoon before, had filled again in the night. Not with clean water this time. With something cloudy. Vespera had opened it again and was cleaning it out with a pad of gauze soaked in something that smelled sharp and green, like crushed pine needles. Oda sat with her jaw set and her eyes on the horizon and did not make a sound.
"Hold this," said Vespera, without looking up.
Lyra held it. A little clay pot of the burnt-honey-and-pine salve, warm from Vespera's belt.
Vespera dipped two fingers in it and spread it over the open blister and the raw heels. Then she took a strip of clean linen from the roll and began to bind the foot. Over and under. Round the heel, round the ankle, back under the arch. Her small blue fingers went very fast, but every turn of the linen lay exactly where it should, flat and smooth, overlapping the one before by the same width each time. Like the lines in a page of good handwriting.
Lyra watched her hands.
She had always liked watching Vespera's hands. Even in the city, in the back room in Hook Lane, with the needles laid out and the inks in their little pots. There was something about them. Small and old and stained to the wrist with indigo, so that they looked like they belonged to somebody else. Somebody who had been dipped in the sea at night. And quick. And sure. They never hesitated. They never went back and did a thing twice.
Vespera tied the linen off with a knot so small Lyra could hardly see it, and tucked the end under.
"There," she said to Oda. "If that fills again, tell me. Don't wait. Tell me."
Oda looked at the foot. Then at Vespera. And nodded once, short. And began to wind the felt back on over the linen.
Vespera wiped her fingers on a rag and began to roll up the needles. Slowly. Longest to finest, the way she always did. Lyra handed her back the pot.
"You do that like sewing," said Lyra. "The binding."
"It is sewing," said Vespera. "Everything's sewing, if you do it right." She tied the strings of the roll. "Over, under. Same width every time. Don't pull too tight. Don't leave it slack. A good binding and a good seam and a good tattoo, they're the same thing. It's all about the tension." She tucked the roll into her rope belt. "Most people pull too tight."
They got up. Kestrel was on her feet at the front of the dip, with the hide over her eyes, looking north. The others were shouldering their packs. Oda stood up and tried her foot, carefully, and her face did not change.
They walked.
Vespera walked beside Lyra. She was very small beside her. Her head came only to Lyra's shoulder. And she walked fast, short quick steps in her felt, like a bird hopping along a wall.
"Your mother did beautiful stitching," said Vespera.
She said it out of nowhere. Not looking at Lyra. Looking ahead, at the backs of the Ashborn, through the slits in her eye hide. In the same flat sharp voice she used for everything.
Lyra stopped walking.
Then she started again, quickly, two long steps to catch up.
"What?"
"Your mother," said Vespera. "Isolde. She did beautiful stitching. Everyone said so. Fine work. The finest in the city, some said. Whitework. Little flowers on linen, white on white, so fine you couldn't see the stitches. You had to hold it up to the window to see them, and then they were there, all at once, like frost." She walked a few steps. "People used to say a Thorne lady's handkerchief was worth more than her husband's horse."
Lyra's heart was going very fast.
She knew that. She did know it. She remembered the handkerchiefs. Her mother's, in a drawer in the dressing room on Crown Walk, a whole stack of them, white on white, smelling of lavender. She remembered holding one up to the window, as a little girl, and seeing the flowers come out of the white like magic. She had not thought of it in years.
She had not known anyone else knew.
"Did you know her?" she said.
Vespera did not answer at once.
She walked. Short quick steps. Her mouth was a thin line under the edge of the eye hide.
"I knew of her," she said.
"That's not what I asked."
"It's what I'm answering."
"Vespera—"
But Vespera's mouth had closed. Lyra saw it. Saw it close, under the hide, into a line so thin and hard it was like a seam pulled too tight. And Vespera walked faster. Two quick steps. Three. Until she was ahead of Lyra, and then ahead of Corin, and then walking beside Oren, near the front, with her small gray head held very straight.
She did not look back.
Lyra stood still for a moment on the Glass.
Then Corin came up beside her on his crutch, and she had to walk again, or be left.
"What did she say?" said Corin.
"That my mother did beautiful stitching."
"Is it true?"
"Yes."
He was quiet for a few steps. Pat. Step. Step.
"Then why are you shaking?"
She looked down at her hands. She had not known she was. But she was. Her gloved hands were trembling a little at her sides, the way they had in the night, after she tried to burn.
"Because I asked if she knew her," said Lyra. "And she said, I knew of her."
"And?"
"And it's a lie."
Corin looked ahead at Vespera's straight small back.
"How do you know?"
"Because nobody," said Lyra, "who only knew of my mother would know about the frost. About holding it up to the window. You'd have to have held one. You'd have to have stood in a room with her, and had her hand you one, and said, hold it up to the light, look." Her voice was not steady. She made it steady. "That's what she used to say. Hold it up to the light. Look. I'd forgotten."
They walked.
Ahead of them, Vespera said something to Oren, sharp, and Oren laughed. Short. And Vespera did not laugh with him.
"Isolde left eleven years ago," said Lyra. "Over the Narrow Sea. In winter. In a hurry. Nobody would tell us why." She was talking to herself now, really. Not to him. "And Vespera's been in the city twenty years. More. In Hook Lane. With her needles."
"You think they knew each other."
"I think," said Lyra, "that everybody in my whole life has known something about my mother that I don't. And I'm tired of it."
She did not say anything else.
Neither did Corin.
But after a while, walking, she felt him move his crutch to his other side. So that his free arm was next to her. And she took it. And they walked like that, for a mile or so, arm in arm, across the shining smoke-colored floor of the world, behind Vespera's small straight back.
The Humming Lump
They came on it in the middle of the morning, and it was Lyra who heard it first.
She did not know she was hearing anything. She was walking arm in arm with Corin, with her eyes on the two bright slits of the world and her mind on her mother's handkerchiefs, and the frost of white flowers coming out of white linen when you held it up to the window. And somewhere under that, under the pat of the crutch and the soft scuff of the felt and the small dry wind, there was a sound.
Low. Very low. So low it was almost not a sound at all. More like a feeling in her teeth.
She stopped.
Corin stopped with her, because her arm was in his. "What?"
"Listen."
He listened. She saw his face change.
It was a hum. Not the ticking. Nothing like the ticking. The ticking was hard and small and even, like a clock. This was soft. And it was not even. It went up and down. Very slowly. Like someone humming to themselves in another room, a long way off, behind a closed door, not caring if anyone heard.
And it was coming from under their feet.
Lyra looked down.
At first she saw only the Glass. Dark brown here, almost black, with one of the long rust-red threads running through it under the surface, and a scatter of small frozen bubbles, like seeds in a slice of fruit. Nothing else.
Then she saw the light.
It was deep. Deeper than the Ash-ghosts in the night had been, she thought. Much deeper. And it was not like them. It did not drift. It did not breathe. It was one place, fixed, a little ahead and to the left, down in the dark like a coal at the bottom of a deep stove. A dull, steady, reddish glow. Not bright. She had to shade the slits of her eye hide with her hand to be sure she was seeing it at all.
And it had a shape.
She went closer. She let go of Corin's arm without meaning to and went a few steps toward it, bending, peering down.
It was a lump. A rough round lump, about the size of a loaf of bread. Not smooth like the Glass. Rough and dark and broken, like a lump of coal, or a cinder. With a crack running across it. A long jagged crack, from one side to the other, and the dull red light coming out of the crack, as if there were a fire inside it, banked down, sleeping. The light lay along the crack like the light that leaks out round a door.
And the hum was coming out of it.
She could hear it better now, bending over it. Still faint. Still so low she felt it more than heard it. But clearer. It was not just going up and down. It was going up and down in a shape. The same shape, over and over.
Three notes.
A low one. Then a high one. Then one a little lower than the high one, that stopped short. As if someone had meant to go on, and then not.
Then a long pause. And then again. Low. High. A little lower.
Lyra's skin went cold all over, under her coat, under the hot sun.
She knew it.
Everybody knew it. That was the thing. Arran had said so, sitting on the log outside Kestrel's tent at Last Thorn, playing it on his bone flute. Everybody knows that. It's what you play when you're bored. The little ones at Last Thorn played it on reeds. Wren knew it. Wren had hummed it, three notes, in the city. Lyra knew four. Her mother's song. Her mother's song that went three notes and then a fourth that went up and hung, and never came down.
And here it was. Three notes. Under the Glass. Coming out of a cracked black lump of something in the middle of nowhere, sixty years down.
She did not know she had made a sound until Arran was beside her.
He had come back down the line. He was bending over too, with his hands on his knees, looking down through the slits of his hide at the dull red crack in the dark. Lyra saw his face. For a moment it was not smiling.
Then it was.
"Oh," said Arran. "Oh, it knows that one."
He straightened up. And took out his flute.
"Arran," said Kestrel. Sharply. From the front.
Arran did not listen. He put the bone flute to his lips, the way he had on the log at Last Thorn, and played.
Low. High. A little lower. Stopping short.
The same three. Clear and thin and bright in the hot still air over the Glass, the first music Lyra had heard since the Edge.
And under them, under the Glass, the lump answered.
She heard it. Everybody heard it. The hum came up, all at once, louder, much louder, so that she felt it in her feet and her knees and the bones of her face. Low. High. A little lower. The same three notes, answering. Coming up through the Glass like a voice coming up a well. And the dull red light in the crack flared. Brighter. Much brighter. Not red now but orange, and then for one moment almost gold, so that the whole crack lit up like the slit of a lantern when you open the shutter, and Lyra could see, for that one moment, that there were other cracks. Many. All over the lump. Fine as hairs. All lit.
And she could see, for that one moment, that it was not one lump at all.
It was many. Small round shapes, pressed together, melted together, like a handful of seeds pressed into a ball. Many of them. More than she could count. Each one glowing, in its own crack.
Then it went dark.
All at once. Like a candle pinched out. The light went, and the hum went, and there was only the black Glass, and the rust-red thread under it, and the bubbles like seeds. And nothing.
Arran took the flute away from his lips.
Nobody said anything.
Kestrel had come back down the line. She did not run. She walked, but very fast, and she came straight to Arran and took the flute out of his hand. Not roughly. But firmly. The way you take a knife from a child.
"No," she said. In the southern tongue. So they would all understand.
Arran looked at her. He had stopped smiling. His face, under the burned stubble of his eyebrows, was very young.
"I only—" he said.
"We know." Kestrel put the flute back into the front of his coat herself. Pushed it down, and patted it, flat. "We know what you only. It knew the tune. You wanted to see if it would sing. It sang." She looked down at the black Glass where the light had been. "And now we go round. Wide. All of us. Now."
She turned and began to walk. Left. Away from the place. In a long curve, out and round, the way you would walk round a sleeping bull in a field.
They followed her. Nobody argued. Even Oren.
Lyra walked beside Arran. She could not help looking back. But there was nothing to see. Only the Glass.
"What was it?" she said.
Arran did not answer.
Kestrel answered. From the front, without turning round.
"Ash," she said. "Old Ash. Very old. From the war. Not one. Many. When the sun came, some of them were standing close. Very close. Holding hands, perhaps. In a house. In a cellar. And the sun came, and they melted together, and went into the Glass together, all in one lump." She walked a few steps. "Most of the lost ones are one. One person. One light. They drift. That one doesn't drift. That one is too heavy. That one sits."
She looked back, once, over her shoulder. At the place on the Glass behind them where there was nothing.
"That one is a whole village," said Kestrel. "Don't wake it."
They walked on.
And Lyra, walking, found that she was humming. Under her breath. Very low. Without meaning to.
Low. High. A little lower.
And then, because it was hers, because it was her mother's, the fourth. Going up. And hanging there. Not coming down.
She stopped as soon as she heard herself.
But Corin, beside her, had heard it too. She saw him look at her. And she saw his lips move, under the edge of his eye hide, as if he were going to say something.
Then they closed again. And he looked ahead. And said nothing at all.
Kestrel
Lyra walked beside Kestrel for a while, after the lump. She did not quite know how it happened. She went up the line to ask how far it was to the next rest, and Kestrel did not answer, and Lyra did not go back.
They walked side by side at the front. Kestrel tall and straight with her spear over her shoulder and the white fur cape lying on her back like snow on a roof. Lyra beside her, trying to walk with her knees bent the way Oren had shown her, sliding her feet, so that she would not go over.
Kestrel noticed. Lyra saw her notice. She looked down at Lyra's feet, once, through the slits of her hide. And then shortened her own stride, a little, without saying anything.
"Is Arran all right?" said Lyra.
"No," said Kestrel. "He is ashamed. It is good for him. He will be all right by the noon rest." She walked a few steps. "He has always been like that. Since he was small. If a thing is shut, he must open it. If a thing is quiet, he must make it sing. Old Hask used to say he was born with his fingers in the honey jar."
She touched the gray line on her face. Forehead to nose. Slowly.
Lyra had seen her do it a great many times now. In the tent of hands. At the rite. At the Edge. And after the light under Corin's hand, in the dark, pressing, as if it hurt.
"Can I ask you something?" said Lyra.
"You can ask."
"What is it like? To carry him. Old Hask."
Kestrel did not answer for a long time. So long that Lyra thought she was not going to. The sun stood up over the Glass, white, and the air over the swells ahead had begun to shake. The felt on Lyra's feet was hot and damp. Somewhere behind them Brin was laughing at something Sef had said, low.
"You were there," said Kestrel at last. "When he died. You breathed him."
"A little. Only a little. You let me."
"Then you know a little."
Lyra thought about it. She thought about the rite. Old Hask in his furs in the middle of the ring, very small, very gray, with his blind white eyes and his little brass bell. The hum going round the ring. And the last breath going out of him, slow, and everyone leaning in. And her own breath, going in. Just a little. A mouthful. Like breathing in the steam off a cup of something hot.
She had felt it go into her. A warmth. A smell, of old smoke and goat and something sweet, like burnt honey. And for a moment, a feeling, very small, that was not hers. A kind of tiredness. A good tiredness, the kind you feel at the end of a long day when there is a fire and a bed.
And then it had settled. And she had not felt it since. Not clearly.
"I can't feel him," she said. "Not really. Sometimes I think I do. When I'm very tired. A sort of—warm. Behind me. Like somebody standing at your back in a cold room." She shook her head. "But I don't know if it's him or if I'm making it up."
"It is him," said Kestrel. "A little of him. The little you took." She walked. "We took more. We were his daughter's daughter. We sat closest. We took the most."
She was quiet again.
"It is like this," she said. "You know when you are a child, and you have a fever, and you lie in bed, and someone you love comes and sits on the edge of the bed? And they do not say anything. They only sit. And you can feel the bed go down, on that side, where they sit. And you know they are there, even with your eyes shut. And you go to sleep."
"Yes," said Lyra.
"It is like that," said Kestrel. "All the time. Someone sitting on the edge of the bed. The bed always a little lower, on that side." She touched the gray line again. "We have carried many. Our mother's mother. Two of our cousins. A man who fell through the ice on the Salt Wells road, when we were a girl, whom we did not even know. Old women. A baby." Her voice did not change at all. "Each one is a person sitting on the bed. We have a very crowded bed."
Lyra did not know what to say. So she did not say anything.
"Most days it is good," said Kestrel. "Most days it is like being in a warm tent with all your family. Most days we are never alone." She walked. "Some days it is very heavy. Some days the bed is so low on every side that we cannot get up out of it."
"What do you do then?"
"Then we get up anyway," said Kestrel. "Because they are sitting on the bed. And they would not want us to lie in it."
They walked.
Lyra thought about Corin. About the old man in his head, who had gone quiet. About what Corin had told Kestrel under the last tree on the ridge, the morning after Old Hask died, and had told Lyra later, by the fire, in a low voice. That Malakor was someone he carried who had not been given and had not been taken. Someone who had come in to hold a door.
She thought about the light under his hand, in the night. Following him.
"The light," she said. "Under Corin's hand. Last night."
Kestrel did not answer.
"You said they don't do that."
"They don't."
"Then why did it?"
Kestrel walked for a long time without answering. Then she stopped. Right there on the Glass, in the sun, with the line coming up behind them. And turned, and looked down at Lyra through her slits.
"Because it knew him," she said.
Lyra stared at her.
"That is what we think," said Kestrel. "We do not know. We have never seen it. Not once, in all our walking. Not in any story Old Hask told. But that is what it looked like. It looked like a dog that has been waiting at a door for a long, long time. And hears a step it knows on the path." She turned and began to walk again. "That is all we think. We could be wrong."
"How could it know him? He's never been here."
Kestrel did not look round.
"No," she said. "He says he has never been here."
And she walked faster, so that Lyra had to slide her feet quickly to keep up, and did not say anything else at all.
