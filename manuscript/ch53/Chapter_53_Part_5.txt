Chapter 53: The Net Closes, Part 5 of 5: An Hour Too Late
An Hour Too Late
Sergeant Moll saw the smoke from nine miles off, and knew.
He was on the towpath above Sallow Hythe, on a borrowed horse, at the second bell after noon. He had been riding since the ninth bell. Before that he had been sitting in the back of Abner Cole's coal cart since before dawn, under the sacks, the way he had ridden north once before, a week ago, on the Monday Brindle round. And before that he had been sitting in Mrs. Marta's kitchen on Tallow Street among forty stopped clocks, at the second bell after midnight, with his cap in his hands, while Mrs. Marta told him something she had sworn on Tuesday she would never tell anybody but Corin Vane.
The barn is the third one past Withy Reach, Sergeant. The one with the broken roof. By the peat cuttings. Hessa owes a goat. She had looked at him across the table with her old dark eyes. And she's not at the barn any more. The Captain told her to go further. Ellis says she went north on Wednesday, past the hay. Nobody knows where. And there's a red light on the river. Going north. Since Saturday night. The chain's had it all day.
The chain had had it all day. Door to door. Step to step. Tallow Street to the Saltgate locks, the lock-keepers' wives to the bargemen's wives, the bargemen's wives to the washerwomen at Brindle. Walked hard. A word, going north, faster than a horse because a word did not have to stop at the barriers.
It had got to Brindle on Sunday night. And at Brindle it had stopped. Because nobody at Brindle knew where Hessa Doon had gone after Withy Reach. Nobody knew there was a place called Sedge End. And a word cannot walk where nobody knows the way.
So it had come back. Down the chain, door to door, step to step, to Tallow Street. She's gone past where we go. Somebody has to ride. And it had got back to Mrs. Marta's kitchen at the first bell after midnight on Midwinter morning. And Mrs. Marta had sent Juno's friend Nell running for the only man she knew who could ride, and who had been a soldier, and who knew what a red lantern on a river meant.
Moll.
Who had sat on the Inquisitor's hand on Thursday in a white room at the Ministry, and not answered. Who knew the Inquisitor would want to know where he went today, and would find out.
He had gone anyway.
Abner's coal cart had got him to Brindle by the ninth bell. Ellis had got him a horse. Ellis's own mare, Bess, the big ugly bay who had bitten a constable at the Bridewell stables a week ago and would bite anybody else she did not like. She did not like Moll. She had tried twice. But she could go. Moll had ridden her north along the towpath from Brindle, past the turf barn with the broken roof, past Withy Reach, past every farm and every hay wharf, with his bad leg aching where it had broken at a ford twenty years ago under an overturned wagon full of children. Asking. At every door. A black barge. A red sail. A big woman at the pole. And every door had said the same thing. Further. North. Toward the fen.
And at Sallow Hythe, at the bread oven, the woman with the floury arms had said: They went out across the fen at noon, mister. The gray coats. On foot, from the dyke. Ten of them. Toward Old Pask's.
And now he was on the towpath above the village, on Ellis's ugly bay mare, and he could see the smoke.
It stood up out of the fen to the north-east, a long way off, over the reeds. A great leaning column of it, black at the bottom and gray at the top, going up and up into the still white sky and bending over at the very top where some high wind caught it, like a tree in a gale. Hay smoke. He knew it. Every soldier knew it. You saw it on every march, in every war, rising off the farms behind the lines.
He kicked the mare.
She went. He did not know how long it took. Along the dyke road, which was iced, so that she slid and stumbled and once nearly went down, and he hauled her up and kicked her on. Toward the smoke. Getting bigger. The reeds going past below the dyke on his right, gold and gray, frozen in, millions of them. The white sky. The cold. His bad leg. The smoke.
At the end of the dyke road, where it turned away from the fen, there were horses.
He pulled the mare up. Hard. Into the lee of a thorn bush on the bank, and got down, and held her nose so she would not call to the others, and looked.
Eight horses, on the road. Good ones. Too good for here. And men getting up onto them. Gray coats. Young men, most of them, red-faced, with black on their faces and their hands, and their long coats scorched at the hems. They did not talk. They got up onto the horses the way men get up onto horses at the end of a long day, slowly, without looking at each other.
And between two of the horses, slung on poles, a litter.
A plain canvas stretcher on two long poles, with the ends of the poles fixed to the saddles of two horses that would walk side by side. Blankets on it. Gray army blankets, piled up. And under the blankets, a shape. Long and thin and very still.
And a face.
Moll saw it as they lifted the litter into its harness. Just for a moment. Gray. Long. Fine-boned. With white hair as thin and soft as a baby's. And a chin that went down square.
And the eyes, open. Looking up at the white sky.
Drum, the old man had said. On the barge. On the Monday. With his eyes alive for the first time in two years. Drum. Drummer. The drummer. Find—
Moll stood behind the thorn bush with his hand clamped over the ugly mare's nose and did not move.
There was nothing he could do. He knew that. He had known it as soon as he saw the horses. One man, with a bad leg, on a horse that bit, against nine. He had been a soldier for twenty years. He knew what nine against one meant.
He watched them go.
They went south, along the dyke road, the way he had come. Slowly. At a walk. Because of the ice, and because of the litter. The gray coats in front and behind, and the litter between, swaying a little on its poles, and the old man's face under the blankets looking up at the sky.
And when they came past the thorn bush, a few yards from him, close enough to touch, Moll did the only thing he could.
He put his hand flat on his own knee. Below the level of the bush, where nobody could see. And tapped.
Once.
And then twice, quick.
One and the double.
Heard.
He did not know if the old man felt it. He could not have. He was on a litter between two horses, under three blankets, three yards away. He could not have heard a finger on a knee.
But his eyes, looking up at the white sky, came down. For a moment. And moved. Toward the thorn bush.
And then the litter had gone past, and the horses, and the gray coats, and they were going away along the dyke road, south, small and dark against the white, and getting smaller.
One man had not got on a horse.
Moll saw him as the others went. A plain man of middle height in a round hat and a gray cape with a mended hem, standing alone at the end of the dyke road where it met the fen, watching the litter go. He waited until it was a long way off. Then he turned round and went back down the bank onto the ice, alone, toward the smoke.
Moll tied the mare to the thorn bush, and followed him.
He did it the way he had done it in the war, when he was young and his leg was whole. Low. In the reeds. Slow. Stopping when the man in the round hat stopped. It took a long time. The man in the round hat did not hurry.
The Patient Grace was gone.
That was the first thing. Moll came out at the edge of the reeds a hundred yards from where she had been, and lay flat on the ice in the frozen stems, and looked, and there was no barge. There was a long black shape in the ice. A shape like the bones of a fish, or the ribs of a dead ship. Black timbers, standing up out of a long black stain in the gray, smoking. A heap of something in the middle that was still glowing, red and orange, under a gray skin of ash. The stump of the mast, black, leaning. And round it, the ice had melted, in a wide black ring, and frozen again at the edges, so that the whole wreck sat in a pool of black water like a coal in a puddle.
And the smell. Burnt hay, and burnt tar, and burnt wood, and something else under them that Moll had smelled in the war and did not let himself think about.
The man in the round hat was standing at the stern.
Where the stern had been. On the ice, at the edge of the black ring of water, by the last of the black timbers. He was looking down at the ash. Just looking. For a long time. His plain face did not do anything.
Then he crouched.
He crouched down at the edge of the black water, and put out one long pale hand, and moved the ash. Gently. The way a man moves leaves on a forest floor, looking for a coin he has dropped. And stopped.
And picked something up.
Moll could not see what it was. It was small. He saw it only for a moment, between the man's fingers, as he lifted it out of the gray. A spark. Blue. A tiny clear blue spark in all that black and gray, catching the low white light.
The man in the round hat looked at it. He turned it over, once, in his long pale fingers.
Then he put it in the pocket of his gray cape. And stood up. And brushed the ash off his knees. And turned round, and walked away across the ice toward the dyke, alone, not hurrying, the way he had come.
Moll lay in the reeds until he was gone.
Then he got up, and went out across the ice to the wreck.
He did not find her at first. He looked at the timbers, and the ash, and the black water. And then he saw, at the stern, under the edge of the glowing heap, a shape that was not a timber. Half under the black ash of something that had been a great sheet of cloth. Red, once. He could still see the red, at one corner, where the fire had not reached.
He did not touch it. He knelt down on the ice beside it, with his bad leg stiff out to one side. And took off his cap. And held it against his chest. And stayed like that for a long time.
At the tiller, which was only a black stump now, sticking up out of the ash at an angle, there was a little twist of something green. Burnt at the edges. A sprig of juniper, tied on with a piece of red wool.
Moll looked at it.
Then, from far off behind him, from out in the reeds to the east, very thin and very high, carried over the flat silent fen on the still air, he heard a baby crying.
He got up.
And went toward it.
It took him a long time to find them. They were not at the Mere. They had not got to the Mere. They were in a turf hut on a little rise of ground, no higher than a man's shoulder, with a sagging roof and no smoke coming out of the hole in the top of it. An old eel-man in a coat made of a dozen coats was standing in the low doorway with a spear, and pointed it at Moll's chest, and did not lower it until Moll said the word Hessa.
Inside, on a heap of reeds, a huge red-bearded man was sitting with his back against the turf wall and his shirt red to the waist and his right arm hanging. And in the crook of his left arm, a baby. Red-haired. Screaming. Holding on to his father's wet shirt with both fists.
The big man looked up at Moll.
He did not ask anything. He did not need to. He had seen the smoke. He had sat in the doorway of this hut with his son and watched it go up, all afternoon, black and then gray, leaning over at the top like a tree in a gale.
He looked at Moll's face, and then at the cap in Moll's hand, held against his chest. And then he looked down at the baby.
"Hessa," said Wat Doon.
That was all.
Moll sat down on the turf floor of the hut beside him, with his bad leg out, and put his cap on the floor, and put his hand on the big man's knee. And left it there.
Outside, the light was going. The short white winter day was turning gray, and then blue, over the fen. And far away to the south, along the dyke road, a litter on two poles between two horses was swaying toward the city. Toward the Hill. With an old man on it, looking up at the sky, who had kept a name.
It was the third bell after noon.
If Moll had ridden one hour faster, he would have been in time to tell her to run.
Collected
The Inquisitor told Wren Halloway at the fifth bell, in the Long Office, very kindly, with a barley sugar in his mouth.
She had been at her desk all day. Midwinter Day, and the Long Office was half empty, most of the clerks gone home at noon to their families and their geese and their three lamps in the window. But the Inquisition did not keep Midwinter. The Inquisition kept everything else. So Wren had sat at her desk, the third desk in the second row by the fourth window, in her white coat with its twelve silver buttons, and copied, all day, in her lovely clear hand. Reports from the Watch houses. Reports from the barriers. A report from the Office of Public Health, that had come up the pipe at noon, about the crypt of Saint Bride's.
Removed: four.
Remarks: The crypt was found empty but for the four, the persons having been removed in the night by persons unknown. An old woman was with the four. She gave her name as Hannah Fitch and was let go.
Wren had copied it three times. Her pen had not shaken. She had felt it want to.
And at the fifth bell, as the light was going over the river, the Inquisitor had come out of his room at the end of the Long Office, in his shirt-sleeves, with his paper bag of barley sugar in one hand, and walked down between the desks, and stopped at hers.
"Cadet," he said.
"Sir."
"Sergeant Moll went for a ride today." He put a barley sugar in his mouth. "North. Very early. On a borrowed horse. A big bay mare that bites. Abner Cole's coal cart took him as far as Brindle, under the sacks, the way it took him a week ago, and he borrowed the mare from a carter called Ellis at the Brindle wharf, and rode north along the towpath asking every door for a black barge with a red sail." He folded the wrapper. In half. In half again. "Mr. Vell found the barge at noon, at a place in the fen called Sedge End, which I had never heard of. Sergeant Moll got there at the third bell. An hour after Mr. Vell had gone."
Wren did not move.
"The old gentleman is on his way to the Hill," said the Inquisitor. "Safe. Warm. Mr. Vell took great care. He'll be with us by the eighth bell." He looked down at the folded wrapper in his fingers. "The bargewoman is dead. I'm sorry for that. Mr. Vell says she was very brave. He says she handed the old gentleman down to him herself, over the side, off the top of a burning stack, rather than let him burn. He was most impressed." A pause. "He doesn't impress easily, Mr. Vell."
Wren's hands were flat on the desk. On either side of the paper she had been copying. She kept them there.
"Sergeant Moll will come back tonight," said the Inquisitor. "He'll ride the mare back to Brindle and come down in the coal cart, the way he went. He'll be in the city by the seventh bell. And he'll go to the landlady's. To Mrs. Marta, on Tallow Street. They always go back to the landlady." He put the folded wrapper down on the corner of her desk. Small. Very small. Eleven folds. "Collect him, Cadet. There. At the seventh. Bring him to Room Nine."
He did not wait for her to answer.
"And then go home and put on your mask," he said. "The white one. I'll want you in the gallery at the Bourse by the ninth bell. It's going to be a very interesting night."
And he went back up the Long Office between the empty desks, humming.
He was humming her song. The whole of it.
Wren sat at her desk with her hands flat on either side of the paper, and looked at the tiny folded square on the corner of the desk, and did not touch it.
She went to Tallow Street at the sixth bell, on foot, in her white coat and her gray cloak, with the snow coming down again, soft and slow, out of a sky like wet slate.
The chandler's doorway opposite Number 14 was empty. Fenwick had been moved, as the Inquisitor had said he would be, on Wednesday. Wren stood in the street and looked at the empty doorway for a moment. Then she looked at the door of Number 14. Gray. Peeling. With a passage behind it, and a stair, and the bottom tread of the stair where a barley sugar wrapper had once been hidden in a crack at the wall end.
She knocked.
Mrs. Marta opened the door herself.
She was a small, square, old woman with a face like a walnut and black eyes like a bird's, in a black dress and a black shawl, with her white hair scraped back in a knot. She stood in the doorway and looked at Wren. At the white coat. At the twelve silver buttons. At the open eye on the badge.
She did not say anything. She just stood aside.
Wren went in. Down the passage. Through the purple door at the end of it. Into the kitchen.
It was just as she remembered it from Wednesday. A low warm room with a black iron range, and a table scrubbed white, and on every wall, on every shelf, on the mantel and the dresser and the window sill and the top of the cupboard, clocks. Forty of them. More. Big ones and small ones, wooden ones and brass ones, a cuckoo clock with a little carved door, a sailor's clock in a round brass case, a long-case clock in the corner taller than Wren. All of them stopped. All of them showing different times. Terrence had mended them all, Mrs. Marta had told her on Wednesday, when he was a night porter at the Ministry, before he came home one morning and sat down. And he had never set them going again.
He was sitting in his chair by the range now. A big old man, gone soft and slack, with white stubble on his chin and his hands in his lap. His right thumb moving. Rubbing. Round and round, on the ball of his first finger. On a key that was not there.
He did not look up.
"Sit," said Mrs. Marta.
Wren sat. At the scrubbed table, in her white coat, with her hands in her lap.
Mrs. Marta did not sit. She went to the range, and moved the kettle onto the hot part, and stood there with her back to Wren and her hands flat on the brass rail.
"He's coming here," she said. Not a question.
"Yes."
"And you've come for him."
"Yes."
The kettle began to tick as it warmed. Somewhere among the forty stopped clocks, very faint, something creaked. One of the old wooden ones, settling in the heat.
"Hessa?" said Mrs. Marta.
Wren did not answer at once.
Then she said, "I'm sorry."
Mrs. Marta's shoulders did not move. Her hands did not move on the brass rail. She stood with her back to Wren and her face to the range, small and square and old, in her black dress, and did not move at all for a long time.
"The baby," she said at last.
"I don't know. The Inquisitor didn't say. I don't think—" Wren stopped. "I don't think Mr. Vell went after anybody else. He had what he came for."
Mrs. Marta nodded. Once.
And then the street door opened, at the end of the passage, and they heard boots. Slow. Uneven. One heavy and one dragging. A man with a bad leg who had ridden a horse all day and sat in the back of a coal cart for two hours and walked from the coal yard at the bottom of the street.
The purple door opened.
Sergeant Moll stood in it.
He was black. All of him. Coal dust from the cart, and ash, gray-black, in his hair, on his face, on his coat, on his hands. His cap was in his hand. His face under the black was gray, and old, older than Wren had ever seen it, older than it had looked on Thursday across the table in Room Nine when the Inquisitor tapped on his papers and he did not answer.
He looked at Mrs. Marta at the range. He opened his mouth.
And then he saw Wren.
She watched him see her. She watched it go into his face. The white coat. The silver buttons. The open eye. The girl from the Long Office, who had sat beside the Inquisitor on Thursday and taken the notes, in her lovely clear hand.
He did not say anything to her. He did not look surprised. He looked at her for a long moment. And then, slowly, he looked away from her, back to Mrs. Marta. As if Wren were not there. As if she were one of the clocks.
"She's dead, Marta," he said. "Hessa."
Mrs. Marta did not turn round.
"She handed him down," said Moll. His voice was very low and very rough. "Off the top of the stack. To Vell. I didn't see it. The eel-man saw it, from the reeds. She handed him down to keep him out of the fire, and then the mast came down on her." He swallowed. "Wat's alive. He's got the boy. They're in the eel-man's hut at the end of the fen. Wat's bleeding, but he'll live. He said—" Moll stopped. "He said to tell the girl. Lyra. He said to tell her that Hessa kept her promise. Robin first. He said she'd know."
The kettle began to sing on the range.
Nobody moved to take it off.
"And the old lord?" said Mrs. Marta, to the range.
"Alive. They've got him. On a litter. Going to the Hill." Moll's hand had closed on his cap so hard that the black cloth was twisted in his fist. "I was behind a thorn bush, Marta. Three yards. He went past me three yards away, looking at the sky. And I couldn't—"
He stopped.
Then he turned, slowly, in the doorway, on his bad leg, and looked at Wren again.
"You've come for me," he said.
It was not a question either.
Wren stood up from the table.
"Yes," she said. "The Inquisitor sent me. Room Nine." She heard her own voice. Clear and even. Her copying voice. "I'm to bring you now."
Moll looked at her.
And then he did something she did not expect. He nodded. Quite gently. The way you nod to a young soldier who has been given an order he does not like and is going to carry it out anyway. As if he understood. As if he had been given orders like that himself, once, a long time ago, in a valley in the north, and carried them out, and had been carrying them ever since.
"All right, lass," he said.
He put his cap on.
He went to the range first. He did not ask. He went past Wren to the range, limping, and stood beside Mrs. Marta, and put his black hand on her small square shoulder. And left it there. And she put her own hand up and laid it on top of his. And they stood like that for a moment, by the singing kettle, the two of them, without saying anything at all.
Then he took his hand away, and came back to the door, and stood beside Wren.
And as they went out, down the passage, toward the gray street door, in the dark, with the snow coming in under it, Wren felt his hand find hers.
Not to hold it. Moll did not hold her hand. He just touched it. His finger on her palm. The way you touch a page you want somebody to read.
Once.
And then twice, quick.
She did not know what it meant. She had sat in Room Nine on Thursday and watched the Inquisitor tap four numbers on a desk at this man, and watched this man sit on the Inquisitor's hand and not answer, and she had not known what any of it meant. She did not know now.
But she knew it was a word. And she knew it was for her.
She did not take her hand away.
They went out into Tallow Street, in the snow, in the dark, the girl in white and the old soldier black with ash. And behind them, through the gray door, through the passage, from the kitchen with the forty stopped clocks, there came a sound that Wren Halloway would write down that night in her gray secret book, after the Bourse, after everything, in her lovely clear hand that did not shake, though it wanted to.
Mrs. Marta, crying. For the first time. The old man in the chair stopped rubbing his thumb, and looked up.
Moll collected by me. Seventh bell. Midwinter. He went quietly. He touched my hand. Once, and twice. I don't know what it means.
I'm going to find out.
