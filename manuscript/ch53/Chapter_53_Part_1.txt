Chapter 53: The Net Closes, Part 1 of 5: Sedge End
Sedge End
Hessa Doon woke on Midwinter morning because the barge had stopped moving.
That was how she always woke. She had been born on a barge, on the Patient Grace's mother, the old Grace, forty-one years ago, in the cabin, with the river going past outside the planks. And she had slept on the water every night of her life since, except for three nights in the Saltgate Infirmary when she was nine with the lung fever, and those three nights she had not slept at all. A barge was never still. Not really. Even tied up tight at a wharf, even with no wind, even on the flattest water in the flattest country in the world, a barge moved. A little. Always. It breathed. It leaned against its ropes and leaned back. The water talked to the planks all night, under your bunk, a long low gurgling murmur like somebody telling a story in the next room.
And this morning it had stopped.
She lay in the dark of the cabin under her two blankets and her old gray coat and listened, and there was nothing. No murmur. No lean. No creak of the ropes. Just a great deep silence all round, and the cold, and Wat's breathing beside her, slow and heavy, and the baby's in the box by the stove, quick and light.
She knew what it was before she got up. She got up anyway.
She did it the way she did everything, which was all at once, with no fuss. Out from under the blankets, feet on the cold boards, coat on over her shift, boots on without lacing them, up the three steps of the ladder and push back the hatch. And the cold came down on her like a bucket of water, and she put her head and shoulders out into it and looked.
Ice.
The river was gone. Not gone. Turned. In the night, while they slept, it had turned to stone. Gray-white stone, flat and still and hard, all the way out from the side of the barge to the reeds, and all the way across to the far bank, and all the way up and down as far as she could see in the half dark before dawn. It had come in from the edges, the way it did, and met in the middle, and locked. She could see the line where it had met, out in the channel, a long rough ridge in the gray, like a seam.
And the Patient Grace sat in the middle of it, held. Like a fly in amber. Like a fish in a frozen pond.
Hessa stood on the ladder with her head out of the hatch and her breath going up in front of her face and said a word that her mother would have slapped her for, forty years ago.
Then she climbed out and looked properly.
They were at Sedge End. That was what the eel-men called it. The last staithe on the Withy, before the river ran out into the peat fens and stopped being a river at all and became a hundred little channels wandering about among the reeds, going nowhere, the way water does in flat country when it has no hill to run down. It was not on any map that Hessa had ever seen. There was no lock here. No toll. No wharf. Just an old landing of black timber, half rotted, sticking out into the water on posts, where the eel-men used to bring their boats in when there were more eel-men. And behind it, on a little rise of ground no higher than a man's shoulder, which was the highest ground for five miles, a turf hut with a sagging roof and smoke coming out of a hole in the top of it. Old Pask's. The last eel-man.
And all round, the reeds.
They went away in every direction, as far as the eye could see. Tall dead winter reeds, taller than a man, brown and gold and gray, with feathery heads gone white with frost. Thousands and thousands of them. Millions. Standing in the ice with their stems frozen in, very still, very straight, like an army that had stopped marching and forgotten why. When the wind came, they would rustle and lean and whisper, all together, with a sound like the sea. There was no wind now. They stood silent. And the sky above them was low and gray-white and huge, bigger than any sky in the city, with the last stars going out in the east and a long thin line of dirty yellow along the bottom of it where the sun was thinking about getting up.
Go further than hay goes.
That had been the message. From the Captain, Oren, the big gray one with the voice like a slow cart. By mouth, by Ellis, on Tuesday night, at the turf barn with the broken roof past Withy Reach. Tell her: go further than hay goes.
And she had. She had poled the Grace north out of the turf barn on Wednesday night, with Wat at the bow and the old lord in the hay and the girl Juno in the cabin with her frostbitten feet, and put Juno ashore at Withy Reach for Ellis to take back to the city, and kept on going. North. Past the last hay wharf. Past the last farm. Past the last village, which was called Sallow Hythe and had nine houses and a chapel with no roof. Into country where there was no hay, because there was no grass, because there was no ground. Just reeds, and water, and peat, and sky.
Further than hay goes.
She had thought they were safe here. She still thought it, a little. There was nobody here. Nobody came here. The gray coats on the towpath, the drum boys with their hooks, the Inquisitor's man in the round hat with his red lantern, none of them knew there was a place called Sedge End. You could not get here by road. You could only get here by water, by the Withy, through forty miles of reed and channel that all looked the same. Hessa knew the way because her grandfather had brought her here when she was six, to buy eels from Old Pask's father. Nobody else did.
But now the water had turned to stone.
And a barge in stone could not move. And a barge that could not move was not a boat any more. It was a house. A black house with a red sail furled on its mast, sitting in the middle of a white field, where anybody could see it from a mile off.
She looked at it. At her barge. At the Patient Grace, black-tarred, low and long, with her hold full of hay that had nowhere left to go, stacked in the waist under its tarpaulins, and the mast with the red sail bundled on its yard, and the tiller, and the little cabin at the stern with smoke beginning to come out of its chimney now as Wat stirred inside and opened the stove. She looked at it the way you look at a face you love when you have just seen it, for the first time, very small and far away, from the top of a hill.
Then she looked at the ice again. At the line in the middle of the channel where it had met and locked. At how thick it was at the edge, where the reeds came down. She stamped on it, from the side of the barge, with the heel of her unlaced boot. It did not crack. It did not even ring. It just took the stamp, the way a floor does.
Two fingers. Maybe three. In one night.
And the sky in the north, behind the reeds, was the color of a bruise, the color the sky went before the hard frosts came. There would be more tonight. A hand's width, by morning. And then they would be here till the thaw. Weeks. A month. In a black house in a white field.
Behind her, the hatch banged. Wat's head came out of it, big and shaggy and red-bearded, with his eyes half shut and his breath smoking.
He looked at the ice. He looked at her.
"Ah," said Wat.
Wat did not say much. He had not said much in the eleven years she had been married to him, and he had said even less since the wreck at Saltgate in the autumn, when a hook had gone into his right shoulder under the deck of the Margery, and she had pulled it out and tarred the wound and tied his arm up and told him he would live, and he had. He looked at things instead. He looked at the ice now for a long time, the way he looked at everything, slowly and all over, the way a man looks at a wall he is going to have to knock down.
"Axes," he said.
"After breakfast."
"Now."
"After breakfast," said Hessa Doon. "It's Midwinter. I'm not cutting ice on Midwinter morning on an empty stomach for anybody. Not for you, not for the old lord, not for the Archon himself." She turned round on the deck. "Get the stove going properly. And put the kettle on. And see if the baby's awake."
Wat looked at her a moment longer. Then he nodded, and went back down the ladder.
Hessa stood on the deck of the Patient Grace in the white silence of Sedge End, and watched the yellow line in the east get a little wider, a little brighter, and the last star go out above the reeds.
She did not know why her hands were shaking. It was only the cold.
The Blue Stone
The old lord was awake. He was always awake when she came.
He lived in the hay room now. That was what they called it, though it was not a room, not really. It was the old brandy hide, from Hessa's grandfather's day, when the Grace had carried more than hay. A space in the middle of the hold, under the stacks, boarded in on four sides and roofed with planks, with a little door at one end that you could only reach by crawling along a tunnel through the hay. From the outside you could not see it. You could stand on the deck and look down into the hold and see nothing but hay. A barge full of hay, going north, like a hundred other barges. And in the middle of the hay, in the dark, warm and dry and smelling of summer, an old man on a straw mattress under three blankets, tapping.
Hessa crawled in to him with a lamp in one hand and a bowl in the other.
It was not easy. The tunnel was low and narrow, made for a boy, and she was not a boy. She was a big woman, broad in the shoulder and the hip, with arms that could fork a load of hay as big as herself up onto a stack in one swing. She went through on her elbows and knees with the lamp held out in front and the bowl held up and the hay brushing her face on both sides and catching in her hair. And at the end of the tunnel she pushed the little door with her head, and it swung inward, and she was there.
The hay room was warm. Warmer than the cabin. The hay held the heat. It was very small, so small that she could not stand up in it, only kneel, with her head bent under the planks of the roof. A straw mattress filled most of the floor. On the mattress, under the blankets, an old man. And on a nail in the boards above his head, a little tin lamp, burning very low, that she kept alight for him day and night because he did not like the dark.
She did not know that. Nobody had told her. She had just noticed. When the lamp went out, his tapping went faster.
"Morning, my lord," said Hessa Doon.
The old man's eyes came round to her.
They were gray. Pale gray, like her own, like the sky over the fens. Very clear. And they looked at her. They did not look through her the way the eyes of the Slates looked through you, on the wharves at Saltgate, the gray men carrying sacks who did not know their own names. They looked at her face. And knew it was a face. And stayed on it.
He did not know who she was. She did not think he knew who anybody was. But he knew she was there.
"Midwinter," said Hessa. "Did you know? It's Midwinter Day. I've brought you porridge. With honey." She stopped. "No. Not honey. The girl said not honey. The doctor said. Not anything sweet. Just salt." She knelt down by the mattress and set the bowl on the boards. "Salt porridge, then. Like a sailor."
She fed him. She did it the way she fed Robin, with a horn spoon, a little at a time, holding his head up with her other hand behind it. His hair was thin and white and very soft, like a baby's. His face was long and gray and fine-boned, with a high forehead, and deep lines from the nose to the mouth, and a chin that went down square. The girl's chin. Lyra's. Hessa had seen it on Friday a week ago when they sat on the deck together, the girl and the old man, and the girl held his hand and tapped on it. The same chin. Set the same way. As if the two of them had both decided something a long time ago and were not going to change their minds.
He ate. Slowly. He swallowed. He still knew how to swallow. Every morning Hessa watched him do it, and every morning something in her chest that had been held tight all night let go a little.
Round his neck, on a fine gold chain, there hung a stone.
It lay on the gray wool of his nightshirt, on his chest, where the blanket had slipped down. Small and round and smooth, about the size of the end of her thumb. And blue. A deep clear blue, like the sky at the very top on a winter morning. It caught the light of the little tin lamp and gave it back, so bright and so clear that it seemed to have its own light deep inside it, the way a coal has.
The girl had put it there.
On the last night, the Friday, before she went away with the harvester in Ellis's hay wain. She had knelt here in the hay room, where Hessa was kneeling now, and taken it out of the pocket of her coat, out of a twist of faded blue velvet, and hung it round her father's neck. Hessa had been in the tunnel, behind her, with the lamp. She had seen her do it.
It was in the clock, the girl had said. Very low. To him, not to Hessa. In our clock at home, Father. The big one in the hall. In a little drawer, at the back, behind the stones. The twelfth drawer. You had the other one, by your bed, the cracked one. Mrs. Hobb used to dust it. This is its twin. It isn't cracked. She had fastened the clasp at the back of his neck with her fingers shaking. Keep it on. For luck. You told me your father gave you yours for luck. So I'm giving you this one.
And the old man had looked at her. And his hand had come up, very slowly, off the blanket, and closed round the blue stone on his chest. And held it.
He was holding it now.
Hessa had not noticed his hand move. But when she looked down from his face, there it was. His thin gray fingers, closed round the stone, over his heart. The way they had been closed round it, most mornings, all week.
"That's right," said Hessa. "You keep hold of that."
She gave him the last of the porridge. She wiped his mouth with the corner of her apron. She settled the blankets round him, and tucked them in at the sides, the way her mother had tucked her in on the old Grace forty years ago.
And his other hand moved on the blanket. And began to tap.
She knew the tapping. Everybody on the barge knew it. They had lived with it for weeks, day and night, the soft dry sound of an old man's fingertip on wool. Four. Two. Nine. Three. Over and over. All day. All night. In his sleep. The girl said he had done it for two years. The Captain said it was drums. Army calls. That the four was witness and the two was hold and the nine was fold and the three was home, and that a sergeant called Moll had answered it once, on this barge, on a Monday, and the old lord had spoken. Drum. Drummer. The drummer. Find—
Hessa had not been there for that. She had been on deck, with the pole. She was sorry. She would have liked to hear his voice.
She listened now.
And it was not four, two, nine, three.
She frowned. She bent closer, with her head under the planks of the roof, and watched his finger on the gray wool.
Three.
A pause.
Three.
A pause.
Three.
Only that. Only the last one. Over and over, slow and steady, with a long pause between, like a clock with only one number on its face.
Three is home, the Captain had said.
"Home," said Hessa Doon, very quietly.
The finger went on. Three. Three. Three.
She put her big rough red hand over it. Gently. The way you put your hand over a baby's when it is fretting in its sleep. And the finger stopped under her palm. And then, after a moment, she felt it move. Once. Against her skin.
And then twice, quick.
She did not know what that meant. Nobody had told her that one. The girl knew. The Captain knew. Hessa did not.
She held his hand anyway.
"Not yet, my lord," she said. "Not home yet. Soon. When the ice goes. When it's safe. I'll take you home myself. I'll pole you all the way back down the Withy and through every lock from here to the Saltgate, and I'll tie up at the bottom of your own street, and I'll carry you up your own front steps on my back if I have to." She swallowed. "That's a promise. Hessa Doon keeps her promises. Ask anybody on the river. Ask Big Abel at the tannery. I've owed him a goat for six years and he knows he'll get it."
The old man looked at her with his clear gray eyes.
And she thought, for a moment, kneeling there in the warm dark in the middle of the hay with his thin hand under hers and the blue stone glowing on his chest, that he smiled.
She could not be sure. It was very small. Just the corners of the mouth. Just a breath.
But she thought he did.
