Chapter 53: The Net Closes, Part 3 of 5: Cutting the Ice
Cutting the Ice
They cut a road for the Patient Grace through the ice at Sedge End, all that Midwinter morning, with two axes and an eel-spear, and it was the hardest work Hessa Doon had ever done.
She had done hard work. She had done nothing else all her life. She had poled a loaded barge up forty miles of river against the current in a day. She had forked hay from dawn to dark at the Brindle wharves with the stacks going up thirty high. She had pulled a hook out of her husband's shoulder in the dark under the deck of a wreck, and tarred the hole, and held him while he screamed. She had pulled a boy of six out of the lock at Saltgate when she was nine, out of the black water between the gates, by his hair, with the gates closing.
This was harder.
The ice was not like wood. Wood had a grain. You could find it, and follow it, and let the axe do the work. Ice had no grain. It had no anything. You swung the axe down on it, and the blade bit, and a chip flew up, white, glittering, a chip the size of your thumbnail, and stung your face. And the ice under it was just the same as before. You swung again. Another chip. Again. Your arms shook. Your back burned. Your hands, inside the old leather mitts, went numb, and then stopped being numb and started being on fire, and then went numb again. And after a hundred swings there was a little rough white hollow in the gray, the size of a basin, and you could see water at the bottom of it, black, and you had to make it forty yards long and twice as wide as a barge.
Old Pask showed them how.
Not to cut down. To cut round. To cut a line in the ice, a long narrow trench, as deep as the ice, all the way through to the water, in a square, like the edge of a slab. And then another line across. And then put the eel-spear in the crack and lean on it, and the whole slab would tip, and slide down, slow and heavy, under the ice beside it, and leave black water behind. One slab at a time. A slab the size of a door. And then the next.
He worked at the stern, where the old channel ran and the ice was thinnest. Hessa worked beside him. Wat worked at the bow.
Wat was the strongest of them. He had been a sapper for four years in the Commonwealth Foot, before she married him, digging trenches and tunnels under walls for the engineers, and he knew how to use an axe the way other men knew how to use a spoon. He did not swing it high. He did not waste anything. Short hard strokes, from the shoulders, with his whole weight behind them, one after another, steady as a clock. Chock. Chock. Chock. The chips flew up round him in a glittering white cloud.
With his left arm.
His right arm, the hooked one, he kept tied against his side in the old sling she had made from a flour sack. He had not used it properly since the autumn. He could lift a cup with it. He could hold the baby with it, in the crook, if somebody put the baby there. He could not swing an axe.
So he swung it with his left. Short and hard and steady. And at the tenth bell, by the sun, Hessa looked up from her trench at the stern and saw that the flour-sack sling had come loose, and the right arm was hanging, and there was red coming through the back of his shirt, high up, on the shoulder. Spreading. Where the old wound was.
She opened her mouth to tell him to stop.
He looked at her across the ice. Across the long black trench they had cut, with the slabs tipping into it one after another and the water steaming in the cold.
She shut her mouth.
They went on.
The baby was in the cabin, in his box, by the cold stove, wrapped in every blanket they had that was not on the old lord. Hessa went down to him every half hour. He was not happy. He was cold, and hungry, and his tooth hurt, and nobody was holding him. He screamed. She held him for as long as she dared, each time, and gave him the end of a rag soaked in milk to suck, and sang to him, the Saltmarsh song, the one about the tide. Then she put him back in the box and went up the ladder and picked up the axe.
The old lord she did not go to. She could not. The tunnel through the hay took too long. She had left him a cup of water by the mattress, and his lamp, and his blankets. She thought of him every time she swung the axe. In the warm dark, in the middle of the hay. Tapping. Three. Three. Three.
Home.
At noon, very faint, from far away in the west, carried over the flat white fen on the still air, a bell rang.
The bell at Sallow Hythe. In the chapel with no roof. Twelve strokes, thin and small and cracked, like a spoon on a pan.
Old Pask stopped and straightened up, with his hand in the small of his back, and looked at the dyke.
Nothing. The long low line of it, a mile off, empty, white, with the sun on it.
"Thirty yards," he said. He looked at the trench. "Thirty-two. Ten more and we're in the old channel. Then she'll float."
Hessa looked at it too.
It went away from the stern of the Grace in a long ragged black lane, a little wider than the barge, through the gray-white ice. Thirty-two yards of black water, steaming, with broken slabs of ice floating in it and tipped up along its edges like gravestones. It had taken them three hours. Three hours, and Wat's shoulder, and her hands. She could not feel her hands at all now. She looked at them and they were somebody else's.
Ten more yards.
"An hour," said Hessa.
"An hour," said Old Pask. "If nobody stops."
Nobody stopped.
She would remember that hour afterward, if there was an afterward. She would remember it better than any hour of her life. The sound of the axes. Chock. Chock. Chock. The white chips flying. The black water. Old Pask grunting as he leaned on the spear, and the slab tipping, and sliding down under the ice with a long slow grinding sigh. The baby screaming in the cabin, and then stopping, and then starting again. Her own breath, rough and loud in her ears. The red on Wat's back, spreading. The sun, low in the south, moving round. The reeds all round them, gold and gray and silent, frozen in, standing very straight.
And the smell. The clean cold smell of broken ice and black water and the peat under it, old as the world.
At the end of the hour Old Pask leaned on the spear in the last crack, and the last slab tipped, and slid down under, and the black water of the trench met the black water of the old channel with a soft low gurgle, like somebody letting out a breath.
And under Hessa's feet, on the deck of the Patient Grace, for the first time since the night before, the barge moved.
Just a little. Just a lean. Toward the stern. Toward the open water. The way a horse leans toward the stable door.
She felt it in her heels. She felt it in her heart.
"Wat," she said.
He was standing at the bow, by the last of his own trench, with the axe hanging from his left hand and the right arm hanging useless at his side and his shirt red to the waist at the back. He had felt it too. He was looking at her.
"She's floating," said Hessa.
And Wat Doon, who never said much, and had said even less since the autumn, put down his axe on the ice, and put his left hand flat on the black tarred side of the Patient Grace, the way you put your hand on the neck of a horse that has done well. And said, quite clearly, to the boat:
"Good girl."
Hessa laughed. She could not help it. It came out of her like a shout, loud and cracked, across the ice and the reeds and the silence.
And from out in the reeds, to the south, behind the open end of the old channel, behind the road they had cut, the way they meant to go, a light answered her.
Red.
Three times. Short. Like a door opened and shut, and opened and shut, and opened and shut.
Red in the Reeds
The man who came out of the reeds was not in a hurry either.
He came out where the old channel went in among them, a hundred yards south of the stern, at the very place where the black lane of water they had cut was meant to lead. The reeds there parted. Not all at once. Slowly. A few stems leaning aside, and then a few more, the way reeds part for a heron, and frost falling off their white feathery heads in a little glittering shower. And a man stepped out onto the ice.
He was a plain man. That was the first thing Hessa thought. A plain man of fifty or so, middle height, middle build, with a round black hat on his head and a gray cape over his shoulders, buttoned at the throat, with the hem of it mended in a long careful line of darker gray thread. His face was plain too. Not handsome, not ugly. Not anything. A face you could look at for an hour and not be able to tell anyone afterward what it looked like. In one hand he held a lantern. A small tin lantern with a red glass and a little shutter on the front, closed now. In the other he held a fishing rod. A long thin one, of pale cane, with no line on it.
He stood on the ice at the mouth of the old channel and looked at the Patient Grace.
Hessa had never seen him. She knew him anyway.
The Captain had told her. On the Monday, at the Brindle toll bridge, with Moll beside him, a red lantern had flashed three times at the willow bend, north. That's Vell, Moll had said. The Inquisitor's man. The one who finds things. And the girl had told her, before that, on the first night, about a man in a gray cape with a mended hem who had taken a pie cloth with an M stitched on it that belonged to Mrs. Marta, and carried it about the city with him like a calling card.
And Corin had told her. The harvester. Weeks ago now, at the start of it all, on the deck, in the dark, in the Saltmarsh, with the gray coats on the bank. If you ever see a man with a red lantern, Hessa, don't wait to see what he wants.
She had waited. She had been cutting ice.
The man in the round hat opened the shutter of his red lantern. And shut it. And opened it. And shut it. Three times. Not toward her. Back over his shoulder, west, toward the dyke.
Hessa turned her head.
On the dyke, a mile off, against the white, there were horses now. She had not seen them come. Six. Eight. Standing in a line along the top of the bank, black against the snow, very still, with men on them in long gray coats. And as she watched, the men got down off the horses, one by one, and came down the side of the dyke onto the fen, onto the ice, and began to walk toward her. Not fast. Spread out in a long line, a few yards apart, the way men walk when they are beating for birds. Toward the black barge in the middle of the white field.
They were coming across the ice from the west, the way Old Pask had said they would. And the man with the lantern was waiting in the south, at the mouth of the only way out.
"Hessa," said Old Pask, very low, beside her.
She did not answer.
"Hessa Doon. They've got the channel. He's standing right in it." The old eel-man had his spear in both hands. "The ice is thin where he's standing. The old channel. A goose's weight. Not a man's." He paused. "He's standing on it like it was a floor."
He was. The man in the round hat stood on the ice at the mouth of the old channel quite calmly, with his lantern in one hand and his fishing rod in the other, and the ice under him did not crack. As if he weighed nothing. As if he had walked out across it a hundred times and knew exactly where every thin place was, and was not standing on one.
Or as if he did not care.
Then he spoke.
"Mrs. Doon," he said.
He did not shout. He did not need to. The fen was so still, and the cold air so clear, that his voice came across the hundred yards of ice to her as if he were standing at the foot of the ladder. A pleasant voice. Mild and polite. A voice for asking the way.
"Mrs. Hessa Doon. Of the Patient Grace. Of the Saltgate, I think. Your mother was a Fenner, and your father was a Doon, and you married a Doon, a cousin. Watkin." He looked at Wat, at the bow, with his red back. "Good afternoon, Mister Doon. That shoulder wants seeing to."
Wat did not say anything. He had picked up his axe again in his left hand.
"Happy Midwinter," said the man in the round hat. "I'm sorry to come today. I know what day it is. I've come a long way, and I've come as fast as I could, and I'm afraid this is when I got here." He set the lantern down on the ice beside his feet. Carefully. "My name is Vell. I'm here for the old gentleman. That's all. Only him. Nobody else need be inconvenienced. Not you, not your husband, not the eel-man, not the little one." He paused. "I heard him crying, earlier. From the reeds. He's got a good pair of lungs on him."
Hessa's hands closed on the axe.
"There's no old gentleman," she said. Her voice came out loud and cracked and rough, the voice she used for shouting across a lock. "There's hay. A barge full of hay that's got nowhere to go because the river's froze. And me. And my man. And my boy. And that's all."
Vell looked at her across the ice.
He did not smile. He did not frown. His plain face did not do anything at all.
"He taps," he said. "Doesn't he? Four, two, nine, three. All day and all night. In the hay." He looked at the barge. At the stacks in the waist under their tarpaulins. "I've a man on the dyke who has very good ears. He was a bargeman's boy, too, once. He heard it this morning, at first light, when it was very quiet. Before you started with the axes." A pause. "It carries, you know. Over ice. Everything carries over ice."
Hessa did not say anything.
The gray coats were halfway across the fen now. She could see their faces. Young, most of them. Red with cold. Serious. Spread out in their long line, coming on, a few yards apart, walking carefully, the way men walk who have been told the ice is thin in places and do not know which places. One of them slipped and went down on one knee and got up again. None of them laughed.
She counted them. She could not help it. Nine.
Nine, and the man in the round hat. And on the dyke, by the horses, two more, who had not come down.
And on the deck of the Patient Grace, Hessa Doon with an axe, and her husband with one arm, and an eel-man of seventy with a spear. And in the cabin, a baby, screaming. And in the hay, an old man, tapping. Three. Three. Three.
Home.
"Mrs. Doon," said Vell, quite gently. "I'm going to count to ten. I'm not going to do anything while I count. When I get to ten, I'd like you to bring him up on deck and hand him down to me over the side, carefully, wrapped up warm, because it's very cold and he's very old. And then I'll go away. And you can take your barge down the channel you've cut, which is a very good channel, by the way, the best I've seen, and go home to the Saltgate for Midwinter. And nobody will ever ask you anything."
He paused.
"And if you don't," he said, "I'm afraid it'll be the hay."
Hessa looked at the line of gray coats on the ice. At what they were carrying. She had not seen it before. She saw it now. Every third man had a pole over his shoulder. A long pole, with something bound round the end of it. Something black and thick and wrapped in rags, that glistened in the low gold sun.
Pitch.
Torches. Not lit. Not yet.
She looked at her barge. At the stacks of hay in the waist under their tarpaulins. Dry hay. Summer hay, from the Brindle meadows. Packed tight, twelve feet high, the whole length of the hold. Round a little boarded room in the middle, where an old man lay on a straw mattress under three blankets, with a tin lamp on a nail over his head because he did not like the dark.
Everything carries over ice.
"One," said Vell.
Fire
"Two," said Vell.
Hessa Doon did not wait for three.
She dropped the axe. It went down on the deck with a clang and she did not hear it. She was already at the hatch, and down the ladder, all three steps at once, into the cold dark of the cabin, where the stove was out and the candle still burned in the stern window for anybody lost on the water. And the baby was screaming in his box.
She took him out. Blankets and all. She did not stop to unwrap him or wrap him better or do anything at all except take him, the whole hot furious screaming bundle of him, up against her chest, with his red head under her chin. He stopped screaming. He always stopped when she picked him up. He hiccuped once, and grabbed a fistful of her shawl, and was quiet.
"Three," said Vell's voice, outside, through the planks. Mild. Patient. Not loud.
She went back up the ladder with Robin.
Wat was at the top of it. He had come back along the deck from the bow. He stood there by the hatch, huge, with his left hand empty now, and his right arm hanging at his side, and his shirt red to the waist. He looked at her coming up with the baby. He did not say anything.
"Take him," said Hessa.
Wat looked at her.
"Four," said Vell.
"Take him," said Hessa Doon. "Wat. Take him. Now."
She held the baby out.
And Wat put out his left arm, his good one, and took his son. The way he took everything. Slowly, and all at once. The whole bundle of blankets and red hair and hiccups went into the crook of his big left arm and against his chest, and his big scarred sapper's hand came up and spread out over the baby's back, and held him there. And Robin, against his father's red-soaked shirt, grabbed a fistful of the wet cloth instead of the shawl, and held on.
"Five," said Vell.
"Pask," said Hessa.
The old eel-man was beside her. He had his spear.
"Your hut," said Hessa. "The way only you know. East. To the Mere. To the boat at the Mere." She did not look at him. She looked at Wat. "Take them, Pask. Take my man and my boy across the fen to the Mere. Now. While they're looking at the barge. Off the bow, on the reed side, where the ice is thick. Through the reeds. They'll not see you in the reeds. They'll be looking at me."
"Six."
"Hessa—" said Wat.
It was the first time he had said her name in a week. Perhaps longer. She could not remember.
"I'm coming," said Hessa Doon. "I'm coming after. I'm going back for him." She jerked her head at the hold. At the hay. "I'm getting him out of the hay and I'm coming after. Go."
"They'll—"
"They want him," said Hessa. "Not me. Not you. Him. That man said so. He said nobody else need be inconvenienced." She heard her own voice. Loud and cracked and very steady, the way it was across a lock. "If they've got him, they'll not chase you. They'll have what they came for. Go, Wat. Take the boy and go. I promised them. I promised the Captain. Save Robin first. That was the promise."
"Seven," said Vell.
Wat looked at her.
He looked at her for a long time. The way he looked at everything. Slowly and all over. The way a man looks at a wall he is going to have to knock down, and knows he cannot.
Then he bent his head. And kissed her. Once. Hard. On the mouth. With the baby between them, hiccuping, and his red beard rough on her face, and his breath smoking.
And turned. And went along the deck to the bow, with Robin in the crook of his good arm, and Old Pask in front of him with the spear. And over the side, on the reed side, where the ice was thick. And into the reeds.
The reeds took them.
They parted, and closed again, the way they had parted for Vell. A few stems leaning, and frost falling off the white heads in a glittering shower. And then still. And there was nothing there. Just reeds, standing very straight, gold and gray, millions of them, frozen in, as far as the eye could see.
Hessa stood on the deck and looked at the place where they had gone.
"Eight," said Vell.
She turned round.
He was still standing at the mouth of the old channel, a hundred yards off, on the thin ice, with his lantern at his feet. He had not moved. He was looking at the reeds where Wat had gone. And then he looked back at her. And his plain face did not do anything. But he had seen. She knew he had seen.
He did not say anything about it.
"Nine," he said.
And Hessa Doon went down into the hold.
She went in through the gap in the stacks by the mast, where the tunnel began. On her knees, and then on her elbows, into the hay, into the dark, with no lamp this time, because there was no time, pushing through the narrow tunnel her grandfather had made for a boy, with the dry summer hay rough on her face and in her mouth and her hair, smelling of the Brindle meadows in June. She could not see anything. She did not need to. She had crawled this tunnel twice a day for weeks. She knew every turn of it.
She heard Vell's voice, outside, behind her, very faint now through the hay.
"Ten."
And then nothing.
And then, a moment later, a sound she did not know. A soft, deep, rushing sound, like a great breath being drawn in. Like wind coming across the fen. Except that there was no wind.
And then the smell.
She knew that. Every bargewoman knew that. Every bargewoman in the world woke up in the night sometimes, sweating, with that smell in her nose, and lay there in the dark with her heart going until she was sure it was only a dream.
Hay burning.
She pushed the little door at the end of the tunnel with her head, and it swung inward, and she was in the hay room.
The tin lamp was burning on its nail. Very low. The old lord was lying on his straw mattress under his three blankets, the way she had left him, with the cup of water by his side, untouched. His eyes were open. They came round to her as she came through the door, on her knees, with hay in her hair.
His hand was tapping on the blanket.
Not three. Not any more.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
Faster than she had ever heard it. Over and over. As if he were calling to somebody very far away, and they were not answering.
"I know," said Hessa Doon. "I know, my lord. I've come for you. I said I would."
And behind her, at the far end of the tunnel, through the hay, she saw a light.
Not red. Gold. Orange. Moving. Getting bigger.
Coming in.
