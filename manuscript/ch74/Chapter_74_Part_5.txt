Chapter 74: The Iron Horse, Part 5 of 5: Her Words
Her Words
She rode north up the valley, alone, against the stream of her own people going south, and Corin rode with her, inside her, and could not get out.
The iron horse ran. He felt it run. Not like a horse. Like a mill wheel, like a great clock gone mad, every stride a crash of iron hooves on the road and a clatter of gears inside the barrel and a long hot hiss of breath. The orchards went past. The mill. The white blossom, shaken off the branches by the thunder of the hooves, blowing across the road behind them like snow. People pressing back against the walls of the lane to let her through, and looking up at her as she went by, and she did not look back at them. She had told them not to look back for her. She would not look back for them.
Through the village. Past the green and the well. Past the smithy with its red forge. Past the chapel on the rise, with the sun over its door.
She did not look at the chapel.
Corin felt her not look. Felt it like a hand pressed hard over a wound.
On. Out of the village. Up the valley, where it narrowed, and the hills came close, and the river ran fast and white between rocks. Up and up. Until the valley opened out again, at its head, into a great wide round bowl among the hills, green, with a little lake in the middle of it that shone like a coin.
The middle.
She stopped the horse at the lake's edge.
And got down.
Corin felt the shock of the ground come up through her boots. Felt the weight of her body. Felt her stand there, on the grass, at the edge of the shining lake, with her back to the south. With her people behind her, far away down the valley, going into the ditch. And the gray tide behind them, coming on.
She took off her helmet.
That was strange. Corin had not expected that. She lifted it off, the iron helmet, the one he was lying under now, on the hot Glass, sixty years from here. She lifted it off and the cool air touched her scalp through the braid, and she held the helmet in her two hands for a moment and looked into it.
Then she put it back on. Tight. And buckled the strap under her scarred jaw.
So I'll not lose my head, she thought. And something in her laughed. A short hard bark of a laugh. Like Arran's.
And then she began.
Corin had burned things. He had burned a great many things. He knew what it was. You found the thing, low down, in the place where you kept it. You held it. You let it go. And the moment took it, and gave you something back.
This was that. And it was not that at all.
She went down into herself like a woman going down into a cellar with a lamp. And Corin went with her. Down past everything. Past the morning and the valley and the chapel and the man's hand. Past her childhood. Past all of it. Down to the bottom. To the place where the words were kept.
All the words. Every one she had. Every word she had ever learned, since she was a baby in a cradle in the top house at Haverstock, saying mama for the first time to a woman with a face Corin could not see.
They were all there. Stacked up. Thousands upon thousands of them. More than Corin had ever imagined one person could carry. And every one of them was hers, and every one of them was a thread, and all the threads ran out from her into the world and tied her to it.
She took hold of them.
All of them.
And she began to give them away.
The first ones went easily. Corin felt them go. The names of the enemy. The gray men. The Ministry. The names of the Ministry's captains, and its mages, and its councillors far away in the city, every one she had ever heard. She let them go like spitting. Gladly. Take them. I don't want them. And they went out of her into the bowl of the hills, and the air over the lake shimmered, and grew hot.
Then the names of things.
Those were harder. Corin felt them start to tear.
River. It went. Out of her. And she looked at the lake and the stream running into it and she did not know what it was. Bright moving thing. Cold. Going. Gone.
Horse. The iron beast beside her. She looked at it and did not know what it was called. Only that it was big and red and hers.
Sword. Bread. Fire. Apple. Blossom. Wall. Road. One after another. Faster now. Pouring out of her. And with every one that went, the air over the lake got hotter, and brighter, and began to hum.
Corin felt each one go. Felt each one tear loose. Every word she lost, he felt the hole where it had been, the way you feel the hole when a tooth comes out. And he could not stop it. He could not let go. He could not do anything but be there, inside her, while she tore herself open.
Then the names of her people.
She stopped.
Just for a breath. On the edge. Corin felt her stand there, at the edge of it, with the lake shimmering and the hum rising and the gray tide coming up the valley behind her.
And then she did it.
One by one. Not all at once. One by one, the way you would say goodbye to each of them at a door.
Hob. Gone. Talan. Gone. Annis. Ralf. Bryn. Gone. Corin knew those names. He had heard them. Lyra had read them off a cross in the snow at Haverstock, with Oren saying each one after her. And now he felt them go out of Sola Vance one by one like lamps going out along a street.
Aldric.
Her brother. Corin felt it. Felt the warmth of him. A big slow laughing man, with a little boy on his shoulders. Her brother.
And the little boy on his shoulders. Corin felt her see him too. A small boy with a round red face and a cap pulled down over his ears, laughing, holding on to his father's hair with both fists. Aldric's boy. Her nephew. Who had gone south, the winter before, to the city, to work at the docks, and written once, and not again.
She had been glad he went. Corin felt it. Glad, now, at the end, with the gold rising round her. Glad that there was one of them a long way off, in the south, where the light would not reach. One of Aldric's, who would live, and have children, and swear by her teeth when he dropped a hammer on his foot, and never know why.
Live, she thought at him. At the boy on the shoulders. Live, and forget me, and live.
She held on to that one.
She held on to it so hard that Corin felt it like a hand closing round his own heart.
And then she let it go.
And the air over the lake turned gold.
And last of all, she gave her name.
Sola.
Corin felt it go out of her. Out of them. Like the last coal tipped out of a stove.
And she stood there at the edge of the shining lake with nothing left in her. Not a word. Not a name. Not her own name. Not his. Only the gold light rising round her, and the hum, and the heat, and the gray tide coming up the valley.
And she opened her mouth.
And she roared.
No words. There were no words left. Only the sound. The great harsh hoarse bellow of her, coming up from the bottom of her chest, with nothing in it but itself. Not a word. A roar. Like an animal. Like a fire. Like the sun, if the sun could shout.
And Corin roared with her.
He could not help it. He was inside her. Her mouth was his mouth. Her chest was his chest. He had no words left either, because she had none, and there was no him that was not her. He roared, and roared, and the roar went out of them both into the bowl of the hills, and the hills threw it back, and the lake shook.
And behind her, down the valley, at the mouth of it, in a long zigzag ditch with stakes in the wall, all along it, a great many men put down their pikes and turned round to see.
The Light
She got back on the horse.
She did not know it was a horse. She did not know the word for any of it anymore. Not horse, not saddle, not ride, not south. But her body knew. Her hands knew. They found the iron reins and her foot found the iron stirrup and she went up into the high frame on its back the way she had gone up into it a thousand times, and the great red beast felt her weight and turned under her.
South.
Toward the valley. Toward the village and the chapel and the orchards and the ditch. Toward the gray tide coming up from the plain with its cold white lamps.
And she kicked it.
And it ran.
Corin had thought it ran fast before. Coming up the valley. It had not. This was fast. This was the iron horse running the way it had been built to run, every gear in its belly screaming, every hoof coming down on the road like a hammer on an anvil, the whole great body of it shaking and roaring and hissing under her. The wind of it tore at her braid. The hills went past in a smear of green. The river was a white flash at the edge of her eye.
And the light came.
It came out of her chest.
Corin felt it start. Behind her breastbone. In the place where the hot hard knot had been, the coal banked all winter, the thing she had always carried and never touched. It was not a knot now. It was open. Wide open. And out of it, out of the place where all the words had been, came light.
Gold.
Not like lamplight. Not like sunlight, even. Thicker than that. Heavier. Like honey, if honey could burn. It poured out of the front of her, out of her chest, through her coat, through the iron of her breastplate. It poured out in front of the running horse in a great wide river of gold, and where it touched the road the road went pale. Corin saw it go pale. Saw it go white-gold, and smooth, and ripple, like sand under a wave. A road of light laid down in front of the iron hooves, straight as a rule, going south.
The Sun Road.
She was making it.
They were making it. She and he. Because he was in her, and the light was coming out of her chest, and it was coming out of his chest too. He felt it. Felt it pour out of him, hot and heavy and gold, every bit of him going out into it, more and more and more, like blood out of a wound that will not close.
It did not hurt.
That was the strangest thing. It should have hurt. It was everything she had. It was everything that was left of her, after the words. Her body. Her bones. Her breath. Her heart. All of it turning to light and pouring out in front of her, down the valley, toward the gray. It should have hurt more than anything had ever hurt.
It did not.
It felt like singing. Like the end of a long song, the last note, held, and held, and held, with all the breath you have, until there is no breath left and you are only the note.
The village went past. On the rise, the chapel. The light poured over it. Corin saw the sun carved over its door blaze white for one moment, as if it had woken up.
The orchards. The blossom. Gold.
And ahead, at the mouth of the valley, the ditch. The long zigzag line of it, with its earth wall and its stakes. And in it, all along it, the men with the sun badges. Standing. Turned round. Their pikes put down behind them. Their faces lifted. Looking at her. At the light coming down the valley toward them like a sunrise.
She saw them.
She had no words for them. No names. She did not know what they were called, any of them, or what she was called, or what the light was, or why. But she saw their faces. Lifted. Not afraid.
And beyond them, the gray. The lamps. The fed mages with their hands held out. Coming on.
The light reached the ditch.
And in the last second, the very last, before it went over the ditch and the men in it and on, out of the valley's mouth, into the gray, and took everything, all of it, the whole world, Corin felt her do one more thing.
Not with words. She had none.
With her hand.
Her left hand. The free one. The one not holding the reins. It came up off her knee, in the gold, in the roar, at the very end of everything. And it moved.
Four quick signs.
Not letters. Not anything Corin knew. Shapes. Made with the fingers and the thumb, quick and sharp, the way a soldier makes a sign across a noisy field to the men on the far side.
One.
The hand flat, and then the first two fingers crossed.
Two.
The fist, closed, pressed to the heart.
Three.
The hand open, palm down, and then folded, the fingers coming down over the thumb, small, tight.
Four.
The hand turned palm up. Open. And held there. Out. Toward the men in the ditch.
Four signs.
Corin did not know what they meant.
But he felt her mean them. Felt every bit of what was left of her pour into those four movements of one hand. The way she had poured herself into the light. As if the light were for the gray men, and the signs were for her own.
Four. And then two. And then nine. And then three.
He did not know why he thought of them like that. As numbers. He did not know where the numbers came from. They came. Four signs, and the numbers under them, like the bones under the glass soldiers' skin.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
And then the gold went over everything.
And there was nothing else.
No Way Back
Gold.
There was nothing else. There was no up and no down, no valley, no horse, no ditch, no gray. No her. No him. Only gold. Everywhere. Thick and warm and heavy and bright, like lying at the bottom of a pool of honey with the sun shining through it.
He drifted in it.
He did not know he was drifting. He did not know anything. There was nothing to know. Knowing was a thing that needed words, and there were no words here. She had given them all. Every one. And he had been inside her when she gave them, and they had gone out of him too, all of them, into the gold.
He did not know what he was.
He did not know that there was a he.
There was the gold. And it was warm. And it did not hurt. And it went on.
After a long time, or no time, something came.
Not into the gold. At the edge of it. Very far off. So far that it was hardly there at all. A sound.
The same sound. Over and over.
Two parts. A short one, then a longer one, falling. Then nothing. Then the same again. Short, and long, falling.
Over and over.
He did not know what it was.
It was a sound, that was all. A sound a throat made. He knew that much, somewhere. That throats made sounds. That there were things called throats. But not what the sound was for. Not what it meant. Not why someone, far off, at the edge of the gold, would make the same sound again and again and again, until their voice cracked on it, and then go on making it anyway.
It did not mean anything.
He did not know what a name was.
He drifted.
And there was something else. Not a sound. A feeling. Somewhere very far off too, at the edge, at the bottom of everything. A pressure. Hard. Something closed round something, and squeezing. Squeezing so hard it hurt.
Hurt.
That was strange. Nothing hurt, in the gold. That was the whole of the gold: that nothing hurt. And yet, far off, at the bottom of everything, there was this one small place that hurt. A place that was being squeezed. By something bigger than it, rough and warm, holding on.
He did not know it was a hand.
He did not know it was his hand.
He did not know that the thing squeezing it was another hand, a big scarred square one, gripping so hard that the knuckles had gone white, and that the man it belonged to was kneeling on hot glass in the white noon sun with his teeth set and his eyes shut and tears running down into his short gray beard, and holding on. Holding on. Not letting go. Because he had said he would not.
He did not know any of that.
He only knew that there was a sound, far off, falling, over and over. And a hurt, far off, holding. And the gold, all round, warm, and kind, and endless, asking nothing.
The gold was easier.
He drifted toward the gold.
The sound came again. Short, and long. Falling. Cracked, now. Broken in the middle, as if the throat making it could hardly make it anymore. But making it anyway.
The hurt squeezed.
He did not know what they wanted.
He did not know that, somewhere, far down at the very bottom of the gold, under everything, under all the words she had given away and he had given with her, there was one thing left. One small thing. That was not a word. That had never been a word. That was older than words.
A voice. Saying the sound.
He had kept it. Before. Once. When he had given everything else away, in a great dome of gold light, a long time ago, he had kept that. Only that. A voice saying the sound. He had held on to it in the dark like a rope.
It was still there.
He did not know it was still there.
But the gold did not have it. The gold had everything else. And it did not have that.
The sound came again.
And something, at the very bottom of him, very small, turned toward it. The way a plant turns toward a window. Without knowing why. Without knowing anything.
Just turned.
A Noise
He opened his eyes.
White.
That was all, at first. White, everywhere, so bright it hurt. A great white blaze over him, and a darker shape in the middle of it, rough, red, round, hanging. And gold at the edges. And heat. Heat on his face, heat under his back, heat in his chest, in his throat, everywhere.
He did not know what any of it was.
He did not know that the white was the sky at noon, over the Glass, with the sun straight overhead. Or that the red round thing hanging over him was an iron hoof, as big as a cartwheel, lifted and frozen sixty years ago. Or that the gold at the edges was light coming through a stretched hide, held up on two poles by a big slow man with a strapped arm and a small sharp woman with a spear.
He did not know what sky was. Or hoof. Or man. Or woman.
There were shapes. And colors. And heat. And noise.
There was a lot of noise.
Someone close by was breathing very hard. In and out. Like a bellows. And someone else was saying something, over and over, low and fast, a long run of sounds with no stops in them. And far off, very faint, coming through the glass under his back and up into his bones, there was something else. A small hard even knocking. Tick. Tick. Tick.
He did not know what it was.
He did not know anything.
And then the gold came up in him.
Not outside. Inside. In his chest. Where it had been, where the light had poured out of her, out of them. It was still there. Not light, now. Not gold. But the thing under the gold. The thing that had made it. The great hot furious roaring thing, with no words in it, that had stood at the edge of a lake and opened its mouth.
It came up.
Up through his chest. Into his throat.
And out.
He roared.
He did not know he was doing it. He did not decide to. His mouth opened and the sound came out of it, huge and harsh and hoarse, tearing his throat, the same sound, the very same, that had come out of her at the lake. A bellow. No words in it. Nothing in it but itself. Like an animal. Like a fire. Like the sun, if the sun could shout.
It went on.
And on.
He could not stop it. He did not want to stop it. It filled him. It was all there was. The roaring, and the white sky, and the red hoof hanging over him, and the heat.
He felt his body arch up off the hot felt with the force of it. Felt his head go back. Felt something heavy and cold slide off his brow and fall with a clang onto the glass beside his ear. Felt his hands clench. Both of them. The right one on nothing. The left one on something warm and rough and hard, that clenched back.
He roared until there was no breath left in him.
And then he lay there, on his back, on the hot felt, gasping. His throat raw. His chest heaving. His eyes wide open on the white sky.
The breathing beside him had stopped. The long low run of sounds had stopped. Everything had stopped. There was only the tick, tick, tick, far off, under the glass. And his own breath, rasping in and out.
And then a face came over him.
Upside down. From above his head. Between him and the white sky. A face. Pale, with the light behind it. Short dark hair, falling forward, sticking to a wet forehead. Eyes, very wide. Red-rimmed. Wet.
A mouth.
The mouth opened.
And made a sound.
Two parts. A short one. Then a longer one, falling.
The same sound. The one from the edge of the gold. The one that had come again and again, cracked and broken, far off, while he drifted.
He looked at the face.
He looked at the mouth, making the sound.
It meant nothing. It was a sound. A noise a throat made. Like the tick under the glass. Like the breathing. Like wind.
The mouth made it again. Closer. Lower. Shaking.
He looked at it with no understanding at all. The way you look at a bird making a noise on a wall. Not unkindly. Not kindly. Just looking. At a thing that was making a sound, and did not know that the sound was only a sound, and did not mean anything at all.
The face above him went very still.
He saw it go still. He did not know what it meant. He saw the eyes in it, wide and wet, looking down into his. Saw them look, and look, and look. Searching. For something.
He did not know what.
He did not have it.
The mouth opened once more. And this time, the sound did not come out of it. Only a breath. Shaking.
And from somewhere beside him, from the warm rough thing that was holding his left hand so hard it hurt, a different sound came. Low. Deep. Broken. Not the same sound. A different one. Many sounds together, running, cracked.
He did not know they were words.
He did not know what words were.
He lay on the hot Glass under the iron hoof, at noon, with two people holding on to him, and looked up at the white sky, and did not know his name.
