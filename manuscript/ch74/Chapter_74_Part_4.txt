Chapter 74: The Iron Horse, Part 4 of 5: Gloves Off
Gloves Off
He took off his right glove himself.
It was hard, lying on his back, with Oren holding his other hand. He did it with his teeth, the way Oren had. Pulling at the fingertips one by one until the glove came loose and he could shake it off. Lyra took it from beside his face and put it somewhere. He did not see where.
And he lay there, with his right hand bare on the hot felt beside him, and looked at it.
Gray. The gray of old ash, smooth and dead, from the wrist to the knuckles. And the fingertips black. Black to the first joint. Numb. He could not feel the felt under them. He had not felt anything with those fingertips since the dome.
He had walked every jar of his working life with this hand. Laid it on the cold glass of a thousand stones at his desk at Intake, in the lamplight, with the clerks gone home. Felt the Ash come up into it. Felt the cold that is not cold, and the pull, and the first thin edge of somebody else.
And every time, every single time, there had been someone with him.
Not in the room. In him.
The old man. Malakor. In the rod first, and then in him. For longer than Corin could clearly remember, every jar he walked, there had been the dry old voice at the back of his head. Telling him where to put his feet. When to stop. When to come out. Not that door, boy. That's a bad one. Leave it. Go on. Go on. She won't hurt you. Out. Now. Out, before it closes.
A guide. A rope. A hand on his shoulder in the dark.
He listened for it now.
Nothing.
The warm dim place in him, with the furs and the low fire, and the dent in the furs. Empty. As it had been since the first step onto the Glass. Count, boy, and then nothing. Not a word. Not a breath. Not even the flicker of attention he thought he had felt, two nights ago, with the light under his palm.
Nothing.
He was going in alone.
He had never gone in alone. Not once. Not that he could remember.
When I stop talking it won't mean I've gone.
He held on to that. Hard. The way he was holding Oren's hand.
"He's not here," he said. Out loud. To Lyra. To anyone.
Lyra's knees pressed against the top of his head through the rolled pack.
"I am," she said.
He shut his eyes.
Then he opened them again, because he wanted to see.
Oren unwrapped the helmet.
He did it slowly. Fold by fold of the coat. Without touching what was inside. And there it was, on the felt beside Corin's shoulder, in the gold light through the shade. Iron. Rust-red. Round, and dented. The deep dent on the left side, as big as a fist. The short brim. The guard at the back. And the long gray braid, coiled on the felt beside it like a sleeping snake.
It looked very small, off the horse. Very ordinary. A soldier's helmet. You could have found one like it in any pawnshop in the city.
Corin did not feel anything from it. Not lying here, a hand's width away. Nothing at all. No cold. No pull.
But he could hear it.
That was the strange thing. He had not expected that. Lying with his ear close to it on the felt, in the hot still air under the shade, he could hear something. Very faint. Not the hum of the Glass. Not the ticking. Something inside the iron. Like the sound you hear when you hold a shell to your ear. A rushing. A roaring, very far off and very small. Like the sound of a great fire on the other side of a thick wall.
He looked at Vespera.
She was kneeling with her needles in front of her and her hands flat on her knees. She was looking at the helmet. Her face was gray.
"You can hear it," said Corin.
"Yes," said Vespera. Very low. "Saints help you. Yes."
Oren lifted the helmet.
With the coat. Through the thick wool. And held it over Corin's face. Corin looked up into the inside of it. Dark. Rusty. Scratched. A leather lining, once, rotted to nothing but a few black threads.
"On the brow," said Lyra. Very quietly. Reading from the page in her memory. "Not the hand. The brow."
Oren lowered it.
Not onto Corin's head. It would not fit on, lying down. Onto his brow. The cold rim of it, wrapped still in Oren's coat, resting on his forehead above his eyes, so that the iron bowl of it stood up from his head like a great rusty flower.
The coat was between them. Wool. Corin could feel the weight of the iron through it, heavy and cold, pressing on the bone of his forehead. But not the iron itself.
"Now," said Oren. Low. "Now. When you're ready. Pull the coat away. Or I will."
Corin lay there.
The roaring in the iron was louder now. Right above his eyes. Like a fire. Like a waterfall. Like a crowd, far off, shouting something he could not make out.
He lifted his right hand. His bare one. Gray. Black at the tips.
And took hold of the edge of Oren's coat, where it lay between the helmet's rim and his forehead.
And pulled it away.
The iron came down onto his skin.
Cold. That was the first thing. So cold it burned. A ring of cold across his forehead, sharp as a blade.
He put his bare black fingertips on the rim of it, to hold it there. Iron under the tips that could not feel. Rust, rough and flaking.
And for one breath, nothing.
One breath.
Then his fingertips felt it.
His dead black fingertips, that had felt nothing since the dome. They felt the iron. They felt it go hot. They felt something come up out of the iron into them, through them, up into his hand, his wrist, his arm, like a spark running up a fuse.
He heard Oren's hand close on his other hand, hard enough to hurt.
He heard Lyra say, "Corin—"
And then he did not hear anything at all.
In
It was not like walking a stone.
He had walked so many. He knew how it went. You put your hand on the cold glass of the jar, or the stone, or the burnt-out skull, and you waited. And after a while, a window opened. Somewhere in front of you, in the dark behind your eyes. A small gray window, like a window in a wall at night. And through it you could see. A room. A face. A street. Someone else's. And you went up to the window, slowly, and looked through. And then, if you were careful, if you were good, you climbed through it. And walked beside them. Inside their memory. Like a ghost walking beside a living person, seeing what they saw, but never quite them. Always a step to one side. Always able to turn round and see the window behind you, and climb back out.
That was walking.
This was not that.
There was no window.
There was no waiting.
The iron touched his skin, and his fingertips felt it, and the spark ran up his arm. And then something hit him.
Not from outside. From inside. From everywhere at once. The way a wave hits you when you are standing on a beach with your back to the sea and do not see it coming. All at once. All over. Cold and heavy and roaring, knocking his feet out from under him, filling his mouth and his eyes and his ears.
And he was under.
Under what, he did not know. There was no up. There was no down. There was a roaring, so loud it was not a sound anymore but a weight, pressing on every part of him. And a rush. A terrible fast rush, like being carried along by a river in flood, tumbling, turning over and over, with no bottom under his feet and no sky above his head and no bank anywhere.
He reached for the window.
There was no window.
He reached behind him, the way he always did, for the way back. For the gray square of light that meant out.
Nothing.
He reached for the old man.
Malakor—
Nothing.
He reached for his own hand. The left one. Where Oren had been holding it. He could not find it. He could not find his hand. He could not find his arm. He could not find anything of himself at all. Only the roaring, and the rush, and the tumbling.
And then the tumbling stopped.
Not slowly. All at once. The way a flood stops when it hits a wall.
And he was standing up.
No. Not standing. Sitting. High up. Higher than a man stands. On something that moved under him. Something huge and hot and alive that heaved and shifted between his knees, and creaked, and ticked, and breathed out a long hot breath that smelled of oil and iron.
He was looking down at a valley.
And it was not his valley. And they were not his eyes. And the hands holding the reins were not his hands.
They were big hands. Square. Brown. A woman's hands, but bigger than most men's, with thick strong fingers and broad knuckles scarred white across the backs. Holding iron reins. Gripping them. Hard.
He tried to look away from them.
He could not. He could not look anywhere she did not look. He could not move anything she did not move. He could not think anything she was not thinking.
Because he was not beside her.
He was not walking with her, a step to one side, the way he had walked with a thousand others.
He was in her.
All the way in. No space between. No window behind. No way to turn round.
She looked down at the valley. And he looked down at the valley. And her heart was going in her chest like a drum, and his was too, because it was the same heart, and the breath she drew was the same breath, and the thing she felt, looking down, was the thing he felt.
And what she felt was this:
Mine.
The whole valley. Green and gold in the morning light. Mine. Every field. Every wall. Every roof. Mine. Mine. Mine. And they are not having it.
The iron horse shifted under her, under him, and ticked, and breathed out its long hot oily breath.
And somewhere very far away, at the very edge of everything, so faint it might have been nothing at all, a voice was saying a word. One word. Over and over.
He did not know what it was.
He did not listen.
The Valley
It was green.
That was the first thing, and it went through her like a knife, and so it went through him. So green. The valley lay below the ridge where she sat the iron horse, and it was every green there was. The deep dark green of old woods along the hills on either side. The bright new green of spring wheat in the fields, in long strips. The gray-green of willows along the river. The green-gold of meadows, with the morning sun on them and the dew still lying, so that they shone.
She had known it all her life. Every field. She knew whose they were. That strip was old Dunnock's, and that one his son-in-law's, and that long one going down to the water was the widow Ashby's, who grew the best barley in the north and would not sell it to anyone she did not like. And Corin knew it too, because she knew it. He did not have to learn it. It was simply there, in her, in him, the way your own kitchen is there when you walk into it in the dark.
The river ran down the middle of the valley, wide and slow and brown, catching the light in long silver curves. There was a mill on it, with a wheel turning, white water coming off the paddles. There were orchards on the slopes above the river, apple and pear and plum, in rows, with the blossom out. White and pink. Like snow that had decided to be kind.
And in the middle of the valley, on a low round rise above the river, a village.
She looked at it, and the ache that went through her was so big that Corin could not breathe.
It was not a big village. A few dozen houses. Gray stone walls and turf roofs and blue smoke going up from the chimneys, straight up, because there was no wind this morning. A green in the middle, with a well. A smithy, with the forge glowing red in the open door. Lanes going between the houses, narrow, with walls on either side, and gardens behind the walls, and washing hung out.
And at the top of the rise, a chapel.
Small. Stone. With a little squat tower and a bell in it, and a yard round it with old stones leaning in the grass. And on the front of it, over the door, carved in the stone, a sun.
She had been married in that chapel. Corin knew it, the way she knew it. Not as a picture. As a feeling. A warmth, low down, behind the breastbone. A man's hand in hers, a big rough hand, sweating. Rain on the windows. Somebody's baby crying at the back. And her mother's face in the front pew, wet, trying not to be.
The man was dead. Three winters dead. Corin did not know how. She did not think about how. She thought about his hand, and then she put it away, the way you put a letter back in a drawer.
The valley was full of people.
Not in the fields. Nobody was working the fields this morning. They were all on the road.
The road came down the valley from the north, from the hills, along the river, and through the village, and on south, out of the valley's mouth toward the plain. And it was full. From end to end. A long dark river of people moving along it. Coming down out of the hills, from all the little valleys round about, from all the farms and hamlets and steadings, and gathering here. In this valley. Below this ridge.
Men with pikes.
Hundreds. More than Corin could feel. More than she could count, and she had tried, last night, by the fires, and given up somewhere in the second thousand. Valley men. Farmers. In their own coats, their own boots, with their skirts hooked back behind them for the march. With the pikes off their barn walls, long and dark, held upright, so that from up here on the ridge the whole road looked like a field of tall dark wheat moving slowly south.
And on every chest, high on the left side, a sun.
She had given them those. Every one. She had had them made, in the smithy down there, and at Haverstock, and Thistle Ford, and Cray, and every forge in the north that would do it. Tin, mostly. Some brass. A round disc with the rays going out. So they would know each other. So that in the smoke and the noise, when everything was gone to blood and fire, a man could look at the man beside him and see the sun on his coat and know: ours.
Corin saw them. Through her eyes. Every one. He saw a man with a beard. A man with a long plait down his back. A boy, thin, too young, in a coat that hung off him. Two men walking shoulder to shoulder, one with his hand on the other's back.
He knew them.
Not as she knew them. As himself. Somewhere deep under her, under the roaring, a small part of him that was still Corin said: I've seen you. I've seen you. In the Trench. In the green. I saw your bones.
And she did not hear it. Because she was not listening for him. She did not know he was there.
She was looking south.
At the mouth of the valley. At the plain beyond it. Where the road went out of the hills and on, flat and straight, toward the south.
At what was coming up it.

They came up the plain like a tide.
Gray. That was all Corin saw at first, through her eyes. A gray line across the whole width of the plain at the valley's mouth, from one side to the other, coming on. Slowly. Steadily. The way the sea comes in over a flat beach. Not fast. But you could not stop it, and you could not get round it, and it would not stop until it had covered everything.
Then she looked harder, and he looked with her, and the gray line became men.
Thousands. In rows. In blocks. Square blocks of men in gray coats, all the same, every coat cut from the same bolt of cloth, every button in the same place. Marching in step. He could hear it now, through her ears. The tramp of their boots, all together, coming up the plain like a heartbeat. And the drums. Not the light quick drums of the valley boys. Big drums. Deep ones. Booming.
And over the blocks, on poles, lights.
Lamps. Hundreds of them. Lifted high on long poles over the heads of the gray men, the way banners are lifted. Not oil lamps. Not candles. A cold white light, too white, too steady, that did not flicker. Corin knew that light. He had sat under it every working day of his life, at his desk at Intake. Ash lamps. Burning Ash. Burning the memories of people who had died, or been emptied, so that the Ministry could see in the dark.
She knew it too. And she hated it with a hate so big and so cold that it made Corin's teeth ache, inside her teeth.
They're burning our dead, she thought. They're burning our dead to see us by.
And among the gray blocks, here and there, walking in the open spaces between them, other figures. Not in rows. Alone, or in twos. In long dark coats, not gray. With their hands held out a little from their sides.
Mages.
She looked at them and Corin felt her know them. Not their names. What they were. Men and women who had been fed. Fed on stones. On bought memories, bought Ash, bought skills, packed into them by the Ministry the way you pack powder into a gun. So that they could burn and burn and burn and never run out. Never have to give anything of their own. Because it was all somebody else's.
And behind it all, at the very back, far off on the plain, a dark lump. Low and wide. With smoke coming off it.
She did not know what that was. Corin did not either. He felt her not know, and be afraid of not knowing.
She looked down at her own people, on the road below. At the long dark river of them with their pikes up like wheat. At the sun badges on their chests. At the boys with the drums, and the women with the water carts, and the old men who should not have come and had come anyway.
And she looked at the gray tide coming up the plain.
And she knew. Corin felt her know it, the way you feel a stone drop into cold water. Not a thought. A weight. Going down.
We can't hold them.
Not with pikes. Not with sun badges. Not with farmers and boys and old men in a ditch. Not against that. Not against the lamps and the fed mages and the thing at the back with the smoke coming off it.
They would come up the valley. Through the orchards. Over the walls. Into the village. Into the chapel. And they would take it. All of it. Every field and every house and every child. And put it in jars. And burn it in their lamps.
Unless.
Corin felt the unless begin. Deep in her. Very deep. A hot hard knot of something, under her breastbone, where he had felt the warmth of the chapel and the man's hand. Something she had been carrying a long time. Something she had always known was there and had never touched. Like a coal kept banked all winter, in case.
He felt her touch it.
She was big. He knew that now, from the inside. Big in the body, broad and heavy and strong, so that the iron horse under her had to be the size it was to carry her. Her shoulders were wide as a door. Her arms were thick. Her hair was gray, iron-gray, and long, and braided tight down her back and pinned up under the helmet so it would not catch.
And her jaw was scarred. Corin felt it. Felt the tight shiny pull of an old burn, all down the left side of her face, from the ear to the chin, that made one side of her mouth not move quite like the other. He did not know how she had got it. She did not think about it. It was just her face. The face she had been looking out of for fifty years.
She lifted her head.
And she turned the iron horse, with her knees and the reins, so that it faced down the slope of the ridge. Toward the road. Toward her people.
And she opened her mouth, and shouted.
Corin had never heard a voice like it. He was inside it, and it nearly knocked him out of her. It came up from somewhere so deep in her chest that it seemed to come up from the ground, through the iron horse, through her. A great harsh hoarse bellow. Like a crow, Arran had said. A crow with a sore throat. It was. And it was also like a bull, and like a storm, and like the bell of the little chapel on the rise, if the bell had been as big as the hill.
She shouted words. Corin understood them, because she did.
"To the ditch!" she roared. "To the ditch! Every one of you! Down to the ditch, and turn and face them, and don't you dare look back for me, because I'll not be there!"
And the long dark river of her people on the road below looked up.
All of them. At once. Thousands of faces turned up toward the ridge. Toward her. On the iron horse, against the morning sky.
And they went.
Corin felt her watch them go. Felt her eyes go over them, one after another, as they turned off the road and went down toward the long new ditch dug across the valley's mouth, a long way south of the village, zigzagging from hill to hill, with its wall of earth and its stakes. Felt her looking at every face. Trying to keep every one.
Ours, she thought. Ours. Ours. Ours.
And then she turned the horse again. Away from them. Toward the north. Up the valley. Toward the hills.
Toward the place in the middle where she was going to go and stand, alone, and do the thing.
Corin did not know what the thing was.
She did.
And she was not afraid. That was the last thing he felt, before she kicked the iron horse into a run and the world began to rush past. She was not afraid at all.
She was furious.
