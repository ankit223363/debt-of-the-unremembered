Chapter 74: The Iron Horse, Part 2 of 5: The Sun Road
The Sun Road
They came out of the Fold Hills an hour after sunrise, all at once, the way they had gone in.
One moment they were in a trough, dark and folded and close, with the walls going up on either side and the strip of sky overhead. Then the trough turned, and turned again, and the walls fell away on the left, lower and lower, until they were only a low ridge of folded glass, and then not even that. And the sky opened out. Huge. White-blue, already shining with heat. And the Glass went away flat in front of them, the old long slow swells of the open Glass, every color of smoke in the morning light, out to the edge of the world.
And across it, straight as a rule, ran the Road.
Corin saw it at once. Anyone would have. It lay across the Glass from left to right, a little below them, where the last of the hills sloped down. A band. A long straight band of paler glass, running away to the north as far as he could see, and away to the south behind the hills, the way they had come, the way the Trench had been.
It was not black. That was what made it stand out. All the rest of the Glass was dark. Smoke-colored. Brown and green and purple-gray. But this band was pale. Almost white. A soft milky gray-gold, like the color of the sky just before dawn, laid down across the dark floor of the world in one dead-straight line.
Wide. As wide as a city street. Wider. Wide enough for a whole column of men to march along it, side by side, with room to spare.
And straight. That was the strangest thing. Nothing on the Glass was straight. The swells rolled, the hills folded, the Trench zigzagged. But this went on in one line, without a bend, without a wobble, as if someone had laid a great ruler across the Glass and drawn along it with a stick of chalk.
North. Toward the middle.
"The Sun Road," said Arran.
He said it quietly. Not the way he had said it in the story, yesterday morning, with a grin. He stood at the bottom of the last slope with his long arms hanging at his sides and looked at it.
"Sola's road," said Kestrel. "The way her charge went."
"Her charge?" said Lyra.
"At the end," said Kestrel. "When she rode out of the Eye. Toward the gray men. With the light in her." She touched the gray line on her face. "This is where the light went first. Before it went everywhere. It went out in front of her in a straight line, the way she was riding. And it burned the road into the Glass. And everything else came after."
They went down to it.
The closer Corin came, the stranger it was. The pale glass of the Road was not smooth, like the dark glass on either side of it. It was rippled. Very finely. Long low ripples, running along the length of it, the way sand ripples on a beach where the water has run back. All going the same way. North to south. As if something had poured along it once, very fast, like water down a gutter, and left its marks.
And it hummed.
He felt it before he heard it. Through the crutch first. Then through his good foot, and up his leg. The same deep low hum that he had felt under the Glass since the first step. The hum of the Glass itself. But louder here. Much louder. A thick steady throb, coming up out of the pale band like heat comes up out of a hearthstone.
And when he stopped at the edge of the Road, at the line where the dark glass ended and the pale began, the hum came up into his teeth.
He stood there with the crutch an inch from the pale glass and did not put it down.
The Ashborn had stopped too. All of them. In a line along the edge. Not one of them had stepped onto it.
"We do not walk on it," said Kestrel. "Never. We walk beside it." She looked along the dark glass at the edge of the Road, going away north. "It goes where we are going. Straight to the Eye. It is the fastest way. But we do not set foot on it."
"Why?" said Vespera.
Kestrel was quiet for a moment.
"Because it is hers," she said. "And she is still on it."
"What does that mean?" said Lyra.
Kestrel was quiet for a long moment, looking along the pale band.
"When Old Hask's grandmother was young," she said, "a man of ours walked on it. A young man. Proud. He said the old women were afraid of nothing, and he would show them. And he stepped onto the Road, here, at this end, where it comes out of the hills." She touched the gray line on her face. "And he walked. North. Along it. Toward the Eye. His friends called to him to come back. He did not turn. He did not answer. He walked faster. And then he ran."
"Where to?"
"We do not know," said Kestrel. "His friends followed him beside the Road, on the dark glass, as far as they dared. Two days. He did not stop to sleep. He did not stop to drink. He ran along the Road in the day, in the heat, and in the night, in the cold, and he did not stop. And on the second day they lost sight of him, ahead, in the shaking air. Still running." She was quiet a moment. "The old women said she took him. That the Road is still her charge, and anyone who steps onto it becomes part of the charge. And runs with her. Until there is nothing left of them to run."
No one said anything.
"It is a story," said Kestrel. "Perhaps he was only mad. Perhaps he was only proud, and too ashamed to turn round." She looked down at the pale glass, an inch from the toes of her felted boots. "But we do not walk on it."
Corin looked at the Road. At the fine pale ripples going away north, all in the same direction, as if something had poured along it very fast.
The hum came up through the crutch into his hand.
And for one moment, very faint, under the hum, he thought he felt something else. A pull. Gentle. Like a hand on his sleeve. This way. Come on. This way.
He took a step back from the edge.
The pull went.
He did not tell anyone.
No one said anything to that.
Tansy had not stopped. She had walked straight to the edge and was standing on the dark side of the line with her toes almost touching the pale. Looking down at it.
"I walk on it sometimes," she said. "When he's not looking. It tickles your feet."
"Don't," said Kestrel.
Tansy looked at her. Then she shrugged, and stepped back.
And the man, beside Corin, did not move at all. He stood at the edge of the Road on Corin's left, with his eyes on the pale band going away north. And his face, for the first time since Corin had seen him, was not empty.
Corin could not have said what was in it. Not quite. It was very small. A tightness round the eyes. A stillness that was not the same as his usual stillness. The look, Corin thought, of a man standing at the edge of a river he once nearly drowned in.
Then it was gone. And the face was empty again.
They walked beside the Road, on the dark glass, going north. The Ashborn in a line along the edge. No one stepped onto it. Corin kept a crutch's length away from it, without being told.
But he could feel it. All the time. The hum, coming through the glass from the pale band on his right, up through the crutch and his foot, into his bones. And something else, under the hum. Something he could not name. A kind of pull. Not like the pull of Ash, in a jar, drawing you in. Something wider. Like the pull of a strong current at the edge of a river, that you can feel against your legs even standing in the shallows.
And ahead of them, a long way off, at the edge of sight, there was a shape.
Standing on the Road.
In the middle of the pale band, alone, in the shaking morning air. A dark shape, rust-red against the white-gold glass. Too big to be a man. Too still to be anything alive.
And it was facing them.
The Iron Horse
It was a horse.
Corin saw that when they were still a long way off. The shape of it came clear out of the shaking air as they walked toward it along the edge of the Road. The long neck. The head. The four legs. The great round barrel of the body. A horse, standing in the middle of the pale band of the Sun Road, facing south. Facing them.
But it was too big.
That was the next thing. As they came closer, it did not get closer the way a horse should. It got bigger. And bigger. Until Corin, walking on his crutch at the edge of the Road with the man beside him, understood that the horse would have been twice the height of any horse he had ever seen. That its back was higher than Oren's head. That a man standing by its shoulder would have to reach up to touch its neck.
And it was not alive. It had never been alive.
It was iron.
They stopped at the edge of the Road, opposite it. Close. A stone's throw. And looked.
It was rust-red. All of it. The red of old blood, of autumn leaves, of the pike heads in the Trench. Sixty years of rust lay over it like a skin, thick and rough and flaking, so that it looked less like metal than like the bark of some enormous red tree. And under the rust Corin could see the shape of how it had been made. Plates. Great curved iron plates, riveted together over a frame, the way the hull of a ship is riveted. The rivets stood out all over it in rows. Big round knobs, as big as his thumb, swollen with rust. Like warts. Like the knuckles of an old hand.
It stood with one foreleg lifted.
The right one. Bent at the knee, the great iron hoof raised a little off the glass, as if it had been about to take a step forward when everything stopped. The other three legs were planted. And round each of the three planted hooves, the glass was cracked. Starred. Long white cracks running out from under each hoof in every direction, like the cracks in ice when you stamp on it. As if the horse had come down on the glass when it was still soft, and been so heavy that the glass had broken under it as it set.
Its head was up. Its neck arched. Iron plates along the crest of the neck, overlapping like the scales of a fish, in a long curved ridge where a mane would be. The head was long, and narrow, and not quite a horse's head. Too many edges. Too many plates. The eyes were two round holes, dark, empty, with nothing behind them. The mouth was a slot. Open a little.
It looked, Corin thought, like a horse a blacksmith might make, if he had only ever heard of horses and never seen one. And had been told they were terrible.
And its belly was open.
On the side facing them, the great curved plates of the barrel had been torn away. Or had fallen. Or had never been put back. There was a gap there, as big as a door, under the place where a saddle would be. And through it, Corin could see inside.
Gears.
Wheel behind wheel. More than he could feel. Wheels with teeth, big and small, packed in together, one against another, the big ones as big as cartwheels and the small ones as small as coins. Rods. Springs, coiled. Chains. All of it rust-red. All of it still. Every tooth of every wheel caught against the tooth of the next, frozen in the middle of turning. Like the inside of a clock the size of a house, stopped.
It did not tick. Nothing in it moved. It had not moved for sixty years.
But Corin, looking at all those dead gears, thought of the Erasers. Of the ticking. Of the iron hand at Last Thorn. And thought: someone made this, too. Someone made this to walk, and carry, and fight. Before anyone made the Erasers.
On its back there was a frame.
Not a saddle. Not leather. A frame. Iron, like the rest. A sort of high seat, with a back and two sides and two great curved iron arms coming round in front, where a rider would sit. Like a chair. Like a throne. Bolted to the horse's back. And empty.
There was no one in it.
But on the front of it, where a saddle would have a horn, there was a knob of iron. And on the knob, something was hanging.
A helmet.
Iron, like the rest. Round, and dented. Badly dented, on one side, as if it had been struck by something very heavy and very hard. Rust-red. With a short brim at the front, and a guard coming down at the back over the neck.
And from the top of it, hanging down over the iron horse's shoulder almost to its knee, a braid.
Horsehair. Long. Very long. Plaited thick and tight, the way a woman plaits her hair for battle so it will not get in her eyes. Gray. Iron-gray. Not rust. The only thing on the whole great red beast that was not rust-red. It hung there, perfectly still in the still hot air, from the top of the dented helmet. As if it had been left there a moment ago. As if whoever had worn it had only taken it off to wipe her brow, and hung it on the saddle, and walked away for a moment, and would be back.
No one said anything for a long time.
Corin felt the man, beside him, go very still. Stiller than usual. As if even his breathing had stopped.
And Corin looked down at the glass around the horse's planted hooves. At the white cracks, starring out.
And beyond them, on the pale ripples of the Sun Road, running back from the horse's hooves the way it had come, he saw marks. Long, deep, straight marks. Gouged into the pale glass. Four of them, side by side. Running back north, toward the middle, as far as he could see, straight as the Road.
The marks of iron hooves.
Galloping.
Coming south. Out of the Eye. Toward the gray men. With a rider on its back, and the sun coming out of her.
And then it had stopped here. With one hoof lifted. And the rider had gone.
And the horse had waited.
Hers
Oren did not say anything for a long time.
He had gone forward, a little, from the others. To the very edge of the dark glass, where it met the pale. He stood there with his toes an inch from the line, the way Tansy had stood. And looked up at the iron horse.
At the lifted hoof. At the arched neck. At the empty frame on its back. At the dented helmet on the saddle knob, with its long iron-gray braid hanging down over the rust-red shoulder.
Corin watched his face.
It did not move. That was the thing about Oren's face, when something mattered. It did not move at all. The scar from his temple to his mouth stayed where it was. His jaw stayed set. His gray eyes stayed on the helmet. Only his hands moved. They came up, slowly, from his sides, and found each other in front of him, and held on. The way a man holds his own hands when he is standing at a graveside and does not know what else to do with them.
The sun was getting higher. Corin could feel it on the back of his neck. The Glass under his crutch was going from warm to hot. Somewhere far behind them, back past the Fold Hills, back at the Trench, three iron men were waking up.
No one hurried Oren.
At last he said one word.
"Hers."
He said it very low. Not to anyone. To the horse, perhaps. Or the helmet. Or the braid.
Corin had heard the word Him in that voice, from the top of a swell, two mornings ago. The voice a man uses for the name of somebody he has fought against for a long time. This was not that voice.
This was the voice a man uses for the name of somebody he has never met, and has been carrying all his life anyway.
"Hers," said Oren again.
Kestrel came and stood beside him. Not too close. On the dark glass, at the edge.
"Yes," she said.
"You knew it was here."
"Yes."
"You didn't say."
"We did not know if we would pass it," said Kestrel. "We do not walk this way. Not on the Road. Not near the horse." She looked up at it. "We have only seen it from far off. From the hills. The old ones told us where it stands. And told us not to go near."
"Why?"
Kestrel did not answer at once. She was looking at the helmet.
"Because she is in it," she said.
Oren turned his head and looked at her.
"Everything else she was," said Kestrel slowly, "went into the Glass. When she made the sun. Her body. Her bones. Her breath. All of it went into the light, and the light went into the ground, and the ground became Glass, and the Glass holds it. The way it holds the soldiers. The way it holds the lost ones." She touched the gray line on her face. "But she was wearing that, when the light came out of her. On her head. Iron. And iron is not Glass."
"So?"
"So the iron kept something," said Kestrel. "Kept it out of the Glass. When the rest of her went into the light, the part of her that was against the iron stayed in the iron. Her Ash. The last of it. What she was thinking, at the end. What she saw." She was quiet a moment. "That is what the old ones say. That her Ash is in that helmet. Whole. Not drifting, like the lost ones. Not held, like the soldiers. Whole. And wild. Sixty years, alone, in a little round box of iron. With nothing to breathe it. With nobody to carry it." Her voice went very low. "With all of that light still in it."
No one said anything.
"We do not go near it," said Kestrel. "Not because we are afraid of the iron. Because we are afraid of her. An Ash like that, kept so long, so whole. If you touched it, you would not walk it. It would walk you." She looked at Corin. Just for a moment. "The old ones say. We do not know. Nobody has tried."
Oren looked back at the helmet.
"My great-grandfather's sister," he said. Very quietly. "Aldric's sister. Sola Vance. General." He said the words the way Lyra had read them off the cross at Haverstock, one at a time. "She gave her words."
"Yes," said Kestrel.
Oren took a step forward.
Onto the pale glass of the Road.
Corin saw Kestrel's hand come up, fast, toward his arm. And stop. And go down again.
Oren did not seem to notice. He stood on the Sun Road, on the fine pale ripples, with the hum coming up through his boots, and Corin saw him feel it. Saw his shoulders go back, and his chin come up. The way a soldier stands when a general rides past.
He walked across the Road to the horse.
No one stopped him. No one followed. They stood in a line along the edge of the dark glass and watched him go. A big gray-bearded man in a torn coat, walking slowly across a band of pale glass toward a great red iron horse with one hoof lifted.
He stopped at its shoulder.
He had to look up. The horse's shoulder was above his head. The empty frame of the saddle was far above that. And hanging from the saddle knob, the helmet, and the long gray braid, coming down over the red iron almost to the horse's knee.
Within his reach.
Oren lifted his hand.
Corin's breath stopped.
But Oren did not touch the helmet. He touched the braid. Just the end of it. The very tip, where it hung down lowest, over the iron knee. He put out one big scarred finger and laid it against the end of the gray horsehair braid. Gently. The way you touch the hair of someone sleeping.
Nothing happened.
The braid was only horsehair. It was not iron. It was not her.
Oren held his finger there for a long moment.
Then he took it away. And stood back. And took off his knitted cap, and held it against his chest, in both hands, the way he had in the Trench.
And stood there, bareheaded, on the Sun Road, in front of his great-grandfather's sister's horse, in the rising heat, for as long as it would take to say every name on the north side of the cross at Haverstock.
Corin did not know how he knew it was that long. But he knew.
