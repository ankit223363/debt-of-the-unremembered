Chapter 74: The Iron Horse, Part 3 of 5: The Man at the Road
The Man at the Road
When Oren came back across the pale glass, the man was standing up.
Corin had not seen him rise. None of them had. He had been sitting on the dark glass a little behind them all, cross-legged, with Tansy beside him under her coat. And now he was on his feet. At the very edge of the Road. Where the dark ended and the pale began.
Tansy was on her feet too, beside him, holding his sleeve.
"He just got up," she said. Her voice was high. "He just got up. I didn't do anything. He just got up and came."
The man did not look at her. He did not look at any of them. He was looking across the Road. At the horse. At the helmet on the saddle knob with its long gray braid.
Not through it. At it.
Corin saw his face. And it was not empty.
It was the face of a man standing at the edge of deep water, looking down at something lying at the bottom. Something he had dropped there, a long time ago. Something he could see, quite clearly, through the water, and could not reach.
His hands were moving. At his sides. The long thin fingers opening and closing. Opening and closing. Slowly. As if they were trying to remember how to hold something.
"What's he doing?" said Oren. Low. He had stopped on the pale glass, a few steps from the edge, with his cap still in his hand.
"I don't know," said Tansy. "I don't know. He's never done this. He's never—" She tugged at the sleeve. "Come on. Come and sit down. It's hot. Come and sit down with me."
The man did not move.
He stood at the edge of the Sun Road, at the line, with his toes an inch from the pale glass. And Corin, watching, saw his foot lift. The left one. The one that turned out. Lift off the dark glass, a little, as if to step forward. Onto the Road. Toward the horse.
And hang there.
And come down again. On the dark side.
He did not step onto the Road.
He stood there, with his foot back where it had been, and his fingers opening and closing at his sides, and his pale eyes on the helmet. And Corin saw his mouth move. Inside the wild beard. Saw the lips part, and shape something.
No sound came out.
The lips closed again.
"Did he—" said Lyra. Behind Corin. Very quiet.
"No," said Tansy. Fast. Too fast. "No. He doesn't. He never does."
But she was staring up at his face with her own face gone white.
The man's lips moved again. The same shape. Corin watched them. Two movements. A small one. And a longer one, a little open.
He did not know what it was. It could have been anything. A word in a language none of them knew. A name. A breath that had forgotten how to be a word.
Or nothing.
The man's hands stopped. Opening and closing. Stopped, half open, at his sides.
And then, very slowly, he turned his head. Away from the horse. Away from the helmet. And looked at Corin.
At him. The way he had in the cave.
Corin felt it go into him like the cold of the Glass at night. Into his chest. Into the place behind his breastbone that had ached since the wave.
The man looked at him for a long moment.
Then he looked back at the helmet.
Then at Corin again.
As if he were asking something. As if he wanted Corin to understand something, and did not have any way to say it, and was trying anyway, with his eyes, the only way he had.
Corin did not understand.
He wanted to. He wanted to so badly that his hands clenched on the crutch. But he did not.
"I don't know," he said. To the man. Out loud. "I'm sorry. I don't know what you mean."
The man looked at him a moment more.
Then his eyes went away. Back to the horse. And through it. Out, past it, to the north. To nothing. The empty look came back into his face, slowly, like water closing over a stone.
He let Tansy pull him by the sleeve.
He went with her, back across the dark glass, without looking at any of them. And sat down again where he had been, cross-legged, in the full sun. Facing the horse.
Tansy sat down beside him, and held his arm in both her hands, and did not talk. Not a word. For once.
Corin stood at the edge of the Road on his crutch for a long time after, looking at the place where the man's foot had lifted, and come down again, and not gone over.
"He knew her," said Lyra, beside him. Very low.
"He can't have. He's not old enough."
"I know." She was quiet. "He knew something."
Corin did not answer.
Behind them, Kestrel said, in the round speech, something short and sharp to the others. And the Ashborn began to put up the shades. With their backs to the horse. And their backs, Corin noticed, to the man as well.
Why
They put the shades up beside the Road, on the dark glass, a stone's throw from the horse. There was nowhere else. The sun was a hand and a half up and already the Glass was too hot to sit on without felt, and there was no trough here, no hollow. Only the flat shining floor, and the pale band of the Road, and the great red horse standing in it with its hoof lifted.
The Ashborn put the poles up with their backs to it. Corin saw them do it. Every one of them, as they worked, kept the horse behind them. As if it were a fire they did not want to look at.
And under the middle shade, in the hot gold light through the hide, they argued.
Corin started it. He had not meant to. He had meant to lie down and close his eyes. But he lay down on the felt and closed his eyes, and the helmet was there, behind his eyelids, hanging on the saddle knob with its long gray braid. And he opened them again.
"The door," he said.
The others looked at him.
"Tansy's door," said Corin. "In the middle. The hump of iron, with the door in it. It's Sola's bunker. It has to be." He sat up. "Malakor's journal is in there. In an iron box. Under her map table. That's what he said. That's what's in the Ledger. The Glass. Sola's bunker. Iron box under her map table." He looked round at them. "That's all he said. He didn't say how to get in."
No one answered.
"He knocks," said Corin. He nodded out through the open end of the shade, at the man, who was sitting cross-legged on the dark glass a little way off, alone, in the full sun, with his face toward the horse. Tansy sat beside him, under a fold of her outside coat held up like a tent. "Every full moon, Tansy says. For two winters. Longer, maybe. He goes to the door and he knocks. And nobody answers."
"So it's shut," said Oren.
"It's shut. Locked, or barred, or—something." Corin did not know. "And Malakor's gone quiet. He said count, boy at the first step and nothing since. If he knew how to get in, he's not telling. And if we get to the door and can't open it—"
"Then we've walked across the Glass for nothing," said Vespera. Flatly. "With three iron men behind us."
"Yes."
No one said anything for a moment.
"She knew," said Corin.
He did not look at the horse. He did not need to. He could feel it, out there, beyond the hide. The helmet on the saddle knob.
"Sola," he said. "It was her bunker. Her place. She'd know how to get in. She'd know what's waiting in there." He swallowed. "And she's in that helmet. Kestrel says. Whole."
The shade went very quiet.
"No," said Vespera.
"Vespera—"
"No." She sat up, small and bent under the low hide, with her blue hands flat on her knees. "You heard her." She jerked her head at Kestrel. "It would walk you. An Ash sixty years old, kept whole in a box, with a sun still in it. The woman who made all this." She flung out one hand at the Glass beyond the shade. "You'd go into that and you would not come out. You'd come out as her. Or as nothing. Or you wouldn't come out at all."
"Nothing can be taken on the Glass," said Corin. "Kestrel says. So it can't take me."
"Nothing can be taken by the Glass," said Vespera. "That helmet is iron. Not Glass. That's the whole point. That's why she's still in it. And if she's still in it, she can still do what Ash does." Her voice was very sharp. "Walking is not taking, harvester. You know that better than anyone. When you walk a jar, you don't take it. It comes into you. You let it in. And if it's big enough, and wild enough, it doesn't leave."
Corin did not answer that.
Because she was right. He knew she was right. He had walked a thousand jars. He knew what it was to let something in. He knew what it was to feel it settle, and not quite go.
He had Vivienne in him still. All of her. From the dome.
"Then it should be me," said Oren.
Everybody looked at him.
He was sitting with his back against a pole, his knees up, his big scarred hands hanging between them. He had not said anything since the horse. His face was quite calm.
"She's my blood," said Oren. "Aldric's sister. If anyone's to go into her, it should be one of hers." He looked at Corin. "Not you, lad. You've got enough in you that isn't yours."
"You can't walk it," said Corin.
"I can try."
"You can't. You don't know how. It isn't a thing you can try." Corin held up his gray hand. "This is what it takes. Years of it. Learning. Burning. You'd put your hand on that helmet and nothing would happen. Or something would happen and you wouldn't know what to do with it."
Oren looked at the gray hand for a long time.
"Then I hold the rope," he said.
Corin did not understand.
"Linus's walking page," said Oren. "The one in your book. Lyra read it out at Last Thorn, before you walked Vespera's jar. Put it on the brow. Let it come up like water. Don't drink." He held up one thick finger. "And at the bottom. Small. You read that bit twice, I remember, because you didn't like it. If the stone was one of a family's, let one of the blood hold the walker's hand. So the stone knows who has come. So the walker has something to hold." His jaw moved. "I'm the blood. I'll hold your hand. I'll hold the rope. And if she tries to keep you, I'll pull you out."
"It's madness," said Vespera. "All of it. Madness."
"Yes," said Lyra.
She had not said anything all this time. She was sitting at the open end of the shade with her knees up, looking out at the horse in the white glare. She did not turn round.
"It's madness," she said. "Vespera's right. It could kill him. Or worse." A pause. "And if we get to that door and we can't open it, we're all dead anyway. With iron behind us and no water and no way back."
She turned round then. And looked at them. At Vespera, and Oren, and Kestrel, and last at Corin.
"So if we're going to do it," said Lyra, "we do it properly."
Properly
They did it properly.
It took a long time. Longer than Corin would have thought. And all the time the sun climbed, and the heat came up off the Glass, and somewhere far behind them the ticking had begun again. Kestrel went out twice to the top of the nearest swell and put her palm on the hot glass, and came back, and said nothing. But Corin saw her face.
Lyra read the page first.
She sat cross-legged under the middle shade with Ledger VIII open on her knees and read it out loud. Linus's walking page, that Corin had copied into the green book in his square black capitals. All of it. Slowly. Twice. The second time, Corin said the words with her, under his breath, the way Oren had said the names after her at the cross.
PUT IT ON THE BROW. NOT THE HAND. THE BROW.
LET IT COME UP LIKE WATER. DO NOT DRINK.
DO NOT REACH. DO NOT PULL. LET IT RISE TO YOU.
IF IT IS DEEP, DO NOT GO DOWN AFTER IT. WAIT.
HAVE SOMEONE CALL YOUR NAME. THE NAME YOU ARE CALLED BY. NOT THE NAME ON YOUR PAPERS. THE NAME SOMEONE WHO LOVES YOU USES.
IF THE STONE WAS ONE OF A FAMILY'S, LET ONE OF THE BLOOD HOLD THE WALKER'S HAND. SO THE STONE KNOWS WHO HAS COME. SO THE WALKER HAS SOMETHING TO HOLD.
IF IT WILL NOT LET YOU GO, LET GO OF IT. FIRST. YOU MUST LET GO FIRST.
Lyra closed the book.
No one said anything.
"Right," said Oren. "Right. Who goes where."
They worked it out. Under the shade, in the heat, in low voices, the way soldiers work out who stands where before a fight.
Corin would lie down. Out in the open, beside the horse, on felt, because the helmet would not come down to him and he could not go up to it. He would lie on his back on the Glass at the horse's lifted forefoot, with his head on a rolled pack. And the helmet would be on his brow.
"I'll get it down," said Oren.
"You won't touch it," said Kestrel. Sharply.
"I'll touch it with my coat. With felt. Not my hands." Oren looked at her. "I'm not daft."
Oren would hold his hand. His left hand. The gray one. The one of the blood, holding the walker's hand. Skin to skin. Oren would take his own glove off for it.
Lyra would sit at his head. Close. Where he could hear her. And call him.
"How will you know when?" said Vespera.
"When I know," said Lyra.
Vespera looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, short.
Vespera herself would kneel at his other side. With her needles. All of them, laid out on a cloth on the felt, longest to finest, and her inks, and her little gold-rimmed pot.
"What for?" said Corin.
Vespera did not look at him. She was unrolling the cloth. "For if something goes wrong."
"What can needles do?"
"Ink is giving," said Vespera. "You remember. The only magic I know that works the wrong way round. If you come out of there and you've lost something, I can put a word on you. Your name. Where you are. Who you are." She laid the finest needle at the end of the row. "It won't put it back. But it'll be there. On your skin. Where you can see it."
Corin looked at the needles.
"And if I come out as her?" he said.
Vespera's hands stopped.
"Then I'll put your name on you anyway," she said. "And she can read it, every morning, and think about whose skin she's in."
The Ashborn would stand round them. Not close. In a ring, at a distance, the way they had stood round the rite at Last Thorn, facing in. Kestrel, Arran, Brin, Oda, Sef, Marrit. With their spears. Not against the helmet. Spears could do nothing against Ash. But against anything else that might come, out of the white glare, while Corin lay on his back with his eyes shut.
"And him?" said Arran. He nodded out through the open end of the shade. At the man, sitting alone in the sun with Tansy under her coat-tent beside him.
No one knew what to say.
"He stays where he is," said Kestrel at last. "Tansy keeps him there."
Tansy, hearing her name, put her head out from under the coat.
"He won't do anything," she said. "He never does anything." She looked at the man. Then at the horse. Then back at the man. And for the first time since Corin had met her, she looked uncertain. "Only he's looking at it. He's been looking at it since we got here. He never looks at anything that long."
Corin looked at the man. He was. Sitting cross-legged in the white sun, with his long thin hands on his knees, and his pale eyes on the iron horse. On the helmet. On the braid.
Not through it. At it.
The way he had looked at Corin, in the cave.
"Keep him there," said Kestrel to Tansy. Quietly.
"I'll try," said Tansy. And put her small hand on the man's arm, and held on.
Then they went out into the glare.
Oren went first. He walked across the pale glass of the Road to the horse, with his coat off and bundled in his arms. And reached up, on tiptoe, at the horse's shoulder, and lifted the helmet off the saddle knob through the thick wool of the coat, the way a man lifts a pot off the fire with a cloth. Carefully. Very carefully. Holding it away from himself, wrapped, with the long gray braid hanging down over his arm.
He did not breathe, Corin thought, all the way back.
Arran and Sef spread the felt at the horse's lifted forefoot. Double. Under the great raised iron hoof, which hung over the place where Corin's head would be like the hoof of a horse rearing over a fallen rider.
Corin lay down.
The felt was hot under his back already, even doubled. The sun was white. The hide shade they had brought out was held over him on two poles by Brin and Oda, and the light through it was gold. Above him, beyond the edge of the shade, the iron hoof. Rust-red. Enormous. The size of a cartwheel. With the cracks starring out under its brothers on the glass beside his head.
Lyra sat down at his head. He felt her knees against the top of his skull, through the rolled pack.
Vespera knelt at his right side. He heard the small clink of her needles on the cloth.
Oren knelt at his left. With the bundled coat in his arms, and the helmet in it. He set it down on the felt beside Corin's shoulder, still wrapped. And pulled off his own right glove with his teeth.
And took Corin's left hand.
Corin felt the big rough warm fingers close round his gray ones. Round the black fingertips that felt nothing. Hard. Holding on.
"Ready, lad?" said Oren.
Corin looked up at the iron hoof, hanging over him in the white sky.
"No," he said.
"Good," said Oren. "Nobody ever is."
Before
But before Oren unwrapped the helmet, Corin said, "Wait."
They all looked at him. Oren with the bundled coat in his hands. Vespera with her needles. Lyra, at his head, upside down above him against the gold of the shade.
"The book," said Corin. "Give me the book."
Lyra found it. In his pack, which was by her knee. She put it into his right hand, the green book, Ledger VIII, with the worn gold tree on the cover. And the pencil stub.
He held the book up over his face, lying on his back. It was awkward. His arm shook a little. But he found the page. THURSDAY. ON THE GLASS. SHE SLEPT. The heron. The Trench. The sun with its eyes shut. HE WAVED AT ME.
And he turned to a new page, and wrote. Lying on his back on the hot felt under the iron hoof, with Oren holding his other hand, holding the book up in the air with the pencil scraping. Square black capitals. Leaning.
FRIDAY. THE SUN ROAD. THE IRON HORSE. HERS.
He stopped. Thought.
I AM GOING INTO SOLA'S HELMET. TO FIND HOW TO OPEN THE DOOR.
OREN HOLDS MY HAND. HE IS THE BLOOD.
LYRA CALLS ME. SHE IS THE VOICE.
VESPERA HAS HER NEEDLES.
He stopped again. His arm was trembling now. He lowered the book onto his chest and rested it there, and wrote the last lines with the book lying on him, not looking, by feel, the way he had written at Last Thorn in the dark.
IF I COME BACK WRONG, READ ME THIS.
MY NAME IS CORIN.
I LOVE THESE PEOPLE.
He stopped.
He had not known he was going to write that. It had come out of the pencil on its own. He looked down along his own chest at it, upside down, and read it, and felt his face go hot.
He did not cross it out.
He closed the book. And held it up, over his head, backward, for Lyra.
She took it.
He heard her open it. Heard the pages turn. Heard her stop. And not say anything, for a long moment.
Then he heard her close it again. Very carefully. The way you close a door on a sleeping child.
"All right," said Lyra. Her voice was not quite steady. "All right. I've got it."
"Read it to me," said Corin. "If."
"I will."
"All of it. Even if I don't know what the words are. Keep reading."
"I will."
He lay there for a moment longer.
Far off, under the felt, under the glass, he could feel the ticking now. Faint. Steady. Coming up through his back. Kestrel had been to the top of the swell again while he wrote. He had seen her come back, and crouch at the edge of the ring of Ashborn, and look at Oren, and hold up one hand. All the fingers. Then close it, and open two.
He could not feel what it meant. But he saw Oren's face when he saw it. And Oren's jaw had gone tight.
They were in the Fold Hills. The iron men. Coming through the folds and the troughs, slowly, without arrows, without a short way. Coming over the top of things, Tansy had said. Straight for him. Like a compass.
They did not have long.
"One thing," he said.
Lyra bent lower. Her short dark hair fell forward over her forehead.
"What?"
"For today. You've not given me one yet. For today." He swallowed. His throat was very dry. "In case."
She did not say don't say in case. He was grateful for that. She was quiet for a moment, upside down above him, so close that for a moment there was nothing in the world but her face and the gold light behind it.
"The first time I saw you," she said, "I had a sword at your throat."
He looked up at her.
"In the dark," said Lyra. "In a room full of drawers. In the city, at the start of everything. I had my hood up and a red book against my chest and the point of my sword right here." She touched two fingers to the hollow of his throat, very lightly. "And you didn't beg. You didn't call for anyone. You stood there with my sword at your throat and told me about a scribe called Pell, who burned a whole week of himself to make one flash of light and ran four steps and died, so nobody would ever take what was in that drawer." Her mouth moved. "And then you said, and now you've taken it. As if you were angry with me. For him. For a man you'd never met."
He did not remember.
"My sword shook," said Lyra. "I'd never had it shake before. And I thought, who is this man, who gets angry for the dead. And I took the sword away." Her eyes were very bright. "That's your one thing. For today."
He lay there and looked up at her.
"I don't remember," he said.
"I know."
"But I'll keep it."
"I know." She straightened up. The gold light came in again. "Now go and find the door."
