Chapter 74: The Iron Horse, Part 1 of 5: Leaving the Cave
Leaving the Cave
Corin woke to the sound of a girl talking.
For a moment, lying on his back on the curved green floor with his arm over his eyes, he did not know where he was. Only that it was not dark, quite, and not light, and that the air was cold on his face and warm under him, and that somebody very close by was saying, "—and you can't wear rags, she said, you have to wear the felt, it's better, so I've got to wear the felt, but I don't know how you tie it, so you'll have to show me, because you don't talk but you can still show, you can show things with your hands—"
Then it came back. All of it. The Fold Hills. The arrows. The round hole and the green bubble. The girl in three coats.
The man.
He took his arm off his eyes.
The bubble was gray now, not green. The starlight through the hole had gone, and the first thin light of the day was coming instead, very faint, very cold. Everyone was moving. Packing. Rolling felt. Somebody was coughing. Oren was standing bent under the low curve of the roof with his hands in the small of his back, groaning.
And at the hole, where he had been sitting all night, the man was still there. Sitting, with his back to them, facing out. The way Lyra said she had seen him in the night.
Tansy was kneeling beside him with one of her huge boots in her lap and a strip of gray felt in her hands, trying to wind it round her bandaged foot, and talking.
"Here," said Arran. He knelt down beside her. "Like this. Round the heel first. Then up. Then tie it here, at the top, so it doesn't slide."
Tansy watched his long fingers do it. Then she took the felt off and did it again herself, the same. Exactly the same. Her magpie fingers.
"Like that?"
"Like that."
"Good," said Tansy. She jammed the boot on over the felt. It went more easily now. She stamped it on the floor. "That's better. That's much better. Why didn't anybody tell me about felt before?" She started on the other foot. "Two winters I've been wearing rags. Two winters. And all the time there was felt."
"There's always felt," said Arran. "You just have to know a goat."
They ate standing. Bread. A mouthful of water each. Kestrel went round with the skin, and Corin saw her stop by the man at the hole, and hold the skin out to him. Not to his mouth. Just out. In front of him.
The man did not look at it.
Kestrel held it there for a long moment. Then Tansy reached up and took it from her, and held it to his lips, and tipped it, and he drank. One mouthful. His throat moved. Then Tansy gave it back.
"He forgets," said Tansy. "I told you. You have to put it there."
Kestrel took the skin back. She did not say anything. But Corin saw her face, and it was not afraid this morning. It was something else. Something more like the look she had given the glass soldiers in the Trench.
Then Tansy stood up, in her three coats and her uncle's boots, with her chalk stub in her fist, and looked round at all of them.
"Right," she said. "Everybody ready? You have to stay close. And do what I say. Even if it's silly. Especially if it's silly. The short way's got some silly bits."
"How silly?" said Oren.
"You'll see," said Tansy. "You're quite big. You might not like it."
She went out through the hole, head first, like a rabbit going the wrong way out of its burrow.
And the man got up.
Corin had not seen him stand before. He did it all at once, and without seeming to use his hands. One moment he was sitting cross-legged in the round mouth of the cave, and the next he was standing, bent under the curve of the glass, with no movement in between that Corin could see. Like water. Like a cat. He was taller than Corin had thought. Taller than Corin. Almost as tall as Oren, but thin as a rake, so that his dark stiff coat hung on him the way the coats hung on the glass soldiers.
He went out through the hole after Tansy. Bent double. Without a sound.
The others went after him, one by one.
Corin went last. As always. He got down on his good knee and his hands and crawled out through the round black mouth with Arran pulling his crutch after him, and stood up in the hollow between the three waves in the gray first light, and looked round.
And the man was waiting.
Not for the others. Tansy was already at the far side of the hollow, at the mouth of a trough Corin had not seen in the dark, waving her arms and saying something to Kestrel. The others were going toward her. But the man was standing just outside the cave, by the chalk ring round the hole. Still. Not looking at anything.
And when Corin got the crutch under his arm, and took his first step, the man stepped too.
Beside him. On his left side. The bad side. The side where Lyra usually walked.
Corin stopped.
The man stopped.
Corin looked at him. The man did not look back. He stood there at Corin's left shoulder, a little in front, with his empty hands at his sides and his pale eyes on the far trough where Tansy was waving, and did not move.
Corin took another step.
The man took another step.
Lyra had come out of the hole behind them. Corin heard her stop. He did not look round. He did not need to. He could feel her looking. At the man, walking on Corin's left. In her place.
"It's all right," he said. He did not know who he was saying it to.
He went on across the hollow toward the far trough, on his crutch, with the silent man walking beside him, step for step. And after a moment, he heard Lyra come after them, and fall in on his other side. His right. The good side, where she never walked, because there was nothing there to catch.
She did not say anything.
But her shoulder touched his arm. And stayed there.
In Step
It was Lyra who saw it. A mile out from the cave, perhaps. Corin could not have said how far. The troughs of the Fold Hills did not go in miles. They went in turnings.
Tansy led. She went fast, much faster than Kestrel had gone the night before, scrambling and sliding and hopping in her felted boots, with her too-long coat swinging behind her and her chalk stub in her fist. She did not follow the arrows. She had drawn the arrows. She went the way she knew, which was sometimes the way the arrows pointed and sometimes not at all, and she talked the whole time.
"—this bit's called the Elbow, because it bends like one, and this bit's the Gullet, because it's narrow and you feel like you're being swallowed, and that bit, there, up on the left, see the hole, that's where I found the mirror, it was just lying there, I don't know who left it, the Glass gives you things sometimes if you look—"
The Ashborn came behind her. Then Oren. Then Vespera. Then Corin, on his crutch, with the man on his left and Lyra on his right. And Marrit, very slow, at the back, with Brin.
The light came up as they walked. Gray first. Then a cold pale blue, along the crests overhead, while the troughs stayed dark. Then the first pink.
And Lyra, on his right, said very quietly, "Corin. Look at your feet."
He looked.
His feet were doing what they always did. The crutch went down, pat, on the folded floor. Then his bad leg swung through, stiff at the knee. Then his good leg. Then the crutch again. He had been walking like that for so long now that he did not think about it. Crutch, swing, step. Crutch, swing, step.
"Not yours," said Lyra. "His."
Corin looked at the man's feet.
They were in boots. Old ones. Black, cracked, the soles worn thin. Too big, Corin thought, or the feet too thin. They went down on the folded glass beside his own, steady and quiet. Left. Right. Left.
In step.
Not with the crutch. The crutch made its own time. But with Corin's feet. When Corin's good right foot came down, the man's right foot came down. At the same moment. Exactly. When Corin's stiff left leg swung through, the man's left leg swung through. Not stiff. The man's knees bent; he walked like a man with two good legs. But he took the step at the same moment Corin did. And the same length. As if he were matching himself to a stride he knew.
Corin missed a step. On purpose. He held the crutch a moment longer before he put it down.
The man missed a step too. Held his foot. Put it down when Corin did.
"He's been doing it since the cave," said Lyra. Very low. "I've been watching. Every step. He didn't do it with Tansy. He walked in front of her all night, two winters ago, from the Ridges, and she says he never once kept step with her. He just went where he went." She was quiet. "But he's keeping step with you."
Corin did not answer.
He was looking at the man's left foot. At the way it came down.
The boot turned out. A little. The toe pointing a little out to the side, away from the body, every time it touched the glass. More than the right foot did. Not much. You would not notice it, unless you were looking. Unless you had been looking at it, two mornings ago, at the bottom of a line of small melted footprints in the Glass, going south.
His own left foot did that. Lyra had said so. He had seen it himself, beside the footprints. The left foot turned out more than the right.
The man's did too.
The same angle. Step after step. Left, turned out. Right, straight. Left, turned out.
Corin's mouth was dry.
Lots of people walk like that, he thought. He had thought it two days ago, too. He thought it again now, very firmly. Lots of people. It's only a way of walking. Half the clerks at Intake probably walked like that.
"Corin," said Lyra.
"I see it."
"The footprints—"
"I see it," he said again.
She did not say anything else.
They walked. Crutch, swing, step. And beside him, the man's boots, coming down on the folded floor in the same time, the same length, the left one turned out at the same small angle.
Corin looked up at the man's face.
He was not looking at Corin. He was not looking at anything. His pale eyes were on the trough ahead, on Tansy's swinging coat, on nothing. His wild beard moved a little in the cold air. His face was quite empty.
And his feet kept step with Corin's as if they had done it a thousand times before. As if they had learned the stride a long time ago, walking beside someone smaller, someone whose legs were shorter, and had matched themselves to it, and had never forgotten.
Corin did not know where that thought came from.
He did not like it.
He looked away. At the walls. At the first pink light along the crests. At anything.
And walked on, with the silent man in step beside him, and did not miss another step, because he did not want to see the man miss it too.
The Short Way
Tansy had not lied. The short way had silly bits.
The first one came just after sunrise, when the light was coming down pink and gold into the troughs and the reflections of the sky were blazing in every curving wall. The trough they were in ended. Not in a turning. In a wall. A great black fold of glass, sloping steeply up in front of them, so high that its crest was lost in the glare.
And at the bottom of it, where it met the floor, a slit.
A long low gap, as if the wave had not quite come down to meet the ground when it froze. A crack between the bottom of the fold and the floor of the trough. Wide, wider than a cart. But low. No higher than Corin's knee.
"Under," said Tansy. And lay down flat on her front on the cold glass, and wriggled into it, and was gone.
There was a silence.
"Under," said Oren. He looked at the slit. Then down at himself. "Saints."
"It gets higher," said Tansy's voice, from inside the crack. Muffled. "After a bit. Not much. But a bit."
The man went next. He lay down and went into the slit the way water goes into a drain. Smoothly. All at once. His long thin body seemed to fold itself flat without effort.
Then the Ashborn. Then Vespera, small enough that it was easy, grumbling about her knees. Then Lyra, with her sword pushed in ahead of her.
Then Corin.
He lay down on his front on the glass. It was very cold. The crack was a black line in front of his face, low and wide. He could see nothing in it but the soles of Lyra's boots, going away, and beyond them a gray glimmer, very far.
He pushed the crutch in ahead of him. And then himself.
It was the worst thing he had done since Last Thorn. Worse than the slope out of the Trench. He could not crawl on his knees; the stiff one would not bend. So he went on his belly and his elbows and his good knee, dragging the bad leg behind him like a dead thing, and the roof of the crack pressed down on his back, cold and smooth and heavy, and the floor pressed up on his chest, and there was no room. No room to lift his head. No room to turn. He could feel the weight of the whole fold of glass above him, the whole frozen wave, a mountain of it, resting on nothing but the air in this crack.
He did not think about that.
He thought about Lyra's boots, in front of him. Left. Right. Going on.
Behind him, he could hear Oren swearing. Steadily. Without stopping. In a low furious mutter that went on and on, all the way through. Corin had never heard so many words for the Saints.
And then the roof lifted. A little. Then more. And there was gray light, and then gold, and the crack opened out, and he was crawling up a slope into the morning, into a new trough on the far side of the fold, with Lyra's hand reaching back for his.
Oren came out last, very red in the face, with his coat torn at the shoulder.
"Silly," he said to Tansy. "Silly, she says."
"I said you might not like it," said Tansy.
The second silly bit was a crack too, but a tall one. A split in a wave, running up from floor to crest like the gap between two curtains, so narrow that they had to go through it sideways, one at a time, with their packs held over their heads and the cold glass pressing on their chests and their backs at the same time. Brin nearly stuck. Arran and Oda pulled him through from the far side by his good arm while he groaned.
The third was a ridge. A sharp thin crest between two troughs, like the ridge of a roof, that they had to walk along for a long way. One at a time. With the troughs falling away steeply on either side, and the glass of the ridge no wider than a plank, and the morning sun beginning to strike up off it hot and white.
Tansy ran along it. Actually ran, in her uncle's boots, with her arms out for balance and her coats flapping, like a girl running along the top of a wall.
The man walked along it the way he walked everywhere. Without hurry. Without looking down. As if it were a road.
Corin could not walk it.
He stood at the end of it, on his crutch, and looked at the narrow white ridge going away in the sun, and the drops on either side, and knew that he could not. Not with the crutch. Not with the knee. One slip and the crutch would go, and he would go after it.
He did not know what to do.
And then the man came back.
He was halfway along the ridge. Corin saw him stop. Turn. And walk back. Along the ridge, toward Corin, as easily as he had gone along it. And stop in front of him, at the end of the ridge, on the narrow white glass.
And turn his back. And crouch down.
The way Oren had crouched, in the Trench. To be climbed on.
Corin stared at the thin back in its dark stiff coat.
He heard Lyra, behind him, catch her breath.
No one said anything.
"He's never done that either," said Tansy, from the far end of the ridge. Her voice was small and high. "Never. Not for me."
Corin looked at the man's back for a long moment.
Then he handed his crutch to Lyra. And put his arms round the man's thin neck. And let himself be lifted.
The man stood up as if Corin weighed nothing. He was very thin. Corin could feel every bone of his back, every knob of his spine, through the coat. But he stood up without a sound, and turned, and walked along the ridge in the white sun with Corin on his back. Step after step. Steady. Not looking down. As if he had carried something like this before, a long time ago, along some other narrow way. Something heavier, perhaps. Or something lighter. Something he had been afraid to drop.
Corin did not look down either.
He looked at the back of the man's head, at the matted hair a hand's width from his face, and breathed in the smell of him. Old smoke. Dust. Cold. And something under it, very faint, that he did not have a name for. That was not a smell of the Glass at all.
It made his chest ache the way it had ached in the cave.
At the far end of the ridge the man crouched again, and let him down, gently, onto the glass. And straightened up. And stood beside him, on his left, looking at nothing. As if nothing had happened.
Lyra came along the ridge behind them with the crutch and gave it back to Corin without a word.
Tansy was staring at the man with her mouth open.
Then she shut it, hard, and turned round, and stamped off down the far side of the ridge into the next trough, talking very loudly about nothing at all.
Tansy Talks
She came back to him in the next trough. Not to the man. To Corin.
She fell in on his right, between him and Lyra, without asking, the way she did everything. Swinging her arms. Her too-long coat brushing the folded floor behind her. And for a while she did not say anything, which Corin had already learned was the strangest thing Tansy could do.
Then she said, "He carried you."
"Yes."
"He doesn't carry things." She kicked at a ridge in the floor with her felted boot. "He doesn't pick things up. Not ever. If he drops something, he leaves it. I have to pick it up. If I fall over, he waits. He doesn't help me up. He just waits, till I get up myself." She kicked the ridge again. "Two winters. He's never carried me. Not once. Not even when I hurt my foot so bad in the first summer I couldn't walk, and I had to crawl to the shrine on my hands and knees, and he walked beside me the whole way and didn't pick me up."
Corin did not know what to say.
"I'm not cross," said Tansy. "I'm not. I just want to know why."
"I don't know why," said Corin.
"He waved at you, too." She looked up at him sideways. Her eyes were very bright and very sharp, the way they had been at the mouth of the cave. "And he walks in step with you. I saw. The pretty one saw too." She nodded at Lyra, past him. "He doesn't walk in step with anybody. He doesn't do anything with anybody. He's Nobody."
She said the name the way you say the name of a dog. Fond. Fierce.
"Do you know him?" she said. "From before? From wherever you're from?"
"No."
"Are you sure?"
Corin opened his mouth to say yes.
And did not.
Because he was not sure. He was not sure of anything, about his own life, before the last few months. There were holes in him. Big ones. Rooms with the doors off. Old Hask had put his hand on Corin's chest and said so. A room in you with the door off its hinges. Somebody went out of it in a hurry. He had lost so much, and been emptied, and burned, and had things taken, that he could not have said for certain what he had and had not done ten years ago. Or twenty.
He could have known this man. He could have known anyone.
That was the most frightening thing he had thought in a long time.
"No," he said slowly. "I'm not sure. I don't think I know him. But I can't be sure." He looked down at her. "I can't be sure of much. Things get taken out of me. A lot of things."
Tansy looked at him for a long moment.
"Like him," she said.
"A bit like him."
"Then maybe you both forgot," said Tansy. "Maybe you knew each other, and you both forgot, and his feet remember and yours don't." She seemed pleased with this. "Feet remember things. My feet remember the way to every shrine. Even when I'm asleep. I could walk to them asleep."
She skipped ahead a few steps, and then came back.
"If you find out," she said, "will you tell me? Who he is? His real name?" She was not looking at Corin now. She was looking ahead, at the man's thin back going along the trough on Corin's other side. "I'd like to know. So I can call him it. Instead of Nobody." She was quiet a moment. "I've only ever called him Nobody. It's not a very nice name, really. I just didn't have another one."
"I'll tell you," said Corin. "If I find out."
"Promise?"
"Promise."
"Good," said Tansy.
And she ran on ahead, to the front, to Kestrel, and began talking again at once, very loudly, about the Gullet and the Elbow and a place up ahead she called the Teeth.
Corin walked on in silence.
On his left, the man walked beside him. In step. Not looking at him. Not looking at anything.
Corin looked at the side of his face. At the wild beard and the pale eyes.
What's your name? he thought. What did they call you? Who called you it? Was it someone who loved you?
The man did not answer. He never would. He did not hear thoughts any more than he heard words.
But his left foot came down on the folded glass at the same moment as Corin's. At the same angle. Turned out, a little, more than the right.
And behind them, far behind, back past all the folds and the troughs, back over the Trench, Corin felt it through the crutch before he heard it: the first tick of the morning.
The sun had found the iron men.
And they had begun to walk.
