Chapter 37: Gideon's Price, Part 5 of 5: The Fifth Arch
The Fifth Arch
He took the bread himself.
Nobody wanted him to. Vespera said he would open the leg. Mother Grue said he would fall down. Lyra did not say anything, but she looked at him in a way that was worse than either. He took the ash crutch from the wall anyway, and got it under his arm, and got his good leg under him, and stood. The fourth arch swung once, slowly, and came back.
"It's my price," he said. "I said yes to it. I should carry it."
Nobody argued after that.
Lyra carried the sack. He carried one loaf, under his free arm, against his ribs. It was still a little warm. It smelled the way bread smells when you are very hungry, which was the way it always smelled in the arches.
They went down the tunnel. Slowly. The crutch went tock on the stained brick, and then his good foot, and then the bad one, swung stiff from the hip, and then the crutch again. Tock. Step. Swing. Past the brazier in the third arch, where Mother Grue's soup pot sat empty and scraped. Past Wick on his sack, sorting. Past the doorway into the fifth.
The fifth arch was the darkest.
There was no brazier in it. There had been, once. Corin could see the black ring on the brick where it had stood. But the coal had run out, or the brazier had been sold, or both, and now the only light came through the doorways at either end, from the arches on each side. It was cold. Much colder than the others. The dye ran down the walls here too, in thin stripes, but it had frozen in places, so that there were little icicles of blue and red hanging from the curve of the roof, glinting faintly.
And there were people in it.
The woman with three little ones was sitting against the far wall, on a heap of rags, with all three of them in her lap and under her shawl, so that she looked like a hen with chicks. The man who could not walk was lying on a door laid on two bricks, with a blanket over his legs and his two children curled against him, one on each side. And near the doorway, a woman who did nothing but cough was sitting with her back to the wall and a girl beside her. A girl of about ten, thin as a stick, with enormous eyes.
They all looked up when Corin came in.
They looked at the crutch first. Then at the sack in Lyra's arms. Then, all of them at once, at the loaf under his arm.
Nobody said anything.
Corin went to the woman with the three little ones first. Because she was nearest. Because the smallest of the three was crying, very thinly, the way Gideon had said. The same noise as Nell. He stopped in front of her on his crutch, and he could not bend, so Lyra took the loaf from under his arm and knelt and held it out.
The woman looked at it.
She did not take it at once. She looked at it, and then at Lyra, kneeling in the brown dress with her short ragged hair, and then up at Corin on his crutch. Her face was gray and thin and had been pretty once, and was very, very tired.
"What's it for?" she said.
It was not ungrateful. Corin understood that. It was the question of somebody who had learned that nothing in this city came without a price, and wanted to know the price first, before it was too late to say no.
"It's paid for," said Corin. "It's from Gideon."
The woman stared at him.
"Gideon?"
"He asked for it," said Corin. "For the fifth arch. A loaf every day. Until Midwinter." He did not know how to explain the rest. "It was his price. For a thing he did for me."
The woman did not say anything for a long time.
Then she took the loaf. With both hands. And held it against her chest, the way Mother Grue held things, and bent her head over it. And the three little ones under the shawl put out their hands, all at once, like the beaks of birds in a nest.
Lyra went to the man on the door. And then to the coughing woman and her girl. She gave a loaf to each. The man on the door said thank you three times, very loudly, and then began to cry, and turned his face to the wall. The coughing woman tried to say something and could not, for the coughing, and in the end only nodded and nodded. The thin girl with the enormous eyes took the loaf from Lyra's hands and held it, and did not eat it, and did not give it to her mother. She just held it. And looked at Corin.
"You're the harvester," she said.
Her voice was small and hoarse.
"Yes."
"The one with the book. That Wick talked about. Before."
"Yes."
She looked at the crutch, and his leg.
"Did the gray coats do that?"
"Yes."
She nodded slowly, as if that were what she had expected.
"My brother was a harvest," she said.
Corin did not understand. Then he did.
"He was on the glass," said the girl. "At Saint Bride's. With the doctor. In the autumn. He had the fever. From the river. He used to talk in a soldier's voice, about marching." She held the loaf tighter. "And then the doctor put the glass on him, and he got better. Nearly. And then he went back to the steps."
"What steps?"
"The river steps. By the Fish Stairs. Where we play." The girl looked at her mother, who was coughing again, bent over with it, and then back at Corin. "There's a gentleman comes. On Fridays. In the morning. To the steps. A nice gentleman. Thin. In a black coat, with a little badge on it. He's from the Health. He says so." She paused. "He gives out sweets."
The fifth arch was very quiet.
Corin did not move on his crutch.
"What sort of sweets?" he said.
"White ones." The girl held up her thumb and finger, a little way apart. "Little. Round. Like peppermints. They taste like peppermints. They're very nice." She looked down at the loaf. "He gives everybody one. All the children on the steps. Every Friday. He says it's to keep the river fever off. He says the Health is very worried about us." A pause. "He's got a kind voice."
Lyra had gone very still, beside Corin, kneeling on the cold brick.
"Did your brother have one?" said Corin.
"He had lots," said the girl. "He liked them. He went every Friday. He said they made his head feel light." She was quiet for a moment. "And then he got the fever again. Worse. And they took him back to the doctor. And this time, when the doctor put the glass on him—" She stopped.
Corin waited.
"It came out too fast," said the girl. "Mam said. In a day. And when it was all out, he didn't know us." She looked at the loaf in her hands. "He's still at Saint Bride's. On the floor. He's all right. He eats. But he doesn't know us. He doesn't know his name." She looked up. "The doctor says he might never."
Nobody said anything.
The coughing woman had stopped coughing. She was looking at her daughter with an expression that Corin did not want to look at.
Something that loosens it, Doctor Silas had said. Something that makes it come away. I can smell it on their breath. Sweet. Like a sweet shop.
A gentleman from the Health.
The Office of Public Health. The matter is under review.
"What's your name?" said Corin to the girl.
"Bet."
"Bet. Does the gentleman have a name? Did he ever say it?"
Bet thought about it.
"No," she said. "He never said. But he smells." She wrinkled her nose. "Not bad. Nice. Like the sweets. Like peppermint. All over. His coat and his gloves and everything." She paused. "And he's got a little bag. Black. With a clasp. And the sweets come out of it in a paper twist, and he gives them out one at a time, and he says There we are. There's a good child. That'll keep the fever off." She did the voice. Soft and kind and a little tired. It was a good imitation. It made the back of Corin's neck go cold. "Every Friday."
Corin took out the Ledger.
He could not write standing up, on a crutch. Lyra saw. She got up off her knees and held the book open for him, against the cold brick wall, while he wrote. His hand was shaking, from the fever and from something else.
Bet, fifth arch, about ten. Her brother: Saint Bride's, slag fever in autumn, drawn, nearly well. Went back to the Fish Stairs. A gentleman "from the Health" comes every FRIDAY MORNING to the river steps and gives the children white round sweets that taste of PEPPERMINT. "To keep the river fever off." Thin. Black coat, small badge. Kind voice. Smells of peppermint all over. Black bag with a clasp. Paper twists. "There's a good child."
Brother got fever again. Drained in a day. Doesn't know his name.
Doctor Silas: "Something that loosens it. Sweet, like a sweet shop."
Office of Public Health.
He stopped. He looked at what he had written.
Then he wrote one more line, and underlined it.
It is being done to them on purpose.
Lyra closed the book. She did not say anything. But he saw her face, and her face was white, and her mouth was a hard straight line, the way Oren's went before a fight.
Bet was still holding her loaf. She had not eaten any of it.
"Is it the sweets?" she said. "That made him not know us?"
Corin looked at her for a long moment.
No lies between you.
It was not his rule, with her. She was not his partner. She was a girl of ten in a cold arch with a loaf of bread. He could have said no. He could have said I don't know. He did not know, not for certain. Not yet.
"I think it might be," he said.
Bet nodded slowly.
She did not cry. She did not say anything. She looked down at the loaf in her thin hands, and then she broke off the end of it, and gave it to her mother. And broke off another piece, and ate it herself, very slowly, looking at the wall.
"Then I won't have any more," she said, with her mouth full. "When he comes. I'll say no thank you."
"Don't say anything," said Corin. "Don't let him see you say no. Just don't go to the steps on Friday."
Bet looked at him.
"Why?"
Corin did not answer that.
He turned on his crutch, slowly. Tock. Step. Swing. And went back out through the doorway into the light of the fourth arch. And did not look back, because if he looked back, at the thin girl with the enormous eyes eating her bread in the cold, he did not think he would be able to go on.
Before the Ninth Bell
Lyra found him an hour later, in the fourth arch, alone.
Vespera had gone to fetch something from the arches at the far end. Gideon was still asleep by the doorway. The needles turned overhead. Corin was sitting in the broken chair with his gray-gloved hands on his knees. Looking at nothing.
She sat down on the end of the door-table.
She had put Ellis's cap back on. He was glad. He did not know why. He did not mind the short hair. He found, looking at it, that he did not mind it at all. But it was easier, somehow, with the cap on. For her, he thought. Not for him.
"The gentleman from the Health," she said.
"Yes."
"With the peppermints."
"Yes."
"Is he the one Doctor Silas wrote to? At the Ministry?"
"I don't know." Corin looked at his hands. "Maybe. Maybe he's the man who opened the letter. Maybe he's the man who wrote back. The matter is under review." He was quiet. "Maybe he's just a clerk with a bag of sweets, doing what he's told, who thinks it really does keep the fever off."
"You don't believe that."
"No."
They sat for a while.
"Corin," said Lyra.
"Mm."
"Give me the book."
He looked at her.
"The Ledger," she said. "While you're in there. Tonight. Give it to me." Her rough voice was very steady. "You can't take it in. If they catch you, they'll take it. Everything in it. Every name, every door. Moll. Mrs. Marta. Hessa. The barge. Father." She paused. "Me."
Corin did not move.
She was right. He knew she was right. He had known it since the afternoon, since he had said tonight out loud under the needles. He had been sitting here for an hour not thinking about it.
He had never been without the Ledger. Not once. Not in six years. Not in the sewer, or the cellar, or the sluice, or the hay. It lived against his heart. He read it every morning, from the first page, to find out who he was. If he lost it—
If you lose it in there, said Malakor quietly, you lose them all. Every person in it. Kross would have the whole map of your life. And you would not even remember what was on it.
I know.
She is right.
I know.
He did not take it out himself. He found he could not make his hand do it. He looked at her.
"Inside my coat," he said. "The left side."
She got up off the table. She came and stood in front of the chair. And she reached into his coat, very carefully, the way she had reached into Juno's coat for the oilcloth parcel, and found the Ledger against his heart, and drew it out.
It was warm from his body. She held it in both hands.
He felt it go. Not the way a memory goes. Not the smooth flat place. Just an absence, against his chest, where something heavy had always been. As if somebody had taken a stone out of his pocket that he had carried so long he had forgotten it was a stone.
"If I don't come out," he said.
"Don't."
"If I don't come out. By the half after eleven. Don't come in after me." He held her eyes. "Take the book. Go to Vespera. Go to the barge. Read it. All of it. From the first page." He paused. "Everything I know is in there. Everything I've found. The Bourse, and the crates, and Nym, and the sweets. Lot 41. All of it. You could finish it without me."
Lyra stood very still, holding the book.
"And the first page?" she said.
"What about it?"
"It says READ THIS FIRST. You told me. You read it every morning." Her voice cracked very slightly. "What does it say?"
Corin looked at her for a long moment.
"Read it," he said. "If I don't come out."
"And if you do?"
"Then give it back to me," said Corin, "and don't."
She looked at him. Then she nodded. Once. Like Hessa.
She put the Ledger inside the front of the brown arches dress. Against her own heart. And put her hand flat over it, outside the cloth, and held it there.
And Corin sat in the broken chair with his hands on his knees and an empty place against his chest, and looked at her standing there with her hand over his life, and thought that if he had to give it to anyone, of everyone in the world, it should be her.
He did not say that either.
But he did not have to write it down. He would remember it. He was almost sure.
A Tune in the Dark
Vespera worked on his hands until the light went.
She did not bind them. She said so, over and over, in a low cross voice, while she worked. This is not binding. This is not anything. This is paint on a cracked wall. It will hold for an hour if you're lucky and five minutes if you're not, and if you sweat it will run, and if you put your hands in water it will come off in your fingers like soot. She said it to the pots, and to the needles, and to his hands, and never once to his face.
But she worked.
She mixed something in a saucer, from four of the little pots, with a bone knife. Gray-brown, the color of ordinary skin in winter, the color of a clerk's hands. She painted it onto his fingers with a soft brush, one finger at a time, over the gray. Over the backs of his hands. Over his wrists. Up his forearms to the elbows, and past them, as far as the gray went. It was cold and it smelled of linseed and it dried tight, so that his skin felt like the skin of a drum.
"Don't bend your fingers," she said. "Not till it's dry."
He did not bend his fingers.
And while she worked, he watched the arch.
Lyra had gone to sleep again. She had sat on the end of the door-table with her short ragged hair and Ellis's cap in her lap, with his Ledger inside the front of her dress and one hand over it, and said nothing, and then, some time while Vespera was mixing her paints, she had leaned sideways against the wall, and her eyes had closed. Nell had put a blanket over her. She was still there now. Asleep sitting up, against the brick, with her head tipped to one side and her hands loose in her lap.
Mother Grue had gone back to her brazier in the third arch to see to the bread. Corin could hear her, through the doorway, cutting loaves, and calling down the tunnel for the fifth arch to come and get it, and the sound of feet, and children, and somebody laughing for the first time all day.
And Gideon was asleep on the floor.
He had not meant to sleep, Corin thought. He had sat down by the doorway after the talking, cross-legged, in his new brown boots, and looked at them. He had looked at them for a long time. He had taken them off, and looked inside them, and put them on again, and laced them, slowly, and looked at them some more. And then, still looking at them, he had lain down on his side on the stained brick, with his knees drawn up and his hands tucked under his cheek, the way very small children sleep. And shut his eyes.
He had not slept at all last night. Corin knew that. He had walked up and down the sixth arch until midnight, thinking about what he wanted. And then he had drawn a map. And then he had sat on Mother Grue's knee and watched a doctor cut off two of his toes.
He was sleeping now the way people sleep who have not slept for a long time. All at once. All the way down. With his mouth a little open, and his thin chest going up and down, very slowly, under the too-big coat.
Vespera painted. The light from the crack in the roof went from gray to blue to almost nothing. She lit a candle on the door-table. The needles turned over their heads, chiming.
And Gideon, on the floor, began to hum.
It was very quiet. Corin almost did not hear it. A low, flat, steady sound, coming from the boy's closed mouth, so soft that at first it was only a kind of breathing. Then it was more than breathing. It had a shape. It went up a little, and down, and along, and up again. A tune. Not a song. No words. Just a few notes, over and over. Slow. Heavy. The kind of tune a man hums when he is carrying something, to keep time with his feet.
Mmm, mmm-mmm. Mmm, mmm-mmm-mmm.
Vespera's brush stopped.
Corin felt it stop, on the back of his left hand. It stopped in the middle of a stroke, and stayed there, cold and wet against his skin.
He did not look at her. He looked at the boy on the floor.
Gideon slept. His face was quite still. Quite calm. The way it always was. Nothing moved in it at all. Only his mouth, a little open, and the tune coming out of it, low and steady and slow, as if it had been waiting a long time somewhere very far down inside him, under everything that had been taken, and had found its way up at last because there was nobody awake to stop it.
Mmm, mmm-mmm. Mmm, mmm-mmm-mmm.
In the doorway to the third arch, Mother Grue appeared.
She had a loaf in one hand and the bread knife in the other. She had come, Corin thought, to ask Vespera something, or to bring her a crust. She stopped in the doorway.
She stood there.
Corin saw her face. In the candlelight, and the red glow from the brazier behind her. He saw it change. Not much. The small sharp brown face in its nest of shawls went still, and then it went white, all at once, the way milk goes white when it boils over. And the black-currant eyes went wide. And the loaf fell out of her hand.
It hit the brick with a soft thump and rolled a little way and stopped.
Nobody picked it up.
"That's his," whispered Mother Grue.
Nobody said anything.
"That's his," said Mother Grue again. Her voice was cracked and thin and shaking. "That's Big Gideon's. That's what he used to hum. On the wharf. Carrying. Every day. All day. You could hear him coming before you saw him." She had put her free hand on the door frame. Her old knuckles were white. "That's it. That's the very one. I'd know it anywhere. I haven't heard it in nine years."
She stared at the boy on the floor.
"He was four," she said. "He was four when they went. He can't remember it. He can't. He told me. He doesn't remember his dad at all."
Gideon slept.
And hummed.
Mmm, mmm-mmm. Mmm, mmm-mmm-mmm.
Corin sat in the broken chair with Vespera's brush stopped on the back of his hand, and the cold paint drying tight on his skin, and listened to it.
Thread, he thought. Thread. Thread. Thread.
Rope.
Behind his left ear, Malakor said nothing at all. But Corin felt him there. Listening too. Very still. The way the boy's toes had been still, under the cracked leather, since Tuesday.
The tune went round once more, slow and heavy and steady. Then it faded. Not stopped. Faded. The way the sound of a man's feet fades when he walks away down a long wharf in the dark, carrying something, until you cannot hear him any more.
Gideon slept on, with his mouth a little open, and his hands under his cheek, and his face quite calm.
Nobody moved for a long time.
Then Vespera's brush began again, on the back of Corin's hand. Slowly. One stroke. Then another. Her hand was not steady. He could feel it shake.
"Ninth bell in an hour," she said. Her voice was very low and very rough. "Keep your fingers straight."
Corin kept his fingers straight.
And in the doorway, Mother Grue bent down, very slowly, with a crack of old knees, and picked up the loaf from the floor. And brushed it off. And stood there holding it against her chest in both her root hands, the way you would hold a baby. And looked at the boy on the floor. And did not say anything else at all.
Outside, far away across the city, the bells began to ring the eighth hour.
Corin counted them.
Eight.
One more, and it would be time to go.
