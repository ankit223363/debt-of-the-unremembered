Chapter 37: Gideon's Price, Part 3 of 5: The Map: The Humming Door
The Map: The Humming Door
The middle was the last part.
Gideon looked at it for a long time before he drew anything. The inner circle, the egg with corners, empty, in the middle of the patch of brick, with the ring passage round it and the little squares of the rooms sticking out of the ring like teeth. He looked at it with the burnt stick held up in front of him, not touching the wall.
"I don't know the middle," he said at last.
Corin waited.
"Nobody goes in the middle," said Gideon. "Not Coker. Not Mistress Fane. Not the men in the counting room. Not the big men." He paused. "Not Mister Gant, even. I asked him once. When I was nine. Before I stopped asking things. I said, what's in the middle? And he hit me." He did not seem to mind. "So I didn't ask again."
"But there's a way in."
"There's a door." Gideon put the stick to the wall, on the north side of the inner egg, the side nearest the top of the patch. He drew a small square, very carefully. Then he drew lines across it, close together. Bars. "Here. An iron door. With bars across it. It's not very big. A man could go through it, if he bent." He looked at it. "It hums."
"Hums?"
"Like bees. All the time. Very low. You can hear it from the ring passage, if it's quiet. A sort of mmmmmmm." He made the sound, flat and steady, through his closed lips. It went on for a moment in the arch, low and strange, and stopped. "If you put your hand on it, it shakes. Like a cat purring. I did it once." He paused. "Mister Gant saw me. He pulled my hand off it so hard he nearly broke my arm. And he said, Never touch that, you little fool. If you touch it, it remembers you."
The arch was very quiet.
It remembers you, said Malakor, very softly, behind Corin's left ear. A warded door. Bound with Ash. It will know every hand that has ever touched it. And it will tell someone.
Corin wrote it down. Iron door, north side of the middle. Barred. Hums like bees. Shakes under the hand. "If you touch it, it remembers you."
Vivienne's vault has three doors, the Duke had said to Lyra, in the dark gallery, to the ceiling. And two of them are not doors.
He had written that down too. Lyra had told him, on the riverbank. It was on the page before this one.
One door, he thought. An iron one that hums and remembers. And two that are not doors.
And a room with no floor plan, because the man who built it was put into a crate.
"Is there another way in?" he said. "Not the door. Anything. A grating. A chute. A hole in the wall."
Gideon thought about it.
"I don't know," he said. "I never looked. I wasn't allowed in the middle. I only know the outside." He considered the inner egg. "But it's big. The middle. You can tell from the ring passage. It goes on a long way round. Too big for one room." He paused. "And sometimes, when you're in the ring passage near the west side, near the big room by the wet door, you can feel it in the floor. Under your feet. A sort of—" He frowned. The crease again. "Like the humming. But lower. Like something very big breathing."
Corin did not say anything.
He wrote it down.
Gideon drew the last things.
He drew the lift. A square on the east side of the ring passage, near the kitchen end. A shaft in the wall, he said, with a wooden platform in it on ropes, and a great wheel at the top that two men turned to pull it up and let it down. It went up to the kitchen. To the stone-floored room full of copper. Corin knew that room. It was in the Ledger. A girl on her knees picking up broken tarts.
"And the stairs," said Gideon. "Beside the lift. Stone ones. Going up. To the kitchen too. The footmen use them. And the waiters, on big nights." He looked at Corin. "You'll come down those. On Midwinter. With your tray. If you're a waiter."
Corin looked at the little charcoal stair on the wall.
"Yes," he said.
Gideon drew one more thing. A short line, on the ring passage, just outside the square that was the lift. A bench.
"That's where the big men sit," he said. "When there's nothing to carry."
Corin's pencil stopped.
"They sit there," said Gideon. "Side by side. On the bench. With their hands on their knees." He put his own hands on his own knees, to show. "They don't talk. They don't move. They don't go home. They just sit there, all night, until somebody comes and tells them to carry something. And then they get up, both together, and carry it. And then they come back and sit down again." He was quiet for a moment. "I used to go past them. With messages. Every time. And they never looked at me."
He looked at the little charcoal line of the bench.
"I never looked at them either," he said. "Mister Gant said not to. He said, Don't look at them, Gideon. That's what you'll be, if you don't work. That's what happens to hollow ones that are no use. Look at the floor." He paused. "So I looked at the floor."
Nobody said anything.
Mother Grue had gone quite still beside Corin. He could feel her not moving, under all the shawls, the way you feel a stone in a stream. Her old root hands had closed together in her lap.
Corin did not look at her.
He looked at Gideon. At the thin boy kneeling in front of the patch of brick in the red firelight, with the burnt stick in his hand, looking at a charcoal line he had drawn to show where two men sat.
Big Gideon Hollow, he thought. And his brother Saul. The two of them could lift a hogshead of rum between them like a basket of eggs. They went to the war together, and came back together, and never said a word about it.
They went carrying for the Bourse when the boy was four. And never came home.
And the Bourse said there had never been any Hollows working there.
They sit on a bench by the lift, side by side, with their hands on their knees. They don't go home.
One of them used to hum.
Thread, said Malakor. Thread. Thread. Thread. How many threads before it is a rope, harvester?
Corin did not answer.
He wrote it down. All of it. The bench. The hands on the knees. They don't go home. And at the end, in letters so small that he could barely read them himself:
I think it is them.
Then he closed the Ledger.
"That's all," said Gideon. He sat back on his heels and looked at the wall. The whole patch of brick was covered now, in black lines, squares and circles and marks and little stairs. The ring passage, and the rooms, and the iron door that hummed, and the wet door on the river, and the bench. "That's all I know. That's all of it." He looked at it a moment longer. "Have you got it?"
"Yes," said Corin.
"All of it?"
"All of it."
Gideon nodded. Then he took the sleeve of his too-big coat, and pulled it down over his hand, and reached up to the top left corner of the map, where Cinder Lane began. And began, slowly and carefully, the way he had drawn it, to rub it out.
Gideon's Name
It took him a long time to rub it out.
The charcoal did not want to come off. It smeared, and spread, and left a gray shadow on the brick, so that the map was still there, faintly, under the smudge, like a ghost of itself. Gideon spat on his sleeve and rubbed again. And again. Slowly. Patiently. The way he did everything. Starting at Cinder Lane and going round, the ring passage and the rooms and the store rooms and the lift, the iron door and the wet door, until there was nothing on the wall but a broad gray stain, the size of a door, that could have been anything. Old soot. Damp. The ghost of a fire.
He left the bench till last.
Corin saw him do it. He saw him rub out everything else, all round it, and leave that one short line on the brick by itself, outside the place where the lift had been. And then stop, with his sleeve over his hand, and look at it.
The little charcoal line. Where two men sat, side by side, with their hands on their knees.
Gideon looked at it for a long moment.
Then he rubbed it out.
He sat back on his heels and looked at the gray stain. His coat sleeve was black to the elbow. There was charcoal on his face, a long smear across one cheek, where he had pushed his hair back without thinking.
"Gone," he said.
"Yes," said Corin.
Gideon got up. He came back across the arch to the brazier, and sat down cross-legged in front of it again, where he had sat to tell his price, and held his black hands out to the coals. Not to warm them. Corin did not think he knew they were cold. Just because that was what people did, by a fire. He had seen them do it.
Nobody said anything for a while.
Mother Grue had not moved. She sat in her shawls, very still, looking at the boy. Corin did not know what she was thinking. He thought he could guess.
He looked at Gideon too.
At the thin face, with the charcoal on it. At the dirty fair hair, falling over the pale eyes. At the long thin hands held out to the coals, with the fingers spread.
He did not know what he was looking for. A likeness, perhaps. Something in the face. Something in the hands. Something he could hold up beside a huge silent man on a bench by a lift, in the dark, and say yes. That. There.
He had never seen the men on the bench. He did not know their faces. He had nothing to hold up.
"Gideon," he said.
"Yes?"
"Is that your name?"
Gideon turned his head and looked at him.
"Gideon," said Corin. "Is it your real name? The one your mother gave you?"
Gideon thought about it.
He did not seem surprised by the question. He did not seem anything. He turned his head back to the coals and looked at them for a while, with his hands held out.
"No," he said.
Mother Grue drew in her breath. Very quietly.
"It's my dad's," said Gideon. "Gideon was my dad's name. Not mine." He turned his hands over, so that the backs of them were to the fire. "When I was little, my mam called me something else. I don't know what. I don't remember it. I was very little." He paused. "And then my dad went carrying. And didn't come back. And my mam got sad. And one day she started calling me Gideon instead."
Nobody said anything.
"I asked her why," said Gideon. "I remember that. I asked her. I said, that's Dad's name. And she said, I know. So there'll still be one in the house." He turned his hands back over. "So I was Gideon after that. Everybody called me it. Even Mister Gant. And I stopped being the other one." He was quiet. "I think she forgot the other one too, after a bit. She was very sad."
The brazier hissed.
Far down the tunnel, the child in the fifth arch had stopped crying. Somebody was singing to it, very low, a tune with no words.
"Does it matter?" said Gideon. He looked at Corin with mild interest. "For the map? Do you need my real one?"
"No," said Corin.
His voice was not steady. He did not try to make it steady.
"No," he said again. "It doesn't matter for the map."
"Good," said Gideon. "Because I haven't got it."
He went back to looking at the coals.
And Corin sat on his sack in the red light with the Ledger in his lap and the fever going hot and cold in him, and did not write anything down. Not yet. He could not. His hand would not have held the pencil.
He thought: so there'll still be one in the house.
He thought of a woman alone in a room in the Lower Docks, with a small boy, and a man's name, and a happy day borrowed from a lady in violet, sitting in her like a warm stone, going cold.
He thought of two men on a bench.
He thought, I have to see their faces.
And then the arch tipped.
Not the way it had tipped before, a little, and come back. It went all the way. The brazier swung up and over, and the colored walls went sideways, and the floor came up very slowly toward his face, and he thought, quite calmly, oh. Here it is.
He heard Mother Grue say something, sharp and high.
He heard Gideon say, with mild interest, "He's falling over."
And then the stained brick floor of the third arch, which was cool, and smelled of dye, and was the last thing he felt for a long time.
Fever
He was in the grass.
It was very tall. Taller than he was. Green and gold and moving, all round him, whispering, with the sun coming through it in long bright blades, so that the light lay on his arms in stripes. It smelled of summer. Of hay, he thought, though it was not hay. Of something before hay. Grass that had never been cut.
He was small. He knew that. He could tell from the height of the grass, and from his hands, when he held them up in front of his face. They were small hands. A child's hands. There was no gray on them at all.
Somewhere, not far off, a wheel was creaking.
Creak. Creak. Creak.
Slow and heavy, like the wheels of Ellis's wain on the north road. And with it, under it, keeping time with it, another sound.
A drum.
Not loud. Not a war drum. A small one. Tapped, not beaten. The way you tap a table with your fingers when you are waiting for something.
Tap tap tap tap.
He stood up in the grass, on his small feet, to see.
There was a road. A dirt road, white with dust, going away between the tall grass in a long curve toward a line of dark trees. And on the road, moving slowly away from him, a wagon. A big farm wagon with high wooden sides, painted blue once and faded nearly white, pulled by two gray horses with their heads down. There were people in the back of it. Children. He could see their heads over the high sides. A lot of heads. Small ones. Going away.
And sitting on the tailboard of the wagon, with his legs hanging over the edge and a little drum on his knees, there was a boy.
He was older than Corin. Ten, perhaps. Eleven. Thin and brown, with a straight back and short dark hair and no scar on his face at all. He was looking down at the drum on his knees, not at the road, not at the grass, not at Corin. And his hands were moving on it. Lightly. With his fingertips.
Tap tap.
The wagon creaked.
Tap tap tap tap tap tap tap tap tap.
Corin counted it. In the dream, he could count. He could count all of it, as far as he liked. Nine.
Tap tap tap.
The wagon went on down the white road, slowly, toward the trees. Getting smaller. And Corin stood in the tall grass with his small hands at his sides and watched it go, and wanted to call out, and did not know what to call. He did not know the boy's name. He did not know any of their names.
And then somebody behind him took his hand.
A small hand. Smaller than his. Warm and sticky, as if it had been holding sweets. It held on to his fingers very tightly.
He did not turn round. He could not, in the dream. He could only stand there and feel the small hand in his, and watch the wagon go.
"Where are they going?" said a voice. A little girl's voice. Very close, at his elbow. "Corin. Where are they going?"
He opened his mouth to answer.
And the grass caught fire.
It did not burn the way grass burns. There was no smoke. There was no heat. It simply went, all at once, from green and gold to white, the way a page goes white when you hold it too close to a candle, and then to gray, and then to nothing. And the road went. And the trees. And the wagon, and the boy on the tailboard with his drum, and the heads of the children. Gone. All of it. Swept clean.
And the small hand in his pulled. Hard. Toward the road. Toward the wagon going away. As if somebody on the wagon had called its name.
And let go.
He felt it go. One moment it was there, warm and sticky, holding on. The next there was only the shape of it, against his palm. And then not even that.

He tried to open his eyes. They would not open. They were too heavy. He tried to say something. He did not know what. He thought it might be the little girl's question. Where are they going? He thought it might be the boy's name, if he had known it. He thought it might be ten.
What came out was a sound. He did not know what it was.

Only the dark, and the cold cloth on his face, and somewhere very far above him, a long way off, a man's voice saying knife, and a girl's voice saying no, and a man's voice saying yes, or he loses the leg, and then he loses the rest of him, and then nothing.
