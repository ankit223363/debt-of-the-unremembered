Chapter 37: Gideon's Price, Part 2 of 5: Boots, Bread, and One Feeling Back
Boots, Bread, and One Feeling Back
"Boots," said Gideon.
He stuck out his feet, one after the other, into the light of the brazier.
Corin had seen Gideon's boots before. It was in the Ledger. His boots are too small. His toes come through the ends. He doesn't notice. But he had not looked at them properly. Not here, in the firelight, with the boy's thin legs stretched out on the stained brick.
They were not boots, really. They had been, once. A child's boots, black leather, with laces. Now the leather was gray and cracked and split down both sides, and the laces were bits of string, and the soles had come away at the front so that they flapped. And through the ends of both of them, where the leather had worn right through, Gideon's toes stuck out.
They were the wrong color.
Not pink. Not even white with cold. A strange, dull, waxy gray, like candle-ends. And at the very tips, on the two biggest toes, something darker. A bruised, purple-black, like the skin of a plum.
Mother Grue made a sound.
She leaned forward out of her shawls, with her stick, and looked. Corin saw her face change.
"Gideon," she said. "How long have your feet been like that?"
Gideon looked at his toes with mild interest.
"I don't know," he said. "I don't look at them much. They don't hurt." He wriggled the gray ones. The black ones did not wriggle. "Those two have stopped moving. Since Tuesday. I thought it was the boots."
Mother Grue put her root hand over her mouth.
Corin knew what it was. He had seen it in jars. On men who had been out on the ice too long. On men who had lain in snowdrifts, drunk, all night. The cold gets into the ends of you, and stays, and kills the flesh, and the flesh goes black. And if nobody cuts it off, the black spreads.
And Gideon could not feel it. Gideon had sold his cold, when he was eight, one bit at a time, and then all the rest. He sat out on the stairs and the railings and the roofs all night in winter and did not shiver. He walked in the snow with his toes through his boots and did not mind. It had been the one good thing about being hollow, he had said once. That and nobody bothering to hurt you.
And all that time, his feet had been dying.
"That's why," said Gideon. "Boots. Proper ones. That fit." He considered his feet again. "Not because of the cold. I don't mind the cold. Because Nell cries."
"Nell?"
"When she looks at my feet. She cries. I don't like it." He paused. "I don't like it. Not like that. I mean, I don't want it. It's like a noise in my head that won't stop. I want it to stop." He looked up at Corin. "So I want boots. So Nell won't look at my feet and cry."
Corin looked at him for a long time.
"Yes," he said.
His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat and said it again.
"Yes. Boots. Tomorrow. I'll find them."
Gideon nodded, as if a box had been ticked.
"And Doctor Silas looks at those toes," said Mother Grue. Her voice was shaking. "Tonight. I'll send Tobin. Tonight, Gideon, do you hear me?"
"All right," said Gideon. He did not seem to mind either way.
He put his feet back under him.
"The second thing," he said, "is bread."
"Bread," said Corin.
"For the fifth arch." Gideon turned his head and looked down the tunnel, into the dark. "There's a woman in the fifth arch with three little ones. And a man who can't walk, with two. And the coughing woman's girl. That's six." He counted on his fingers, slowly, to be sure. "Six. They don't get enough. Mother Grue gives them soup but there isn't enough soup, there's never enough, and the little ones cry at night. I hear them. All night. The same noise as Nell." He looked back at Corin. "So I want a loaf for the fifth arch. A big one. Every day. Until Midwinter."
"Until Midwinter," said Corin.
"That's when you're going in. Isn't it. Midwinter. You said." Gideon frowned slightly. That small crease between the pale brows again. "I don't know what happens after Midwinter. Nobody does. So I only asked till then." He paused. "Is that fair?"
Corin did not answer at once.
He thought about where he would find a loaf a day for ten days. He had no money. Not a penny. Everything he owned was in his coat. Hessa had given him bread and cheese for the road, and it was gone. He thought about Vespera, and the Duke's card in Lyra's pocket, and Juno, and the step women, and Moll at his desk with the paper with the red seal in his drawer.
He would find it.
"Yes," he said. "That's fair."
Gideon nodded again.
Then he was quiet for a long time.
He sat cross-legged on the brick with his hands on his knees and the fire on one side of his face, and looked at the coals in the brazier, and did not say anything. And Corin understood that this was the thing he had walked up and down the sixth arch for. Not the boots. Not the bread. The third thing.
"The third thing," said Gideon at last.
He did not close his eyes this time.
"I want one back," he said.
Corin did not move.
"One feeling," said Gideon. "Of mine. One of the ones I sold. Back." He was looking at the coals. "Not now. I know you can't do it now. I know you haven't got it. I know it's somewhere. In a jar. In a crate. In some rich lady's ice at a summer party, maybe, and gone." His voice did not change. "But if you ever find it. If you're ever down there, in the lower rooms, or anywhere, and you see a jar with my name on it. Or you know it's mine. Any of them." He paused. "Bring me one back."
The arch was very quiet.
Mother Grue had her hand over her mouth again. Her small black eyes were wet.
"Which one?" said Corin.
His voice was very low.
Gideon thought about it. Corin could see him thinking. The small crease between the brows. The pale eyes on the coals.
"Not happy," he said. "I don't know what happy's for. Mother Grue says it's nice, but I don't know what nice is either." He paused. "Not sad. Sad sounds like a lot of work. Nell's always sad." Another pause. "Not frightened. Frightened's no good to anybody. Mister Gant said it's the best crop. That means nobody wants to keep it."
He was quiet.
"Cold," he said.
Corin stared at him.
"I want my cold back," said Gideon. "If it's anywhere. If you find it." He looked down at his own feet, tucked under him, in the cracked gray leather with the black toes hidden. "So I'll know when to come in."
Nobody said anything.
The brazier hissed. A coal fell. Far down the tunnel, in the fifth arch, a child began to cry, thin and tired, and somebody hushed it.
Corin sat on his sack with the fever going hot and cold in him and looked at the thin boy on the brick in front of him. And something happened in his chest that he did not have a name for. Something that hurt worse than the leg.
So I'll know when to come in.
He did not know if he could do it. He did not know if feelings, once sold and bottled and stacked in a crate, could ever be found again, let alone put back. He had never heard of it being done. The Ministry did not do it. The Bank did not do it. Nobody did it. Once a thing was out of you, it was out.
But he had found Ash in jars that nobody else could read. He had found a dead scribe's last minutes in a gutter. He had found a forgotten war in the scraps of other men's burnt endings.
If you ever find it.
"I'll look," said Corin.
Gideon looked up.
"Down there," said Corin. "In the lower rooms. When we go in. I'll look for it. Every crate. Every jar. Every label." His voice shook and he did not stop it. "I'm good at labels. I spent twelve years reading labels. If it's there, if there's anything with your name on it, I'll find it." He paused. "I can't promise it's there. I can't promise I can put it back. But I'll look."
Gideon looked at him for a long time with his pale, calm, empty eyes.
"That's not yes," he said.
"No."
"It's I'll look."
"Yes."
Gideon thought about it.
"All right," he said at last. "I'll look is all right." He put his hands flat on his knees again. "That's the three things. Boots. Bread till Midwinter. And look for my cold." He nodded once, to himself. "That's my price. Do you say yes?"
"Yes," said Corin.
"Then it's sold," said Gideon. "Properly." And for the first time since Corin had known him, something happened to his thin mouth that was almost, nearly, very nearly, a smile. There and gone. Like the twitch of a sleeping dog. "I'll draw it now. While I remember. Have you got a burnt stick?"
The Map: Cinder Lane
Mother Grue gave him a burnt stick from the edge of the brazier, a long one, black at the end and still warm.
Gideon did not draw on paper. He did not ask for any. He looked round the arch, at the stained brick floor, at the curving walls with their colored stripes running down. Then he got up, and went to the back wall of the third arch, where the brick was driest, and knelt in front of it.
There was a patch of wall there, about as big as a door, where no dye ran. The bricks were dark and old and plain. He put the end of the burnt stick against the top left corner of the patch, very carefully. And drew a line.
Corin watched him.
He had moved his sack, slowly, with Mother Grue's help, so that he was sitting against the side wall with his bad leg out and the back wall in front of him in the light of the brazier. Mother Grue sat beside him in her shawls. Lyra slept on.
Gideon drew without talking at first. Long, straight, careful lines in charcoal on the old brick. A long one going across. Then a shorter one going down. Then a little square, in the corner where they met.
"That's the Bourse," he said, without turning round. "The back of it. The big round wall. I can't draw round, so it's straight." He tapped the long line across. "That's Cinder Lane. It goes along the back. It's very narrow. Two carts can't pass." He tapped the little square. "That's the Bell. On the corner. Where Cinder Lane meets Tanner's Steps."
Corin knew the Bell. Not from memory. From the Ledger. A tavern on Cinder Lane. Low and black, with a bell hanging over the door that has no clapper. The ice door is in the alley behind it.
Tam.
Gideon drew a small mark behind the Bell. Two lines, side by side, flat.
"The ice door," he said.
He did not say anything else about it. He did not need to.
He moved the stick along the line of Cinder Lane, toward the right, and stopped about halfway, and drew a small square against the long line of the Bourse wall. And inside the square, a little slanting line, like a lid lifted on a box.
"The coal chute," he said. "It's in the pavement. Against the wall. An iron flap, as big as a table, with a ring in it. The coal carts stop there and two men lift the flap and they tip the coal down. It goes down a slide. Into the coal cellar." He drew a line going down from the square, slanting, to another square below. "That's the cellar. It's under the street. It's black. All black. The walls and the floor and the ceiling. You can't see anything. You go down the slide on your bottom, with the coal, and you land on the heap."
"You went down the coal chute?" said Corin.
"Lots of times." Gideon did not turn round. "Mister Gant sent me. With messages. From the Bell to the Bourse, and back. When they didn't want the messages to go by the street." He drew another line, from the coal cellar square, going right, a short way, to a little circle. "The door's here. In the coal cellar wall. A little iron door. Low. You have to bend. And behind it there's a stool, and on the stool there's a man."
"What man?"
"Coker." Gideon tapped the circle. "Old Coker. He's fat and he's deaf in one ear and he smells of beer, and he sits on the stool all day and all night with a lantern and a stick, and when you knock he opens a slot in the door and looks at you and you say selling or you say message, and he lets you in." He paused. "Or he doesn't. And then you go back up the coal chute, which is hard, because it's slippery, and the coal comes down on you."
"All day and all night?"
"Except Sundays." Gideon turned round at last and looked at Corin. "On Sundays, at the ninth bell, Coker goes to the Bell. For his supper. He always goes. Every Sunday. He has a pie and four pints and he sings, and then he comes back at the eleventh bell, and he's asleep on his stool by the half hour." He said it the way another boy might have said the time of a tide. "Nobody's on the stool on Sundays from the ninth to the eleventh. Except Sundays when it's a big week. Then they put a younger man on. Coker's nephew."
"Is this a big week?"
"Midwinter's a big week," said Gideon. "But that's next week. Not this." He considered. "This Sunday it'll be Coker. Unless he's dead."
Corin was quiet for a moment.
Tomorrow was Sunday.
Tomorrow, said Malakor softly. With that leg. With that fever.
Tomorrow, thought Corin. Because next Sunday it'll be the nephew. And next Sunday will be the night before Midwinter, and every lot will be in the building, and every guard Vivienne has. He shut his eyes for a moment, against the heat behind them. Tomorrow is the only night the stool is empty.
Gideon had turned back to the wall.
He drew a long line from the circle that was Coker's stool, going right, along under the line of the Bourse wall. A passage. And then, every so often along it, little marks. Short lines crossing it.
"Steps," he said. "The passage goes down. Not all at once. In bits. A bit of flat, then some steps, then a bit of flat, then some more." He touched the first mark. "Here there's five." The second. "Here there's eight." The third. "Here there's a lot."
"How many?"
Gideon looked at him. The pale eyes rested on Corin's face, calmly, for a moment.
"You can't count past nine," he said.
Corin did not answer.
"Juno told me," said Gideon. "Yesterday, when she came through on her way north. She said you'd burned it. She said you count bridges in nines and start again." He did not seem to think this was strange. "So I'll tell you in nines." He looked at the mark. "There's two nines, and four more. You go down nine. Then nine again. Then four."
Corin looked at the charcoal mark on the brick.
Two nines and four. He could hold that. He could hold each nine, and then the four. He did not know what the whole of it was. He could not feel the size of it. But he could walk it.
"Thank you," he said.
Gideon nodded and went on drawing.
"At the bottom of the two nines and four," he said, "the passage goes under the Bourse. Right under it. Under the round floor with the tables." He drew a big rough circle, in charcoal, filling most of the patch of wall. "This is the lower rooms. They go all the way round. Under everything." He stopped, with the stick lifted. "It's big. It's the biggest place I've ever been. Bigger than the arches. Bigger than the Bell's cellars. It goes on and on, all the way round, in the dark, and you can walk and walk and walk and come back to where you started and not know."
He looked at the circle he had drawn.
"I'll do the inside," he said. "Next. It's harder. There's a lot of it." He turned his head. "Are you writing it down?"
Corin had the Ledger open on his good knee. He had been writing. Fast. Small. In his sloping hand, with the pencil shaking a little from the fever. Cinder Lane. The Bell on the corner of Tanner's Steps. Ice door behind. Coal chute halfway along: iron flap in the pavement, ring. Slide down onto the heap. Black cellar. Little iron door, low. Coker on the stool: fat, deaf in one ear, beer, lantern, stick. Knock, say MESSAGE. SUNDAYS: Coker goes to the Bell at the 9th bell, back at the 11th, asleep by the half. Stool empty 9th to 11th. Passage down: 5 steps, flat, 8 steps, flat, then two nines and four.
"Yes," he said.
"Good," said Gideon. "Because I'm going to rub it out after. Mother Grue says. Nothing on the walls in the arches that a gray coat could read."
He turned back to the big charcoal circle, and lifted the burnt stick, and began on the inside.
The Map: The Long Room
He drew a second circle inside the first. Smaller. Not quite round, because he could not do round, so it came out more like an egg with corners. And between the two circles, he drew a ring.
"The ring passage," he said. "It goes all the way round. Under the edge of the floor upstairs. It's wide. You could drive a cart down it, nearly. It's got a floor of stone and a roof of brick, all in arches, like here." He glanced round at the arches of Mother Grue's tunnel. "But dry. And clean. They sweep it. Every morning. And there's lamps."
"What kind of lamps?"
"Little ones. On hooks. Every so often. Not gas." Gideon thought. "Oil. Yellow. They smoke. They don't give much light." He paused. "Except some of them are different."
Corin looked up from the Ledger.
"Some of them are brass," said Gideon. "With no flame. Just a gray light inside. Like smoke in a jar." He drew a little mark on the ring passage. Then another, a long way round. Then a third. "Here. And here. And here. By the doors that matter."
Corin's skin went cold under the fever.
It's got a gem in it instead of a wick, Vespera had said. A burned gem. Ash, set in glass. What it does is find other Ash.
"When you walk under them," said Corin slowly, "what happens?"
Gideon shrugged.
"Nothing, to me," he said. "I haven't got any Ash. I never burned anything. They took it all before I could." He considered. "But Mister Gant's hands go gray. When he walks under them. Gray like smoke, on his skin. Just for a moment. He doesn't like it. He always pulls his sleeves down."
Corin wrote it down. His pencil was shaking.
Ash lamps on the ring passage. Brass, gray light. Three. By the doors that matter. Gant's hands go gray under them. Gideon's don't.
He looked at his own hands, in Hessa's gray wool gloves, on the open page.
Thursday, Vespera had said. I'll bind it on Thursday.
Tomorrow was Sunday.
He did not say anything. He went on writing.
Gideon drew the rooms.
They came off the outer side of the ring passage like teeth on a cog, going out away from the middle. He drew each one as a small square, with a line joining it to the ring. And as he drew each one he told it, slowly, carefully, the way he had told Tam's last walk. As if it were a thing he had to get exactly right, or it would not count.
"The selling room," he said. The first square, nearest the passage from Coker's stool. "That's where the lady in the white apron is. Mistress Fane. She's got a table and a chair and a lamp and a box of glasses. The chair's got straps on the arms. You don't need them. Nobody fights. But they're there." He moved on. "The counting room. Next door. That's where the jars go after. A long table. Two men with ink and labels. They write on the jars and put them in racks." He moved on. "Then the store rooms."
There were a great many store rooms. He drew them one after another, going round, each one a little square. Corin could not count them. He did not try. He wrote store rooms, many, all the way round the east side and left it at that.
"They're full of crates," said Gideon. "All of them. Stacked up to the roof. Wooden crates, like for oranges, with straw in, and the jars in the straw. Hundreds. Thousands. In rows. With little paths between, just wide enough for the big men." He paused. "It smells of straw. And of the jars. Jars smell, a bit. Did you know? Like a church after the candles go out."
Corin knew. He had spent twelve years in the Archive. He had smelled that smell every day of his life.
"How do you know what's in the crates?" he said.
"Chalk," said Gideon. "On the ends. Colored chalk. A mark." He thought. "Blue for cold. Black for frightened. Gray for sad. Red for summers. Yellow for little kind things. Green for skills, like rope and nets." He paused. "And some of them have a white mark too. As well as the color. A little white mark. Like a key. Or like a bird on a string. I don't know what that's for. It's only on some."
Corin stopped writing.
He was seeing a landing stage over a ditch, at night, at the last tannery. A stack of square wooden crates, nailed shut, stenciled in black. NYM & SONS. FINE LEATHER. NOT TO BE OPENED BEFORE DELIVERY. And under the stencil, smaller, a little mark like a key. Or a bird on a string. In the dark, he had not been able to tell which.
He had thought nothing of it.
He had let it go.
But somewhere at the back of his head, the part that had once been a very good Archivist had made a small, tidy note. And put it on a shelf.
And now it took it down.
Crates of hides, at a tannery on the canal. North of the city. Going somewhere by water.
Crates of jars, in the Bourse. With the same mark.
"Gideon," he said. "The crates with the white mark. Where do they go?"
"Out," said Gideon. "Not up. The others go up, some of them. In the lift, to the kitchen, and then to the floor upstairs, for the rich people to buy. But the ones with the white mark don't go up. The big men carry them the other way." He moved the burnt stick round the ring passage, to the far side, the west side, away from Coker's stool. "To here."
He drew a square there. Bigger than the others. And on the far side of it, at the very edge of the patch of brick, where the dye began, he drew a wavy line.
"The river," he said.
Corin stared at the wavy line.
"There's a door," said Gideon. "Down here. At the bottom. Through the big room and down some more steps. A door in the wall that goes straight out onto the water. Under the bank. Where you can't see it from the street, or the bridge, or anywhere." He drew a small arch in the wavy line. "The wet door. That's what Coker calls it. The wet door. Boats come. At night. When the tide's up. Little ones, low, with no lights. And the big men carry the crates with the white marks down the steps and through the wet door and put them in the boats. And the boats go away."
"Where?"
"Up the river," said Gideon. "I think. They turn that way." He pointed with the stick, up and to the left. North. "I watched once. From the bank. It was dark. They went up the river and under the bridges and I couldn't see them any more."
North. Up the river. To the canal mouth. To the locks. To the Long Pond, and the Tanners' Ditch, and a landing stage at the last tannery, with crates stacked on it, waiting.
NYM & SONS. NOT TO BE OPENED BEFORE DELIVERY.
Corin did not know where Nym was. He did not know what it was. A man's name. A town. A country. He did not know.
But he knew, now, that somewhere at the end of a long cold road of water, somebody was waiting for crates of Lower Docks children's cold and fright and sad, and summers and kindnesses and the skill of tying a net, packed in straw and stenciled as fine leather.
He wrote it down. All of it. His hand was shaking so badly now that the letters went up and down the page like a drunk man's footsteps. White mark on some crates. Key or bird on a string. SAME AS THE TANNERY CRATES. NYM & SONS. They don't go up to the floor. They go out by the WET DOOR to the river, at night, high tide, in low boats with no lights. Boats go NORTH.
Where is Nym?
He underlined it twice.
"Gideon," he said. "When they wrote on your jars. In the counting room. What did they write?"
Gideon thought about it.
"Not my name," he said. "They didn't write names on the cheap ones. Only on the good ones, for upstairs." He paused. "On mine they wrote a letter. And a number." He looked at Corin. "H. And then the number of the jar. All the hollow ones got H. Mister Gant said it was so they'd know which were ours."
H.
Corin looked at the letter on the page in front of him, in his own shaking hand. And thought of a name on half the graves in Saint Bride's yard.
He did not say anything.
"Blue chalk," he said instead. "And an H on the jar. That's what I'm looking for. For your cold."
"Yes," said Gideon.
He turned back to the wall.
