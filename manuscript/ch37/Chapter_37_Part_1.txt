Chapter 37: Gideon's Price, Part 1 of 5: Soup in the Arches
Soup in the Arches
Mother Grue's soup was made of whatever there was, and on Saturday night there was not much.
There were turnips, because a cart had tipped over on Wharf Street in the morning and Tobin and the other thin boy had run. There were onions. There was a ham bone that the butcher on Cooper's Row gave Mother Grue every Saturday, for reasons nobody had ever explained, wrapped in a page of the broadsheet. There was a handful of barley. And there was salt, and pepper, and water from the trough at the end of the sixth arch, which was only a little bit blue.
It was the best soup Corin had ever eaten.
He did not know if that was true. He could not remember all the other soups. But he sat on his sack by the brazier in the third arch with the bowl on his knees, a wooden bowl worn smooth by a hundred hands, and the steam coming up into his face, and he thought it must be. It was hot. It was thick. It tasted of ham and pepper and the turnips were soft and sweet, and it went down into him and spread out, warm, the way the brandy had on the barge.
They were all eating. Everyone in the arches. Mother Grue had made it in a great black pot on the brazier in the second arch, the biggest pot Corin had ever seen, with a lid that took two people to lift, and the smell of it had gone all the way down the tunnel of arches, and people had come. From every arch. With bowls and mugs and tins and, in one case, a hat. And Mother Grue had sat beside the pot on her heap of shawls with a ladle as long as her arm and given every one of them some. The man with the new face. The man in the sack apron. The girl with the broom, Nell, back now in her own brown dress, with her cap and apron folded away. The two thin boys. A mother with three small children, who held out four bowls and got four. An old soldier with one arm. A woman who did nothing but cough. Gideon.
And Wick. Mother Grue had taken his bowl to him herself, slowly, leaning on her stick, and put it down beside his piles of paper, and put the spoon in his hand. And closed his fingers round it. And Wick had looked at it for a long time, with his faded eyes. And then, very slowly, he had begun to eat.
Lyra was asleep.
She had eaten half her bowl, sitting on the sack beside Corin in the brown arches dress, with her mother's gray gown folded away in a bundle under her head. And then she had stopped. With the spoon halfway to her mouth. And sat there, looking at nothing. And after a while, without a word, she had put the bowl down on the brick and lain down on her side on the sack with her knees drawn up, and shut her eyes.
She had not moved since.
Corin had put the horse blanket over her. Ellis's blanket, brown, with the red stripe. She had not woken.
He sat beside her now and ate his soup slowly and did not look at her too often.
He had the Ledger open on his knee, beside the bowl. Not to write. To read. The page she had written, on the riverbank, in the lamplight, with her bare red hand. I had five. Now I have four. He had read it eleven times. He knew it was eleven because he had made a small mark in the margin each time, a little upright stroke, the way he had counted the bridges on the north road. Nine strokes and then a line through them. Then two more.
He did not know why he kept reading it.
Because you could not stop her, said Malakor. And you could not go with her. And you could not have paid it for her, because it was not yours to pay. A pause. So you read it. It is the only thing you can do.
Corin did not answer.
He closed the Ledger.
His leg hurt.
It had hurt all day. That was not new. It had hurt in the wain and in the barn and all the way down the Hill, and under Mother Grue's green spirit, and every time he moved. But it was a different hurt now. He had noticed it change while Lyra was gone, standing in the mouth of the arch on the broom handle, watching the steps. It had stopped being a sharp hurt, like a knife. It had become a slow one. Deep and hot and throbbing, in time with his heart. As if there were something inside the leg that was alive, and growing, and did not like being there.
And he was hot.
He had not noticed that until the soup. He had thought it was the brazier. He had thought it was the soup. But he had taken his coat off, and he was still hot, and his face was hot, and there was sweat on his forehead and on his upper lip and down the back of his neck. And at the same time, under the heat, in his bones, in the back of his skull, he was cold. Shivering cold. A small steady shiver that came and went, like a dog shaking water off itself.
He knew what it meant.
He had seen it before. On men he had knelt beside, in the mud of the Lower Docks and the back rooms of the Ink Quarter, with their wounds bound up in dirty rags. He had seen it in the jars. The fever that came after the wound. Hot and cold at once. It came on the second day, mostly, or the third. And then either it broke, or it didn't.
It is the rod, said Malakor. It was dirty. It had been in the straw. In the horse dung. In the turnips at the gate. It went deep.
I know.
You need a doctor.
I know.
And you are going to do nothing about it, because you have a cellar to map, and a boy to bargain with, and a girl asleep beside you who has just given away her mother's last words, and you do not want to be the next thing she has to carry.
Corin put his bowl down on the brick.
He did it carefully, so that it would not make a sound and wake her. His hands were shaking. He looked at them. The gray wool gloves, and under them the gray. He pressed them flat on his knees until they stopped.
At the far end of the arch, on his sack, Wick had finished his soup. He put the bowl down. He wiped the spoon on his sleeve, very carefully, and laid it in the bowl. Then he picked up a scrap of paper from the nearest pile, and looked at it, and laid it on another pile.
Tap. Tap.
Corin watched him.
He thought of Lord Thorne, under the hay. Tap tap tap tap. He thought of Moll at his desk in the Watch house, tapping in fours and twos while he lied about a cloth. He thought of a hand opening on his palm in the gold light, and one finger coming down. One.
And of Wick, who tapped, but only ever twice.
He did not know what it meant. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps everybody who had been emptied tapped, the way everybody who had been drowned went blue. Perhaps the hands went on doing something when there was nobody left to tell them to stop.
He took out the Ledger again and wrote, very small, in the margin of the page before Lyra's.
Wick taps. Twice. Always twice. Lord Thorne taps four, two, nine, three. Moll taps fours and twos. Is it the same thing? Ask Moll.
Then he put the pencil back in the spine, and leaned his hot head against the cold brick wall of the arch, and shut his eyes, and waited for the shivering to stop.
It did not.
The Hollow Men
Mother Grue came and sat beside him a little after the ninth bell.
She came slowly, leaning on her stick, with her shawls trailing on the stained brick behind her like the train of a gown. She lowered herself onto the next sack, between Corin and the brazier, with a long sigh and a crack of old knees. She did not say anything for a while. She held out her root hands to the coals, and looked at the fire, and Corin looked at it too.
Down the tunnel, the arches were settling for the night. People were lying down on their rags and sacks, in heaps, in families. Somebody was singing to a child, very low. Somebody else was snoring. The man with the new face was sitting with his back to the wall in the second arch, with his eyes open, touching his own cheek, again and again, as if to make sure it was still there.
"He's asking for you," said Mother Grue.
Corin turned his head.
"Gideon," said Mother Grue. "He's been walking up and down the sixth arch since the soup. Up and down. Up and down. Like a dog that's lost something." She sniffed. "He's thinking. He said he would. He's never in his life thought about anything that long."
"He said he'd tell me in the morning."
"He won't last till morning." Mother Grue held her hands closer to the coals. "He's not used to wanting things. It's like a stone in his boot. He'll want it out by midnight."
Corin was quiet.
"How long has he been here?" he said. "With you. In the arches."
Mother Grue did not answer at once.
"Since he was eight," she said. "Since they let him out from under the Bell. He walked here. On his own. In the snow, with no boots. I found him sitting in the mouth of the first arch in the morning, quite blue, with his chin on his knees, watching the river." She shook her head slowly. "I said, where's your mam? And he said, she's sad. And I said, where's your dad? And he said, gone carrying." She was quiet. "That's all he'd say. Gone carrying. Like it was a place."
"Carrying what?"
"For the Lady." Mother Grue looked at him with her black-currant eyes. "You didn't know? No. You wouldn't. Why would you." She turned back to the fire. "His dad was a docker. Lower Docks. Big man. Huge. Him and his brother, both of them. The Hollow boys, they called them, on the wharf. Not because of what they were. Because that was their name. Hollow. Old Lower Docks name. Half the graves in Saint Bride's yard say Hollow on them."
Corin did not move.
"Big Gideon Hollow," said Mother Grue. "And his brother Saul. The two of them could lift a hogshead of rum between them like it was a basket of eggs. They went to the war together, when they were young. Came back together. Never said a word about it, either of them. Not one word, in all the years." She paused. "And then, when the boy was four, the docks went bad. No ships. No work. And a man came down the wharf one morning in a plum coat and said the Lady at the Bourse wanted big men for carrying. Good pay. Steady. Every day." She looked at the coals. "And they went. The two of them. Together. Like they did everything."
"And?"
"And they never came home." Mother Grue's voice did not change. "Not that night. Not the next. Not ever. His mam went up to the Bourse to ask, and they told her at the kitchen door that there were no Hollows working there. Never had been. And shut the door in her face." She was quiet for a long time. "That's when she started being sad. That's when she went and borrowed a happy day off the Lady, to put in her, like a warm stone. And you know the rest of that."
Corin did know the rest of that. It was in the Ledger. So they took me instead. To pay. I was eight.
He sat on his sack with his hot face and his cold bones and the throbbing in his leg, and he thought about two huge men in the cellars of the Bourse. Carrying crates. Up and down. All day. Together. Who never talked. Who never looked at you when you walked past.
One of them used to hum.
He thought of Vespera's knife stopping, a quarter of an inch from his face.
"Did he sing?" said Corin. "Big Gideon. Did he ever—"
Mother Grue turned her head sharply and looked at him.
"Hum," she said. "He hummed. Always. Not a song. Just a tune. The same one, over and over, under his breath, all day long, while he carried. You could hear him coming down the wharf before you saw him." Her old eyes had narrowed in their nest of wrinkles. "How did you know that?"
Corin did not answer.
He did not know how to answer. He did not know, yet, if he was right. It was only a thing a boy had said, and a thing an old woman had said, and the place where they touched. It was a thread. It was not a rope.
And if he was right—
He looked down the tunnel of arches, toward the sixth, where a thin boy in a too-big coat was walking up and down, up and down, in the dark, thinking about what he wanted.
You are going to tell him, said Malakor quietly.
Not yet.
Why not?
Because I don't know. Because it's a thread, not a rope. Corin closed his eyes. And because he can't feel it. If I tell him his father is in that cellar, carrying crates, emptied, he'll say thank you and ask me what time we're going. And then I'll have to watch him not feel it.
The voice was quiet for a moment.
That is not a reason, it said. That is a fear.
I know.
Mother Grue was still looking at him.
"Harvester," she said. "You know something."
"I don't know anything," said Corin. "Not yet."
She looked at him a moment longer with her small black eyes. Then she grunted, and turned back to the fire.
"When you do," she said, "you tell me first. Before him." She held her root hands to the coals. "I'm the one that'll have to pick up whatever's left."
She was quiet for a moment, with her twisted hands spread to the coals.
"I've been picking up what's left for forty years," she said. Not to him, really. To the fire. "That's what the arches are. Did you know? That's all they've ever been. Not a home. Nobody lives here because they want to. It's where the city puts down what it's finished with." She turned her hands over. "Old soldiers that came home with half their heads. Girls the Lady used up. Boys like him. Men like Wick, who said the wrong thing in the wrong tavern. Mothers with no husbands and no rooms. Children with no mothers." She sniffed. "They come down the river steps, one by one, and they stand in the mouth of the first arch, the way he did, and they look in. And I say, come in, then. Mind the step."
Corin did not say anything.
"My mother kept the arches before me," said Mother Grue. "And her mother before her. There's always been a Grue under the Dye-Works. The city doesn't know our name. The city doesn't want to. But the river does." She looked at him sideways out of her shawls with her black-currant eyes. "And now there's a harvester in my third arch with a hole in his leg and a book in his coat and a look on his face like he's found a thing he wishes he hadn't."
She went back to looking at the coals.
"I'll pick that up too," she said. "When it comes. Whatever it is. I always do."
Corin did not answer.
But he took out the Ledger, and wrote it down, very small, in the margin under the note about Wick.
Gideon's father: Big Gideon HOLLOW, docker, Lower Docks. His brother Saul. Huge. Went to the war together, came back together, never spoke of it. Went carrying for the Bourse when the boy was four. Never came home. The Bourse said no Hollows ever worked there. Big Gideon hummed. Always the same tune.
The two porters who carry the crates in the lower rooms. Gideon says one of them used to hum.
Tell Mother Grue first.
He was writing the last word when he heard the footsteps.
Quick, light, even. Coming up the tunnel from the sixth arch. Not walking up and down any more. Coming straight.
He closed the Ledger.
Gideon Has Thought
Gideon stopped at the edge of the brazier's light.
He stood there, in his too-big coat and his too-small boots, with his thin hands hanging at his sides, and looked at Corin. Then at Mother Grue. Then at Lyra, asleep under the horse blanket. Then back at Corin.
"I've thought," he said.
"It's not morning," said Mother Grue.
"No." Gideon did not seem troubled by this. "I thought faster than I thought I would. I didn't know I could." He considered it. "I thought about it walking. Up and down. It's easier walking. Things go round in your head when you walk, and come back, and you can look at them again."
"Sit down, then," said Mother Grue. "Before you fall down. You've walked a mile up and down that arch."
Gideon sat down.
He did it the way he did everything, slowly and all at once, folding his thin legs under him on the stained brick in front of the brazier, so that he was sitting cross-legged facing Corin, with the fire on one side of him and the dark on the other. The light lay along half of his face. The other half was in shadow. His pale eyes were quite calm.
"Do you want to hear it?" he said.
"Yes," said Corin.
"All of it? Or one thing at a time?"
Corin looked at him.
"One thing at a time," he said.
Gideon nodded, as if that were the right answer. As if he had been told, once, that it was polite to let people choose.
"Mother Grue says you have to say what you want properly," he said. "Out loud. So everybody hears. Then it's fair. Mister Gant said the same, when I was selling, but Mother Grue says it's the only thing she and Mister Gant ever agreed on." He looked at Mother Grue. Mother Grue snorted. "So I'm going to say it properly. And you can say yes or no to each thing. And if you say no to one, I'll think again. But I won't think again tonight. I'm tired of thinking."
"All right."
Gideon put his hands flat on his knees. He closed his eyes. Lightly. The way he had closed them in Mrs. Marta's kitchen, Corin thought, or wherever it had been, a long time ago, when he told the route of Tam's last walk. The Ledger said so. He closed his eyes like a child saying a prayer it has said every night of its life.
"For the map," said Gideon, "I want three things."
He opened his eyes.
The arch was very quiet. Even the snoring down the tunnel seemed to have stopped. Mother Grue had folded her root hands in the lap of her shawls and was watching the boy with her small black eyes, and not moving at all. Beside Corin, under the horse blanket, Lyra stirred, and sighed, and lay still again.
Corin waited.
And while he waited, the fever came up through him in a long slow wave, hot and then cold, from his leg to the top of his skull. The arch tipped. The brazier swam. He put one hand flat on the brick beside him and pressed until it stopped.
Gideon watched him do it. With mild interest.
"You're ill," he said.
"It's nothing."
"It's your leg." Gideon looked at the leg, stretched out on the sack in front of Corin, with Lyra's bandage on it under the trouser. "It smells. I can smell it from here. Like the boys under the Bell who cut their feet on the glass and didn't tell." He said it without any feeling at all. "Two of them died."
Mother Grue's head turned sharply toward Corin.
Corin did not look at her.
"Tell me the first thing," he said to Gideon.
Gideon looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded.
"The first thing," he said, "is boots."
