Chapter 37: Gideon's Price, Part 4 of 5: Doctor Silas
Doctor Silas
When he woke, it was gray.
Not the gray of the fever, or the gray of the grass going to ash. The plain, thin, ordinary gray of a winter morning, coming in through the crack in the roof of the fourth arch, where Vespera's needles hung on their rope. He was lying on his back on something soft. Sacks, and a blanket. His head was on a rolled-up coat. He could see the needles over him, hundreds of them on their threads, turning very slowly in the draft. Chiming, very softly. Like rain on glass.
He was cold.
That was the first thing he understood. He was cold. Ordinary cold. Not the burning, shaking, inside-out cold of the night. Just a man lying in a brick arch in winter with not enough blankets. His face was cool. His hands were cool. He was very weak. He felt as if somebody had taken all his bones out in the night and put them back in a slightly different order.
And his leg hurt. Not the hot, deep, living throb. A new hurt. Clean and sharp and sore, like a fresh cut.
"Don't move it," said a voice.
Corin turned his head.
There was a man sitting on the broken kitchen chair beside him. A tall man, so tall that sitting on the low chair his knees came up nearly to his chest, folded like a heron's legs. He was thin. Very thin. His long arms were folded across his knees, and his long hands hung down from them, clean and steady. His coat was black and old and shiny at the elbows, and his collar was not clean, and he had crooked spectacles on a long nose, and behind the spectacles his eyes were tired. Very tired. The tiredest eyes Corin had ever seen. Careful, and kind, and so tired that they seemed to have gone past being tired into something else.
"Doctor Silas," said Corin. His voice came out as a croak.
"So you do remember me. That's something." Doctor Silas did not smile. "You were calling me other names in the night. Several of them. Most of them were Malakor." He looked at Corin over his crooked spectacles. "I don't want to know."
Corin did not tell him.
"Your leg," said Doctor Silas. "Since you'll want to know, and since you won't believe anybody else. The rod went in about five inches. It missed the bone and the big vessel, by the width of my thumb. It didn't miss the dung and the straw and whatever else was on it. By last night you had a pocket of poison in there the size of a hen's egg." He said it the way a clerk reads out a bill. "I opened it. I drained it. I washed it out with something that would have made you scream if you'd been awake, and you weren't, so you didn't. I packed it with Mother Grue's honey and a lot of clean lint, and I left it open, so it can drain. It's going to hurt for a week. It's going to leave a hole you can put your finger in, for a year. And if you walk on it, Mr. Vane, it is going to open again, and fill again, and the next time, I will not be close enough to come."
Corin lay still.
"How long?" he said.
"Until you can walk on it?" Doctor Silas considered. "Properly? Two weeks. Without a stick? A month." He paused. "Without dying? That's up to you."
"I have to walk on it tonight."
Doctor Silas did not say anything for a moment.
He looked at Corin over his crooked spectacles with his tired, careful eyes. Then he took off the spectacles, and rubbed the bridge of his long nose between his finger and thumb, and put them back on again, a little more crooked than before.
"Of course you do," he said.
He did not argue. Corin had expected him to argue. He sat on the broken chair with his long arms on his knees and looked at the needles turning overhead, and did not say anything at all.
"Gideon," said Corin. "His feet. Mother Grue was going to send—"
"I've seen to Gideon."
Something in the way he said it made Corin go still.
"I took two toes off him," said Doctor Silas. "At the second bell. The two big ones. They were dead. They'd been dead since Tuesday, by the look. Another week and it would have been the feet. Another week after that, the legs." His voice did not change at all. "He sat on Mother Grue's knee and watched me do it. With great interest. He asked me what I was going to do with them afterward. I said burn them. He asked if he could watch that too." He was quiet for a moment. "He didn't feel a thing. Not the knife. Not the needle. Nothing."
Corin shut his eyes.
"He said to tell you," said Doctor Silas, "that he still wants the boots. He said he'll need smaller ones now. He said he thought that might be cheaper."
Corin did not open his eyes for a long time.
When he did, Doctor Silas was looking at him.
"I've had eleven of them through the crypt since the autumn," said the doctor quietly. "Hollow ones. Children. Feet. Hands. Ears. One boy with a burn on his back the size of a dinner plate, from lying against a brazier all night to keep warm and not knowing it was burning him." He looked at his long clean hands. "They come in walking. They don't cry. They sit on the end of the bed and watch me cut, and ask questions. And then they go away again. And some of them come back next month with something else gone."
He was quiet.
"And that's not even the worst of it," he said. "Not this winter."
Corin waited.
Doctor Silas did not say anything for a while. He looked at the needles. He looked at the long table of Vespera's inks, and the old blue pot with the gold rim, which was not there any more, Corin noticed. Vespera had taken it. He looked at the crack in the roof, and the gray light coming down.
"The fever children," he said at last. "In the crypt. You remember them."
"Yes." It was in the Ledger. Saint Bride's crypt. Two rows of beds. Children with bright glassy eyes, speaking in other people's voices. A girl of four in braids, saying in an old man's voice: two bad knees and a cough. The slag. Other people's burned Ash, dumped in the river by the Ministry carts, getting into the children who lived by the water. Leaking into them. Filling them with strangers.
"There were thirty in the autumn," said Doctor Silas. "There are fifty-two now. I've run out of beds. I've got them on the floor." He paused. "That's not the worst thing either. I expected more. It's winter. The river's low. The slag sits on the mud at low tide and the children play on the mud. It's always worse in winter."
"Then what?"
Doctor Silas took off his spectacles again.
"They're draining faster," he said.
Corin did not understand.
"The slag goes in," said Doctor Silas. "The stranger's Ash. And it sits in them, and it talks through them, and if I'm lucky, if I'm very lucky, I can draw it off. Slowly. Over weeks. With glass and patience." He turned the spectacles in his long fingers. "That's how it was in the autumn. Weeks. Now it's days. I put a glass on a child in the morning, and by the evening it's cloudy, and by the next morning it's full. They're giving it up so fast. Too fast. As if something's loosened it in them. As if something's been put into them that makes it come away."
The needles chimed overhead.
"And when it comes away," said Doctor Silas, very quietly, "it doesn't come away clean. It takes things with it. Their own things. A name. A face. The way home." He put the spectacles back on. "I had a girl last week who drained in a day and a half. Six years old. When the glass was full, she didn't know her own mother. Her mother was sitting right there, on the end of the bed, holding her hand."
Corin lay very still on the sacks.
"Something's in them," said Doctor Silas. "Something that wasn't there in the autumn. I don't know what. I can smell it, sometimes. On their breath. Sweet. Like a sweet shop." His mouth twisted. "I've written to the Ministry. Twice. To the Office of Public Health. They wrote back and thanked me for my interest and said the matter was under review."
The Office of Public Health. Corin did not know why the words should make the back of his neck go cold. They were only words. The name of an office, on the Hill, full of clerks.
Bulk lots, he thought. For the trade. Lower Docks. By the crate.
Kindnesses, small. Suitable for blending.
"Where do the drained glasses go?" he said. "When they're full. What do you do with them?"
Doctor Silas looked at him.
"What do you think I do with them?" he said. "I can't keep them. I've no shelves. I've no Archive. I'm one doctor in a crypt with fifty-two children on the floor." He was quiet for a moment. "A cart comes. Every Friday. From the Ministry. To take the slag away. To the proper place, they say. For disposal." He paused. "A cart with no crest on its door, and a driver in a plum-colored scarf."
The arch was very quiet.
Plum, thought Corin.
Everything at the Bourse is plum.
He did not say it out loud. He did not need to. He saw Doctor Silas see him think it. The tired eyes behind the crooked spectacles went very still, and stayed on his face.
"You're going in there," said Doctor Silas softly. "Aren't you. Tonight. With that leg. Wherever it is. That's what this is."
"Yes," said Corin.
Doctor Silas looked at him for a long, long time.
Then he unfolded his long legs, one at a time, and stood up off the broken chair, slowly, the way a heron gets up off a riverbank. He stood over Corin with his head bent under the curve of the arch and his long hands hanging at his sides.
"Then if you find out what's in my children," he said, "you come and tell me. Before you die of that leg." He paused. "If you can manage it in that order."
What Nell Saw
Nell brought him water after the doctor had gone.
She came in through the doorway from the third arch with a tin mug held in both hands, very carefully, so that it would not spill, and knelt down beside his sacks and held it out. It was cold, and it was very faintly blue, and it tasted of the dye and the brick and the cold, and he drank all of it.
She took the mug back. She did not get up.
She knelt there on the stained brick in her brown dress, with her broom-callused hands folded round the empty mug, and looked at him. She was fourteen, Mother Grue had said. She looked younger. She had a small pointed face, and a great deal of dark curly hair tied back with a rag, and a way of looking at things very hard, as if she were trying to learn them by heart before somebody took them away.
"You were very bad," she said. "In the night."
"I know."
"You don't know. You weren't there." She said it quite seriously. "I mean you were. But you weren't. You were talking. To somebody who wasn't there. You kept saying where are they going. Over and over. And then you'd say a name, and then you'd stop."
Corin did not move.
"What name?" he said.
Nell thought about it. Her small face screwed up.
"I don't know," she said at last. "It was all mumbled. It started with an E, I think. Or an L. I couldn't hear." She looked at the mug. "Miss Thorne heard. She was right by your head. She heard it all. But she didn't say."
Corin lay very still on the sacks.
"What else?" he said.
Nell looked at him for a moment, as if deciding whether she was allowed to tell.
"The doctor came at the first bell," she said. "Tobin fetched him. He ran all the way to Saint Bride's in the dark. The doctor came with his bag and he looked at your leg and he said a word I'm not supposed to say, and then he said knife." She swallowed. "And Miss Thorne said no. Like that. Loud. And the doctor said yes, or he loses the leg. And she looked at him for a long time. And then she said what do you need."
The needles turned overhead, chiming.
"He needed somebody to hold you," said Nell. "Your leg. Hard. So it wouldn't move. Because you were moving. Shaking all over. And Mother Grue's too old, and Vespera was boiling the water, and I'm too little." She looked down. "So Miss Thorne did it. She held your leg. Both hands. And she held it while he cut."
Corin shut his eyes.
"You screamed," said Nell. "Once. Right at the start. And then you didn't. You went quiet. Very quiet. And Miss Thorne was holding your leg, and she was white as a sheet, and the doctor was cutting, and there was—" She stopped. "There was a lot. A lot of stuff. It smelled. And she didn't let go. She didn't even look away. She just held on."
She was quiet for a moment.
"And she sang," said Nell.
Corin opened his eyes.
"While he cut," said Nell. "She sang. Not loud. Just low. To you. A song about a river." She frowned, trying to remember. "The river runs down and the river runs wide. Something like that. And something about a little fish. And the stars sitting on a sail." She looked at him. "I didn't know Miss Thorne could sing. She's got a funny voice. All rough. But she sang it all the way through. Twice. And then she started again."
Corin did not say anything.
He could not.
He lay on the sacks in the gray light under the turning needles, with the clean sharp ache in his leg where a doctor had opened it and a girl had held it shut, and thought of a woman on a barge in the fog, singing to her son. So sleep, little fish, while the water goes by. Lyra had heard it once. Only once, through the cabin wall, while she knelt in the hay with her father. And she had remembered it.
She had sung it to him while he screamed.
And he did not remember any of it.
No, said Malakor softly. You do not. You were not there. You were in the grass.
"And Gideon?" he said, when he could.
"Gideon watched," said Nell. Her face changed. "The whole time. From Mother Grue's knee. With his feet all bandaged where his toes were. He watched the doctor cut you, and he watched Miss Thorne hold on, and he listened to her singing." She was quiet. "And when it was over he said, Is that what singing's for?"
"What did she say?"
"She didn't say anything," said Nell. "She was crying. She'd stopped singing and she was crying. Not loud. Just crying, with her hands still on your leg." Nell looked down at the mug. "Gideon looked at her for a long time. And then he said, I'd like to know what that's like. Crying. I don't remember."
The arch was very quiet.
"And then she went out," said Nell. "At the fourth bell. When the doctor said you'd live. She went out on her own, in the dark, and she didn't say where. And she came back at noon with no hair." She looked up at him, very hard, the way she looked at everything. "I'm not supposed to tell you about the hair. She said. She said she'd tell you herself."
"She did," said Corin.
"Oh." Nell considered this. "Then I haven't told you anything."
She got up off the brick, with the empty mug, and went to the doorway. And stopped, and turned round.
"She cut it with the doctor's knife," she said. "The one he used on your leg. She washed it first. In the trough." A pause. "She said it was the sharpest one there was."
Then she went out.
Tonight
Lyra came back at the noon bell.
Corin heard her before he saw her. Her quick steps on the stained brick of the tunnel, coming from the river end. Nell's steps beside her, quicker, almost running. And a sound he did not know. A soft, heavy, swinging bump, like a sack being carried.
He was sitting up by then. Doctor Silas had gone back to his crypt at the tenth bell, folding himself out through the arches like a heron through reeds, with his bag. Before he went he had bound Corin's leg again, tight, from the knee to the hip, with clean linen over the lint and the honey, and propped it on the crate. And then, without a word, he had taken a stick from a bundle of them against the wall, a long ash stick with a fork at the top, and padded the fork with rag, and leaned it against the wall beside Corin's sacks. And gone.
Corin had looked at the crutch for a long time.
Then Lyra came through the doorway from the third arch, and he stopped looking at it.
She was carrying a sack over her shoulder. A big one, lumpy, with the smell of new bread coming out of it, so strong and warm in the cold arch that every head in the tunnel had turned as she passed. And in her other hand, by their laces, swinging, a pair of boots.
Small ones. Brown leather. Not new. But whole. With soles that did not flap, and toes that were not worn through, and proper laces, knotted together.
She was wearing the brown arches dress, and Ellis's flat cap.
And under the cap, there was no hair.
Or not none. There was some. It came down from under the cap to just below her ears, and stopped. Short and ragged and uneven, as if it had been cut very quickly with a knife, or with a pair of shears that were not sharp, by somebody who was in a hurry. It stuck out at the back. It was darker at the cut ends than he remembered, where the light had not reached it.
She had had a great deal of hair. He had it written down. Dark. Long. To the middle of her back when it's down. She ties it up with whatever is nearest. He had seen it this morning, before the fever. Spread out on the sack beside him under the horse blanket, dark against the brown wool, while she slept.
It was gone.
She stopped in the doorway and looked at him. Sitting up. With his eyes open and his face, he supposed, a more ordinary color than it had been in the night.
Something went across her face. Fast. Gone. Like the shadow of a bird.
"You're awake," she said.
"Yes."
"Doctor Silas said you'd wake at noon. He said if you didn't wake at noon—" She stopped. "Well. You're awake."
She put down the sack of bread. Carefully, on the brick, by the brazier. She put the boots down beside it.
Corin looked at the boots.
Then he looked at her hair.
She saw him look. She lifted her chin.
"There's a wigmaker," she said, "on Comb Street. Behind the fish market. Madame Hesper sends girls to him. He pays well for long dark hair that's never been dyed. The ladies on the Hill want it for the winter fashion." Her rough voice was perfectly steady. "He gave me two crowns and four shillings. That's boots, and a loaf for the fifth arch every day till Midwinter, and a little over." She took a small cloth purse from the pocket of the brown dress and held it out to him. "The little over is for you. For whatever you need tonight."
Corin did not take it.
He sat on his sacks with his leg on the crate and looked at her.
"You didn't have to," he said.
"No."
"I said I'd find it. I said I'd—"
"I know what you said." She put the purse down on the crate beside his leg. "And you were lying on the floor of this arch at midnight with your eyes rolled back and Doctor Silas cutting your leg open with a knife, and Gideon sitting on Mother Grue's knee watching his own toes come off." Her voice did not rise. "So I found it instead."
She turned away. She picked up the boots by their laces again.
"Where is he?"
"The sixth arch," said Corin. "Mother Grue made him lie down."
Lyra nodded. She went to go. Then she stopped, in the doorway, with the boots swinging from her hand, and did not turn round.
"It'll grow," she said. "Hair grows. That's the thing about it. It isn't like the other things." A pause. "It comes back."
She went through the doorway into the next arch.
Corin sat and looked at the empty doorway for a long time.
Then he took the Ledger out of his coat, and opened it, and wrote.
Sunday. Noon. Lyra cut off her hair and sold it to a wigmaker on Comb Street for two crowns and four shillings. Boots for Gideon, bread for the fifth arch till Midwinter. She says it comes back.
He looked at that line for a while.
Then he wrote under it: The Duke said wear the gray, and a white mask. He did not say anything about her hair.
And under that, because it was true, and because there was nobody to see: I would have given anything for her not to have done it. And I have nothing to give.
They talked about it in the afternoon. All of them. In the fourth arch, under the needles.
Vespera, on her stool, with her writing-covered arms folded. Mother Grue, in her shawls, on a sack. Lyra, on the end of the door-table, with her short ragged hair and Ellis's cap in her lap. Gideon, on the floor, cross-legged, in his new brown boots, which he kept looking at. Nell, by the doorway. And Corin, in the broken chair, with his leg on the crate and the ash crutch leaning against the wall beside him.
"Tonight," said Corin.
Nobody said anything.
"It has to be tonight," he said. "Gideon says the stool is empty on Sundays from the ninth bell to the eleventh. Coker goes to the Bell. Next Sunday is the night before Midwinter. Every lot will be in the building. Every guard Vivienne has. And it'll be Coker's nephew on the stool." He looked at them. "Tonight is the only night."
"For what?" said Vespera. "What are you going in for, Corin? You're not stealing Lot 41 tonight. It's not even in the building yet, if it's in the fourth tier. Vivienne won't have it brought up from wherever she keeps it till the day." She looked at him hard. "So what's tonight for?"
Corin did not answer at once.
"To see," he said. "The way in. The ring passage. The lamps. The iron door that hums. The wet door. Whether Gideon's map is still true. Whether anything's changed since he was there." He paused. "If I go in for the first time on Midwinter Night, with a tray in my hands and Vivienne's whole household round me, I'll be going in blind. I need to have walked it."
"And that's all?"
Corin looked at her.
Vespera looked back. Her plain wide face did not move.
"No," said Corin. "That's not all."
He did not say the rest. He did not say the crates with the white mark. He did not say Nym. He did not say two men on a bench, side by side, with their hands on their knees. He did not look at Gideon, on the floor in his new boots.
But Vespera's eyes went to Gideon. And stayed there for a moment. And came back.
"No," she said quietly. "I didn't think it was."
"I'm coming," said Lyra.
"No."
"I'm coming, Corin. You can't walk. You'll need somebody—"
"I'll need somebody outside." He looked at her. "In Cinder Lane. In the dark. By the coal chute. With a light and a lookout and a way out if it goes wrong. Somebody who'll know if Coker comes back early." He paused. "Somebody who isn't going to fall down two nines and four of stone steps in the dark with a hole in her leg."
Lyra opened her mouth.
Then she shut it again.
"And the lamps?" said Vespera. "The gray ones. By the doors that matter. You can't walk under them with those hands. Not tonight. I can't bind you in an afternoon."
"Then I won't walk under them," said Corin. "Gideon knows where they are." He looked at the boy. "Gideon will take me round them."
Gideon looked up from his boots.
"Yes," he said. "I know where they are. I'll take you round." He considered. "There's one by the wet door. You can't get round that one. You'll have to go under it."
Nobody said anything.
"Then I won't go to the wet door," said Corin.
Vespera looked at him for a long time.
"You're lying," she said.
"Yes," said Corin.
Vespera did not say anything else. She got up off her stool, and went to her door-table, and began, very slowly, to unstopper little pots.
