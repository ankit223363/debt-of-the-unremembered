Chapter 76: Oren's Watch, Part 2 of 5: Sef Goes Up
Sef Goes Up
Sef came out of the heap an hour before dawn.
Oren heard him first. The rustle of the hides. A soft grunt. Then a thin dark shape stood up at the edge of the ring of shades and stretched, slowly, with both arms over its head, and looked round.
It saw Oren on the hoof. It came over.
Sef was the quiet one of the Ashborn. Quieter than Brin, even, though Brin hardly spoke at all. Brin was quiet the way a big rock is quiet. Sef was quiet the way a fox is. He was thin and brown and not very tall, with the same short-shaved head as the others and the same gray line down his forehead to his nose, and a long plait at the back tied with blue thread instead of red. He walked very lightly. Even on the felt, even on the glass, Oren could hardly hear him walk.
He stopped beside the horse and looked up at Oren. Then he looked back south. The way they had come.
"I'll go up," he said in the southern tongue. Slowly. He did not have much of it. "Look back."
Oren nodded.
There was a swell a little way to the south of the camp. Not a big one. The Glass out here was mostly flat, but not all of it. Every so often it rose up into a long low hump, smooth and round, like the back of some great animal lying asleep under a sheet. From the top of one you could see a long way. Further than you would think. Because there was nothing in the way.
Sef went off toward it. Oren watched him go. A thin dark shape against the double stars, going away, getting smaller, until he was a mark on the glass, and then a shape on the top of the swell, standing very still, with one hand up to shade his eyes, though there was no sun to shade them from. Old habit. All the Ashborn did it.
He stood there a long time.
Oren waited. He did not like waiting. Nobody likes waiting. But he had done a great deal of it, in thirty years, and he was good at it. You did not fidget. You did not think about what might be coming. You sat, and you breathed, and you kept your hand near your sword, and you let the time go past you like water past a post.
The sky in the east had begun to change. Not much. Not light yet. Just a little less black. A gray so dark you could not have said it was gray, unless you had been looking at black all night.
Sef came back down off the swell.
He came faster than he had gone. Not running. The Ashborn did not run on the Glass, unless they had to. But fast. And when he got to the horse he stood there for a moment breathing through his nose, and then he said a word in his own tongue, and then he shook his head and found the southern one.
"Lights," he said.
Oren got up off the hoof.
"The iron?"
"No." Sef shook his head again. "Iron is there. Far. Still. Frozen. Three. Like yesterday." He held up three fingers. Then he put that hand down, and held up the other. Four fingers. "But behind. Much behind. Lights. Four. Small." He made a shape with his hand, a little swinging shape, like a man carrying something by a handle. "Moving."
"Moving now? In the dark?"
"Moving now." Sef looked at him. "Toward us. Slow. But walking."
Oren stood very still.
The iron things froze at night. Everybody knew that. When the sun went down the cold got into their joints, into the tick-tick-tick of their insides, and they stopped. Wherever they were. In the middle of a step, sometimes. And stood there till the sun came up and warmed them. That was why the Ashborn walked at night. That was the whole of it.
But lanterns did not freeze. And the hands that carried lanterns did not freeze, either. Not if they were in good gloves.
"Men," said Oren. "Not the iron ones."
Sef nodded.
"The man with the silver stick?" said Oren. "Arran's man? Come back?"
Sef thought about it. Then he shook his head.
"He was one," he said. "Alone. Northwest. These are four. From the south. From the Edge." He looked back toward the swell, as if he could still see them from here. "They walk together. In a line. Like soldiers."
Like soldiers.
Oren felt it then. That thing at the back of his neck. The cold finger. Not the Glass. Something older than the Glass. Something he had felt on a hundred nights in the war, lying in a ditch, when the sentry came back and said there's somebody out there.
He looked south himself. He could not see them, from here. There was nothing to see. Only the dark glass going away under the stars, and the line where it met the sky. But he knew they were there now. Four little lights. Coming on through the night, steadily, over ground where the iron things had stopped. Not afraid of the cold. Not afraid of the dark.
Who walks on the Glass at night, and is not afraid?
He did not know. But he did not like any of the answers.
"Wake Kestrel," he said. "Quietly. Don't wake the rest. Not yet."
Sef went.
Oren stood beside the horse with his sword in his hand, point down, and looked south, at nothing, and waited for the dawn.
A Soldier Wakes
Kestrel came out of the heap without a sound, the way she did everything, and listened to Sef, and went up the swell herself, and came back down. She did not say much. She said, We see them, and Four, and Men, and then she looked at Oren for a long moment with her sharp dark eyes, and he knew she was thinking the same things he was thinking, and liking them as little.
Then she went to wake the others. Because the dawn was coming, and the shades had to go up, and that did not wait for anybody's lights.
Oren watched them come out. One by one. Out from under the hides into the gray, cold, nearly-light air, stiff and slow and blinking, the way people always come out of a heap. Brin first, big and slow, rubbing his face and wincing on his blistered heels. Oda, quick, already reaching for her spear. Marrit, all bent over, muttering. Vespera, very slowly, one hand and then the other. Tansy, wide awake at once, the way children are, already talking.
And then the boy.
Oren had been waiting to see how he would come out. Whether he would come out at all. Whether they would have to lift him onto a pole like Arran, or lead him by the hand. Whether he would know where he was.
He did not do any of those things.
He came out the way Oren came out.
It happened very fast. One moment the hides were lumped over him, still. The next they were thrown back, all at once, with one arm. And the boy was up. Not sitting up and then getting up, the way a man does in the morning. Up. In one movement. Off his back and onto his right foot, with the stiff left leg swung out straight to the side for balance, and the ash crutch somehow already in his left hand. Oren had not seen him reach for it. It had been lying on the hide beside him, and then it was under his arm, and he was standing.
Standing on the ball of his right foot. Not the heel. The ball. Weight forward. Knee a little bent. Ready to go any way at all.
And his eyes were not on the people round him. Not on Lyra, who was sitting up in the heap with her hand out toward him. Not on Vespera, or Tansy, or the silent man. His eyes had gone straight past all of them, south. The way they had come. The way in.
Then they moved. Quickly. South, and then a little to the east, along the line of the swell. Then to the west. Then back south. Checking. Oren knew exactly what they were checking, because his own eyes did it every morning of his life without asking him. Where can they come from? Where is the low ground? Where is the high ground? How far can I see? Where would I put a man, if I were them?
The boy's eyes went over all of it in the time it takes to breathe in. And then stopped, on the swell. Where Sef had stood. Where you could see furthest.
And the boy's head tilted, a little. As if to say, There. That's where I'd watch from.
Oren felt something go cold in his stomach.
It was not fear. Not quite. It was the thing you feel when you look in a dark window at night and see a face looking back, and it takes you a heartbeat to know it is your own.
Because that was him. That was Oren. That was the way he had come up off the ground every morning since he was a boy in the war. The ball of the foot. The weight forward. The eyes on the way in before anything else. Before your friends. Before your breakfast. Before you even knew you were awake. He had learned it in a ditch, with a sergeant's boot in his ribs. He had learned it so well that he could not unlearn it now if he tried. And he had tried. Lord Thorne had laughed at him for it, once, the first year at the house. Oren, you get up like a man who expects to be shot.
And the boy had never been in a war. The boy was a clerk. A harvester. A man of papers and jars and Ledgers. Oren had seen him get up in the mornings a dozen times, in the crypt, in the barn, in the inn at Haverstock. He got up like a clerk. Slowly. Grumbling. Feeling about for his boots with his eyes shut. Asking what time it was.
Not like this.
This was not the boy's. It was hers.
The boy stood there for a breath longer, on the ball of his foot, with his eyes on the swell.
Then something in his face changed. It went soft. Puzzled. He blinked. And he looked down at himself, standing there, and at the crutch under his arm, as if he did not know how he had got there. As if he had woken up in the middle of a step. And his weight came back off the ball of his foot, onto the heel, and his shoulders came down, and he swayed a little, and put the end of the crutch down hard on the glass to steady himself.
"Oh," he said. Hoarse. To nobody.
Then he turned his head and saw Lyra. And his face changed again. Into something Oren knew. Something that was the boy's, all the way through.
He smiled at her. Tired. Crooked. A little lost.
And Lyra got up out of the heap and went to him.
Oren looked away. Some things a man does not need to watch.
He looked at the swell instead. Where the boy's eyes had stopped. Where you could see furthest.
She had known that, then. Sola. She had come up off the ground in the mornings and looked for the high place before anything else. The way he did. The way his grandfather had, perhaps, and his great-grandfather, and all of them, back and back, to the woman on the iron horse.
Twenty years younger. That was what he had seen, when the boy came up out of the hides. Not the boy at all. Himself, twenty years ago. Before the scar. Before the gray. With all his words.
He did not like it.
He did not know why he did not like it. But he did not.
Shades Up
The sun came up the way it always did on the Glass. All at once.
One moment the east was gray. The next there was a thin line of white fire along the edge of the world, so bright that Oren could not look at it, and the whole Glass lit up with it at the same moment, from one side to the other, the way a dark room lights up when somebody throws open a shutter. Every swell and hollow went gold. Every crack shone. The double stars went out, above and below. And the long shadow of the iron horse came sliding out across the glass toward the west, as long as a street, with Oren's own shadow lying beside it, and the small shadows of everyone else.
And with the light, almost at once, came the heat.
Not much yet. Not like noon. But you could feel it on your face. On the back of your hands. Coming down out of the sky, and coming up off the ground too, faintly, as the glass began to warm. By the third hour you would not be able to put your bare hand flat on it. By noon it would cook a man's foot through worn felt. Arran knew that now.
So the shades went up.
They did it every dawn, and they were quick at it. The Ashborn were very quick. They had been doing it all their lives. Kestrel shouted, and the hides came off the heap and were shaken out, and the long poles were stood up in their places in a ring, and the hides were lashed between them with the leather cords, slanting, so that they cast the widest shade they could. In the shade, the felt mats went down, and the blankets on top of those, so that nobody had to sit on the glass itself. In the middle went the water, and the food, and Vespera's pot.
Oren helped. He could tie a knot as well as anybody, and better than most, and he was tall enough to reach the tops of the poles without stretching. But he did it slower than usual, because of his back. And twice, bending to pick up a cord, the pain came up through him like a nail and he had to stop, and straighten up, and breathe, and wait for it to go.
Nobody said anything. He was grateful for that.
When the shades were up, he went to look at Arran.
The young man was lying where they had put him in the night, on a pile of hides with his wrapped foot up on a rolled coat. His face was gray under the brown. His lips were cracked. But he was awake, and he grinned when he saw Oren coming, the way he always grinned, as if everything in the world was a very good joke that only he had been told.
"Captain," he said. "Come to see my beautiful foot?"
"Is it beautiful?"
"It's the most beautiful foot on the Glass. Ask Vespera. She's been looking at it all night. She can't keep away."
Vespera was sitting beside him with her bag open on her knees. She did not look up. But Oren saw the corner of her mouth move, just a little.
"It's bad," she said. "It's not going to rot. I've seen to that. But he won't walk on it for a week. Maybe two. And he'll limp for the rest of his life, whatever he says."
"Good," said Arran. "I'm very good at limping. I had a lot of practice yesterday."
Oren crouched down. Slowly. Hand on knee. He looked at the foot. It was wrapped from the toes to the ankle in clean linen, and the linen was soaked through with something yellow, and it smelled of honey and of something else, something sharp, that Vespera put on burns. Under the linen, the foot was swollen up like a loaf.
He had seen feet like that in the war. Men who had marched on them too long, in the heat, in bad boots, until the skin gave up. Most of them had kept their feet. Not all.
"You did well," he said. "Yesterday. The trail."
Arran's grin went, for a moment. He looked up at Oren. Surprised. As if nobody had said that to him before, not in so many words. And then the grin came back, but smaller. Truer.
"Thank you, Captain."
"Don't do it again."
"No, Captain."
Oren nodded and got up. Slowly. Hand on knee. The long breath out through his nose.
And as he straightened up, he heard it.
Far off. Very far. So faint that at first he thought it was in his own head, the way you sometimes hear a sound in your ears after a long day of loud noise. A thin, quick, steady sound. Like a clock in another room. Like a dozen clocks in a dozen rooms, all a little out of time with each other.
Tick. Tick. Tick. Tick.
He turned his head south. Everybody did. All round the ring of shades, people stopped what they were doing and turned their heads, the way a field of grass all turns at once when the wind changes.
The sun had found the iron ones.
They were out there, somewhere, past the swell, too far to see. Standing frozen on the glass where the cold had caught them, three tall gray shapes with their collars up, as they had stood all night. And now the sun was on them. Warming the iron. Getting into the joints. Waking the tick-tick-tick inside. And in a little while, when they were warm enough, they would take the next step. The one they had stopped in the middle of, at dusk. And then the one after that.
Toward here.
"How far?" said Oren.
Kestrel was standing at the edge of the shade, with her hand up over her eyes. She did not answer for a moment.
"Far enough," she said at last. "For today. They will not come at us in the day. Not across this. The glass is too hot for us to run, and they know it. They will come slow. Walk until noon, rest when the glass is hottest, walk again at evening. They will not reach us before dark." She lowered her hand. "And then they freeze. And we walk."
"And the four?"
Kestrel's face did not change.
"The four do not freeze," she said.
And went back into the shade, to help Marrit with the water.
The Signs
It was the middle of the morning when Oren saw the boy's hands.
They were all in the shade by then. All of them except Sef, who had gone back up the swell with a strip of hide over his head to keep the sun off, and lay there flat on his belly watching south. The rest sat or lay on the felt mats under the slanting hides, in that thick, still, golden heat that came in under the edges of the shade and pressed down on everything. Nobody talked much. It was too hot for talk. Marrit was asleep with her mouth open. Tansy was drawing on the glass with a bit of chalk, outside the shade where she could reach, frowning, with her tongue between her teeth. Oda was mending a cord.
The boy was sitting up. Against a pole. With his stiff left leg out in front of him and his crutch across his lap.
He had been trying to talk to Lyra.
Oren had been watching, from the other side of the ring, with his eyes half shut. Pretending to sleep. He was good at that. The boy had been talking, slowly, in that new croak of his. Short words. Simple ones. Water. Hot. Yes. And Lyra had been sitting beside him, very close, nodding at everything, the way you nod at a child who is learning to say things. Patient. Too patient. Oren could see what it cost her.
And then the boy had wanted to say something bigger. Oren saw it in his face. Something more than water or hot. He saw the boy's mouth open, and his eyes go far away, the way they did when he was reaching for a thing. Reaching down into himself for it, into whatever the inside of him looked like now, with the old woman's black words on the outside.
And it had not come.
Oren knew that look. He had worn it himself. A man reaching for a word that is not there. The step that is not there in the dark. The boy's mouth stayed open. His forehead creased. He swallowed. He tried again. And nothing came, and nothing came, and Oren saw the panic start, small, at the back of his eyes.
And then the boy's right hand moved.
Not like a man's hand moves when he is trying to show you something. Not pointing. Not waving. It came up off his knee quick and flat and sure, as if it had been waiting all its life to be asked. And made a shape in the air.
Four fingers up. Thumb folded in. A short sharp push forward from the shoulder.
Then two fingers. Pressed together. Brought down, flat, like a man laying a plank across a ditch.
Then the whole hand. Open. Turned over, and closed, slowly, from the little finger to the thumb, as if folding something very small. One finger after another. Nine movements in it, if you counted. Oren counted.
Then three fingers. Drawn back toward the chest. Laid flat over the heart.
And then the hand stopped. And sank back down onto the boy's knee. And lay there.
The boy looked at it. As if it belonged to somebody else.
Oren was on his feet.
He did not remember getting up. He was just up. His back did not hurt. Nothing hurt. He was standing in the middle of the shade with his heart going like the boys used to beat it on the march, and everybody was looking at him.
"Do that again," he said.
The boy looked up at him. Frightened.
"Do that again," said Oren. Quieter. He made himself say it quieter. "Please. Your hand. What you just did."
The boy looked down at his hand. Lifted it. Looked at it. And Oren saw that he did not know how. That it had come up out of him by itself, the way the throw had come at noon, and the waking this morning, and he did not know where it had come from, and could not call it back.
But then his face went still. And far. The way it had gone just before. And his hand came up again, by itself, and did it.
Four. Push.
Two. Lay down.
Fold. Nine.
Three. Heart.
Oren sat down. Hard. On the felt, right where he was. As if his legs had gone.
"Those are the calls," he said.
Nobody said anything. Lyra was staring at him. So was Vespera. So was Kestrel, from the edge of the shade.
"The calls," said Oren again. "From the war. The old ones. Before the war, some of them. The ones the boys beat out on the — on the march. On the big round things. With the sticks." He waved his hand. It did not matter. "When it was too loud, or too quiet, or you couldn't show yourself, the sergeants made them with their hands instead. So you could see. Across a field. I learned them all. When I was twelve. Before I could hold a pike."
He held up his own hand. Big, scarred, slow. Made the first one.
"Four," he said. "Witness. Stand where you are and watch. Don't move. Don't fight. See it."
The second.
"Two. Hold. Hold the line. Whatever comes."
The third. The slow folding hand. He did it badly. His fingers were too thick. But he did it.
"Nine. Fold. Close up. Draw in. Make yourselves small."
The last. Three fingers. Over the heart.
"Three," he said. And his voice did something strange on it. "Home. Come home. It's over. Come home."
He put his hand down.
The boy was looking at him. So was everybody.
"Witness, hold, fold, home," said Oren. "That's the order they always came in, at the end of the day. When it was done. When the fighting stopped. The sergeant would stand up on something high and make them, one after the other, so everybody could see. Stand and look at what happened. Hold your place. Draw in to your mates. Come home." He shook his head slowly. "I haven't seen anybody make those in twenty years. Not since the war. I didn't know anybody else remembered them."
He looked at the boy.
"Where did you get those?"
The boy opened his mouth. Shut it. Opened it again. Oren could see him reaching for it. Reaching down.
"From her," he said.
Very quietly. In his own croak. His own voice.
And Oren understood.
Not the calls of the Ministry's army, then. Not first. Older than that. Hers. Sola's people. The valley rebels. They had made those signs on their hands, sixty years ago, out here, before there was any Glass, when this was grass and orchards and a river. Signing to each other across the fields. Witness. Hold. Fold. Home. And then somebody had carried them south, after. A man who never said where he came from. And they had gone into the army. Into the beats on the march. Into a sergeant's hand on a hill at the end of the day. Into a boy of twelve, learning them in a ditch.
Rebel signs, before they were anybody's calls.
His grandfather's. His great-grandfather's sister's.
His.
Oren sat on the felt with his big hands in his lap and could not say anything at all.
