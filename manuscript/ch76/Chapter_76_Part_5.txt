Chapter 76: Oren's Watch, Part 5 of 5: The Page
The Page
When the red was going out of the sky, Oren got up and went to the boy.
The boy was sitting where he had sat all day, against his pole, with the silent man beside him. He had the Ledger open on his knee. Lyra had given it back to him in the afternoon, and he had been looking at it ever since. Not reading, exactly. Turning the pages. Slowly. Touching the writing with the tips of his black fingers. Stopping, sometimes, on a page, and looking at it a long time, with the small reading frown, and then turning on.
He looked up when Oren's shadow fell across him.
"Oren," he said. In the croak. He said Oren's name carefully, the way he said everything now. As if checking it.
"Can you write?" said Oren.
The boy looked at him. Then down at the Ledger. Then at his own right hand. He flexed the fingers. Slowly. The gray ones, with the black tips.
"I think so," he said. "I wrote. This afternoon. A little." He turned back a page and showed Oren. Oren could not read it, of course. But he could see it. A few lines of the boy's square capitals. Smaller and shakier than usual. But there.
"Good," said Oren. "Then I want you to write something for me."
The boy waited.
Oren crouched down. Slowly. The hand on the knee. The long breath. He crouched there in front of the boy in the last red light, with his big scarred face close to the boy's tired gray one, and for a moment he did not know how to say it.
"There's a page in there," he said. "Mine. You made it. In the winter, in the city. The page where you put the things I've lost. The words." He did not look away. "I can't read it. But I know it's there. You showed me once. You put your finger on it and said, That one's yours, Oren. Everything you spend, I'll write it here, so it's kept somewhere."
The boy's face changed. A little. As if something had moved, far down.
He turned the pages. Back. And back. Further than Oren would have thought, past the old woman's pages and Lyra's and the pages of the north, back into the thick of the book, where the writing was the boy's own, bold and square and sure. And stopped.
Oren knew the page. He knew it by the shape. A list down the left side, short lines, one under the other. Six of them. And a heading at the top in bigger letters, and the heading had a line under it.
"SPENT," said the boy. Reading it. "OREN." He touched it. "This one?"
"That one."
The boy looked at the list. His lips moved. Oren knew what he was reading. He did not need to hear it. The six holes. The shut. The cold that's hard. The thing you sit on. The little one for soup. The white flowers. The round things the boys beat. All of them written there, in the boy's hand, in words Oren would never be able to say again, or read, and kept. Kept somewhere. At least kept.
"Under that," said Oren. "At the bottom. I want you to write something."
The boy found the pencil in the spine of the book. It was very short now, worn down to a stub. He held it in his gray fingers and looked at Oren.
"What?"
Oren had thought about this all afternoon too. While he was teaching. While he sat with Lyra's head on his shoulder. He had thought about it until he had it right, word by word, the way he had once had to learn the calls. And now he said it slowly, one word at a time, so the boy could keep up.
"Sola Vance," he said.
The boy wrote. Slowly. The pencil scratched on the paper.
"My blood."
Scratch. Scratch.
"She gave her words."
The boy's pencil stopped. Just for a moment. He looked up at Oren. Then down. And wrote.
"So did I."
The boy wrote that too. And then he stopped, and sat looking at what he had written, and did not look up for a long time.
Oren held out his hand.
The boy put the book in it.
Oren held it. Close. In the last of the light. He looked at the page. At the list he could not read, down the left side. And at the bottom, under it, the new lines. Four of them. Crooked capitals. Bigger than the boy's usual hand, and shakier, the letters leaning against each other like tired men at the end of a march. He could not read a single one.
But he knew what they said.
He looked at them for a long time. He followed the shapes with his eyes. The first word, with its curly first letter, like a snake standing up. That was her. Sola. The second, short and straight. That was him. Vance. Both of them. On one line. Together.
He had never seen his own name written down before. Not that he knew of. He had never been able to tell.
He could tell now.
"Is that right?" said the boy. Worried. "Did I get it right?"
"I don't know," said Oren. "I can't read it."
He kept looking at it anyway.
After a while he gave the book back. Carefully. Both hands, the way you give back something that belongs to somebody else and matters to them.
"Thank you, lad," he said.
The boy took it. He did not close it. He sat with it open on his knee at Oren's page, looking down at the four crooked lines.
"Oren," he said.
"What?"
"Is it true? So did I?"
Oren did not answer for a moment. Then he nodded. Once.
"Most of them," he said. "Not all. Not yet."
And he got up, slowly, with his hand on his knee, and went to help them take the shades down. Because the light was going, and it was nearly dusk, and they had twelve miles to walk.
Dusk
The sun went down the way it had come up. All at once.
One moment it was a great swollen red ball sitting on the western edge of the Glass, so close and so huge that it looked as if you could walk to it. The next it was half gone. The next it was a line. And then it was gone altogether, and the whole sky over them went from red to purple to a deep cold blue in the time it takes to pack a bag, and the first stars came out in the east, and the heat began to drain out of the ground under their feet like water out of a cracked jar.
They packed in the last of the light.
The shades came down. The poles were pulled up and tied in bundles. The hides were rolled and strapped and slung on backs. The felt mats went on top of the hides. Everything else went into the packs. Kestrel stood in the middle of it all and watched, and every now and then she said a word in her own tongue, sharp, and somebody went back and picked up a thing they had dropped, or retied a knot that was not tight enough.
They made the litter for Arran out of two of the long shade poles and a hide stretched between them, lashed tight. He lay on it on his back with his bad foot strapped up on a rolled coat, grinning at the sky.
"This is very comfortable," he said. "I may never walk again. I may just be carried everywhere for the rest of my life. Like a lord."
"Lords don't stink of goat," said Brin, taking one end of a pole.
"This one does," said Arran happily.
Oda took the other front pole, and Sef and Lyra took the back, for the first hour, and they lifted him, and he swayed up off the glass between them like a man in a boat, and said, Whoa, and laughed.
Oren took two packs.
His own. And the boy's.
He did not ask. He just bent down, with the long breath and the hand on the knee, and picked up the boy's pack off the glass where it lay, and slung it over his right shoulder on top of his own. It was not heavy. The boy did not have much. The Ledger, wrapped in oilcloth. A water skin. A few strips of meat. A spare shirt. The jars, in their padded box. But two packs is two packs, on a bad back, and he felt it at once, down low on the left side, like a hand squeezing.
He did not let it show on his face.
The boy saw anyway. Of course he did. He was standing on his own foot and his crutch, a few paces off, with Lyra at his right side and the silent man at his left, and he looked at Oren with the pack, and opened his mouth.
"It's nothing," said Oren, before he could speak. "You can't carry and walk on a stick. I can carry and walk on two legs. That's all. It's nothing."
The boy shut his mouth. He looked at Oren a moment longer. Then he nodded.
"Thank you," he said. In the croak.
"It's nothing," said Oren again.
It was not nothing. They both knew it was not nothing. But that was what you said. That was what you always said. And the boy, who had been a clerk, and was not a fool, and had a dead general in his hands, let him say it.
They set off.
Kestrel at the front, as always. Then Tansy, with her blue lamp swinging on its wire, and the small flame bobbing in the dark like a fallen star. Then the litter, with Arran on it, and the four bearers walking in step, quietly, their felted feet going shff, shff on the glass, and Lyra at the back corner turning her head every few steps to look behind her. Then the boy, with the silent man on his bad side, going slow, the crutch going down and the stiff leg swinging and the good foot following. Then Vespera, and Marrit, and the rest.
And at the back, the last, where he always walked, Oren.
No. Not the last. Not tonight.
He had walked at the back every night since the Edge. That was his place. The rear guard. The man between the walkers and whatever was behind. He had always taken it, in the army, when he could. He liked it there. He liked knowing that there was nobody behind him but the enemy.
But tonight, when he dropped back to take his place, there was somebody there already.
The silent man.
Oren did not see him move. He never did. One moment the man was walking beside the boy, on his bad side, as he had walked all the way from the Fold Hills. The next, when Oren looked round, he was behind. At the very back. Walking alone, a few paces behind the last of them, in his stiff dark coat, with his long arms hanging. Facing forward. But behind.
Nobody had told him to.
Oren looked at him. The man did not look back. He walked on, steady, his long legs eating up the glass without any hurry, keeping exactly the same distance behind the rest, as if he was tied to them by a string.
The rear guard.
Oren thought about saying something. Telling him to go back to the boy. Telling him this was Oren's place.
Then he thought about the man in the horse's shadow, doing his father's stroke faster than his father ever had.
And he said nothing. He fell in a little ahead of the man, behind Vespera and Marrit, and walked.
The last of the blue went out of the sky. The stars came down to the edges of the Glass on every side, above and below. The cold came up through the felt and into the soles of his feet. And somewhere far off to the south, the ticking slowed, and slowed, and stopped.
Night March
They walked.
There is not much to say about a night march, Oren had always thought, except that it goes on. In the war he had done a great many of them, and they had all been the same, and he remembered almost nothing about any of them except the cold and the dark and the back of the man in front. You walked. You did not think. You put one foot down and then the other one, and then the first one again, and you did not count, because counting made it longer. And after a while you were not a man walking anymore. You were just a pair of feet going on, under the stars, with something heavy on your back.
This was like that. And not like that.
It was quieter, for one thing. In the war there had always been noise on a night march. Boots. Harness. Somebody coughing. A sergeant hissing at somebody to shut up. Here, with the felt on everybody's feet, there was hardly any sound at all. Only the shff, shff of the bearers. The small dry tap of the boy's crutch, every two steps, going down on the glass. And under it all, very faint, so faint that you only heard it when you stopped listening for it, the hum. The high, thin, endless hum of the Glass, like a finger going round the rim of a cup.
And it was colder. The cold came up out of the ground through the felt, through the soles of his boots, into his feet and his ankles and his shins, slowly, like water rising in a well. By the second hour he could not feel his toes. By the third he could not feel his feet. He walked on them anyway. You did.
They changed the bearers every hour. Kestrel called it. She did not shout. She did not need to. She said one word in her own tongue, not loudly, and the whole line stopped, and the four on the litter set it down very gently on the glass, and four others came forward and took it up. And Arran, on the litter, said something every time, some joke in his own tongue that made the Ashborn snort, and then they went on.
Oren did not take a turn on the poles. Kestrel would not let him. She looked at the two packs on his back the first time he came forward, and then at his face, and said, No, and that was the end of it. He did not argue. He did not like it. But he did not argue.
He walked behind the boy instead.
He watched him. It was a thing to do. And he was worried, though he did not say so. A stick and one good leg and a night of twelve miles, on a body that had been half dead the day before. Oren had seen strong men fall down on less.
The boy did not fall down.
He walked slowly. Very slowly. Slower than the litter, so that every little while the litter had to stop and wait for him, and Kestrel would look back, and the boy would raise one hand to say I'm coming, and come. Crutch down. Stiff leg swinging out and round. Good foot forward. Crutch down. Again. Again. Like a heron, Oren thought, and almost smiled. A heron walking through shallow water. Picking each step.
But he did not stop. Not once.
And somewhere in the second hour, Oren heard him humming.
Very low. Almost under his breath. So low that at first Oren thought it was the Glass. Then he knew it was not. It had a tune. Not much of one. Three notes. Going up, and then coming down, and then going round again. Over and over. In time with the tap of the crutch.
Oren did not know the tune.
But it made the back of his neck go cold. Because for a moment, on the dark glass, with the stars below and above, he was a small boy on the back step of a house in the city on a summer evening, with his grandfather beside him, humming something under his breath that he would never say the words of.
It was not the same tune. He was nearly sure it was not the same tune. His grandfather's had been longer, and slower, and sadder. But it was the same kind of thing. The same way of doing it. The humming of somebody who is carrying something heavy, and has found that a few notes over and over make it easier to carry.
"What's that?" said Oren. Quietly. Coming up beside him.
The boy stopped humming. He looked round at Oren. Surprised. As if he had not known he was doing it.
"What?"
"That tune. You were humming."
The boy thought about it. Walking. Crutch, swing, step.
"Hask," he said at last. "I think. It's Hask's." He frowned. "He hummed. When he worked. When he was sitting with somebody. He's in me. A bit of him. I carry him." He said it simply, the way you would say I have a stone in my boot. "It helps."
Oren walked beside him for a while.
"How many?" he said. "How many are you carrying? Now?"
The boy did not answer at once. Crutch, swing, step.
"I don't know," he said. "I can't count them. Not anymore." He almost smiled. "The numbers went. A long time ago. Some of them. The big ones." Crutch, swing, step. "But a lot. Hask. Arran's people, some. The soldiers in the trench, a little. Her." A pause. "Mostly her."
"Is it heavy?"
The boy stopped walking.
He stood on the dark glass on his one good foot and his stick and looked at Oren. And his face, in the starlight, was very tired, and very young, and older than Oren had ever seen it.
"Yes," he said.
Then he went on walking.
After a while, Oren heard him start humming again. The three notes. Up and down and round. And Oren, who had never sung a note in his life, and could not carry a tune in a bucket, found that he was walking in time with it. Crutch, swing, step. Up, down, round.
And it did help. A little.
Him
They walked for three hours before Oren looked back.
He had not meant to look back at all. You did not, on a night march. Not often. It slowed you down, and it frightened the others, and there was nothing you could do about what you saw anyway. You kept your eyes front, on the back of the man ahead, and your ears open behind, and you walked.
But the Glass was not flat here. Not quite. It rose, slowly, so slowly you hardly felt it in your legs, into another of those long low swells, like the back of a sleeping beast. And when he came to the top of it, he stopped. Because from the top of a swell you could see a long way. And he was a soldier, and a soldier who comes to high ground on a night march and does not look back is a fool.
The others went on, down the far side. He heard them going. The shff, shff of the bearers. The tap of the boy's crutch. Tansy, very faintly, singing something under her breath to her lamp. The silent man passed him on the top of the swell without looking at him, and went on down after them, and Oren was alone.
He turned round.
The night lay behind him like a great dark sea.
The stars were very bright. They came all the way down to the ground on every side, and the ground gave them back, so that the whole world behind him was stars. Stars above, and stars below, and nothing between but the faint gray shine of the glass, going away and away to the south, to the edge of everything.
And on the glass, far off, there were shapes.
He saw them after a moment. When his eyes had got used to looking. Three of them. Very small, from here. Thin and tall and dark against the shine. Standing still. Quite still. A little apart from each other, in a rough line, the way men stand when they have been walking in a line and stopped all at once.
The iron ones.
Frozen. As Sef had said. The cold had come up out of the glass at dusk and got into them, into the joints and the ticking hearts, and stopped them where they stood. They stood out there in the starlight with their collars up and their hats down, as still as gateposts, like men waiting at a door that nobody was going to open. They would stand like that all night. Until the sun came up and warmed them. And then they would take the next step.
Oren looked at them for a long time.
They did not move. They could not.
And then, beyond them, he saw the other thing.
It was very small. So small that at first he thought it was a star. A low one. A star sitting right down on the edge of the world, where the sky met the glass. Yellow, where the others were white. And then he saw that there was another one beside it. And another. And another. Four of them. In a line. Small yellow lights, close to the ground, a long way behind the frozen iron.
And they were moving.
Not fast. Slow. Steady. Swaying a little, each one, the way a lantern sways when a man carries it by the handle. The way Sef had shown him that morning with his hand. Coming on. Through the cold and the dark. Past the place where the iron things had frozen. Over glass that had stopped everything else.
Toward him.
Oren stood on the top of the swell with the two packs on his back and his hand on his sword and watched them come.
He thought about the four lights. He had been thinking about them all day, in the back of his mind, while he taught and talked and watched the man do his father's stroke. Four men. Walking in a line, like soldiers. Not afraid of the dark. Not afraid of the cold. Coming on, on the Glass at night, where nothing came on.
And he thought he knew, now, who was at the front of them.
He did not know how he knew. He had never seen the man walk on the Glass. He had seen him in the city. Not often. Enough. Quiet. Still. Watching. A man who did not hurry, ever, because he never needed to. A man who walked into rooms the way the cold comes up out of the glass at night. Slowly. Without a sound. And everything in the room stopped.
The Ministry did not send clerks onto the Glass. It did not send Enforcers, or the silver-stick men, or the iron things, on their own. Not this far. Not after the boy. Not after what the boy carried.
It sent the one man it had who would not stop.
Oren watched the four small lights come on through the dark, steady as a clock, past the frozen iron, toward the swell where he stood.
The cold bit his nose. His back ached. The two packs pulled at his shoulders. Below him, on the far side of the swell, he could hear the others going on into the north, the tap of the crutch, the shff of the bearers, Tansy's tiny song.
He did not go after them. Not yet. He stood a moment longer. Looking south.
"Him," he said. Very quietly. To nobody. To the stars. "He walks in the dark."
Then he turned his back on the lights, and went down the swell after the others, into the night.
