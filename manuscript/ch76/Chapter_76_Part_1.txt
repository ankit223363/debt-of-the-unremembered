Chapter 76: Oren's Watch, Part 1 of 5: The Watch
The Watch
Oren sat on the iron horse's lifted hoof with his sword across his knees, and for the first time in years, nobody could take anything from him.
He had not meant to sit there. He had meant to lie down. He had told Lyra he would lie behind her in the heap and keep the cold off her back, and he had done it, for a while. He had lain there under the hides with the smell of goat and felt all round him, and Lyra's short dark hair under his chin, and listened to the boy breathe in front of her. Right up until the boy woke. And after that, after the noise and the laughing and the crying, after everybody had settled down again, his back had begun to hurt. Not a sharp hurt. A deep, slow, grinding one, down low on the left side, where he had hit the glass at noon. It would not let him lie still. So in the end he had slid out from the edge of the heap, very carefully, an inch at a time, the way you slide out of a bed you share with a man who has a knife. And he had pulled his coat round him and walked away across the glass to the horse.
Somebody had to watch. That was what he told himself. Kestrel had said they would not walk tonight. That meant somebody had to watch. And he was awake anyway. And a man who is awake and has a sword might as well be useful.
So here he was.
The horse was very big. Bigger than any real horse he had ever seen, and he had seen the great gray chargers of the Ministry cavalry, and the black ones his lordship's father had bred in the stables behind the house on the Hill. This one was taller than both. It was made of iron plates, riveted together, gone red-brown and scaly with rust and then glazed over, all of it, with a thin skin of glass, so that in the starlight it shone. It stood with three hooves down on the Glass and one lifted, the front left, bent at the knee, caught in the middle of a stride that had stopped sixty years ago and would never finish. Its head was up. Its iron mouth was open. There was a frame on its back where a rider had sat, a thing of iron bars and straps, and the straps were gone, and the bars were bent.
The lifted hoof was about the height of a man's knee off the ground. It was flat on the bottom. Wide as a plate. It made a good seat, if you did not mind cold iron. Oren did not mind. He had sat on worse.
He sat with his back against the horse's leg and his sword across his knees and looked out at the night.
It was very cold. The cold on the Glass was not like the cold anywhere else. It bit the end of your nose first. Then the tops of your ears. Then it found the gap between your collar and your neck and went down your back like a wet finger. He pulled his collar up. He breathed out through his nose, slowly, and watched the breath smoke white in the starlight and drift away.
And the stars.
Saints, the stars.
He had seen stars before. Of course he had. In the war, on the march, lying in a ditch with his cloak over his head and the rain coming through it. On guard on the walls of the house on the Hill, a thousand nights, with his lordship's lamp still lit in the study window behind him. But not like this. Not ever like this. Here there was no smoke from a city, no mist off a river, no trees, no hills, nothing at all between him and them. And so there were more of them than he had ever known there were. So many the sky was white with them in places, as if somebody had spilled flour on a black table. And they went all the way down. Right down to the ground, on every side, until they touched the edge of the Glass.
And then they did not stop. Because the Glass was a mirror. Not a clear one. A dim one, dark and smoky, like the old glass in a church window. But it gave the stars back. So that when he looked down at the ground in front of his boots, he saw them there too. Fainter. Blurred. A second sky, upside down, under his feet.
Double stars. Stars above and stars below. And him sitting in the middle, on a horse's hoof, in the dark.
He did not mind it. He thought perhaps he liked it.
Under the stars in the ground, there were the other lights.
He had seen them every night since the Edge. The Ashborn called them the quiet ones, or did not call them anything, only nodded at them, the way you nod at a neighbor across a street. Small soft lights, deep down in the glass. Blue, mostly. Some white. One or two a faint gold. They drifted. Very slowly. Like fish in a pond on a summer evening, turning, going nowhere much, coming up close to the top and then sinking away again. They were the dead. Kestrel had told him. The Ash of the people who had been here sixty years ago, when the light came. Burned into the glass. Still there.
A blue one came up near the hoof he sat on. He watched it. It came up close, close enough that he could see it was not round, but shaped a little like a hand, or a leaf, and that it had a darker blue at its heart. It hung there under the glass, a foot below the top. As if it was looking at him.
"Evening," said Oren quietly.
The light hung there a moment longer. Then it turned, slowly, and sank away.
He sat back against the horse's leg.
This was the thing about the Glass. The thing nobody had told him before he came, and that he had found out for himself, on the first night, and kept to himself since, like a coin in a boot. Out here, nobody could take anything from him. Nobody. Not a harvester. Not a mage. Not the Ministry, with its stamps and its forms and its little tax-collecting men with their jars. Not the boy, if the boy had wanted to, which he never would. On the Glass, nothing could be taken. Kestrel had said it. Vespera had said it. The boy had found it out the hard way, at the cairns. Taking did not work. Burning did not work. Giving worked. Only giving.
Which meant that every word Oren had left, he would keep. For as long as he was out here.
He had not had that in a long time.
He had been losing words for a long while now. A word here. A word there. Each one a spell, or a cut, or a thing he had done for somebody that cost a little more than it should. And each one gone for good. Out here, at least, they stopped going. He could sit on this hoof and think about every word he still had, one after another, and know that in the morning they would all still be there.
It was a kind of rest. The only kind he had had in a year.
Behind him, in the ring of shades, someone turned over in the heap. The hides rustled. Then it was still again.
Oren put his big hand flat on the blade across his knees. The steel was so cold it hurt. He left his hand there anyway. It was a good sword. It had been his father's, and his father's father's, all the way back to a man none of them had ever known. Plain. Long. A little heavy at the point. The smith at Haverstock had sharpened it for nothing, in the house where Sola had been born, and said Not in her house. Not to a Vance. And Oren had not understood, then, what that meant. Not all of it.
He understood a little more now.
He looked out at the double stars and the drifting dead and the long dark line where the sky met the glass, and kept his watch.
Going Round
The trouble with keeping your words was that it made you count the ones you did not have.
He did it now. He had not meant to. But it was quiet, and cold, and there was nothing to do but sit and look at the stars, and a man's mind will go where it wants when there is nothing to stop it. His went to the holes.
There were six of them. Big ones. And more small ones, perhaps, that he did not know about, because how would you know? A word you have lost does not leave a sign on the door saying gone. It just is not there when you reach for it. Like a step in the dark that you were sure was there, and your foot comes down on nothing.
The six he knew about, he knew because he had fallen through them. In front of people.
The first had been at the canal. In the city, in the autumn. The thing on the canal that holds the water back, the two great wooden gates with the iron wheels, where the barges wait and the water goes up and down. He had been telling the boy about it. Pointing at it. And the word had not come. He had stood there with his finger out and his mouth open, and nothing. In the end he had said the shut. The shut on the canal. And the boy had looked at him, and understood, and said nothing, and Oren had been grateful for that, and angry, both at once.
He had said the shut ever since. For the canal one. For the thing on a door that keeps it fast, with the key. For the box that held his lordship's papers. All the same hole. All the shut.
Then the cold that's hard. The stuff on the river in winter. The stuff in the gutters in the morning, that cracks when you step on it, that men skate on. Gone. He had found that out in front of Lyra, in the snow, when he tried to tell her to mind her feet on the steps. Mind the — mind the hard cold. And she had looked at him the same way the boy had. And said nothing. And put her hand on his arm for a moment, and taken it away.
Then the thing you sit on. That one was the worst. That one made him feel like a fool every time. Because it was everywhere. In every room. In every inn and every kitchen and every office. He could not go a day without needing it. Sit in the — sit down there. On the thing. Pull up the thing with four legs. He had stopped saying it at all, mostly. He just pointed. Or said a seat. Seat still worked. Seat he had. But seat was not the same, and he knew it was not the same, and every time he said it, he felt the hole underneath it.
Then the little one. The one for soup. The small round thing with a handle that you eat with. That was a stupid one to lose. He had lost it over a bowl of stew in some kitchen in the city, he could not even remember whose, and sat there looking at the thing in his hand and not knowing what to call it, while somebody across the table talked about the price of coal.
Then the flowers. White ones. Tall. The ones at funerals, and on the Ministry's black badges, and on the Ministry's banners. He did not mind losing that one so much. He had never liked them. But it was a hole, still.
And the last. The one from the war. The ones the boys beat on the march. Big round things, skin stretched tight, that you hit with sticks to call the men. To say halt and forward and close up and fall back. He had known all the calls once. Every one. He had been a boy beating them himself, before he was old enough for a pike. And the word for the thing he beat them on was gone. He could hear them in his head. He could feel the sticks in his hands. He could not say what they were.
He had to go round them all. Every day. Round and round. Like a man walking round holes in a road at night, slowly, with a lamp, feeling for the edges with his feet.
And he was tired of it.
That was the truth of it, sitting on the hoof in the cold. He was tired. Not of the war, or the walking, or the cold, or the iron things ticking behind them in the dark. He could stand all that. He had stood worse. He was tired of going round. Of the little stop before every sentence, while he checked the road ahead for holes. Of the look on people's faces when he fell into one anyway. Of the kindness. Most of all the kindness. The hand on the arm. The way the boy and Lyra both said nothing, so carefully, as if he was something that might break.
He was not going to break.
But he was getting older. And the holes were getting closer together. And one day, he thought, there would be more holes than road, and he would stand there with his lamp and not be able to find a way across at all.
And then what?
He did not know. He did not want to know.
He put the thought away. Folded it up small and pushed it down, the way he had pushed down a great many things in thirty years of soldiering. You learned to do that, in the army. Or you did not last.
He looked at the stars instead.
Out here, at least, the holes were not getting any bigger. Out here, the road stayed the road. For a little while.
He would take that.
The Noise in the Heap
He thought about the waking instead. It was a better thing to think about.
It had been, perhaps, an hour ago. Perhaps a little more. He had been half asleep, in the way a soldier sleeps, with one ear open. Lying on his side behind Lyra with his back to the cold and his chest to her back, and his arm over her, and the hides heavy on top of them all. He had felt her go still. That was the first thing. Not the stillness of sleep. The other kind. The stillness of a person who has heard something in the dark and is listening with every inch of her.
He had come awake at once. All the way. That was the army too.
He had not moved. He had lain there with his eyes open in the black under the hides, and waited, with his hand moving very slowly toward the hilt of the sword that lay along his side. Because that was what you did when somebody beside you went still like that. You waited. You got ready.
And then she had lifted the edge of the hide, and the starlight had come in, gray and faint, and lain across the boy's face. Oren had seen it past her shoulder. Just a little. A thin pale stripe of light across a cheek, a nose, an eye.
The eye had been open.
And Oren's hand had closed on the hilt.
He was not proud of that now. But it was true, and he did not lie to himself about true things. At noon the boy had come up off the glass with someone else's eyes and thrown him like a bag of washing. And the howling, in the trench. He had heard it from the top, and it had made the hair stand up on his arms. So when he saw the boy's eye open in the dark, a hand's width from Lyra's face, he had closed his hand on his sword. And waited. Ready to pull her back. Ready to put himself between.
Then the boy had said his name.
Corin.
A croak. A frog's voice. Hardly there at all. But his own. Oren had known it at once, the way you know your own front step in the dark.
He had let go of the sword.
And then the boy had said the other thing. Kettle? Puzzled. As if he was asking a question he did not know the answer to. And Oren had not understood it. Not then. He did not understand it now, not really. Something between the two of them. Something from before he knew them. But he had felt what it did to the girl. He had felt it right through her back, against his chest. Like a bell being struck.
She had started to laugh.
And then to cry. And then both. Great shaking gulps of it, the whole of her going with it, so that he had to put his arm round her harder to keep her from rolling off the hide. He had known her since she was a girl of ten in his lordship's yard, with a wooden sword and scabbed knees, and in all those years he had seen her cry only a handful of times. When her mother went. When her father died. Not when she fell. Not when she was hurt. Not when the boy lost his name at noon. Lyra Thorne did not cry. Everybody in that house knew it. She went white, and she went quiet, and she went at things with her sword.
She had cried now. She had cried as if something had broken in her, and what came out of the break was not pain but the other thing. The thing that comes after the pain stops.
And Oren had lain there with his arm round her and let her. And looked over her head at the boy's face in the stripe of starlight. At the frown on it. That small reading frown that the boy did over the Ledger, over every page, as if the words might run away if he did not hold them still with his eyes.
He had seen that frown, and he had thought: That's him. Saints keep him. That's the boy.
The heap had woken up round them, then. The little one, Tansy, sitting bolt upright at the bottom and shouting about the iron. Kestrel's quick sharp voice. The old woman saying nothing, but Oren had seen her eyes open in the dark at the edge, shining. Arran laughing. Somebody, Brin perhaps, saying What? What? in the slow deep voice he used for everything.
And the long thin man behind the boy. Letting go of his wrist. At last.
Oren had watched him do it. One finger at a time. The way you let go of a rope when you are not quite sure the thing on the end of it will stand on its own.
It had stood. The boy had stood. Inside, at least.
They had all settled, after a while. Lyra had stopped crying, slowly, the gulps getting further apart and then stopping, and she had gone to sleep almost at once, like a child does after crying, with her hand still on the boy's face. And the boy had lain there with his eyes open, looking at her, for a long time. Oren had watched him look. And then the boy's eyes had closed too. Not the deep-well sleep of before. An ordinary sleep. A tired man's.
That was when Oren's back had started up.
It was a good thing to think about, sitting on the cold hoof. Better than holes. He thought about it for a long time. The croak in the dark. The girl's laugh going through his chest like a bell.
Kettle?
He still did not know what it meant. He thought perhaps he did not need to.
