Chapter 76: Oren's Watch, Part 3 of 5: Her Voice
Her Voice
For a while nobody moved.
The heat pressed down. Somewhere outside the shade, Tansy's chalk went squeak, squeak on the glass, and stopped, as she looked up to see why it had gone so quiet. Marrit snored once and was still. Far off to the south, very faint, the ticking went on. Tick. Tick. Tick. Like a clock in another house.
Oren sat on the felt with his hands in his lap.
The boy was looking at him. Not frightened now. Something else. A strange, steady, level look, that Oren had not seen on his face before. The look of somebody who is measuring you. Not unkindly. Just measuring. Seeing how tall you stand, and how you hold your hands, and where your weight is, and whether you will run.
The boy's face had gone very still. The way it had at noon, the day before, just before the throw. The way it had this morning, coming up out of the hides. Flat and calm and far off. As if somebody else had come up behind his eyes and was looking out of them.
Oren knew it was coming before it came. He did not know how he knew. The hair stood up on his arms in the heat.
The boy's lips moved.
"Vance," he said.
And it was not his voice.
It was not the croak. It was not the boy's voice at all, the light quick clerk's voice Oren had heard talking and talking for weeks, through crypts and barns and snow. It was low. Lower than the boy's had ever been. And rough. A rasp. The rasp of a voice that had shouted over a great deal of noise, a long time, on many days, and been worn by it, like a step is worn by feet. And under the rasp, something else. Something warm. Something that might once, a long time ago, on a summer evening in a valley, have sung.
A woman's voice.
It said his name the way the old men at the Sun and Pike had said it. The way the widow at the cross had said it, reading the rings of names. Not Vance the way a clerk reads it off a list. Vance the way you say the name of your own family. Your own blood. Like a hand on a shoulder. Like there you are.
Like she knew him.
Oren went white.
He felt it go. The blood. Out of his face, all at once, down and away, as if somebody had pulled a plug. He felt his lips go cold. He felt the scar on his face, from his temple to his mouth, stand out on his skin like a cord, the way it did on a frosty morning. He felt his hands, in his lap, go numb at the tips.
He had been shot at. He had been cut. He had stood in a line of pikes and watched the gray ranks come at him across a field, and not run. He had walked out onto this Glass behind a boy with a stick, knowing the iron things were coming after. He had never in his life felt the blood go out of his face like that.
The boy looked at him a moment longer. With that level, measuring, steady look. As if he was a soldier she was pleased to see. As if she had been waiting for him at the end of a long road, and here he was, at last, late, and she was not going to scold him for it.
Then the boy blinked.
Once.
And it was gone.
Just like that. Like a candle blown out. The stillness went out of his face, and the level look went out of his eyes, and the boy was there again, the boy, the clerk, tired and puzzled and blinking. He looked at Oren. He frowned a little. The small reading frown.
"What?" he said. In his own croak. "Oren? What's wrong?"
Oren could not speak.
"You've gone a funny color," said the boy. He looked round at the others. At Lyra, who had her hand over her mouth. At Vespera, very still. At Kestrel. "What happened? Did I —" He stopped. His face changed. Fear came into it. "Did I do something? Did I throw somebody again?"
"No," said Lyra. Through her fingers.
"Then what?"
Nobody told him.
Oren saw that the boy truly did not know. That he had not heard himself. That the word had come up out of him from somewhere so deep down that he had not even felt it go past. Like a fish that comes up out of dark water and takes a fly and goes back down, and the man on the bank only sees the ring spreading.
The boy looked at him. Worried. Waiting.
Oren made his mouth work.
"Nothing," he said. His voice came out strange. He cleared his throat. "Nothing, lad. The heat. I stood up too quick. Old men shouldn't stand up too quick."
The boy did not believe him. Oren could see that. The boy was not a fool, even now, even with half his words gone and a dead woman in his hands. But he did not push. He looked at Oren for a long moment with his own gray eyes. And then he nodded, slowly, and looked down at his hand again, lying on his knee.
As if he was afraid of what it might do next.
White
He went out of the shade.
He should not have. It was nearly noon. The glass outside the hides was so bright he had to screw his eyes up to slits, and the heat came off it in waves you could see, wobbling the air so that the far swell seemed to float. But he could not sit in there. Not with everybody looking at him. Not with the boy looking at his own hand.
So he went out, with his coat pulled up over his head like a hood, and walked round to the far side of the iron horse, where its great body threw a short black shadow on the glass. And he stood in that shadow, with his back against the hot iron of its leg, and breathed.
Vance.
In her voice.
He had never heard her voice. Of course he had not. She had been dead for years before he was born. Nobody alive had heard her voice except, perhaps, a few old men in Haverstock who had been children when she rode out, and they were mostly dead now too. And his grandfather. His grandfather must have heard it. As a boy, before he went south. His own aunt.
But Oren knew it. That was the thing. That was what had taken the blood out of his face. He had never heard it, and he knew it.
He did not understand how. He stood in the horse's shadow and tried to understand, and could not. It was not a voice he remembered. It was a voice he recognized. The way you recognize a smell from when you were very small, before you had words, and you do not know what it is, but your whole body goes still.
Perhaps it was in the blood. Perhaps that was what blood was. Not a word in a book. Not a name cut in a stone cross. A voice you had never heard that you knew anyway.
He thought of his grandfather. In the yard, with the washing. Showing him the throw. Like this, boy. Hip in. Turn. Let them fall over you. A big old man with a white beard and huge hands, who never said where he came from, and swore by Sola's teeth, and sometimes, on summer evenings, sat on the back step and hummed something under his breath that he would never say the words of.
Oren had forgotten that. The humming. He had not thought of it in forty years.
He thought of it now.
And he wondered, standing in the shadow of the iron horse with the sweat running down his back, whether his grandfather had sat on that step and heard a voice in his head. Low. Rough. Worn by shouting. Saying his name. And whether that was why he hummed. To drown it out. Or to keep it company.
He did not know. He would never know. His grandfather was long dead, and had never said.
But the boy had said it. With her mouth.
Vance.
As if she was pleased to see him. As if he had come a long way, and was late, and she did not mind.
Oren put his hand over his face. Over the scar. And stood there for a long time, in the horse's shadow, while the ticking went on in the south and the glass shone and the sun went up to the top of the sky.
He did not cry. He did not do that. He had not done it since he was a boy in a ditch, and he was not going to start on the Glass, in front of goats and strangers.
But it was close.
After a while he heard a soft step on the glass behind him. He did not turn round. He knew who it was by the step. Short. Quick. Felted.
"Go back in the shade," he said. "You'll cook."
"So will you," said Lyra.
She came and stood beside him in the strip of shadow. Not close. Not touching. A little way off, with her own coat over her head, looking out at the glass the way he was. She did not say anything else. She did not ask if he was all right. She did not put her hand on his arm.
He was grateful for all of that.
They stood there together a while, two people in a shadow, saying nothing.
"She knew me," said Oren at last. Very low.
"I know," said Lyra.
"How can she know me? She never saw me. She's been dead sixty years. How can she know me?"
Lyra was quiet for a moment.
"Maybe she doesn't know you," she said. "Maybe she knows what you are." She glanced at him, then away. "A Vance. Standing up. With a sword. Watching the way in." A pause. "Maybe that's all she ever needed to know about anybody."
Oren thought about that.
Then he took his hand down off his face, and pulled his coat straighter over his head, and turned round.
"Come on," he said. "Back in. Before we both cook."
And they went back into the shade together.
The Old Woman's Hands
Vespera was waiting for him inside.
She was sitting on a felt mat at the back of the shade with her bag open beside her, and when he came in, ducking under the edge of the hide, she looked up at him and pointed at the mat in front of her. Just pointed. With one long brown finger.
"Sit," she said.
"I'm all right."
"You are not all right. You have been walking like a man with a poker down his trousers since noon yesterday. Sit. Back to me."
Oren looked at her. She looked back. Her pale eyes did not blink.
He sat.
It took a long time. Knee, other knee, hand on the felt, the long breath. He sat with his back to her, cross-legged, the way the Ashborn sat, and stared at the slanting wall of the shade in front of him. A hide. Goat. Brown and white in patches, with the hair worn off in places where it had been rubbed against poles for years.
"Coat off," said Vespera.
He took his coat off.
"Shirt up."
He did not want to. He pulled his shirt up anyway, at the back, and held it bunched under his arms. The air in the shade was hot, and still felt cool on his skin after the coat.
He felt her hands.
They were cold. That surprised him. Everything on the Glass was hot in the day, and her hands were cold, like the hands of a woman who has just come in from a winter garden. They went down his back. Slowly. Not gentle, exactly. Careful. Pressing here, and here, and here, with the tips of the fingers, the way you press a loaf to see if it is done. Down the long bones on either side of his spine. Down to the low place on the left, just above his belt.
She pressed there.
He did not make a sound. But his whole body went stiff, and the breath stopped in his chest, and he felt the sweat come out on his face all at once.
"There," said Vespera.
"There," said Oren. Through his teeth.
She pressed again. Softer. Round the edges of it. He felt her fingers find the shape of the hurt. A hard, hot, swollen place, the size of his palm, deep in the muscle.
"Nothing broken," she said. "Nothing torn, I think. Just bruised to the bone, and the muscle round it gone tight as a fist to keep it safe." He heard her bag open. Something unstopped. A smell came, sharp and green and oily, like crushed leaves and pine. "That's what muscles do. When something hits them, they close up round it. Like a hand round a hurt bird. And then they forget to let go."
Her cold hands came back, slippery now with whatever was in the pot. They went round the hurt place. Slowly. In circles. Pressing in. Hard. Then harder.
It hurt worse than the throw had.
Oren sat and let it hurt. He fixed his eyes on a worn patch in the goatskin in front of him, a bare place shaped a little like a bird, and looked at it, and breathed, and did not make a sound. He had done this before. In the war. A surgeon's tent with a bit of leather between his teeth. You found something to look at. You looked at it. You breathed.
"You can make a noise," said Vespera. "Nobody will think less of you."
"I will."
She made a sound behind him. It might have been a laugh. It was hard to tell, with Vespera.
She went on. Round and round. And slowly, very slowly, he felt the fist in his back begin to open. Not all the way. A little. Like a hand that has been clenched all night, opening finger by finger in the morning.
"He said your name," said Vespera. Quietly. Her hands did not stop. "In her voice."
Oren did not answer.
"I heard it," said Vespera. "I've heard a lot of voices come out of people who weren't the people the voices belonged to. I've carried enough of the dead. I've had them talk through my own mouth, once or twice, when I wasn't careful." Round and round. "That was her. That was all her. He wasn't anywhere in it."
"I know."
"It'll happen again."
Oren's back went stiff under her hands. She waited until it let go again. Then she went on.
"Not often, perhaps," she said. "Not every day. But it will. When he's tired, or frightened, or when there's something she wants to say badly enough. She's in him now. Her hands. Her quiet. And now and then, her voice. And the thing she'll want to say most is your name." Round and round. "Because you're what's left of her. Out of everything she gave. You're what's left."
Oren stared at the bird-shaped patch on the hide.
"What do I do?" he said. Very low. "When she says it?"
Vespera's hands stopped. Just for a moment. Then started again.
"Answer her," she said.
He turned his head. Not all the way. Just enough to see the edge of her, out of the side of his eye. Her lined brown face. Her pale clear eyes.
"Answer her?"
"Say I'm here. Or Aunt. Or whatever you'd have said to her, if she'd lived to see you. Say it to his face, in your own voice." Vespera shrugged. "She gave everything she had. Every word. She went out of the world without a single one left to say goodbye with. And she's been waiting sixty years in an iron hat on a dead horse for somebody of hers to walk past." Round and round. "The least you can do is answer."
Oren did not say anything.
He sat with his shirt bunched up under his arms and the old woman's cold hands going round and round on the hurt place in his back, and the green sharp smell of the salve all round him, and looked at the hide in front of him, and did not say anything for a long time.
Then Vespera took her hands away, and wiped them on a rag, and pulled his shirt down for him at the back, the way you would for a child.
"That'll be stiff tomorrow," she said. "Worse than today. Then better. Don't carry anything heavy."
"I'll carry what needs carrying."
"I know you will," said Vespera. "That's why I said it. So I can say I told you."
And she put the stopper back in her pot, and went back to her own mat at the edge of the shade, and lay down with her back to him, and was asleep, or seemed to be, before he had his coat back on.
The Plan
Kestrel called them together when the sun was at the top.
That was the worst of the heat. The time when nobody could do anything but lie still in the shade and sweat. The glass outside the hides had gone from gold to white, too bright to look at, and the air over it shook like water. Even the ticking in the south seemed to have slowed, as if the iron things too were waiting for the worst of it to pass.
They sat in a rough ring on the felt, under the slanting hides, close enough to talk without raising their voices. Kestrel in the middle, on her heels. Arran on his back, with his foot up, listening with his eyes shut. Sef, down from the swell at last, his face burned dark red across the cheekbones where the strip of hide had not covered it. The boy against his pole, with Lyra beside him and the silent man on his other side, where he always was now. On the bad side. Oren across from them, sitting very straight.
Kestrel had drawn on the glass with Tansy's chalk. A rough map. A circle for where they were. A long straight line going north from it, for the Sun Road. A wavy line further north, and a small square on the wavy line.
"Here," she said, and tapped the circle. "The horse." She tapped the square. "Here. A chapel. A glass one. Old. From before." She looked round at them. "From here to the chapel is twelve miles. Walking well, one night. Dusk to dawn."
"With the litters?" said Oren.
Kestrel looked at the boy.
The boy looked back at her. Then he did something Oren had not expected. He put the end of his crutch down on the felt, and his right hand flat on the pole behind him, and stood up. Slowly, this time. His own way. The clerk's way, grunting a little, with the stiff left leg swinging out.
He stood there. On his own foot and the stick.
"Walk," he said. In his croak. And tapped the crutch on the felt, twice.
Kestrel looked at him a long moment.
"One litter, then," she said. "For the brother. Not two."
The boy sat down again. Carefully. He was gray in the face from the effort, and there was sweat on his lip. But he had stood.
Lyra put her hand on his arm. He let her.
"Why the chapel?" said Vespera. She was sitting at the back, a little apart, with her bag on her knees. "Why not straight on? Toward the middle. That's where we're going."
"Because there is nothing else," said Kestrel. "Between here and the middle, the Glass is open. Flat. No swells. No hollows. No rocks. Nothing." She swept her hand across the chalk, north of the square. "If the day finds us out there, there is nothing to put the shades against, and nothing to hide behind. They will see us from a day away. Him and his lights, and the iron. The chapel has walls. It has a roof. Half of one."
Tansy, sitting at the edge of the ring with her chin on her knees, nodded very seriously.
"There's nothing else to hide behind," she said. "Not for a day either side. I know. I've been all over. There's the chapel, and there's nothing." She looked round at them all with her big eyes. "Unless you like hiding behind nothing."
Arran laughed, on his back, with his eyes still shut.
"I like hiding behind nothing," he said. "It's very roomy."
"You will be hiding behind four men on poles," said Kestrel. "Be quiet."
Oren looked at the chalk map. At the long line of the Sun Road. At the square.
"Twelve miles," he said. "Through the night. Carrying one, and the boy on a stick. And the four lights coming on behind." He rubbed his jaw. "They'll gain on us."
"Yes," said Kestrel.
"How much?"
Kestrel did not answer at once.
"They walk faster than us," she said at last. "They are not carrying. They are not lame. But they do not know the Glass. They do not know where the soft places are, where the glass is thin over a hollow and you can go through. They do not know the way the stars move here. They will be careful. Careful is slow." She shrugged. "If we are lucky, they gain an hour. If we are not lucky, more."
"And when we get to the chapel? With them an hour behind?"
"Then we are in a building with walls," said Kestrel, "and they are not."
Nobody said anything to that.
Oren looked at her across the ring. At the narrow sharp brown face and the gray line down it and the short hair and the dark eyes. He had not liked her, at first, at the Edge. She had seemed hard to him. Cold. A woman who said this one and we and never I, as if she did not have one.
He liked her now. He had come to like her somewhere in the last few nights, without noticing. She was a good captain. She did not lie to her people. She told them the bad thing plainly, and then she told them what they were going to do about it, and then she did it.
He had served under worse. He had served under a great deal worse.
"Dusk, then," he said.
"Dusk," said Kestrel. "Rest now. All of you. Sleep, if you can. Tonight is long."
And she reached out with the flat of her hand and wiped the chalk map off the glass, all of it, in one sweep, the way you wipe a table.
The chalk left a pale smear on the glass. Tansy looked at it sadly. It had been her chalk.
Oren lay down on the felt. He did not mean to sleep. He lay on his right side, because his left was the bad one, with his sword along his leg and his head on his arm, and looked out under the edge of the hide at the white glare of the Glass. He could see the Sun Road from there. Pale on pale. Running away north, dead straight, into the shaking air.
Twelve miles, he thought. One night. A boy on a stick and a man on poles and an old woman and a child with a lamp. And four lights behind, that did not freeze.
He had done worse marches. He had done a great many worse marches. He had done one, once, in the war, of thirty miles in a night and a day, in the rain, with a fever, carrying another man's pike as well as his own because the other man had died at the start of it and nobody had told the sergeant. He had done that. He could do this.
But he had been nineteen, then. And he had had all his words.
He closed his eyes. Just for a moment. Just to rest them.
When he opened them again, the light had moved, and he knew he had slept after all. Not long. An hour, perhaps. Long enough for his back to set stiff as a board.
