Chapter 76: Oren's Watch, Part 4 of 5: The Last Lesson
The Last Lesson
He could not sleep again after that. So when the worst of the heat began to slide off the top of the sky, he got up, slowly, and went to find Lyra.
She was not asleep either. He had known she would not be. She was sitting at the edge of the shade with her knees up and her sword across them, oiling the blade with a rag, slowly, though it did not need oiling. The way he did with his own when he could not sleep. He had taught her that, without meaning to. She had watched him do it.
"Get up," he said. "Bring that."
She looked up at him.
"It's too hot."
"It'll be hotter in the chapel, with four men coming through the door." He jerked his head. "Horse's shadow. It's long enough now. Come on."
She came.
The shadow of the iron horse had grown since noon. It lay out across the glass to the east now, long and black and sharp-edged, wide enough for two people to stand in and move. And just beyond the end of it, a few paces off, ran the Sun Road.
Oren had looked at the Sun Road a great deal since they came to the horse. He could not help it. It was a strange thing. A band of paler glass, as wide as a city street, running away north dead straight, without a bend or a waver, as far as the eye could see. Straighter than any road a man could build. It hummed. You could hear it if you stood near. A low steady note, under the other sounds, like a finger on the rim of a glass. The Ashborn would not walk on it. They walked beside it. Always beside.
She had ridden down it. Sola. Kestrel had said so. On this horse, sixty years ago, at the end. Straight down the valley at the gray legions, with the gold light coming out of her chest. And where she rode, the glass had come out pale.
Oren stood in the horse's shadow with his back to the Sun Road and drew his sword.
"Today," he said, "I'm going to show you one thing. Only one. And then you're going to do it till you can't lift your arm."
"What thing?"
He did not answer at once.
He had been thinking about this all morning. Since the boy's hand. Since Vance, in her voice. Before that, if he was honest. Since Haverstock. Since the smith had sharpened his sword in her house and said Not to a Vance. He had been thinking about it in the back of his mind, the way you think about a thing you know you are going to have to do and do not want to.
"There's a stroke," he said at last. "My family's. My father taught it to me. His father taught it to him. All the way back, I don't know how far. It's not in any book. Nobody ever wrote it down." He almost smiled. "We weren't a family that wrote things down."
Lyra was very still.
"I've used it maybe six times in my life," said Oren. "In the war. When there was nothing else. It's a finishing stroke. You don't open with it. You don't play with it. You use it when the other man thinks he's got you." He turned the sword in his hand. "I've never shown it to anybody. I never had a son to show it to."
He did not look at her when he said that. He looked at the blade.
"I'm going to show it to you," he said.
"Why?"
He did not answer for a moment.
"If I lose it," he said, "you'll have it."
Lyra's face changed. "What do you mean, lose it?"
"Saints know." He shrugged. His back hurt when he shrugged. He did not care. "Things go. You know that. I know that. Words go. Faces go. The boy's name went, yesterday, for a whole day. It came back. Most things don't." He looked at her then. Straight. "I'd like this one kept."
She looked at him for a long time.
Then she nodded, once, and drew her own sword, and stepped into the shadow facing him.
"Show me," she said.
He showed her.
He did it slowly first. So slowly it was hardly a stroke at all. More like a shape. The sword starting low, low down on his left side, with the hilt by his left hip and the point down and back behind him, almost touching the glass. As if he had drawn it and let it hang. As if he was tired. As if he had given up.
"That's the lie," he said. "That's what they see. A man who's dropped his guard. A man who's beaten. They come in. They always come in."
Then the rise.
It came up from the hip. Not from the arm. That was the thing. That was the whole secret of it, and it took him twelve years to learn it properly, with his father's stick across his knuckles every time he got it wrong. The arm did nothing. The arm just held the sword. The stroke came from the left hip turning, the left foot pushing, the whole of the body uncoiling upward from the ground like a spring let go, and the sword riding up on top of all of it, across and up, from the left hip to above the right shoulder, in one long rising curve. Under the other man's guard. Under his arm. Up through the gap between his elbow and his ribs, where nobody ever covers, because nobody ever thinks a stroke can come from there.
He did it slowly, and stopped at the top, with the sword high over his right shoulder and his weight all forward on his right foot.
"Like that," he said.
Then he went back to the start. The lie. The low hanging point.
And did it at speed.
Lyra did not see it. He knew she did not. He saw her eyes, and they did not follow it. Nobody's eyes ever followed it. One moment the point was hanging down behind his left heel. The next it was high over his right shoulder, and there had been a sound in between, a single short sound, like a sheet being torn in half, and the air in the horse's shadow had moved. He felt it move. The hot still air, cut, and closing up again behind the blade.
He held it at the top a moment. Then he let the point come down.
He was breathing hard. His back was on fire. He did not care about that either. Because it had been good. It had been as good as he had ever done it. Maybe better. Maybe the best he had done it in his life. Here, in the shadow of her horse, beside her road. With nothing to take it from him. He had felt the whole of it, every part, the hip and the foot and the spring and the curve, all of them working together like the parts of a clock, perfect, the way his father had done it in the yard forty years ago while he watched with his mouth open.
He looked at Lyra.
She was staring at him. White. Her sword forgotten in her hand.
She knew. He could see that she knew. She had trained with the best masters on the Hill since she was a little girl, his lordship had seen to that, and she had seen good swordsmen and great ones, and she knew the difference. She knew she had just seen something rare. Something that might not be seen again. And she was looking at him the way you look at a bird you have never seen before, that has landed for one moment on a branch near you, and that you know will fly.
"Again," she said. Hoarse. "Do it again."
"No," said Oren. "You do it."
And she did.
Badly, at first. Of course badly. Everybody did it badly at first. She did it with her arm, the way everybody does, and the sword came up stiff and short and wrong. He showed her again. Slow. The hip. Not the arm. The arm is a stick on the end of you. The hip is the stroke. She did it again. Worse. Again. A little better. Again.
He walked round her while she did it. He put his hand on her left hip, and pushed, to show her where it turned. He put his boot against her left foot to show her where it pushed. He stood behind her and held her wrist and made her do it slow, with him, the two of them together, the way his father had once stood behind him in the yard.
And she did it again. And again. And again.
The sweat ran down her face. Her short dark hair was soaked flat. Her arm began to shake. She did not stop. He did not tell her to stop. She had always been like that. She would go until she fell down, and then she would get up and go again, and that was why he had liked her, from the first day, when she was a girl of ten with a wooden sword in his lordship's yard and he was a guard of thirty-two who had not wanted to teach a lord's daughter anything.
On the thirtieth time, or the fortieth, he stopped counting, something happened.
The sword came up from her hip.
Not all the way. Not right. Not fast. But from the hip. He saw it. He saw the turn go through her, low down, and the sword ride up on top of it. Just for a moment. Just once.
And the air in the shadow moved.
Lyra stopped. She stood there with the sword over her right shoulder and her chest heaving and looked at him. She had felt it too.
Oren nodded. Slowly.
"That's it," he said. "That's the one. Now do that a thousand more times, and it's yours."
She laughed. Breathless. Half a sob. And put the point down on the glass and leaned on it.
"A thousand?"
"I did ten thousand," said Oren. "You've got a start on me. You've got me to show you. I had a stick across my knuckles." He wiped the sweat out of his eyes with the back of his hand. "Rest. Then we go again."
The Man Does It Once
They went again. And again. Until the shadow of the horse had grown so long that its head lay out across the Sun Road, black on the pale glass, and the heat had begun at last to ease.
People came to watch. Oren had not asked them to. He did not like being watched. But on the Glass there was nothing else to look at, and a man and a girl with swords dancing in a horse's shadow was something. So they came, one and two at a time, and sat at the edge of the shade with their knees up, and watched.
Arran, propped up on his elbows on his hides, grinning. Brin and Oda. Tansy, who sat so close that Oren had to tell her twice to move back, and then sat exactly as close a little further along. Vespera, at the back, saying nothing, with her pale eyes very sharp.
The boy, against his pole. Watching Lyra. Only Lyra. With a look on his face that Oren did not have a word for, and did not try to find one.
And the silent man.
He was sitting beside the boy, as he always was now. With his long legs folded and his long hands on his knees. He had let go of the boy's wrist that morning, after the waking, and he had not taken it again. But he sat as close. And he watched the swords the way he watched everything. With no face at all. As if he was looking at something a very long way behind them. As if they were not there.
Oren did not pay him any mind. He had stopped paying the man any mind days ago. The man was like the horse, or the shades, or the Sun Road. A thing that was there.
Lyra did the stroke again. It came from the hip that time. Not all the way. But more than before. More than halfway.
"Better," said Oren.
She grinned. Her face was red and wet and the grin went right across it.
And the silent man stood up.
Oren did not see him do it. Nobody did. That was what Oren thought about, afterward, for a long time. One moment the man was sitting folded up beside the boy at the edge of the shade. The next he was standing. Not getting up. Standing. The way water stands up in a fountain. All of him at once, in one movement, his long thin body going from folded to straight without any moment in between.
The watchers went quiet.
The man walked out into the horse's shadow.
He did not look at anybody. Not at Oren. Not at Lyra. He walked out slowly, in his stiff dark coat, with his great wild beard and his matted hair and his long thin hands hanging at his sides, and stopped a few paces from Lyra. And looked at her sword.
Lyra looked at Oren. Oren did not know what to tell her. So he did not tell her anything.
The man held out his hand.
Not asking. Not demanding. Just held it out. Palm up. Long and pale and very still.
Lyra looked at it. Then, slowly, she turned her sword round and put the hilt into it.
The man's fingers closed on the hilt.
And something happened to him.
Oren saw it. It was not much. Most people would not have seen it at all. But Oren had spent thirty years watching men pick up swords, and he knew what he was seeing. The man's hand closed on the grip, and his whole body changed. Not a lot. A little. His shoulders came down. His weight shifted, very slightly, onto the balls of his feet. His long thin fingers settled round the leather, one after another, in exactly the right places. Not tight. Not loose. The way a man picks up a pen he has written with every day of his life. The way a carpenter picks up his own hammer.
As if the sword had been the missing part of him, and now it was back.
The man stood in the horse's shadow and let the point drop.
Low. Down by his left hip. The point down and back, almost touching the glass behind his heel. The lie. The beaten man. The tired one, who has given up.
Oren's mouth went dry.
And the man did the Vance cut.
Once.
It came from the hip. All the way from the hip. From the ground, through the foot, through the turn, the whole long body uncoiling upward like a whip cracked from the handle, and the sword riding up on top of it in the rising curve, across and up, from the left hip to high over the right shoulder.
Perfectly.
And fast. Faster than Oren. Faster than Oren had ever done it in his life, even at twenty, even at his very best, even in the war with his life on the end of it. So fast that Oren did not see it either. He only heard it. That one short sound. The sheet torn in half. And then the man was standing there with the sword high over his right shoulder and his weight all forward on his right foot, exactly as Oren had stood an hour before, and the air in the shadow was closing up behind the blade.
Nobody breathed.
The man held it there. One heartbeat. Two.
Then he let the point come down.
He turned the sword round in his hand. Hilt first. And held it out to Lyra, the way she had held it out to him.
She took it. Her hand was shaking.
And the man walked back to the edge of the shade, slowly, in his stiff dark coat, and folded himself down beside the boy again. Long legs. Long hands on his knees. And looked at nothing.
As if nothing had happened at all.
It was Tansy who broke it.
"He's never done that," she said. In a small, high, astonished voice. "Never. Not once. Two winters, and he's never done anything." She looked from the man to Oren and back. "Where did he learn that?"
Nobody answered her.
Oren stood in the horse's shadow with his sword hanging from his hand.
He was thinking of his father. In the yard. The stick across his knuckles. From the hip, boy. Not the arm. The hip. Twelve years before he got it right. Ten thousand times. And when Oren finally did it properly, the first time, at nineteen, his father had put down the stick and said, Good. Now there's two of us in the world can do that.
And Oren had said, Only two?
And his father had said, Only two. Your grandfather's dead. Keep it that way. In the family.
That stroke was not in any book. It had never been written down. It had gone from father to son in one family, in one yard, for as long as anybody knew. Nobody outside it had ever learned it. Nobody could have. You could not learn it by watching. Oren had just spent the whole afternoon proving that. You had to have it put into your body, slowly, over years, by somebody who already had it.
And this man had it.
Not copied. Oren knew copying. He had seen men try to copy strokes they had seen once. They got the shape and missed the spring. This man had the spring. He had all of it. He had it deeper and faster and cleaner than Oren did. As if he had done it ten thousand times. As if he had done it twenty thousand. As if he had had it put into him, slowly, over years, by someone who had it.
A long time ago.
Oren looked across the shadow at the man. At the wild beard and the matted hair and the empty face. At the long thin hands that had settled round the grip like coming home.
"He's done that before," said Oren. Quietly. Not to anybody. "Better than me."
He was quiet a moment longer.
"A long time ago."
His Lordship
They rested, after. In the shade, with the light going long and orange outside the hides, and the heat draining slowly out of the air. Lyra sat with her back against a pole and her sword across her knees and her arm hanging, too tired to lift it. Oren sat beside her. Not close. A hand's width off.
He had not meant to talk. He did not, mostly. But the afternoon had shaken something loose in him. The voice, and the hand-signs, and the man in the shadow doing his father's stroke better than his father ever had. Something had come loose, and was rattling about inside him, and the only way to make it stop, he thought, was to let some of it out.
So he talked about her father.
He had never done that. Not with her. Not since the night his lordship died. There had been no time for it. They had been running ever since, through the snow, out of the city, north. They had not even been there when the family put him in the vault. Lyra had been in the hills with a dying town and a boy who could not remember her, and Oren had been with her, because that was where he was needed. And he did not have the words anyway. He was not good with words even before he started losing them.
He did not have them now, either. But he started anyway.
"Twenty-two years," he said.
Lyra turned her head.
"I was with him twenty-two years," said Oren. "Your father. I came to the house when I was twenty-four. Straight from the army. I didn't know how to do anything but stand at a gate. I didn't know which fork. I didn't know how to talk to a lord. I'd never been in a house with a carpet."
He stopped. Lyra waited. She was good at waiting, with him. She always had been.
"First week," said Oren, "I saluted him. In the hall. Every time he came past. The way you salute an officer. Heels together, hand up, the whole thing. He came down the stairs in the morning in his slippers with a cup of tea, and I banged my heels and saluted, and he jumped and spilled it all down his front."
Lyra made a small sound. Almost a laugh.
"He laughed," said Oren. "He stood there with tea all down him and laughed. Not at me. Not quite. He said, Oren, this is my house, not a barracks. You don't have to salute me in my own hall. And I said, Yes, my lord. And saluted."
The laugh got out of Lyra, then. Small and tired and real.
"And he laughed again," said Oren. "Harder. He had to sit down on the stairs." He paused. "So I did it every day after that. Every morning. When he came down. Heels and hand. All's well, sir. To make him laugh. And he did. Every morning. Twenty-two years. Even at the end, when he — even at the end. When he didn't know me anymore. I'd salute, and say it, and he'd look at me with that smile he had then, the polite one, and I'd think, he doesn't know who I am. And then he'd laugh. Just a little. Every time. As if something in him still knew the joke, even when the rest was gone."
Lyra had stopped laughing. She was looking at him.
Oren looked at his hands.
"He used to sit by the fire in the study," he said. "In the evenings. In the big —" He stopped. He felt the hole open up in front of him. The step that was not there. He went round it. "In the big leather thing. The one by the fire, with the arms. The brown one. That he sat in."
"I know the one," said Lyra quietly.
"He sat in that, with a book. Every evening. And I'd stand at the door. And sometimes he'd read me a bit. Out loud. A bit he liked. He knew I couldn't read. He never said anything about it. Not once in twenty-two years. He'd just read me a bit, sometimes, as if it was the most natural thing in the world, and ask me what I thought. And I'd tell him. And he'd listen." Oren rubbed his jaw. "Nobody ever asked me what I thought about a book before. Or after."
The light outside the shade had gone from orange to red.
"He was a good man," said Oren. "Your father. He was a good man, and a kind one, and a bit of a fool sometimes, and he laughed at things that weren't funny and didn't laugh at things that were. And he was brave. Braver than anybody knew. At the end. What he did, at the end, burning it all. So they couldn't have it." His voice went rough. "I was outside the door that night. I didn't know. I was outside the door with my sword, keeping him safe. And he was inside, doing that. On his own. And I didn't know till the morning."
He stopped. He could not go on. The words had run out. Not lost. Just run out. Like water out of a skin.
Lyra did not say anything.
She just leaned over, slowly, the hand's width that was between them. And put her head on his shoulder.
Oren sat very still.
She had done that when she was ten. Once. In the yard, after she fell off the wall and cut her chin and would not cry. She had come and sat beside him on the mounting block, and put her head on his arm, because she could not reach his shoulder then. And he had sat very still, the way he was sitting now, not knowing what to do, a big man with a sword who did not know anything about little girls. And after a while she had got up and gone back to the wall.
She was not ten now. She was a woman with short hair and a sword-arm like a whip and her father's eyes. And she could reach his shoulder.
He did not know what to do now either.
So he did nothing. He sat still. And let her. And looked out at the red light on the glass and the long black shadow of the horse, and the Sun Road running away north into the coming dark.
After a while, without quite meaning to, he put his big hand on the top of her head. On the short damp hair. And left it there.
