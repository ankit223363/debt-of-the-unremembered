Chapter 61: The Free Valleys, Part 1 of 5: Reed's End
Reed's End
The reeds ended where the world began to climb, and Corin Vane stood at the edge of both with a book in his hand that told him who the girl beside him was.
It was the seventh bell, or near it. There were no bells out here to say so. There was only the light, which had been gray for an hour and was now turning the thin, pale yellow of weak tea, low in the east, behind a long bank of cloud. The cold had a smell. It smelled of rot and mud and old wet rope, and under that, of something clean and sharp that might have been snow coming.
Behind him were the fens. He had spent the whole night in them, lying under the hooped canvas of Wat's punt while the reeds slid past on both sides, and he had not seen them properly until now. Now he turned and looked.
They went on forever. Dead brown reeds, taller than a man, standing so thick that the eye could not get between them. Every one of them was moving, because the wind was moving, and the wind out here never stopped. They rattled. It was a dry, endless sound, like a crowd of people whispering in a room next door. Here and there between them lay black water, still as glass, with a skin of thin new ice on it that ticked and cracked when the wind leaned on the reeds. A heron stood in one of those black pools on one leg, gray as a stone, and did not look at him.
The last of the reeds stopped at a bank of mud and frozen grass. Someone, long ago, had built a jetty there. There were three planks left of it. They were green and soft and sunk at one end into the water, so that the jetty went down into the fen like a ramp. Wat had brought the punt up against it an hour ago, and held it there with his pole while they climbed out, one by one, onto the frozen mud.
Now the punt was a long way off. Corin could see it, just. A small dark shape on a channel of black water, going east, with a red-bearded man standing at the back of it, pushing with his pole, and a basket in the front of it with a baby in it. Every so often the shape went behind a wall of reeds and was gone, and then came out again, smaller.
He watched it until it did not come out again.
"He's gone," said Lyra.
She was standing a little way up the bank from him, with her arms folded inside Doctor Silas's old black coat. The coat was too big for her. The sleeves came down over her hands. Under it she wore a boy's breeches and a sheepskin jerkin and laced boots, and on her head a brown wool cap pulled down over her ears, with her short dark hair sticking out under it at the back. She looked, Corin thought, like a stable boy who had stolen a gentleman's coat. He did not say so. He did not know yet whether she was the kind of person you said that kind of thing to.
That was the trouble. He did not know what kind of person she was.
He had a book that told him.
It was in his hand now. A green cloth book, a little bigger than his palm, with a tree worked on the front of it in gold thread, and a crown in the branches of the tree. Under the tree were words, stitched in silver, in letters that leaned backward as if they were walking into a wind. The dark is the day with its eyes shut. The book had belonged to her mother. Then it had been on her father's desk. Then it had been in the house of a man who hunted him. And last night, in a white room at the top of a gray house, after her father had died, she had put it in Corin's hands and said that it was his now.
He had written in it straight away. He had needed to. His old Ledger was full, and the old Ledger was going to stay in the city with a girl called Juno, under a mattress. So the green book was Ledger VIII. He had written that on the inside of the cover, in his square crooked capitals, before he had written anything else.
He opened it now, standing on the frozen mud, and read the first page.
He did that every morning. He did it before he did anything else, because the first page was where he kept himself. The first page told him his name, and how old he was, and where he had been born, and what he did for a living, and who could be trusted. He did not remember those things. Not the way other people did. He knew them because the page said so, and because he had read the page every morning for so long that he knew the shape of it in the dark.
This was a new page. He had copied it last night from the old Ledger, by candlelight, in the crypt of Saint Bride's, while the girl read the old page aloud to him one line at a time so that he would get it right. His hand had shaken a little. Some of the letters had come out wrong, and he had crossed them out and done them again.
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin Vane. You are twenty-eight years old. You were born in a place called Oakhaven. You do not remember it. You are a harvester.
There was more. The names of people. The rules. Do not burn. If you must burn, write down what it cost. He read them all. Then, near the bottom, the line he had copied with the most care, because the girl had stopped reading it aloud for a moment before she said it, and he had looked up and seen her face.
LYRA THORNE. PARTNER. RULE ONE: NO LIES BETWEEN US. NOT EVEN THE KIND ONES.
And under that, last night, in his own hand:
I DO NOT REMEMBER HER. SHE READ THIS PAGE TO ME. SHE IS COMING AFTER.
And beside the R of AFTER, a small spot, brown now, that had been red when she put it there with her thumb.
She had come after. She was standing a little way up the bank, pretending to look at the hills.
He knew she was pretending, because twice now he had looked up from the page and caught her looking at him, and twice she had turned her head away at once, quickly, and gone back to the hills. He thought she was watching him read about her. He thought, perhaps, that it was not an easy thing to watch.
He did not know her. That was the plain truth, and the page said there were to be no lies, so he would not pretend otherwise. Four nights ago, under a falling roof of glass, he had burned every memory he had of her to hold the roof up, and they had gone into the fire one by one, and he had kept only one thing. Her voice. Her voice saying his name, quick and a little cross. He had that. He could hear it now, if he wanted to, as clearly as if she had just said it.
But her face, when he looked at it, was a stranger's face. A thin, pale face with a small straight nose, and dark brows, and a small worried line between the brows, and gray-green eyes that were too old for the rest of it. A pretty face, he supposed. He did not know if he had used to think so.
He had read a whole book about her. He had read it in the crypt on Tuesday, all of it, every page, and there had been something about her on every page. She found me in the rain at the door of the Inkwell. She hit a constable. She cut her hair and sold it for my leg. She says I walk like a heron. He knew all of those things now. He knew them the way you know the facts in a history book about a country you have never been to.
He closed the green book and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where it always went.
"You read the page," said Lyra, still looking at the hills.
"Yes."
"Every morning?"
"Every morning." He thought about it. "The page says so. On the page."
She made a small sound in her nose that might have been a laugh. "That's very like you," she said, and then she seemed to wish she had not said it, and her mouth went tight.
Oren came up the bank behind them, stamping the cold out of his boots.
He was a big man, broad and gray-bearded, with a face like a weathered fence post and eyes that were always, always looking at the way in and the way out of wherever he was. His left arm was in a sling made out of a flour sack, and his sword hung on his right hip. He had carried two packs out of the punt, his own and Corin's, and he had not asked whether he should. He set them down on the frozen grass now, one beside the other, and straightened up, and looked at the fens, and then at the hills, and then at the two of them.
"Well," said Oren. "That's the boat gone."
"Yes," said Corin.
"That's the river gone, too. And the city." Oren rubbed his beard with his good hand. "I'll miss none of it. Saints help me, I'll not miss one stone of it." He looked north. "What's that way?"
They all looked north.
The land rose up out of the fens in a long, slow swell, white with old snow, and on the top of the swell stood a line of low hills, rounded like the backs of sleeping animals. A sheep path climbed toward them, a narrow brown thread in the white. Beyond the hills the sky was very pale. And at the very bottom of the sky, between two of the hills, so thin and so far away that Corin was not sure he was not imagining it, there was a line of light. A pale, flat, shining line, like the edge of a knife held up against the sky.
"That," said Corin. "That way."
Nobody said the name of it. They did not need to.
He picked up his crutch from where he had leaned it against the jetty post. It was a good crutch, made of ash, with a pad of folded cloth bound round the top. Doctor Silas had given it to him. His left leg was still not right. There was a hole in the back of his thigh, above the knee, where an iron rod had gone in a week ago, and it was healing, but slowly, the way things heal in winter.
He put the crutch under his arm.
"Shall we go, then?" said Lyra. She did not say it to him. She said it to the hills.
"Yes," said Corin. "Let's go."
Out of the Fens
The sheep path was frozen hard, and it was steeper than it looked.
It went up the slope in short zigzags, the way sheep go, never straight at anything. The ground on either side was white with old snow that had melted a little and frozen again, so that it had a crust on it like the top of a pie. Where the path went, the crust had been broken by many small hooves, and the broken bits had frozen into sharp brown ridges. The ridges rolled under his boot. The crutch slid on them.
Corin had learned, in the last week, how to walk with a crutch. You put the crutch forward and the bad leg forward together, and then you swung the good leg past them, and then you did it again. On a flat floor it was easy. On a city street it was not too hard. On a frozen sheep path going uphill, it was a new kind of work altogether. The crutch kept finding the one smooth patch of ice between two ridges. Twice it slid out from under him, and twice he caught himself on his good leg with a jolt that went up his spine into his teeth.
The third time, Oren's hand was there under his elbow before he had finished starting to fall.
"Steady," said Oren.
"I'm steady."
"Aye. You look it." Oren let go, but he stayed close, a half step behind, on the downhill side. He did not say anything else about it. After a while Corin stopped noticing he was there, which he thought was probably the point.
Lyra went ahead.
She did not go far ahead. She went exactly far enough that she was not walking beside him, and then she stayed there, as if somebody had tied a length of string between her and him and she was keeping it tight. Every so often she stopped and looked back, not at him, but past him, at the fens, as if she were checking that they were still there. And she was counting. He could hear her. Not loud. Under her breath, the way a person hums without knowing it. Forty-one. Forty-two. Forty-three.
He could hear the words, but the big numbers did not mean anything to him. That was one of the things the page did not say, because he had not known it when he wrote the page. He could feel numbers up to nine. One, two, three: he knew what those were, the way he knew what his own hands were. But past nine there was nothing. The words were there, ten and twenty and forty-three, but when he reached for what they meant, his hand closed on air. He had burned the rest a long time ago, in a cellar, for a spell he did not remember casting.
So when she counted, it was only a sound to him. A small, steady, private sound, like a clock. He found that he liked it.
He tried something.
He had not spoken to Malakor since the night before. There had not been time. And there was always a little fear in it now, in reaching inward, because of where Malakor lived. The old man had lived for years and years inside a silver rod. Then, on the night of the dome, he had moved, the way a man moves house in the night. He had moved into Corin. Into the room inside Corin where Lyra used to be.
Corin could not describe that room, even to himself. It was not a real room. But it had a shape, the way a missing tooth has a shape when you put your tongue in the gap. It was the shape of everything he had burned to hold up the dome. And it was not empty anymore. An old man sat in it now, dry and sour and kind, and talked.
Malakor.
For a moment there was nothing.
Then the answer came, and it was wrong. Not the words. The sound of them. Malakor's voice was always close, close as his own thoughts, just behind his left ear. Now it was thin. It was far away. It was like a man talking through a wall, in the next room of an inn, when you can hear that he is talking but you have to hold your breath to catch the words.
I hear you, boy. I'm here.
You sound strange.
It's the Glass. A pause, as if the old man were catching his breath. We're a long way from it yet, and I can feel it already. It's like walking toward a big fire. You feel the heat on your face long before you see the flames.
Does it hurt?
No. Another pause. It's quiet, that's all. It's very, very quiet, the Glass. Nothing burns there. Nothing is taken there. A thing like me is made of burning and taking. The voice went thinner still, then came back. Never mind me. It's only the edge of it. Walk.
Corin walked.
The path climbed out of the last of the fen-mist, and the air changed. It got colder, and cleaner, and drier. The smell of rot fell away behind them. He could smell snow now, real snow, and sheep, and something else, faint and sweet, that he could not name.
He stopped at the top of a zigzag to breathe. His leg was hot and aching, the good kind of aching, the kind that meant it was working. His hands were cold in spite of Silas's gray wool carter's gloves.
His hands were always cold now.
He pulled off the left glove and looked at the hand.
It was gray. Not the gray of dirt, or of cold. It was the gray of slate, of the stone they put on roofs in the city: a smooth, dark, even gray, with a faint shine to it, as if it had been polished. The gray went up past his wrist, up his forearm, past his elbow. He knew without looking that it went past that too, up the back of his arm, almost to the shoulder. A hand's width below the shoulder, less a finger. Doctor Silas had measured it with his own hand.
And the tips of his fingers were black. Not gray. Black, like burned paper, as far as the first joint. He could not feel them. He could tell them to move, and they moved, but when they touched something, there was nothing. It was like touching the world through thick wool.
No more burning, Silas had said. Not a spell. Not a candle. If you burn again, the gray goes up. And when it reaches your face, Corin, you will be a Slate. You will be one of those men who carry crates and do not know their sons. Do you understand me?
He understood.
He put the glove back on.
Lyra had stopped too, a little way above him on the path. She had seen him look at the hand. She did not say anything about it. She only stood with her arms folded inside the big black coat and waited, and when he started to climb again, she started again too, and kept her string tight.
Fifty-eight, she said, under her breath. Fifty-nine. Sixty.
At the top of the slope the ground leveled out, and the hills were close now, close enough to see that they were not smooth, as they had looked from the fens. They were rough with heather and gray rock, and the snow lay in their hollows and not on their tops, where the wind had scoured it off. Between the two nearest hills, a valley opened, and down the middle of the valley ran a road.
It was a very old road. He could see that even from here. It ran between two low walls of piled gray stone, and the walls ran on and on, up the valley and over the shoulder of the next hill and out of sight, like two gray threads somebody had laid across the country and forgotten to pick up.
Oren came up beside him and looked at it, and his face did something Corin had not seen it do before. It softened.
"Oh, now," said Oren. "Would you look at that."
"What is it?"
"A drove road." Oren said it the way another man might say a good dinner, or a warm bed. "They drove the cattle down to the city on those, in the old days. Hundreds of beasts at a time. Thousands. Walls on both sides so they'd not wander." He breathed out a long, white breath. "We marched on one once. In the war. Four days on a road like that, and never saw a soul, and never got lost, because you can't get lost between two walls."
He started down toward it at once, without waiting for them, as if it were somebody he knew.
Lyra looked at Corin.
For the first time that morning, she looked at him straight, and did not look away.
"He likes roads," she said. "He always did. He used to say a road was the only honest thing in the world, because it only ever goes where it says."
Corin thought about that.
"Did I know that?" he said. "Before?"
She was quiet for a moment.
"I don't know," she said. "I don't know what you knew. I only know what you said."
Then she turned and went down after Oren, toward the road between the walls.
Questions
The road was easier.
It was flat, more or less, and the stones of it had been worn smooth by a hundred years of hooves, and between the walls the wind could not get at them so badly. The snow on the road was trodden into a hard gray crust by sheep and the odd cart, and the crutch did not slide on it. Corin found a rhythm. Crutch and bad leg, good leg. Crutch and bad leg, good leg. After half an hour he stopped thinking about it.
Once he stopped thinking about his feet, he started thinking about her.
She was walking a little ahead of him again, on the left side of the road, close to the wall. Oren was out in front, a long way out, walking with his head up and his good hand swinging, like a man walking home. The girl walked with her head down. She walked fast and light, on the balls of her feet, and every so often she put out her hand and touched the top of the wall as she passed, the way a child does, without seeming to know she was doing it.
He had read a whole book about her, and he still did not know what to say to her.
That seemed wrong to him. He thought about it for a long time, walking. It seemed to him that if he had read a whole book about her, he ought to know her a little. And he did know things. He knew a great many things. He just did not know what any of them felt like.
So, after a while, he did the only thing he could think of. He asked.
"Can I ask you something?"
She looked round. "You can ask."
"Things from the book. From the old Ledger. I read it on Tuesday, in the crypt. All of it." He moved his crutch and his leg. "There are things in it I don't understand. Things I wrote. I don't know what I meant by them."
She slowed down, until she was walking beside him instead of ahead. She did not look at him. She looked at the road.
"Go on, then," she said.
"It says you hit a constable with a teapot."
There was a pause.
"It was a jug," said Lyra.
"It says teapot."
"Well, it was a jug. A milk jug. Blue and white. It was the only thing on the table." She frowned at the road. "You weren't even there. You only heard about it after. Write that down."
"Why did you hit him?"
"He put his hand on my father." She said it flatly, as if it were a fact about the weather. "He was taking my father to the Health, and my father didn't want to go, and he put his hand on him to make him go. So I hit him with the jug, and then Oren sat on him."
Ahead of them, Oren, who could not possibly have heard, lifted his good hand and waved it once over his head without turning round, as if to say yes, I remember.
Corin found that he was nearly smiling.
He took the green book out of his coat and opened it to the next clean page, and leaned the book on the top of the wall, and wrote with the stub of pencil he kept in the spine. His letters were crooked. They were always crooked. His black fingertips could not feel the pencil, so he held it in his fist, like a child.
IT WAS A JUG. BLUE AND WHITE. HE PUT HIS HAND ON HER FATHER.
She watched him write it. He felt her watch, and then felt her stop watching, very suddenly, the way a person takes their hand off something hot.
When he looked up, she had put both her hands deep inside the sleeves of the black coat, so that they did not show at all.
He did not ask about that. He did not know why she had done it, but he thought he could guess, and he thought guessing out loud would be unkind. She could not write. She had been able to, a week ago. She had paid it away, folding something small into her father's dead hand. Now every time he wrote a thing down, he was doing a thing in front of her that she could not do.
He put the book away.
They walked.
After a while she said, without looking at him, "Is that all? The jug?"
"No. There's a lot."
"Then ask."
"It says you can't cook."
"I can cook."
"It says you burned porridge on a barge."
"That was the barge's fault." A pause. "It was a very bad stove." A longer pause. "All right. I can't cook. Hessa could cook." Her voice went small on the name, and then came back. "Next."
He knew who Hessa was. He had read that too. A woman on a barge, with a red-bearded husband and a baby. Dead now. Burned with her boat, after she handed the baby to her husband and her father to the gray coats. The book said so. He had written it himself. He did not remember her face.
"It says you sing," he said. "To babies. And to me, once, when I had a fever."
"Everybody sings to babies."
"It says you sang Hessa's song. In the crypt. When Silas was cutting my leg."
This time she did not answer for a long time. They passed a gate in the wall, an old wooden gate gray with lichen, tied shut with a rope. Beyond it a field of white went up the side of the hill, with a few dark shapes of sheep on it, all facing the same way, away from the wind.
"Yes," she said at last. "I sang it. You were holding my hand so hard I thought you'd break it. I had to sing something, or I'd have screamed." She kicked a stone on the road, and it went skittering ahead of them. "You said afterward I sang flat."
"Did you?"
"Yes." Something like a smile went across her face and was gone. "I always sing flat. My mother sang. I don't."
He did not write that down. He thought he would remember it. He was not sure why he was so sure.
"One more," he said.
"Go on."
He had been saving it. He did not know why. It was the one that had stopped him, on Tuesday, in the crypt, so that he had put the Ledger down on his knees and sat looking at nothing for a long time, until Mrs. Doyle came down the steps with the bread.
"It says you cut your hair. And sold it. On Comb Street. For two crowns and four shillings. To pay for my leg."
She stopped walking.
She stood quite still in the middle of the road, with her hands in her sleeves, and her face went the way a window goes when somebody draws the curtain across it from the inside.
"Not that one," she said.
"I only want to know—"
"I know what you want to know." She was not angry. That was the strange thing. She was very calm, and very pale. "I'm not going to lie to you. That's the rule. It's in your book. So I'm not going to tell you a lie, and I'm not going to tell you the truth, either. Not about that one." She drew a breath. "Not that one. Not yet."
The words went into him and found something.
He did not know what it was. It was not a memory. He had no memories of her. It was more like the feeling you get when you walk into a room you have never been in, and you know, before you see it, exactly where the window will be.
Not yet. He had read those words. In the old Ledger, on a page about a hay wain in the dark, in his own square capitals: SHE SAYS NOT YET. WHEN I CAN. He had read them on Tuesday like a line in a history book. Now he heard them in her voice, the one thing of her he had kept, and the line in the book and the voice in his head came together, the way two halves of a torn page fit along the tear.
It was not a memory. It was only a fit. He let it go.
"All right," he said. "Not yet."
She looked at him for a long moment, and he could not read her face at all.
Then she nodded, once, and turned, and walked on ahead of him again, fast, on the balls of her feet. Her hand came out of her sleeve and touched the top of the wall as she passed it, and went back in.
