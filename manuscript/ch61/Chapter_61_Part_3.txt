Chapter 61: The Free Valleys, Part 3 of 5: Bread by the Wall
Bread by the Wall
They stopped to eat when the sun was as high as it was going to get, which was not very high.
There was a place where the road bent round the foot of a hill, and the wall on the north side had been built up higher than the rest, thick and solid, with a big flat stone set into it like a bench. Somebody had made it for exactly this, a long time ago. A place for drovers to sit out of the wind. The snow had not drifted there. The ground in the lee of the wall was bare and brown, with dead bracken flattened on it, and when Corin let himself down onto the stone bench, the wind was suddenly gone, and the weak sun lay on his face, and it was almost warm.
He closed his eyes.
He heard Oren drop the packs. He heard the girl sit down at the other end of the bench, as far from him as the bench allowed. He heard the stream, much smaller now, a thread of water talking to itself somewhere under the snow. And under everything, in his teeth, the hum.
When he opened his eyes, Oren was undoing the sack that Doctor Silas had packed for them.
Silas had packed it in the crypt last night, while they were saying their goodbyes, without asking anybody what they wanted. He had packed it the way he did everything: quickly, tiredly, and exactly right. Two round loaves of brown bread, wrapped in a cloth. A lump of hard yellow cheese. A twist of paper with salt in it. A little bag of oats. Six apples, small and wrinkled from the winter store. And a stone jar with a cork in it, and a label on the side in Silas's tall, untidy writing: HONEY. FOR THE LEG. ALSO FOR EATING. DON'T TELL ME WHICH.
Oren read the label without reading it. He could not read. He held the jar up to Lyra, and she read it out to him, and he laughed his short barking laugh.
"That man," said Oren. "That man should have been a sergeant."
He tore a loaf in three with his one good hand, and passed the pieces round, and cut cheese with his knife against his thumb. Lyra uncorked the honey and held it out, and Corin dipped the end of his bread into it.
He had to take his gloves off to eat. He did not like to. But the bread kept slipping in the thick wool, so he pulled the gloves off with his teeth and laid them on the stone beside him.
The bread was hard on the outside and soft in the middle, and the honey was dark and slow, and he could taste it perfectly. That was one thing the gray had not touched. He could taste. He could smell. He could see and hear. It was only his hands.
He tried to tear a second piece off his bread, and it fell.
He had not felt it go. That was the trouble. His fingers were closed round the crust, and then they were closed round nothing, and the bread was on the ground, in the dead bracken, honey side down.
He picked it up. That was harder than it should have been. He could see his fingers. He could tell them to close. But when they touched the bread he could not feel it, so he did not know how hard to hold, and the first time he squeezed too little and it slipped out again, and the second time he squeezed too hard and it broke in two in his hand and fell again.
He sat and looked at it.
He was not angry. He was too tired to be angry. He only sat, with his black fingertips held out in front of him, and looked at the two pieces of bread on the ground, and thought: this is how it will be now. This is all the rest of it.
A hand came into the edge of his sight.
It was the girl's hand. She had taken her glove off too, and her hand was small and pale and very cold-looking, with the knuckles red. There was a little bump on the side of her second finger, a hard little bump, the kind people get from holding a pen for years. She picked up the two pieces of bread. She brushed the bracken off them, briskly, the way you brush crumbs off a child's coat. Then she took her own piece of bread, the whole piece, and broke it neatly into three, and dipped each one in the honey, and put them down in a row on the stone beside his knee, where he could pick them up one at a time with his whole hand, like a child picks up a stone.
She did all of it without a word. Without looking at him. As if she had done it a hundred times before.
Then she seemed to see what she had done.
Her hand stopped. Her face went red, all at once, from the collar of the black coat right up to the edge of the wool cap. She put her glove back on very quickly and folded her arms and looked hard at the hillside on the other side of the road, as if there were something there she urgently needed to see.
Corin looked at the three pieces of bread in a row by his knee.
He picked up the first one. It was easy. It was the right size for his hand.
"Thank you," he said.
"It's nothing."
"It isn't nothing."
She did not answer. Oren, at the other end of the stone, was very busy cutting cheese.
Corin ate the bread. All three pieces. Then he wiped his fingers on his coat and took the green book out and found the pencil in the spine, and opened to the page where he had written about the jug.
He wrote slowly. The pencil was hard to hold. He held it in his fist.
SHE BREAKS BREAD LIKE SOMEBODY WHO HAS DONE IT BEFORE.
He looked at it for a while. Then he wrote, underneath:
I THINK SHE HAS DONE IT BEFORE. FOR ME.
He did not show it to her. He put the book away.
They sat in the lee of the wall a little longer, out of the wind, in the thin sun. Oren ate an apple, core and all, and fed the stalk to nothing in particular. The girl took off her cap and pushed her hand through her short hair, and it stood up on end, and she put the cap back on over it.
Her hair was very short. It came down only to the bottom of her ears. It was dark, almost black, and it was cut badly, unevenly, as if someone had done it in a hurry with a knife. Corin knew who had done it, and with which knife, and why. The book had told him. But he looked at it now and thought, for the first time, not about the book, but only about the hair. That it must have been long, before. That she must have had to choose.
She caught him looking. She glared.
"What?"
"Nothing."
"It's not nothing. You were looking at my hair."
"Yes."
"Well, don't."
"All right."
She glared at him a moment longer, and then something in her face gave way a little. Not much. A crack, like the first crack in the ice on a pond.
"It's very cold," she said, "with it short. Nobody tells you that. The back of your neck gets cold all the time." She pulled the cap down at the back. "That's all."
"I'll remember," said Corin.
She looked at him strangely when he said that.
Then Oren stood up and stamped his boots and said, "Right. If we sit here any longer we'll freeze to the stone, and they'll find us in the spring and put up a cairn." He shouldered both packs. "Up."
They went up.
The Boy on the Ridge
The snow began in the middle of the afternoon.
It did not begin all at once. First the sky above the hills went from pale to gray, and then from gray to a soft, heavy, yellowish white, like old linen. The wind dropped. That was the strangest part. All day it had come down the valley at them, hard and steady, and then it simply stopped, as if somebody had shut a door, and in the quiet Corin could hear the stream again, and his own breathing, and the creak of Oren's pack straps.
Then one flake came down in front of his face. He watched it fall all the way to the road. It was large and soft and very slow. It landed on the trodden snow and lay there, a small white star on the gray, and did not melt.
Then there was another. And another. And then they were everywhere, falling straight down out of the still air, big and slow and silent, so thick that the far side of the valley went soft and then went gray and then went away altogether.
The girl stopped and turned her face up and let them land on it.
She stood like that for a long moment, with her eyes shut. Snow landed on her eyelashes. Snow landed on her lips. She did not brush any of it away.
Corin did not know why she was doing it. He thought perhaps she did not know either.
"We'll lose the road," said Oren.
He said it calmly. But he had stopped too, and he was looking at the walls on either side of them, and Corin saw him measuring them with his eyes. The walls were low here, low and broken, and the snow was already starting to fill the gaps.
"We won't lose it," said Corin. "The walls."
"The walls are going under." Oren nodded up ahead, where the road climbed toward the next shoulder of hill. Up there, the walls were hardly more than two lines of white bumps in the white. "Another hour of this and you'll not see them at all. And it'll be dark in two."
Nobody said anything.
They went on. There was nothing else to do.
The snow made the world very small. It closed in round them like the walls of a tent. Corin could see Oren, a few steps ahead, a broad dark shape going gray with snow on its shoulders and back. He could see the walls on either side, for now. He could see his own crutch going down into the white and coming up again. Beyond that, nothing. A soft, gray, falling nothing, everywhere, in every direction, so that after a while he could not have said which way was up the hill and which way was down, if it had not been for the pull in his legs.
And the hum.
The hum was still there. It came from in front of them, from up the road, from the north. He could not hear it any better in the snow, but he could feel which way it came from, the way you can feel which side of a room a fire is on with your eyes shut. And he found, after a while, that he was walking toward it. That every time the road bent, his feet bent with it before his eyes had found the bend.
He did not say anything about that.
They had been walking in the snow for perhaps half an hour when Oren stopped and put up his good hand.
Corin stopped. The girl stopped behind him.
"What?" she said, very low.
Oren did not answer. He was looking up and to the right, at the slope of the hill above the road.
Corin looked.
For a moment he saw nothing but snow. Then the snow thinned a little, the way it does, a gap in the falling, and he saw that there was a low ridge up there, a bare rib of rock running along the side of the hill. And on the ridge, standing quite still, there was a boy.
He was a small boy, not yet near being a man. He was wrapped in something big and dark, a coat or a blanket, with snow on the shoulders, and he had a stick in his hand, a long crooked shepherd's stick taller than he was. A dog stood beside him. A black-and-white dog, low and thin and alert, with its ears up and its nose pointing straight down at them. It did not bark. Neither of them moved.
The boy was looking at them.
He looked at them for a long time. Corin could not see his face, only the pale shape of it inside the dark wrapping, but he could feel the looking. It was not frightened. It was careful. It went from Oren, to Corin, to the girl, and back to Corin, and stayed there.
On Corin's hands. He was sure of it. On the gray gloves, and the gray wrists above them.
Then the boy said something to the dog, too low to hear, and turned, and was gone over the other side of the ridge. The dog went after him like a black-and-white shadow. The snow closed in again where they had been.
Nobody spoke for a moment.
"They'll know we're coming," said Oren. His voice was very even. "Whoever's up there. That boy's gone to tell them."
"Good," said Lyra.
Oren turned and looked at her.
"Good," she said again. She had pulled her cap down hard over her ears, and there was snow on her eyebrows, and her face was white with cold. But her voice was steady. "If they know we're coming, then nobody gets a fright. Nobody comes out of a door with a pitchfork because three strangers walked out of the snow. They'll have time to look at us. They'll have time to decide."
"And if they decide we're not welcome?"
"Then we'll know that too," said Lyra, "before we've knocked."
Oren looked at her a moment longer. Then he grunted, the way he did when somebody said something he could not argue with and did not want to agree with, and turned back to the road.
Corin had not said anything. He was thinking about the boy's eyes on his hands.
Malakor.
This time it took longer for the answer to come. And when it came, it was so thin and so far that he could barely tell the words apart. It was like hearing someone speak across a field in a high wind.
...here...
There's a village. Up ahead. Do you know it?
A long, long pause.
...valley people... said Malakor. ...don't pay... don't burn... good people... Ministry forgot them...
The rest was lost.
Malakor?
Nothing. Then, very faint, a sound that might have been a dry old laugh, or might have been only the wind starting up again over the ridge.
...walk, boy...
Corin walked.
The road went up over the shoulder of the hill, and on the top of the shoulder the snow thinned and almost stopped, and the light came back a little, a dull yellow-gray light, low in the west. They stood on the top and looked down.
Below them was another valley. A small one, round, like a bowl. A river ran across the bottom of it, a real river this time, wide and shallow, with black water moving fast over flat stones between white banks. And on the near bank of the river, close to the water, with smoke going up from it straight into the still air in many thin gray threads, more than he could count, there was a village.
And on the bank in front of the village, waiting by the river, there were people.
Thistle Ford
They went down into the valley in the last of the light.
The road went down in long loops, and the snow had stopped, and the air was so still that Corin could hear the river long before they reached it. It was a loud river, for its size. It ran fast and shallow over flat stones, and the stones made it talk in a hundred small voices, all chattering at once. Along both banks there was ice, white and thick, reaching out into the water like hands, and in the middle the water ran black and quick and free.
The village was on the near bank.
It was not a big village. Corin tried to count the houses and gave up after nine, which was as far as counting went for him. More than nine, then. Low houses, built of the same flat gray stone as the walls of the drove road, with thick roofs of turf that had grass growing on them under the snow, so that from above the houses looked like small hills with doors in them. Every house had a chimney, and every chimney had a thread of smoke going straight up into the still air, blue-gray against the darkening sky. The smoke smelled sweet and earthy and strange. Not coal. Not wood. Something older.
"Peat," said Oren, sniffing. "They burn the bog. Saints, I've not smelled that in twenty years."
There was no wall round the village. There was no gate. There were no lamp posts along the one muddy street, and no Ash lamps by the doors, and no notice boards with the Ministry's seal on them, the blue round seal with the white open hand that Corin had seen on every corner of every street in the city. There was nothing at all to say that this place belonged to anyone.
At the river's edge, where the road met the water, two tall posts stood up out of the shallows, painted white. Then two more, a little further out. Then two more. They marked the ford: a line of white posts going across the river, two by two, like a row of very thin men holding hands to show you the way.
And on the bank in front of the first posts, waiting, stood the people of the village.
Corin counted them, because he could. Eight. Eight people, standing in a loose line across the end of the road, between the three of them and the houses.
They were men and women both, all of them grown, none of them young. They wore thick coats of brown and gray wool, and Corin saw, as they came closer, that most of the coats were army coats. Old army coats, cut down and patched and let out and patched again, with odd buttons sewn on where the brass ones had been lost, but army coats all the same. Each of them held a staff. Long, plain, wooden staves, as tall as they were, held upright, with the foot of the staff resting on the ground.
Nobody raised a staff. Nobody shouted. Nobody moved.
They only stood there, very politely, in the gray dusk by the river, and waited.
Oren stopped a few long paces from them. Corin stopped beside him. Lyra came up on his other side.
For a long moment nobody said anything at all. The river chattered. Somewhere in the village a dog barked, once, and then was quiet.
Then the line of villagers moved, a little, in the middle. Two of them stepped aside. And out between them, walking slowly, came the smallest old woman Corin had ever seen.
She came up to his chest, if that. She was brown. Not brown with dirt or sun, but brown all through, brown and wrinkled, like a walnut, or like an apple that has been kept all winter in a loft. Her face was so lined that it was hard to see where her eyes were, until she looked at you, and then you saw them very clearly: small, black, bright, and very sharp. She wore a man's army coat that came down to her ankles, with the sleeves rolled up four times, and only one brass button left on it, high up, at the throat. In her mouth there was a clay pipe. It was not lit. Corin could see it was not lit. There was no smoke, and no smell of tobacco. She held it in the corner of her mouth all the same, and moved it from one side to the other as she looked at them.
She looked at Oren first. Up and down. His beard, his sling, his sword.
She looked at Lyra. At the cap, the black coat, the boy's breeches, the face under the cap.
Then she looked at Corin.
She looked at him for longer than she had looked at the other two put together. She looked at his crutch, and his bad leg. She looked at the gray gloves, and the gray wrists above them, and her black eyes went very still and very bright.
Then she looked at his face. And she kept on looking.
Corin did not look away. He did not know why, but it seemed important not to.
At last she took the pipe out of her mouth.
"Davin said three," she said. Her voice was low and slow and a little hoarse, and it had a lilt in it that he had not heard before, a way of going up at the ends of things. "A big one, a small one, and one with gray hands. Aye. That's three." She put the pipe back. "Where are you going?"
Corin opened his mouth.
"North," said Lyra, before he could.
The old woman did not look at her. She kept looking at Corin.
"North," she said. "Is that right?"
"Yes," said Corin.
"There's nothing north of here but the Lip. And past the Lip there's nothing but the Glass." The pipe moved from one corner of her mouth to the other. "Nobody goes north of here."
"We do," said Corin.
She looked at him a while longer.
"Aye," she said slowly. "Aye. I can see you do."
One of the men in the line behind her moved his staff a little, and said something, low, in that same lilting voice. Corin did not catch it. The old woman lifted one small brown hand without turning round, and the man stopped.
"Them in the gray," said the old woman. "Are they after you?"
There was a pause.
Corin felt the girl go stiff beside him. He felt Oren's weight shift, very slightly, onto the balls of his feet. He thought about the page. Rule one. It was not a rule for strangers. It was a rule for him and her. But he thought about it anyway.
"Yes," he said. "They are. They're a long way behind. But they're coming."
The girl drew in a breath.
The old woman did not seem surprised. She nodded, slowly, as if he had told her it was going to rain.
"Aye," she said. "Well. They mostly are." She looked at the line of people behind her, and something passed between them, some look that Corin could not read. Then she turned back.
"I'm Abbe," she said. "Granny Abbe, they call me, though I'm granny to none of them. This is Thistle Ford." She stepped aside, out of the road, and the line of villagers stepped aside with her, opening a way between them to the houses. "You'd better come in out of the cold, the three of you, before you fall down in it. There's broth in the hall."
