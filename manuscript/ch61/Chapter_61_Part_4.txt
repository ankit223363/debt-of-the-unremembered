Chapter 61: The Free Valleys, Part 4 of 5: The Long Hall
The Long Hall
The hall stood in the middle of the village, a little apart from the houses, on a rise of ground above the river.
It was the biggest building in Thistle Ford, and the oldest. Corin could see that at once. Its walls were made of the same gray stone as everything else, but the stones were bigger, and darker, and set more carefully, and the turf on its roof was so thick and old that a small bush was growing out of it near the chimney. Its door was wide enough for two men side by side, and it was made of oak gone almost black with age, and it was carved.
The carving was a sun.
A round sun, in the middle of the door, with straight rays coming out of it all the way round, reaching almost to the edges of the wood. It had been carved deep, once. Now it was worn so smooth by a hundred years of hands pushing the door open that the rays were only soft ridges, and the round face of the sun was polished pale and shining in the middle, like the toe of a statue that every pilgrim touches.
Granny Abbe put her small brown hand flat on the sun and pushed, and the door swung inward.
Warmth came out. Warmth, and light, and a smell of peat smoke and boiling mutton and wet wool and people, so thick and so good that Corin stopped on the step and simply breathed it.
Inside, the hall was one long room, longer than it was wide, with a roof of dark beams high overhead, black with a hundred years of smoke. A fire burned in a great stone hearth at the far end, a peat fire, low and red and steady, giving out more heat than light. Down the middle of the room ran a long table made of planks laid on trestles, with benches on both sides, and on the table there were wooden bowls and wooden spoons and a big iron pot with a lid on it and steam coming out from under the lid.
The walls were hung with things.
Corin looked at them as he came in, because he could not help it. Old pikes, long iron-headed spears on ash shafts, rusted dark, hung in pairs crossed over each other, all down one wall. A drum, round and flat, with its skin cracked and gone the color of tea, hanging from a peg by its cord. A row of helmets, plain iron caps, dented. Belts. Buckles. A pair of boots, very small, a child's boots, nailed up by their heels over the door, with dried heather stuffed in them.
And at the far end, over the hearth, a banner.
It was very old and very torn. It had been red once, he thought, or orange. Now it was the color of a dead leaf, and half of it was gone, burned or rotted or cut away, so that it hung from its pole in rags. But in the middle of what was left there was still a shape sewn onto it in yellow cloth, faded almost to white.
A sun. The same sun as on the door.
The hall was full of people. More than had been at the river. They sat on the benches with their bowls, and stood by the fire, and leaned against the walls, men and women and old people and children, in their army coats and their patched wool. They had been talking when the door opened. Now they were not. Now they were all looking at the door, at the three strangers standing in it with snow melting on their shoulders.
Granny Abbe did not seem to notice.
"Sit," she said. "Sit, sit. You're letting the warm out."
They sat.
Somebody, a big quiet woman with red forearms, ladled broth out of the pot into three wooden bowls and set them down in front of them. It was mutton broth, thick and gray and greasy, with barley in it and bits of onion and turnip, and lumps of meat that fell apart when you touched them with the spoon, and it was the best thing Corin had ever tasted. He was sure of that, even without being able to remember anything else he had tasted. He ate it as fast as he could without burning his mouth. Beside him, Oren ate faster. Across from him, the girl held her bowl in both gloved hands and drank from the rim, like a child, with her eyes shut.
The people of Thistle Ford watched them eat.
They were not rude about it. They did not stare openly. But Corin could feel the looks, coming from every bench and every wall, the way you feel a draft on the back of your neck. And he could feel where most of them went.
To his hands.
He had taken his gloves off to hold the spoon. He had not thought about it. He thought about it now. His hands lay on the plank table on either side of his bowl, gray as slate, with their black tips, in the red light of the peat fire, and every eye in the hall kept coming back to them and sliding off again.
A child near the fire, a girl of four or five, said something to her mother in a high clear voice. Corin did not catch the words, but he caught the tone of them, and he saw the mother put her hand quickly over the child's mouth and pull her close.
He put his gloves back on.
Granny Abbe, at the end of the bench, saw him do it. She did not say anything. She only moved her cold pipe from one corner of her mouth to the other.
When the broth was gone and the bowls had been filled again and that was gone too, she leaned forward and put both her small brown hands on the table.
"Now, then," she said. "Names."
There was a little stir along the benches. People leaned closer.
Corin looked at the girl. She looked at him. He thought she was asking him something with her eyes, and he did not know what it was. Then he thought perhaps she was asking whether to lie.
He shook his head, very slightly.
She turned to Granny Abbe.
"My name is Lyra," she said. "This is Corin." She stopped. Then she put her hand on the big man's arm beside her, the good arm, and said it clearly, the way she might have said it in a drawing room, years ago, introducing a guest. "And this is Captain Oren Vance."
The hall went silent.
It had been quiet before. Now it was silent. It was the kind of silence that has a shape. Corin felt it come down over the long room like a cloth thrown over a cage. Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. A spoon that somebody had been holding halfway to their mouth stayed halfway. The peat fire whispered in the hearth, and it was the loudest sound in the world.
Every face in the hall had turned to Oren.
Oren did not seem to understand. He looked round at them, slowly, from face to face, with his spoon still in his good hand and broth on his beard, and his own face went puzzled and then wary, the way it went in the city when a door opened that should have stayed shut.
"What?" he said. "What is it?"
Nobody answered him.
At the end of the bench, Granny Abbe had gone very still. She was looking at Oren with her small black eyes, and the cold pipe had stopped moving in her mouth. Then she took it out, slowly, and held it in her hand.
"Vance," she said.
"Aye," said Oren. "That's my name. What's wrong with it?"
Somebody near the wall made a sound. It might have been a laugh. It might have been something else.
Granny Abbe put the pipe back in her mouth.
"Nothing's wrong with it," she said. "Nothing at all." She looked round the hall once, and the silence broke, all at once, into a low murmur, like the river breaking over its stones. People turned to each other. People whispered. The child by the fire said something again, and this time her mother did not stop her, and the word she said was Vance, and she said it the way a child says the name of someone in a story.
Granny Abbe held up her hand, and the murmur dropped.
"Tomorrow," she said to Oren. "Haverstock. It's over the next hill. There's a thing there you'll want to see." She looked at him a moment longer, with something in her face that Corin could not name. "Not here. Not tonight. Tomorrow."
The Child by the Fire
After that the hall did not go back to how it had been.
The talk started again, but it was a different kind of talk. Lower. Quicker. People kept looking at Oren, and then looking away, and then looking back. An old man with a white beard down to his belt got up from a bench near the fire and came all the way down the long table, slowly, leaning on his staff, and stood in front of Oren, and looked at him for a long time without a word. Then he put out a hand like a bundle of twigs and touched Oren's sleeve, once, very lightly, the way a person touches a picture in a church. Then he went back to his bench and sat down and did not look at Oren again.
Oren sat very still through all of it. When the old man had gone, he looked at Corin, and Corin saw that he was frightened. Not the way he was frightened of a fight. A different way. The way a man is frightened when everyone in a room knows something about him that he does not.
"Saints," Oren said, under his breath. "What have I done?"
"Nothing," said Lyra. She put her hand on his good arm and left it there. "You haven't done anything. They know your name. That's all."
"Everybody knows my name. It's only a name."
"Not up here, it isn't," said Lyra.
Corin did not hear what Oren said to that, because something was tugging at his sleeve.
He looked down.
It was the child. The little girl from by the fire, the one whose mother had put a hand over her mouth. She had come all the way down the hall on her own, while nobody was watching, and she was standing beside him at the end of the bench, holding a fold of his coat sleeve in her fist. She was very small. She had a round face and round cheeks red from the fire, and fair hair in two short plaits tied with wool, and she was looking, not at his face, but at his hands.
His gloves were on. She was looking at them anyway. As if she could see through them.
"Hello," said Corin.
She did not say hello. She said, "Does it hurt?"
Corin looked at her.
He thought about the question. He thought about it properly, because she had asked it properly, the way children do, as if it were the only question in the world and she would wait all night for the answer.
"No," he said. "It doesn't hurt. It's cold. And I can't feel things with it. But it doesn't hurt."
She thought about that.
"Can I see?"
He looked round for her mother. Her mother was by the fire, talking to another woman with her back to them. Nobody else was watching. Lyra and Oren were bent together, talking low.
He pulled off his right glove with his teeth, and laid his hand on the table between them, palm up.
The child looked at it for a long time.
She did not seem frightened. She seemed interested, the way children are interested in a beetle, or a dead bird, or a stone with a hole in it. She looked at the gray of the palm, smooth and dark and faintly shining in the firelight like a wet roof. She looked at the black tips of the fingers. She bent close and looked at the lines on the palm, the life lines and heart lines that fortune-tellers read on the Hill. They were still there. Gray on gray, but there.
Then, before he could stop her, she put out one finger and touched the middle of his palm.
He did not feel it. He saw it. He saw her small pink finger, with a little dirt under the nail, pressed into the middle of his gray hand. He saw her press a little harder, to see what would happen. And he felt nothing at all, not the warmth of her, not the weight, nothing, as if she were touching a picture of a hand.
"It's cold," said the child.
"Yes."
"Like a stone in the river."
"Yes."
She took her finger away. She looked up at him, at his face, for the first time.
"Granny Abbe says gray men go over the Lip," she said, "and they don't ever come back."
Corin did not know what to say to that. So he did not say anything.
The child thought about his silence for a while. Then she put her hand in the pocket of her pinafore and took something out and put it in the middle of his gray palm, where her finger had been.
It was a ring of dried apple. A thin brown wrinkled ring, sticky, with a bit of fluff on it from her pocket.
"That's for you," she said. "For the road."
"Thank you."
"You have to eat it," she said severely. "Not keep it. Mam says if you keep apples they go bad."
"All right," said Corin. "I'll eat it."
She nodded, satisfied. Then her mother turned round from the fire and saw her, and made a sound, and came down the hall very fast, and scooped her up under the arms and carried her away, saying something low and quick in that lilting valley voice. Over her mother's shoulder the child looked back at Corin, and lifted her hand, and waved. A small wave. Fingers opening and closing.
Corin found that he had lifted his own gray hand, and was waving back.
He looked down at the apple ring in his palm. He could see it. He could not feel it at all. He closed his fingers round it, carefully, the way you close your hand round a moth so as not to hurt its wings, and he ate it, because he had said he would.
It was very sweet.
When he looked up, the girl, Lyra, was watching him. She had seen it all. He did not know how long she had been watching.
She did not say anything. She only looked at him, across the table, in the firelight, with that face he could not read. And then she looked away, quickly, and went back to talking to Oren in a low voice, and did not look at him again for a long time.
The Doll
After the second bowl of broth, the hall began to empty.
People went out into the dark in twos and threes, wrapping their coats round them, nodding to Granny Abbe as they passed her, and sometimes nodding to Oren, too, quickly, shyly, as if they were not sure they were allowed. The children were carried out asleep. The big woman with the red forearms took the iron pot off the fire and carried it away somewhere, and came back without it, and banked the peat down so that it glowed instead of burning. In the end there were only the three of them left at the long table, and Granny Abbe at the end of the bench, and a lamp.
It was an oil lamp, a plain clay one, with a short wick and a small yellow flame. It was the only lamp in the hall. Corin looked at it for a long time before he understood what was strange about it.
It was only a lamp.
In the city, every lamp was something else as well. The gas lamps on the Hill ran on gas. The lamps in the slums ran on Ash, on other people's burned-out memories, and gave a hard blue-white light that made everyone's face look dead. The lamps by the Ministry doors were Ash-finding lamps, made to flare when someone with Ash in them walked past. There was no lamp in Veridia that was only a lamp.
This one was oil, and a wick, and a flame. It burned yellow and a little smoky, and it smelled of mutton fat, and it did not care who you were.
"You don't have Ash lamps," he said.
Granny Abbe looked at him over her cold pipe.
"We don't have Ash," she said. "We don't burn, up here. Not since my grandmother's day." She tapped the pipe against her teeth. "We don't burn, and we don't sell, and we don't buy. When the clerks come up from the city with their books and their stones and their tax, they come over the Lip, and they look at the Glass, and then they go home again, and they never come back." A small dry sound, like a laugh with the laugh taken out. "The Glass is a bad neighbor. But it's a good wall."
Corin wanted to ask her what she meant. He did not. He thought she would tell him or not tell him as she chose, and that asking would only make her choose not.
It was the girl who spoke next.
She had been very quiet since the silence over Oren's name. She had sat with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up and her arms round them, inside the big black coat, and watched the fire. Now she turned her head and looked at Granny Abbe.
"Can I ask you something?"
"You can ask."
Corin felt something odd at that. You can ask. It was what she had said to him that morning, on the road. He did not know why it should matter. It did.
"There's a doll," said Lyra. "A rag doll. A baby has it. A friend's baby. Robin." She spoke slowly, picking the words. "It's an old doll. Very old. The cloth's gone thin. It's got a blue dress, faded nearly gray, and a face of plain cloth with no mouth, and one eye. A button eye. The other one's gone. And the stitches—" She stopped. She held up her gloved hand and made a small movement with two fingers, slanting them. "The stitches all lean back. Backward. Like this. Every stitch on it. As if whoever sewed it was leaning away from what they were sewing."
Granny Abbe had taken the pipe out of her mouth.
She did not say anything for a long moment. The peat fire whispered. The lamp flame stood up straight and still.
"Where did the baby get it?" she said at last.
"An old man had it. In his rafters. On the river, in the fens." Lyra frowned. "He said a lady left it with him, years ago. A lady in a dark blue cloak. She was asking the way to the coast. She said to give it to the one who comes after."
"The one who comes after," said Granny Abbe. She said it as if she were tasting it.
She looked at Lyra for a long time. Then she looked at Corin, and then at the fire.
"That's the old kind," she said. "The old valley kind. Back-stitched. You don't see them now. My grandmother made them, and her mother, and nobody since." She turned the cold pipe over and over in her small brown fingers. "That's the kind they made for the children who go over the glass."
Nobody spoke.
"What children?" said Lyra.
Granny Abbe did not answer.
"What children? Who went over the glass?" Lyra had sat up. Her arms had come out of the black coat, and her hands were tight on her knees. "You said nobody goes north of here. You said it to him at the river."
"Nobody does," said Granny Abbe. "Now."
"Then—"
"I'll not tell it." The old woman's voice was not unkind. It was very tired. "It's not mine to tell. It's not a story. It's the dead." She put the pipe back in her mouth and bit on it. "Ask at Last Thorn. Past the Lip, at the edge, where the thorn trees end. There's folk there who know it better than I do. The Ashborn." She looked at Corin again, at his gloved hands on the table. "Ashborn don't lie about the dead. They can't. They carry them."
Corin did not know what that meant. He thought he would find out.
Lyra opened her mouth again, and then closed it. She sat back against the wall. She did not put her arms back inside the coat. Corin saw that her hands were not quite steady.
He thought he knew why. He had been there, on Wednesday, in a bare gray study on the Hill, when the Inquisitor had turned a green book round on his desk so that she could see it. He had watched her face as she read the silver words on the cover. The dark is the day with its eyes shut. Stitched in her mother's own hand, Kross had said. Her mother had come from over the water, and had a stitch of her own. A stitch that leaned back.
The green book was inside his coat. Against his chest. He could feel the shape of it.
He did not take it out. He did not say anything. He did not think it was his to say.
Granny Abbe stood up.
She did it all at once, the way very old people sometimes do, as if she had decided to and her body had simply obeyed. She was not much taller standing than sitting.
"There's a loft," she said. "Over the byre, at the back. Straw, and blankets, and the apples. Hild'll show you." She nodded at the big woman, who had come back in and was standing by the door. "You'll go on to Haverstock in the morning. I'll send Davin with you as far as the cross."
She went to the door. She stopped there, with her small brown hand flat on the worn sun.
"The one who comes after," she said again, quietly, to nobody. Then she went out into the dark.
