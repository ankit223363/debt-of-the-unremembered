Chapter 12: Niles the Pawnbroker, Part 2 of 5: Gutter Street
Gutter Street
The Ink Quarter began where the smell changed.
Corin noticed it at the top of a steep little street called Tanner's Rise, where the last of the Docks' fish smell and tar smell gave out, and something else came up to meet it. Sharp and dark and bitter, like burnt sugar and wet iron and old pennies all at once. Ink. Printer's ink, and the lampblack they made it from, and the oil they mixed it with, and the hot metal of the type. It hung over the whole quarter like weather. It was in the stones of the walls and the wood of the doors and the faces of the people. Corin saw a man go past with a stack of paper on his shoulder and his hands black to the wrists, as if he had been wearing gloves of ink. He saw a boy sitting on a step with his fingers stained blue, eating an apple, and the apple had blue fingerprints on it.
The houses here were tall and thin and crooked, and they leaned toward each other across the narrow streets, so that their top floors nearly touched, and the sky was only a thin bright ribbon high above. Every second building was a printing house, or a type foundry, or a bindery, or a shop that sold paper, and from every one of them came the same sound. Clack. Clack. Clack. Clack. The presses. Hundreds of them, all over the quarter, going all day and half the night, so many and so steady that after a while you stopped hearing them, the way you stop hearing your own heart.
Needle Street was the widest street in the quarter, and it was not very wide. It ran down the middle of the Ink Quarter like a spine, and at the far end of it, on a corner, there was a building that Corin knew before he reached it.
He did not remember it. But it was in the Ledger, in his own hand, more than once. The Inkwell printing house stood on the corner of Needle Street, black and squat in the dark, like a toad asleep.
It looked like a toad in the daylight too. A low wide building of black brick, crouched on the corner with its big dark windows like a pair of half-shut eyes. Its door was shut. Its windows were dark. No presses clacked inside it. There was a notice pasted on the door, white and new, with the Ministry's closed eye at the top. Corin did not go close enough to read it.
Beside the Inkwell, running off Needle Street into shadow, there was a lane.
It was narrow. Narrower than the alley behind Market Row. Its cobbles sloped down into a gutter in the middle, a deep stone channel, black and greasy, where the ink and the oil and the wash-water of the printing houses had run for a hundred years. That was why it was called Gutter Lane. The Ledger said so. And at the far end, where the lane came up against a blank wall, there was a heap. Old paper, sodden and gray. Broken crates. Twisted type. A cracked chamber pot. All the things that the Inkwell and its neighbors had wanted to throw away and not wanted to pay to have taken.
Corin stopped at the mouth of the lane.
He did not mean to. His feet stopped. He stood on the corner of Needle Street with the presses clacking all around him and looked down the narrow black throat of Gutter Lane at the heap at the end of it, and something in his chest went tight and cold, like a hand closing.
A young man had died on that heap. Not long ago. A scribe, with an oak-leaf button on his cuff and a red sock in his boot and a letter from his sister in his pocket. He had run four steps. And a woman in a white shell mask had stood over him, holding a silver cup, while a big man with huge hands held him down.
Corin did not remember it. He had read it this morning. But his body remembered something. His right hand had gone, by itself, to the long pocket at his ribs, and closed on the rod.
Easy, said Malakor quietly. It is only a lane, boy. Only rubbish and stones. Whatever happened here has gone. I took it. You took it, with me. It is in a jar on a shelf in the Third Basement with your writing on the label. A pause. He hummed, at the end. Do you remember? No. Of course you do not. But you wrote it on the jar.
"There's a notice on the door," said a voice beside him. "Of the Inkwell. It says the building is closed by order of the Ministry, pending an inquiry into the death of a person unknown."
Corin turned.
Lyra Thorne was standing at his elbow. He had not heard her come. She was not wearing her mother's gray gown. She was wearing a long brown coat, old and plain and patched at the elbows, with the collar turned up, and under it a dark gray dress, and boots that had been mended many times. Her short black hair was bare. And at her left hip, under the long brown coat, he could see the shape of something long and straight, hanging from a belt. The coat had been cut wide on that side to hide it, and it almost did.
Her face was tired. There were dark marks under her gray eyes, like thumbprints. But her chin was up.
"A person unknown," said Corin.
"Pell Dorran," said Lyra. "They know his name. They wrote it on three forms. I saw them." She looked down the lane at the heap. "They just don't want to say it on a door."
They stood for a moment together on the corner, looking down Gutter Lane.
"You got my note," said Lyra.
"Yes."
"You read it?"
"Yes."
"All of it?"
Corin looked at her.
"Home," he said.
Lyra's face did something. It was very small and very quick, and then it was gone, and her chin was up again.
"He said it twice more," she said. "At breakfast. Holding the cup. Looking at the marks underneath. Home. Home. And then he put it down and went back to the window and tapped." She let out a breath. "Four, two, nine, three. Over and over. Faster than I've ever heard it. Mrs. Hobb said he kept it up all morning. She said it was like having a woodpecker in the house."
"What do you think it means?"
"I don't know." Lyra turned away from the lane. "I don't know what anything means anymore. I only know that everything my aunt gives me has a hook in it, and everything my father says is in a language I can't read." She started walking. "Come on. It's this way. Mr. Pruett says it's at the bottom, by the water."
Gutter Street was not far. It ran off the bottom of Needle Street, past the Inkwell, downhill, toward the south, and as it went down the houses got older and lower and darker, and the presses got quieter, and the ink smell got thinner, and a new smell crept in to take its place. Water. Old water. Still water, that had been sitting a long time without going anywhere. Mud and weed and rot.
"The Cut," said Lyra.
At the bottom of Gutter Street, where it ended, there was a canal.
It was not like the river. The river was wide and brown and slow, and moved, and went somewhere. The Cut was narrow and black and straight, as straight as a ruler, walled in on both sides by high walls of green-slimed brick, and it did not move at all. It lay in its stone trench like a long strip of black glass, with a skin of oil on it that made rainbows in the low sun, and rubbish floating in it, sodden paper and a dead cat and an old boot, perfectly still. A narrow iron footbridge crossed it. On the far side there were warehouses with their windows bricked up.
Corin looked at the Cut for a long time. Something about it bothered him. He did not know what.
You have been over it, said Malakor. On the roofs. With the girl with the key. She caught you. It is in the book. "I caught you over the Cut. That's once."
Corin did not remember. But he felt his stomach drop, very slightly, the way a stomach drops on a high place.
Gutter Street itself was a row of shops. Two rows, one on each side of the cobbles, all the way down the hill from Needle Street to the Cut. And every one of them, as far as Corin could see, was the same kind of shop.
Pawnshops.
They stood shoulder to shoulder, low and dark and narrow, with small barred windows and doors with iron grilles, and over almost every door there hung the sign of the trade: three brass balls, hanging from an iron bar, one ball higher than the other two. Some of the balls were bright and polished. Most were dull and green with age. Some had a ball missing, so that only two hung there, like a bunch of grapes that a bird had pecked at.
And in the windows, behind the bars, there were the things that people had brought to Gutter Street and never come back for.
Corin could not stop looking at them as they walked down the hill. He did not want to look. But his eyes went to them, one window after another, the way they went to the thing that did not belong.
Wedding rings, a whole tray of them, gold and silver and brass, with little paper tags tied to them with string. A violin, without strings. A pair of false teeth on a china saucer. A stuffed owl in a glass dome, with one glass eye missing. Medals. Dozens of medals on faded ribbons, laid out on black cloth, all of them from the same war, the war nobody talked about. A child's christening gown, yellow with age. A soldier's sword in a dented scabbard. A doll with a cracked face. A set of surgeon's knives in a velvet case. Spectacles. Shoes. Pocket watches, a hundred of them, all stopped, all showing different times, as if every one of them had stopped at the exact moment its owner gave it up.
And in one window, on a shelf by itself, a glass jar with a cork in it, half full of something gray and soft that looked like dust and was not.
Corin looked away from that one quickly.
Ash, said Malakor. Somebody's. Sold to a pawnbroker for a few coppers by someone who did not know what it was. Or who knew very well. A pause. Do not look at it, boy. It will look back.
They came to the bottom of the street.
The last shop before the Cut stood a little apart from the others, as if it did not quite want to be part of the row. It was narrower than the rest. Older. Its walls were black timber and white plaster, the plaster gone gray and cracked, the timber twisted with age so that the whole front of the shop leaned a little to the left, like a man listening at a door. Its window was small, with thick old glass in little diamond panes, so bubbled and uneven that you could not see clearly through it. Only shapes. Gold and silver and the dark gleam of polished wood.
Over the door, on a rusted iron bar, there hung three brass balls. They had been polished that morning. Corin could tell. They were the brightest thing on Gutter Street, shining in the low sun like three small golden moons.
And under them, on a board, in old faded letters, black on a ground that had once been red:
N. NILES. MONEY LENT ON ALL GOODS. NO PLEDGE SOLD BEFORE ITS TIME.
And under that, smaller, in letters that looked as if they had been painted on much later, by a different hand, in white:
NOR AFTER IT, WITHOUT DUE NOTICE.
Lyra stood in front of the door and looked up at the sign.
"No pledge sold before its time," she read quietly. "Mr. Pruett said that. He said it's the only shop on Gutter Street that means it."
She put her hand on the door.
Corin saw her hesitate. Just for a moment. Just long enough to take a breath, the way she had taken a breath on the top step of Number 11 last night, in the light and the lilies.
Then she pushed.
A bell rang inside. Not a deep heavy bell, like Madame Hesper's. A small, thin, cracked bell, like a spoon tapped on a cup that had been dropped once and mended. It tinkled twice, and stopped.
And from the dark inside the shop, a voice said:
"Shut the door. You're letting the Cut in."
Niles
Corin shut the door. The cracked bell tinkled again, once, and was still.
It was dark inside. Not black. Brown. A deep warm brown dark, like the inside of an old violin, and it took his eyes a long moment to find their way through it. The only light came through the little diamond panes of the window, thick and bubbled, so that it fell into the shop in broken yellow lozenges, scattered across the floor and the shelves like coins that somebody had dropped.
And the shop was full. Fuller than the Needle and Thimble. Fuller than any room Corin had ever been in, or could remember being in. It was long and low and narrow, running back from the door into the dark, and every wall of it, from the bare floorboards to the black beams of the ceiling, was shelves. And every shelf was packed.
Not with junk. That was the first thing he noticed. The windows of the other shops on Gutter Street had been full of sad, broken, dusty things, thrown together. This was not like that. Everything here was clean. Everything was in its place. Everything had a little brown paper tag tied to it with string, and on every tag there was a number, written in very small, very neat black ink.
Candlesticks, in rows, polished. Teapots, silver and pewter and brass, with their spouts all pointing the same way. Books, hundreds of them, with their spines straight. Boxes of every size, wooden and leather and lacquered, stacked like bricks. Musical instruments: a cello, a harp, three violins in their open cases, a brass horn that caught a lozenge of yellow light and glowed. A rack of walking sticks. A rack of swords. A long glass case on the counter full of small bright things on black velvet: rings and brooches and watches and lockets and cufflinks and snuffboxes, all of them tagged, all of them gleaming.
And clocks.
The back wall of the shop was clocks. Dozens of them. Small clocks on brackets and big clocks on the floor, carriage clocks and mantel clocks and a clock shaped like a ship and a clock shaped like a cathedral and a cuckoo clock with a little wooden bird sitting outside its door, frozen with its beak open. And every one of them was going. Every one. Ticking. Tocking. Some fast, some slow, some loud, some so soft you could barely hear them. A hundred small steady voices in the brown dark, all talking at once, all saying the same thing, in a hundred slightly different rhythms.
Now. Now. Now. Now.
And under them, faint, from somewhere further back in the shop, behind a curtain, a sound that was not ticking. A deep slow sound. Like a heartbeat. Like somebody knocking, very gently, on a door a long way off.
Thock. A long pause. Thock.
"Well?" said the voice. "Are you going to stand in my doorway all afternoon like a pair of hat-stands? Come where I can see you. I've only got the one eye, and it doesn't care for the dark."
There was a counter halfway down the shop, a long dark wooden counter with a brass grille along the top of it, like a ticket office, with a little arched gap in the middle of the grille where money and things could pass through. Behind the counter, on a tall stool, under a single small oil lamp with a green glass shade, a man was sitting.
He was small. Not short, exactly, though he was not tall. Small, the way a sparrow is small. Neat and compact and quick, with narrow shoulders under a brown woolen waistcoat, and a white shirt with the sleeves held up above the elbows by black garters, and a pair of very clean hands lying flat on the counter in front of him, side by side, like two pale fish. He was old. Sixty. Seventy. His head was bald and brown and shining, with a few long white hairs combed carefully across the top of it from one ear to the other. His face was narrow and lined and very still.
And he had one eye.
The left one was there. Dark brown, almost black, bright and quick and wet, like a bird's eye. It moved over Corin and Lyra as they came down the shop toward the counter, very fast, up and down, side to side, taking everything in. The right one was not there. Where it should have been, there was a patch. Not a black patch, the kind pirates wore in children's books. A patch of soft brown leather, stitched with tiny careful stitches, and in the middle of it, sewn on like a button, a small brass ball.
One brass ball. The sign of his trade. Where his eye had been.
Around his neck, on a black ribbon, hung a jeweler's glass, the little black tube that a man screws into his eye to look at small things. It lay on his waistcoat. He had nowhere to screw it into but the one eye, Corin thought, and he probably did, a hundred times a day.
"Niles," said the small man. "N. Niles. On the sign. Nobody knows what the N stands for, and that is how I like it." His bright eye went to Lyra and stayed there. "And you are a Thorne."
Lyra stopped at the counter.
"How do you know?"
"Your face, miss. You have your grandfather's nose. Edmund Thorne. He came in here once, forty years ago, when I was a boy behind my father's counter, and pledged a little gold watch for three days to pay a gambling debt that his wife was not to know about. He came back for it on the second day. With interest." Niles's thin mouth moved, very slightly, at one corner. "I remember every face that ever pledged in this shop. It is the only thing I have ever been any good at." His eye moved to Corin. To the gray coat. And stayed there longer. "And you, sir, are the Ministry. Which is less usual. In this shop."
"I'm with her," said Corin.
"Are you," said Niles. "Well. That is a sentence I have not heard a gray coat say before." His clean hands did not move on the counter. "I have rules, miss. Four of them. They are not written on the sign, because the sign is not big enough. So I say them to everybody who comes to my counter for the first time, and I say them once, and then I expect them to be remembered." He held up one finger. Its nail was very short and very clean. "One. No pledge is sold before its time. Not for any money. Not for any person. Not for the Archon himself, if he came down Gutter Street in his carriage and knocked on my door."
A second finger.
"Two. A ticket is a promise. Whoever holds the ticket holds the pledge. I do not ask how they came by it. I only ask to see it."
A third finger.
"Three. I do not buy Ash." His bright eye went to the window, toward the shops up the hill, toward the jar with the cork in it, half full of something gray. "Others on this street do. I do not. A man's things are a man's things. A man's mind is not a thing. I will lend on a watch. I will not lend on what the watch was for."
A fourth finger.
"Four." He laid all four fingers back down on the counter, beside the other hand. "You may ask me anything you like about what is in this shop. You may not ask me who brought it in. That is between me and them, and it stays there, until the day I die. After that, you may ask my book." He tilted his head toward a great ledger lying on the counter at his elbow, bound in brown leather, almost as big as a paving stone. "My book does not keep secrets. But it does not talk while I am alive."
He looked at them both with his one bright eye.
"Well?" he said. "What do you want?"
Lyra put her hand inside the collar of her gray dress. Corin saw her fingers go past the handkerchief, and past the folded square of Rowan's note, and stop on the slip of paper that had been wrapped in oilcloth under the third stone of a garden step. She drew it out. She unfolded it. She laid it flat on the counter, under the brass grille, in the light of the green lamp, and pushed it through the little arched gap toward the pawnbroker's clean hands.
"I've come about a clock," she said.
Niles did not pick up the paper. He leaned forward a little on his stool, and looked down at it, with his one eye, from about a hand's width away. He read it. Corin saw the eye go along the lines, once, slowly. They will tell you the clock was sold. It was not sold. I took it back before the auction. It is with N. on Gutter Street. I could not pay to get it out again. Forgive me. Do not let her have it. B.
The eye stopped at the B.
And Niles sat back on his stool, very slowly, the way an old man sits back when his legs have suddenly gone weak, and put one clean hand up over his face, over the leather patch and the brass ball and the one bright eye, and held it there.
The clocks ticked. A hundred of them. Now. Now. Now.
And behind the curtain at the back, far off, the deep slow sound. Thock.
"Mr. Niles?" said Lyra.
The small man took his hand away from his face. His eye was wet. Wetter than it had been. But his narrow face had not changed at all.
"So he is dead, then," he said quietly.
Lyra went very still.
"Who?" she said. "Bertram?"
"Bertram Ashcombe," said Niles. "Steward to the house of Thorne for twenty-nine years. Yes, miss. Bertram." He laid his hand flat on the counter again, beside the other. "You did not know. No. You would not. Nobody would. He had nobody to tell."
"He went to his sister," said Lyra. Her voice had gone thin. "In the country. Two years ago. He said—"
"He had no sister," said Niles. "He was an only child, born in a room over a saddler's shop on Tanner's Rise, sixty-eight years ago. He told me so. Many times. On the first Tuesday of every month, for a year and a half, when he came in here to pay the interest on that clock." The bright eye looked down at the slip of paper on the counter. "He never missed a month. Not one. Rain or snow. He would come in at the third bell after noon and stand exactly where you are standing, miss, and count out his coins on my counter, and I would write them in my book, and he would ask to see the clock, and I would let him, and he would stand in front of it in the back room for exactly as long as it took the big hand to go once round, and then he would go away again." Niles was quiet for a moment. "And then last spring, on the first Tuesday in May, he did not come. Nor in June. Nor in July. And I knew."
"Knew what?"
"That something had happened to him, miss. A man like Bertram Ashcombe does not miss a payment. Not because he is afraid of losing the pledge. Because he gave his word." Niles looked at the note. "And now here is his hand, telling you to forgive him. And telling you not to let her have it." His eye came up to Lyra's face. "He only ever wrote forgive me once before, to my knowledge. On the back of his first ticket. Under his name. And I asked him what it meant, and he said it was none of my business, which was true."
Nobody said anything.
Corin stood at the counter in his gray coat and looked at the note in the green lamplight, and felt the three stones at his hip go cold. Not warm. Cold. Like three small pebbles taken out of a river in winter.
Dead, he thought. The man who let the carriage in. The man who sent Oren away. The man who saved the coats, and hid the key under the step, and took the clock away before the auction. The man who went to a sister in the country. Like Doctor Aske.
Who had no sister either, said Malakor quietly. I would wager on it.
Lyra had not moved. She stood with her hands flat on the counter on either side of Bertram's note, and her face was white, and her gray eyes were wide and dry and fixed on the little brass ball where the pawnbroker's right eye should have been.
"He taught me to ride," she said. Very low. "Bertram. When I was four. On a fat pony called Clover. He walked beside me round and round the paddock for a whole summer with his hand on the bridle, and he never once let go, and he never once let me know he was holding on." She swallowed. "I thought he went to the country. I thought he was sitting in a garden somewhere, being cross with his sister's geese."
"Perhaps he is, miss," said Niles gently. "Somewhere. In some fashion." He reached across the counter, slowly, and with one clean finger turned Bertram's note round, so that it faced Lyra again, and pushed it back through the little arched gap in the grille toward her. "But he is not coming to my shop on the first Tuesday of the month any more. And somebody else is."
Lyra looked up.
"Somebody else?"
Niles reached out and laid his hand on the great brown ledger at his elbow.
"I told you my rules, miss," he said. "I have already said more about Bertram Ashcombe than my rules allow. I said it because the dead do not hold a man to his promises the way the living do, and because his own hand on that paper has already told you his name. But the one who has been paying the interest on that clock since he stopped is not dead. And I cannot tell you who that is." He opened the ledger. Its pages were thick and creamy and covered from edge to edge in the same very small, very neat black handwriting as the tags. He began to turn them. "But you may look at the clock. That is not against my rules. Anybody may look at a pledge, if they can say what it is." He found a page. He laid his finger on a line. "And you may look at my book. Not at the names. At the numbers. Numbers are not secrets. Numbers are only numbers."
He turned the great book round on the counter, so that it faced them through the brass grille. And he took his finger away.
And behind the curtain at the back of the shop, the deep slow sound went on, patient and far away, like something knocking very gently on a door that had been shut for a long, long time.
Thock.
