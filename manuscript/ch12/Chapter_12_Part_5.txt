Chapter 12: Niles the Pawnbroker, Part 5 of 5: The Price
The Price
"Twenty gold," said Lyra.
She said it to the clock. Not to Niles. As if the clock might be persuaded to lower its own price.
"Twenty gold," said Niles. "And the ticket. Before the third bell after noon on the first Tuesday of November." He paused. "Or the ticket alone, before that day, to ask for more time. Once."
"And without the ticket?"
"Without the ticket, miss, I cannot give it to you. Not for twenty gold. Not for a hundred. Not if you were the Archon's own daughter." He said it very gently, the way a doctor says a thing he wishes he did not have to say. "Rule two. Whoever holds the ticket holds the pledge. If I gave it to you without the ticket, then on the day somebody walked in with the ticket in their hand, I would be a thief. And I have been many things on Gutter Street, miss, in sixty years. But I have never been a thief."
Lyra did not argue. Corin saw her not argue. He saw her stand very straight, in her old brown coat, and fold her hands in front of her, and look up at the clock, and do a sum in her head. And he saw the answer of the sum come into her face, slowly, like a stain coming up through cloth.
She had sold handkerchiefs in the market on Fridays, the Ledger said. She had paid two silver to keep her mother's portrait out of a sale, and it had been all she had that month. She lived over a stable for nothing, because the man who owned the stable had once been lent money by her father. She had three silver in a tin on a shelf, perhaps. Four, in a good week.
Twenty gold was not a sum. Twenty gold was a wall.
"I'll find the ticket," said Lyra.
"I hope you do, miss."
"I'll find the ticket, and I'll find the money, and I'll be here before the third bell." Her chin was up. Her voice did not shake at all. "Before she is."
Niles looked at her for a long moment with his one bright eye.
"I believe you will try," he said. "I have seen that face before. On your grandfather. He came back for his watch on the second day."
Corin had not said anything for a long time. He had been standing at the edge of the square of light, with his hands at his sides, looking at the clock.
Not at the face. Not at the stones, or the hands, or the little dial with the tiny silver hand pointing at three. At the case. The long dark body of it, between the face and the lion's feet. The glass door. The brass pendulum hanging still in the dark behind it, like a held breath.
He had been looking at it since the first thock. And all that time, at his ribs, in the long right pocket, the rod had been getting colder.
Not the cold of fear. Not the cold of the white room on Crown Walk, or of the silver cup with its spiral turning the wrong way. A different cold. A listening cold. The cold the rod went, the Ledger said, in the moment before a harvest, when the Ash was near and the silver could feel it, the way a dog can feel a storm coming long before anyone hears the thunder.
Malakor, he thought.
Yes, said the rod. Very low. I know. I have felt it since we came down the step.
What is it?
Ask the little man, said Malakor, if you may touch the glass.
"Mr. Niles," said Corin. "May I touch it?"
The pawnbroker turned his eye on him.
"The clock?"
"The glass. The door. Over the pendulum." Corin did not move his hand toward it. "I won't open it. I won't move anything. I just want to put my hand on the glass."
Niles looked at him for a long time. At the gray coat. At the right side of it, where the long pocket ran from the ribs to the hip.
"Rule three," he said quietly. "I do not buy Ash. And I do not have it harvested in my shop."
"I'm not going to harvest anything," said Corin. "I promise you that. I just need to know if it's there."
Thock, said the clock.
Niles closed his one eye. When he opened it again, he looked very old, and very tired.
"Bertram used to put his hand on it," he said. "On the glass. At the end. Every month. Just before he went." He stepped back. "Go on, then. Gently."
Corin went forward into the square of light.
The clock was much taller close to. He had to tilt his head back to see the face, the dark blue enamel with its painted stars and its ring of eleven dull stones and its one empty silver claw. The carved roses and thorns went up the case beside him, inches from his face, so fine and so close that he could see the marks of the carver's tools in the walnut. And the smell. Close to, under the dust, under the cold, under the oil and brass of the room, there was a smell on the wood.
Beeswax. And saddle soap. And something else, very faint. Old pipe tobacco. The kind old men smoke in the evening, sitting by a kitchen fire, with their boots off.
Bertram, thought Corin. He did not know how he knew. He had never met the man. Or he had met him and did not remember. But the smell went into him and stood there, quiet, like a man standing in a doorway with his hat in his hands.
He took off his right glove. The gray one.
He put his bare hand flat on the glass of the little door, over the still brass pendulum.
The glass was cold.
And then it was not.
It was like putting his hand on the side of a sleeping animal. Under the glass, under the wood, under the brass and the wheels and the long dark hollow of the case, something was there. Not moving. Not awake. But there. A warmth, very faint and very deep, the way the ground is warm under snow. And folded. That was the strange thing. That was the thing that made his breath catch in his chest. It was folded. Small. Very small. Over and over and over on itself, so tight and so neat and so careful that it took up almost no room at all, like a letter folded so many times that it is no bigger than a thumbnail.
And it was knocking.
He felt it. Under his palm. Through the glass. A tiny push, from inside the fold. From the very middle of it. The way a heart pushes against the wall of a chest.
Thock, said the pendulum, an inch from his hand, against the side of the case.
Corin took his hand away.
He stood very still in the square of light, with his bare hand held a little away from his body, the way you hold a hand you have just burned. He looked at his palm. It was white, and cold, and there was nothing on it at all.
Ash, said Malakor.
The rod's voice was very quiet. Quieter than Corin had ever heard it. Not cold. Not sour. Something else. Something that, in a man, might have been grief.
An old man's, said the rod. Somebody burned, in front of this clock, boy. Not long ago. And they did not let it go. They did not let it blow away the way Ash blows away when a man burns and does not care where it goes. They folded it. On purpose. Small. And they put it in there. Into the case. Into the clock. A pause. I have only ever seen that done twice. By people who knew exactly what they were doing. It is very hard. It costs a great deal. And it only works if the one who folds it is the one whose Ash it is.
Bertram, thought Corin.
I would think so.
He burned. Here. In front of the clock. And he folded what was left and put it inside.
Yes.
Why?
So that it would be kept, said the rod. So that it would not be lost. So that it would be here, waiting, when the right person came and told the clock what time it was. A pause. That is what the knocking is, boy. It is not the clock. It is him. What is left of him. Trying to get out.
Corin put his glove back on. His hand was shaking, a little, and the gray wool caught on his fingers, and he had to pull it straight.
Lyra was watching him. Niles was watching him. Neither of them spoke.
"There's something inside," said Corin. His voice did not sound like his own. "Ash. Folded. An old man's." He looked at Lyra. "I think it's Bertram's."
Lyra did not move. Her face went white, and then very still.
Niles let out a long slow breath.
"The last time he came," he said, "in April. The last Tuesday he came. He stood here longer than usual. A long time. Longer than it takes the big hand to go round. And at the end, he put his hand on the glass, like you did. And then he asked me to leave him alone with it for a moment." The pawnbroker's voice was very low. "I went back into the shop. I heard nothing. I gave him a quarter of an hour. And when I came back, he was standing there with his hat on, ready to go, and he looked—" Niles stopped. "He looked smaller. That is the only word I have. As if something had been taken out of him. And he shook my hand, which he had never done before, and he said, Thank you, Mr. Niles. For keeping it safe. And he went out. And he never came back."
Thock.
And in the cold square of light, under the skylight, with the clocks of the shop ticking faintly through the wall behind them, now, now, now, a hundred small voices that did not know anything was wrong, Lyra Thorne put her hand flat on the glass of her grandfather's clock, over the place where Corin's had been.
She did not have a rod. She could not feel the Ash. Corin knew that. She could feel only cold glass, and under it, wood, and under it, nothing.
But she kept her hand there anyway. For a long time. Until the next knock came.
Thock.
And under her palm, through the glass, Corin saw her flinch.
"He's in there," she said. Very quietly. "Isn't he. What's left of him."
"Yes," said Corin.
"Waiting."
"Yes."
"For someone to tell it what time it is."
She took her hand away.
Ask her, said Malakor suddenly. Sharply. Ask her what her aunt said about the clock. In the long gallery. Yesterday. When you were hiding in the wall.
Corin did not remember it. But it was in the Ledger, in his own hand, on the crowded pages from the back stairs of Number 3, and he had read those pages that morning, sitting on the edge of his bed, while the baby cried downstairs.
"Yesterday," he said slowly. "In the long gallery. Your aunt said the clock was nothing. A box of wheels. She said it was only standing in front of the little door, and it was the key she wanted." He looked up at the great dark case. "And then in May she came here and offered a hundred gold for it. Five times what it was pledged for."
Lyra turned.
"She lied," she said. "One of the times. Either to Gorse, or to Mr. Niles."
"Or she didn't know, yesterday," said Corin. "What we know now."
"What?"
"That it isn't just a clock anymore," said Corin. "That Bertram put something in it." He looked at the brass pendulum, hanging still. "And that it's trying to get out."
Four, Two, Nine, Three
It was Lyra who saw it.
Not Corin, with his trained eyes that always went to the thing that did not belong. Not Niles, who had stood in front of the clock every first Tuesday for two years and looked at every line of it through his jeweler's glass. Lyra. Who had sat on the floor of the long gallery when she was small, with her back against the window seat, before she could read the time, and looked at the clock the way you look at a fire.
They were at the door of the back room. Niles had picked up the heavy bar to put it back across the oak. Corin had his foot on the step. And Lyra had stopped, halfway up, and turned round, and was looking back at the clock in its square of fading light, the way you look back at a house you are leaving.
"Corin," she said.
He turned.
She was not looking at him. She was looking at the face of the clock. At the silver hands, pointing among the painted stars. At the little dial below them, with its tiny hand.
"Read me the time," she said.
"What?"
"The time. On the clock. Where the hands are. Read it to me." Her voice was very strange. Very quiet and very fast, the way a voice goes when it is trying to hold on to something before it gets away. "I can't. My eyes won't do it. Read it to me."
Corin looked up at the face.
The short hand, pointing just past the gray-blue stone at four. Not quite halfway to five.
The long hand, pointing between the stones, among the stars, just short of the sixth hour. One small mark short. He counted the little silver ticks on the ring between the stones, five to each hour. Twenty-five. And four more. Twenty-nine.
The tiny hand on the little dial below, among the numbers one to seven. Pointing at three.
"Four," he said slowly. "The hour is four. The minute is twenty-nine. And the day is three."
He heard himself say it.
He heard it the way you hear a bell ring in a house where you thought nobody was home.
Four.
Two. Nine.
Three.
Nobody moved.
In the back room, the clocks of the shop ticked faintly through the wall, now, now, now, a hundred small voices, as if nothing had happened at all. Niles stood with the iron bar in his clean hands and his one bright eye going from Lyra's face to Corin's face and back again, not understanding. The skylight had gone the color of a bruise. The great dark clock stood in the middle of the floor in the last of the gray light with its silver hands frozen at twenty-nine minutes past four on the third day of the week, among the stars.
And somewhere a long way off, on the other side of the city, over a livery stable on Linden Walk behind a gate with a stone pineapple on it, a man who did not know his own name was sitting at a window. Corin could see him. Not see. Know. The way he knew the shape of the Ledger against his heart. A man at a window, smiling at the swallows, with his left hand lying in his lap, and on the wrist of it four faded letters, FOLD, and his right hand raised to the wall beside him.
Tapping.
Four. Then two. Then nine. Then three.
Over and over. For two years. Faster, this morning, than Lyra had ever heard it.
"It's the time," said Lyra.
Her voice broke in the middle.
"It's the time on the clock," she said. "That's what he's been doing. All this time. At the window. Every day. He hasn't been tapping a nursery rhyme, or a drum call, or nothing. He's been telling us the time." She put both hands over her mouth. Over the top of them, her gray eyes were enormous. "Four. Two. Nine. Three. Twenty-nine minutes past four. On a Wednesday. He's been saying it for two years and none of us—I didn't—I sat beside him every evening and I never once—"
She stopped.
"It's the time it stopped," said Corin.
He said it slowly. Feeling his way. The way you feel your way down a dark stair.
"It's the time the clock stopped. Twenty-nine past four, on a Wednesday. And your father knew it. He's the only one who could have. Because he was the one who stopped it." He looked at the empty silver claw at twelve. "He stopped the hands himself. On the night he burned, in the long gallery. And then he folded that moment away inside himself, small, the way the Ledger says he folded something away three days after Oakhaven, with one word on his wrist, so I know to look for it. And then he burned everything else. Every other thing he was. Everything except that. Four, two, nine, three." Corin's hand had closed on the rod in its pocket. "He couldn't keep his name. He couldn't keep you. But he kept that. He held on to it. And he's been tapping it on the wall for two years, so that somebody would hear."
"Listen to the walls," said Lyra.
She whispered it.
Corin did not know what that meant. He did not remember. But it was in the Ledger. Lord Thorne's "listen to the walls". His own hand. He had written it down without knowing.
And Bertram knew too, said Malakor.
The rod's voice was very low and very steady, the voice of a man setting down a heavy load very carefully, so as not to break it.
Bertram knew. That is why he took it out of the house before the auction. That is why he paid for it, every month, out of his own pocket, for a year and a half. That is why he stood in front of it with his hat in his hands. That is why, at the end, when he knew he was not going to be able to come back, he burned himself in front of it and folded what was left and put it inside. A pause. It's waiting, Mr. Niles. For someone to tell it what time it is.
Tell it, thought Corin. Tell it what time it is.
Four, said Malakor softly. Two. Nine. Three.
Corin took his foot off the step.
He turned round, in the doorway of the back room, and looked at the clock. At the long dark case with its carved roses and thorns going up and up. At the glass door over the still brass pendulum. At the wood beside the glass, where Bertram's hand had rested every month, and polished it, so that it shone.
He took a step back down into the room.
And Niles said, "No."
It was the first time the small man had raised his voice. It was not loud. But it was sharp, and sudden, and it stopped Corin like a hand on his chest.
The pawnbroker had put down the iron bar. He was standing in the passage at the top of the step, between Corin and the shop, with both his clean hands held up in front of him, palm out, the way you hold up your hands to stop a horse.
"Not here," said Niles. "Not now. Not in my shop." His one bright eye was wide. "I do not know what you are going to do. I do not want to know. But I know that look, sir. I have seen it on men who are about to break something they cannot mend." He shook his head. "That clock is a pledge. It is not yours. It is not the young lady's. It is not mine. Until the ticket is brought, it belongs to nobody but the man who pledged it. And whatever is inside it—" His voice wavered, just for a moment. "Whatever is inside it, it is his too. And he is not here to say yes."
Corin stood on the step.
He looked at the small man with one eye and a brass ball sewn where the other should be, standing in his passage with his hands up. He looked at the clock. He looked at Lyra.
Lyra had taken her hands away from her mouth. Her face was white and wet and very still.
"He's right," she said.
"Lyra—"
"He's right, Corin." Her voice was low and steady now. "It isn't ours. Not yet. And it isn't ours to open. Not without the ticket." She looked at the clock for a long time. "Bertram did this. Not for us. For whoever brought the ticket. That's who he was waiting for. That's who he meant to tell." She swallowed. "If we tap on that glass now, without it, then we're no better than her. Coming in here with a hundred gold. Taking what isn't ours because we can."
Nobody said anything.
Thock, said the clock.
Corin let go of the rod in its pocket. His fingers had left marks in his palm, he found, little curved dents, where they had been holding it.
"The ticket," he said.
"The ticket," said Lyra.
She turned to Niles.
"Mr. Niles. You said Bertram was born over a saddler's shop. On Tanner's Rise. Sixty-eight years ago. He told you so, many times." Her chin came up. "Did he ever tell you where he lived? After he left us? When he came here every month, where did he come from?"
The pawnbroker lowered his hands.
He looked at her for a long moment, with his one eye. Then he looked past her, at the clock, in the last of the gray light, as if he were asking it something.
"Rule four, miss," he said quietly. "I may not tell you who pledged a thing." He paused. "But Bertram Ashcombe is dead. And the dead, as I said, do not hold a man to his promises the way the living do."
He looked down at his own clean hands.
"He came up the hill," said Niles. "Every first Tuesday. From the river. I watched him from my window, the first time, because I wondered. He came up Tanner's Rise and down Needle Street and across the bottom of Gutter Street. Always the same way." He looked up. "And once, in the winter, when it was very cold, he came in with saddle soap on his hands. The smell of it. Strong. And I said, you've been working, Mr. Ashcombe. And he laughed, which he did not often do, and said, I've gone back where I started, Mr. Niles. A room over a saddler's. Only it's not the same saddler, and it's not the same room, and I'm not the same boy."
"Tanner's Rise," said Corin.
"I did not say so," said Niles. "I said he came up it."
They went out through the shop, between the shelves of tagged and gleaming things, past the hundred ticking clocks, now, now, now, and the cuckoo clock with its wooden bird frozen with its beak open. At the door, Niles stopped them.
He did not say goodbye. He reached under his counter, and brought up a small square of card, and pushed it through the little arched gap in the brass grille.
It was one of his tickets. Blank. With N. NILES printed at the top, and the three brass balls, and a line for a number, and a line for a name, and the terms in very small print underneath.
"So you know what you are looking for," said Niles. "It will look like that. Only older. And with seven seven one four written on it, in my hand. And Bertram's name." His bright eye moved from Lyra to Corin. "And it will be somewhere he thought nobody would look. He was that kind of man."
Lyra took the card. She put it inside her collar, with the others.
"Three weeks," said Niles. "Remember. The first Tuesday of November. The third bell after noon." He looked at them both. "Before she does."
The cracked bell rang as they went out. Twice. And stopped.
It was dusk on Gutter Street. The low sun had gone behind the warehouses across the Cut, and the black water lay in its stone trench like a strip of night, with the first lamps of the far side shaking in it. The pawnshops were lighting their windows, one by one, all the way up the hill, and behind the bars the sad and lovely things glowed in the lamplight: the wedding rings, the medals, the stopped pocket watches, the jar with something gray inside it.
Lyra stood on the step of Niles's shop and looked up the hill toward Needle Street.
"Tanner's Rise," she said. "Tomorrow. After you finish at the Bank."
"Yes."
"And tonight I'm going home, and I'm going to sit with my father, and I'm going to listen to him tap." Her voice was very quiet. "And when he taps, I'm going to tap back. Four, two, nine, three. On the wall. So he knows somebody finally heard."
She looked at Corin.
"Do you think he'll know?" she said. "Do you think there's enough of him left in there to know?"
Corin thought about a man at a window, smiling at swallows, with FOLD on his wrist. A man who had kept one thing, out of all the things he was. One small thing, folded very small, and held on to it through everything, and tapped it out on a wall every day for two years, for a daughter who did not understand.
"I think," he said slowly, "there's exactly enough of him left in there to know that."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded once, the way her mother must have nodded, and turned, and walked away up Gutter Street in her old brown coat, with her sword hidden at her hip, past the lit windows, toward the Hill.
Corin watched her go.
Then he took out the Ledger, there on Niles's step, in the last of the light, and wrote. He wrote it all. Pledge 7714. Twenty gold. Eighteen ticks, then a gap, then five masks. Five different hands. For seven seven one four. Ring-pieces of Marrowmere gold. Because it's owed. The gray seal, and the first two marks, the same as the brooch. The same house. The lady in white in May. He won't be coming. Not any month. Did it come with all twelve stones? A hundred gold, on the first Tuesday of November. The clock. Eleven stones and one empty claw. The knock. Bertram's Ash, folded small inside the case, knocking to get out. And the hands.
He wrote the hands last. Very carefully. In letters bigger than the rest.
THE CLOCK STOPPED AT 4:29 ON THE THIRD DAY. FOUR. TWO. NINE. THREE.
LORD THORNE IS TELLING US THE TIME.
BERTRAM: "IT'S WAITING FOR SOMEONE TO TELL IT WHAT TIME IT IS."
DON'T TOUCH IT WITHOUT THE TICKET. LYRA SAYS. SHE'S RIGHT.
FIND THE TICKET. TANNER'S RISE. A ROOM OVER A SADDLER'S. THREE WEEKS.
And then, because it came to him, standing on the step with the black water of the Cut at his back and the lamps coming on one by one up the hill:
The day dial says three. Lyra says one is Monday. So three is Wednesday.
Today is Tuesday.
Tomorrow is Wednesday.
He looked at the words for a long time. He did not know why they made the back of his neck go cold. It was only a day. Only a number on a little dial. Clocks stopped on all kinds of days.
And at twenty-nine minutes past four tomorrow, said Malakor very softly, from the long pocket at his ribs, it will be exactly the time on that clock. For one minute. Somewhere in this city. A pause. I wonder who else knows that.
Corin put the Ledger away.
He walked up Gutter Street in the dusk, past the lit windows and the sad bright things behind the bars, with the three stones warm at his hip and the rod cold along his ribs and the list folded small under his arm, close to his heart.
Behind him, through the thick bubbled glass of the last shop before the Cut, a hundred clocks ticked. Now. Now. Now.
And under them, in the back room, in the dark, patient and far away, something knocked once on the inside of a door that had been shut for a long, long time, and waited to be let out.
