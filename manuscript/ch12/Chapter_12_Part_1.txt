Chapter 12: Niles the Pawnbroker, Part 1 of 5: Tuesday
Debt of the Unremembered · Arc 1
Tuesday
The words were the first thing he saw. Before the fish-shaped stain. Before the crack in the ceiling. Before he knew his own name.
They were on a paper pinned to the very top of the board above his bed, above all the other papers, above BOOTS FIRST and READ THE BOOK BEFORE YOU DO ANYTHING ELSE. Big black letters, pressed so hard that the pencil had torn through in two places.
ASK MALAKOR: WHO ELSE CUT HIS SPIRAL TO THE LEFT?
HE KNOWS. HE WON'T SAY.
MAKE HIM.
Corin lay on his back with the book on his chest and looked at it for a long time.
He did not know who Malakor was. He did not know what a spiral was, or why it mattered which way it turned. He did not know whose hand had written it, except that the hand was frightened and angry and very tired, and that the letters leaned the way his own fingers wanted to lean when he lifted them and moved them in the air above the blanket, as if holding a pencil that was not there.
So it was his. He had written it. And so he believed it.
He read the book.
It took a long time, the way it always took a long time. The gray light in the little window above him went from the color of dishwater to the color of weak tea. Somewhere below, in the house, a baby was crying, and a woman's voice was singing to it, off-key, and a door banged, and a girl's voice shouted something about porridge. The words in the book went past him like the scenery outside the window of a tram. A name. A birthplace. A sister. A fear of the dark. A Ministry with a stone eye. A rod. A voice in the rod. A girl with short black hair and a sword. A girl with a brass key. A boy who called him Inspector. A dead man and a red sock. A bank with glass counters. A woman with a gold tooth.
And then the last pages. Yesterday's. Crowded edge to edge, the way they always seemed to be at the end. A coat of deep green wool. A hall full of lilies. A woman in white with a red mouth. Cups with masks in the bottom that blinked up through the tea. A white shell mask on blue velvet. And a silver cup beside it, with a spiral cut inside it that turned the wrong way.
He closed the book.
He got up, and put on his boots first, because the board said so. Then the rest. Then he took the gray coat down off the hook behind the door.
It was heavy. He felt the weight of it in its different places as he put it on. The long hard thing along the right side, under the arm. The small warm thing low on the right, near the hip. The empty pocket over his heart. He put the book in the empty pocket. It fit.
And under the left arm, in a place he had not known about until his fingers found it, a second pocket. Small. Secret. With something folded small inside it, and something round and flat and hard.
Good morning, said a voice from the long hard thing at his ribs.
Corin stood very still in the middle of the small gray room.
Malakor, said the voice. The rod. Page four. You always stop like that. A pause. And you have read the board, I see. I can tell by your back. You have the back of a man who is about to ask a question he will not like the answer to.
Corin took the rod out of the long pocket.
He had not decided to. His hand did it. It was silver, and cold, and longer than his forearm, and the word MALAKOR ran down its length in old letters worn smooth by many hands. Around the tip, fine lines had been carved in a spiral, winding round and round the silver like the thread of a screw. He turned the tip toward the gray light of the window and followed the lines with his eye. Round. And round.
To the right. With the sun. The way a clock turns.
"Who else cut his spiral to the left?" said Corin.
The rod was silent.
"The board says you know. The board says you won't say. The board says make you." Corin looked down at the silver in his hand. "I don't know how to make you. I don't know anything. I don't know what you are. I don't know who I am. I've been awake for an hour. But I wrote that board, and I believe it. So I'm asking."
For a long time there was nothing. The baby downstairs had stopped crying. A cart went by in Tallow Street, its wheels rumbling on the cobbles.
Then Malakor spoke.
That is a good question, he said. His voice was very quiet. Not sour. Not mocking. Just quiet, and old, and careful, like a man walking across a frozen pond. But it is the wrong one.
"What's the right one?"
The right one, said the rod, is why a Councillor of the Ministry keeps a siphon cup in a glass cabinet in her sitting room. Like a jewel. Like a keepsake. A siphon cup is not a jewel, boy. It is a tool. A dirty one. It is what you use to take something out of a person who does not want to give it. A pause. And the other question. The one under that. Who gave it to her?
Corin waited.
You want a name, said Malakor. I understand. You live on names. Your whole life is a list of names in a book. But if I give you this one, boy, you will write it down. And then it will be in the book. And anyone who takes the book will have it. And anyone who reads over your shoulder on a tram. And a woman with a gold tooth who reads people through their hands. His voice went lower. Some names are not safe to write down. Not yet. Not until you know what to do with them.
"Then when?"
When you have seen the cup again, said the rod. And held it. And felt which way the Ash in it wants to turn. Then you will not need me to tell you. You will know. A long pause. And then, if you still want me to say the name out loud, I will say it. I give you my word. For whatever the word of a broom is worth.
Corin stood with the cold silver in his hand for a long moment.
Then he put it back in the long pocket. And he took out the book, and turned to a fresh page, and wrote under yesterday's last line:
Asked Malakor. He would not say. He said: "Some names are not safe to write down. Not yet." He said: "Why does a Councillor keep a siphon cup like a jewel? Who gave it to her?" He said when I have held the cup myself, I will know. He gave his word.
And under that, smaller:
I believe him. I don't know why.
When he got to the bottom of the narrow crooked stairs, past the scratched words on the fourth-floor landing and the little girl's door with the cat's saucer outside it and the empty chair by the round window on the second floor, there was a paper waiting for him.
It was folded in four and pushed under the crack of his own name, where somebody had chalked it on the wall at the foot of the stairs, a long time ago: VANE, TOP. The paper was cream-colored, but cheap, the kind of cream that is only white gone a little yellow. It smelled faintly of horses.
He unfolded it.
The hand was neat and clear and slanting. He knew it. It was in the book too, here and there, on pages where somebody else had written beside him. Lyra Thorne.
Corin —
I did not sleep. I sat at the window with Father and I watched the swallows come at dawn and I thought about the note in the oilcloth. N. on Gutter Street. I asked Mr. Pruett, who knows every pawnshop from here to the sea because he has pawned his watch in most of them. He says there is only one N. on Gutter Street. Niles. The last shop before the Cut. He says Niles is honest, which is a strange thing to say about a pawnbroker, and that he has only one eye, and that he never sells a pledge before its time, not for any money.
I am going there today. After you finish at the Bank. Meet me at the corner of Needle Street and Gutter Lane at the third bell after noon.
Do not wear the green.
Do not tell Juno. She will want to come and it is not a place for her.
— L.
P.S. I turned the cup over last night. The one from my aunt. I looked at the marks under it with Father's magnifying glass for an hour. I could not read them. Neither could Father. But when I held it up to him, he stopped tapping. He looked at it for a long time. And then he said one word. He has not said a new word in two years.
He said: "Home."
Corin read the last line three times.
Then he folded the letter very small and put it inside the back cover of the book, beside a cream envelope that smelled of lilies and a gray slip with a red stamp, and went out into Tallow Street.
It was a cold bright morning. The rain had blown away in the night and left the sky scrubbed clean, a pale hard blue, and the cobbles of Tallow Street shone wet in the low sun like the backs of fish. Smoke went straight up from the chimneys in long gray threads. A man was sweeping the step of the chandler's on the corner, and a boy was shouting newspapers, and two women in shawls were standing at the pump, filling buckets and talking, and they stopped talking as Corin went by in his gray coat, and started again behind him.
He walked down toward the river and Lantern Quay. He did not hum. He did not know why he did not hum. There was a line in the book, in his own hand, that said Don't hum near the glass. He did not know what it meant. He obeyed it anyway.
But all the way down the hill, under his breath, under the noise of the morning, one word went round and round in his head like a wheel.
Home.
The Line
The line outside the Bank was longer on a Tuesday. The Ledger did not say so. Corin simply saw it. It came all the way along Lantern Quay from the white steps of the Bank to the foot of the Salt Stairs, and up the Salt Stairs, two and three abreast, and out of sight round the corner at the top. Hundreds of people. Standing in the cold bright morning with their collars up and their hands in their pockets and their breath going up in small white clouds, waiting to sell an afternoon.
He walked past them, the whole long length of them, the way he walked past them every morning, he supposed. They looked at his gray coat. They looked away.
He was halfway along the quay when he noticed the young man.
He did not know why he noticed him. There was nothing different about him. He stood in the line like all the others, near the front, a few places from the white steps. He was tall and thin, with the broad bony shoulders of a man who carries heavy things for a living, and he wore what all the dockers wore: a short canvas jacket, stiff with tar and salt, and canvas trousers, and boots that had been mended with wire. There was a docker's hook hanging from his belt, a curved iron hook with a wooden handle, the kind they used for pulling bales out of the holds of ships. His hair was fair and cut very short, and his face was young. Twenty, perhaps. Not more.
And his face was smiling.
Not at anything. Not at anyone. It was a small, soft, pleasant smile, the kind of smile a man might wear on a summer afternoon, lying in long grass with his eyes half shut, listening to bees. It sat on the young docker's face in the cold morning, in the long gray line, quite still, quite steady, and it did not move at all. And his eyes, above it, were blue, and clear, and empty, like windows in a house where nobody has lived for years.
Corin knew that smile.
He did not remember it. But he had seen it, the Ledger said, on an old man in a chair by a round dirty window on the second-floor landing of Number 14. On a lord at a window over a stable, tapping on a wall. A Slate, the people in the Docks called them. Wiped clean. You could write anything on them, and it would not stay.
The young docker was a Slate. And he was standing in line to sell.
What can he have left? Corin thought. What can a Slate have left to sell?
Strength, said Malakor quietly. The feel of his own arms. The way his back knows how to lift. A man like that does not remember why he works, but his body does. And the Bank will buy that too, if he lets it. A day at a time. A week at a time. A pause. Until one morning he wakes up and his hands do not remember how to close round a hook.
Corin walked on. Up the white steps. Past the two Bank porters in dark green with gold piping, who nodded their small cold nods. In through the great doors, into the warmth and the blue gemstone light and the sweetish smell of a room where a great many people have been crying.
Warden Briggs was at his ledger stand by the slag-room stairs, the way he always was, the Ledger said. Heavy and gray, in his dark green coat a size too small, with the brass plate sticking out over his stomach like a shelf. WARDEN. INTAKE. BRIGGS. He looked up as Corin came along the hall. His small pale eyes were tired.
"Vane," he said.
"Warden."
"Family business again today, is it?"
Corin stopped.
"How did you know?"
"You've got the look." Briggs turned a page in his big ledger. "Man with somewhere to be has a look. Stands on the balls of his feet. Keeps looking at the clock." He did not look up. "I've been watching men in this hall for twenty years, Vane. I know the look of a man who's going to leave early, same as I know the look of a man who's going to cry at the counter. Same look, half the time." He dipped his pen. "Four glasses. Same as yesterday. Clear four and you can go at the second bell after noon."
"Thank you, Warden."
"Don't thank me. I'm not doing you a favor. I'm doing the arithmetic. You clear four glasses in the time Bucket Man used to clear one." Briggs made a mark. "Off you go. And don't hum."
Corin went toward the slag-room stairs.
As he passed the long counter, the great doors behind him opened to let in the next of the line, and he turned his head, a little, from the edges of his eyes, the way he had been taught.
It was the young docker.
He came in out of the bright morning with his hook at his belt and his soft empty smile, and he walked down the long bright hall, past the glasses waking and going dark, waking and going dark, to a stall near the end. He put his bare hand flat on the glass. The clerk behind it, a young woman in dark green with white gloves, laid her hand on top of his without looking at him. The glass woke. Faint. Blue. A few inches round the docker's palm. Hardly any light at all.
And Corin, turning back toward the stairs, saw Briggs.
The Warden had stopped writing. He was standing at his ledger stand with the pen in his hand, very still. He was not looking at the docker. He was looking at the wall. At a place on the cream-painted wall above the slag-room door, where there was nothing at all to look at. His heavy gray face had gone a darker gray, the color of wet slate.
And his free hand, the one that did not hold the pen, had come up and pressed flat against his chest. Over the brass plate. Over BRIGGS. The way it had pressed there on Friday, the Ledger said, when Corin told him his name.
The glass at the end of the counter went dark. The clerk pressed a small cloudy stone against it. The docker took his hand away. He looked at his palm, the way they all did, puzzled, as if it belonged to someone else. The clerk put two coppers into it. He closed his fingers on them. They did not close very well.
Then he turned and walked back up the long bright hall toward the doors, smiling at nothing, and went out into the morning.
Briggs did not watch him go. Briggs did not move until the great doors had closed behind him. Then he took his hand away from his chest, and looked at it, and wiped it slowly on the front of his green coat, as if there were something on it that would not come off.
He saw Corin looking.
"What?" said Briggs. Roughly.
"Nothing, Warden."
"Then get down those stairs."
Corin went down the stairs.
The slag room was cold and blue and silent. The clouded glasses stood in their racks along the walls like sheets of frozen breath. He found the first one, and took out the rod, and held its carved tip flat against the cloud, and let it listen. The scraps came out of the glass and into the silver, thread by thread. A smell of tar. A woman's laugh. The bottom of a well. Rain on a window. None of them anybody's.
But he worked slowly that morning, and his mind was not on the glass.
Who was he? he thought. The docker. Why did Briggs look at the wall?
I do not know, said Malakor. But I know what a man looks like when he is trying not to see something. I have watched a great many men not see things, boy. Your Warden was not seeing that young man so hard that it nearly broke him.
At the second bell after noon, Corin came up the stairs with four clear glasses behind him.
Briggs was at his stand. He made his mark. He did not look up.
"Four," he said.
"Four."
"Go on, then."
Corin went along the hall toward the doors. Halfway there, he stopped. He did not know why. He turned round.
"Warden," he said.
Briggs looked up. Slowly. His small pale eyes were very tired.
"The young man," said Corin. "The docker. With the hook. Does he come every week?"
Briggs looked at him for a long, long moment. His heavy face did not change at all. But his hand, on the ledger, closed slowly into a fist, and the pen in it bent, and the nib made a long black scratch across the page.
"Every Tuesday," said Briggs. "Third stall from the end. Two coppers." He looked down at the black scratch on his page. "He's been coming for eight years."
"Do you know his name?"
"No," said Briggs.
He said it very fast. Too fast. Like a door slamming.
Then he picked up a blotter and pressed it hard on the black scratch, and kept it pressed there, and did not look up again.
Corin stood for a moment longer in the warm bright hall. Then he turned and walked out through the great doors, down the white steps, past the long patient line, into the cold.
On the quay, at the bottom of the Salt Stairs, he took out the Ledger and wrote it all. The docker with the hook. The soft empty smile. The third glass from the end. Two coppers. Every Tuesday. Eight years. Briggs looking at the wall. Briggs's hand on his chest. Do you know his name? No. Too fast.
He looked at the page for a long time. He did not know what it meant. He thought that perhaps it did not mean anything. That perhaps it was only one more sad thing in a city made of sad things, and that if he wrote down every one of them the Ledger would be a thousand volumes long and he would spend his whole life reading it.
He underlined Too fast anyway.
Then he put the Ledger away, and went up the Salt Stairs, and turned toward the Ink Quarter.
