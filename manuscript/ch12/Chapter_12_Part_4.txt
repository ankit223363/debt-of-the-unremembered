Chapter 12: Niles the Pawnbroker, Part 4 of 5: The Receipt
The Receipt
"You said a seal," said Corin.
Niles turned his one bright eye from the clock to him.
"In the shop. When I asked why you'd drawn a mask in your book. You said: because that is what was on the seal." Corin did not look away from the small man's face. "What seal?"
The pawnbroker was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, slowly, as if Corin had passed some small test that he had set without saying so.
"Wait here," he said.
He went back up the step, and along the short dark passage, and through the curtain. They heard his quick light footsteps on the boards of the shop, and the sound of a drawer, and then another, and then a pause, and then the footsteps coming back. He came down the step into the cold square of light with something in his hand.
It was a book. Not the great brown ledger. A smaller one. Thin, and long, and narrow, bound in plain gray card, with a strip of black cloth down the spine. He opened it on the lid of the carved chest beside the clock, under the skylight, and turned the pages with one clean finger until he found what he wanted.
"My receipt book," he said. "Every payment that crosses my counter, I write a receipt. Two halves, with my seal across the middle, so that one half goes to the person who paid and one half stays here. The half that stays is called the counterfoil." He laid his finger at the edge of the page. "That way, if anybody ever says I took money I did not write down, or wrote down money I did not take, I can show them both halves, and they will match."
The page was a row of small slips, each one torn neatly down a dotted line, so that only the left half was left in the book. Corin could see the little numbers printed at the top of each one. And the dates. And under the dates, the same small neat hand: Received, one gold, against pledge 7714.
And across the torn edge of each slip, where the two halves had once been joined, there was a seal. Half a seal. A circle of dark red wax, pressed with a little brass stamp, and torn through the middle when the receipt was torn, so that half the circle was in the book and half had gone away with whoever had paid. The half in the book showed half of three small balls. Niles's sign.
All the slips were the same. Eighteen of them. The red half-seal. The three half-balls.
And then the last five.
On the last five slips, beside Niles's red half-seal, there was a second seal.
It was not red. It was gray. A pale silvery gray, like ash, or like the skin of a fish, and it shone a little in the light from the skylight, the way wax does when there is something mixed into it. It had not been torn. It sat whole, a small perfect circle no bigger than a fingernail, on the counterfoil, beside Niles's broken red one. As if whoever had paid had not been content to take away half of Niles's seal. They had wanted to leave something of their own behind.
And pressed into the gray wax, very small and very sharp, there was a mask.
A little half-mask. Two round eyes. The curve of a nose.
"The boy in June asked for my receipt book," said Niles quietly. "Before he paid. He opened it to the counterfoil I had just written, and he took a little stick of gray wax out of his pocket, and a little silver seal on a string round his neck, and he held the wax to my lamp until it dripped, and he pressed the seal into it, there, beside mine. And then he said for seven seven one four, and he put his ring of gold on my counter, and he went away." Niles touched the edge of the page. "And every one who came after him did the same. The girl. The old woman. The fisherman. The veiled lady. Each one with a little stick of gray wax, and the same little silver seal, on a string, round the neck."
"The same seal," said Corin. "All five of them. Five different people. And the same seal."
"The very same," said Niles. "I have looked at them with my glass. Every line. Every mark. The same."
"So they passed it from one to the other."
"Or there are five seals, made by one hand, so alike that even I cannot tell them apart." Niles's thin mouth moved. "I do not know which would frighten me more."
Lyra had bent over the receipt book. She was looking at the last seal, the one from October, the newest, a week old. She did not touch it.
"There's something on it," she said. "Around the edge. Under the mask."
Niles took the jeweler's glass from the ribbon round his neck, and without a word held it out to her.
Lyra screwed it into her right eye, awkwardly, the way someone does who has never done it before, and bent close over the page, so close that her short black hair fell forward over her face. She was very still for a long time.
"Marks," she said. "Around the rim. Under the mask. Like the ones under the cup." She did not lift her head. "Like the ones on—Corin. Give me the tracing. The one from Madame Hesper's. From the brooch."
Corin took out the Ledger. He opened it at the back cover, where the loose things were. A letter folded small. A cream envelope that smelled of lilies. A gray slip with a red stamp. And a square of thin paper, folded very small, the way the Ledger said he had folded it in a dressmaker's shop on Saturday, without knowing why.
He unfolded it. The front of the brooch. The side. The back. And on the back, every tiny mark that Madame Hesper had copied from the inside of the silver, and that he had traced from her book, line by line, holding his breath.
He laid it on the carved chest beside the receipt book. Lyra moved the glass from the seal to the tracing. And back. And to the tracing. And back.
Nobody spoke.
Thock, said the clock.
Lyra lifted her head. She took the glass out of her eye. There was a red ring around her eye where it had pressed. She held the glass out to Corin.
"You look," she said. Her voice was very quiet. "Tell me I'm wrong."
Corin put the glass to his eye.
The world went huge and close and blurred and then, all at once, sharp. The gray wax filled everything, a great gray field, with the mask pressed into it like a face pressed into snow. Around the rim of the field, under the mask's chin, the marks. Tiny lines and curves, as sharp as cuts. He counted them. There were seven.
He moved the glass to the tracing. His own lines, in pencil. Madame Hesper's marks, as he had copied them. He counted those too. There were nine.
He went back to the seal. To the first mark on it. A short straight line, with a curl at the top, like a shepherd's crook.
To the tracing. The first mark. A short straight line, with a curl at the top.
The seal. The second mark. Two small curves, back to back, like a pair of waves.
The tracing. The second mark. Two small curves, back to back.
The seal. The third mark. A dot inside a ring.
The tracing. The third mark. Not a dot inside a ring. Something else. A slanting line, with a hook.
He went over it again. And again. Slowly. Mark by mark. He made himself be sure, the way he had made himself be sure which way a spiral turned.
Then he took the glass out of his eye.
"The first two," he said. "The first two are the same. Exactly the same. After that they're different."
Lyra let out her breath.
"The first two," she said.
"Yes."
"What does that mean?"
Corin did not know. He looked at Niles. Niles shook his head slowly. He did not know either.
I know, said Malakor.
Corin went very still.
Not what the marks say, said the rod. I cannot read Marrowmere scratches any more than you can. But I know how names work, boy. Everywhere. In every country I was ever carried through. A name is two parts. The part that is yours, and the part that is your family's. A pause. If two names begin the same way, and then go different ways, it is because the people who own them come from the same house. And then went their separate ways.
Corin looked down at the two papers lying side by side on the lid of the carved chest in the cold light. The gray seal with its seven marks. The pencil tracing with its nine.
The same house, he thought.
Your lady's mother's true name begins with two marks, said Malakor. And whoever has been paying for this clock since the spring has a seal that begins with the same two. Make of that what you will. The old voice went very low. I would make a great deal of it, if I were you. And I would be very, very careful who I told.
"Corin?" said Lyra. "What is it?"
Corin folded the tracing. Small. Very small. He put it back inside the cover of the Ledger.
"Malakor says it's a family name," he said. "The first part. The part that's the same. He says whoever paid for the clock comes from the same family as your mother."
Lyra did not move.
She stood in the square of pale light from the skylight, in her old brown coat with the sword hidden under it, beside her grandfather's clock, and she looked at the little gray seal in the pawnbroker's receipt book. At the mask pressed into it. At the seven tiny marks round its rim.
"Her family," she said. "She had a family. In Marrowmere. And nobody ever told me."
"Nobody told anybody, miss," said Niles gently. "From what I hear. Not on the Hill."
"And they're here. In the city. They've been here since June. Coming to this shop. Paying for her father-in-law's clock. With their own gold." Lyra's voice was rising. "Why? Why would they? What is this clock to them?"
Nobody answered.
And behind them, in the middle of the cold quiet room, the great dark clock with the stars on its face and the empty silver claw at twelve o'clock gave its small, patient, single knock, as if it were answering a question it had been asked a long time ago, and had been waiting ever since for somebody to hear.
Thock.
The Lady Who Came Before
"Mr. Niles," said Corin. "You said it would be easier to answer some questions in front of the thing they're about."
The pawnbroker had closed the gray receipt book. He stood with it held against his brown waistcoat, in both hands, the way a priest holds a prayer book. He did not look at Corin. He looked at the clock.
"I did," he said.
"We're in front of it."
"So we are."
"Has anybody been here? Asking about it? Not to pay the interest. To buy."
Niles was quiet for a long time. The light from the skylight lay on his bald brown head and made it shine. The clock stood over him, tall and dark, with its silver hands pointing at nothing among the stars.
"Rule four," said Niles at last, "is about who brings things in. Who pledges. Who pays. It says nothing about who comes in and does not pledge, and does not pay, and goes away again with nothing." His bright eye came round to Corin. "I have been thinking about that since you first asked. Whether my rules cover it. I have decided that they do not."
"So someone came."
"Someone came." Niles put the receipt book down on the lid of the carved chest. He folded his clean hands in front of him. "In May, miss. The second week. The week after Bertram did not come."
Lyra went very still.
"It was the middle of the afternoon," said Niles. "Raining. And there was a carriage. That was the first thing. A carriage does not come down Gutter Street. There is no room to turn it at the bottom, by the Cut. It has to back all the way up again to Needle Street, and the horses do not like it. So when I heard wheels, I went to my window." He nodded back toward the shop. "It was a closed carriage. Black. No crest on the door. Very good horses. And a very big man on the box."
"How big?" said Corin.
"Big enough that when he got down, the carriage rose on its springs." Niles's thin mouth moved. "He did not come in. He stood outside my door. In the rain. With his back to it. Like a wall. Nobody came down Gutter Street for the next half hour, and I do not think it was the weather."
Gorse, Corin thought.
He did not say it. He did not need to. He saw Lyra's hand close, at her side, on the fold of her old brown coat, over the place where the sword hung.
"And then she came in," said Niles.
"She."
"A lady. In white." The pawnbroker's bright eye went far away for a moment, the way eyes do when they are looking at something that is not in the room. "I have had ladies in my shop before, miss. Not many. Ladies send their maids, or their husbands, or a man of business. But sometimes one comes herself, in a veil, with her face hidden, because she does not want her maid or her husband or her man of business to know what she is pledging. And they are always frightened. Always. You can smell it. They stand at my counter with their hands shaking and their rings in a handkerchief and they cannot look at me." He shook his head slowly. "This one was not frightened. And she did not wear a veil."
"What did she look like?"
"Rule four, miss." But he said it gently. "I will tell you she was tall. And that she smelled of flowers. Very strongly. The whole shop smelled of them for a week after. My clocks were full of it. I opened every case, and it was in there, in among the wheels, like dust."
Lyra closed her eyes.
"Lilies," she said.
Niles did not say yes. He did not say no. He only looked at her, with his one bright eye, for a long moment.
"She came to my counter," he said, "and she smiled at me, and she said, Mr. Niles. You have something of my family's. And I said, I have a great many things of a great many families, madam. Which one? And she said, A clock. A tall one. With twelve stones. And I said, I may have. Are you here to redeem it? And she said, No. I am here to buy it."
"And you said no," said Corin.
"I told her rule one." Niles's voice did not change. "No pledge is sold before its time. And she smiled, and she said, Fifty gold. Which is two and a half times what I lent on it. And I told her rule one again. And she said, A hundred. And I told her rule one a third time. And she stopped smiling." He paused. "Only for a moment. Then she started again. A little differently."
"Differently how?"
"Like a woman who has decided to be patient." Niles unfolded his hands and laid one of them, flat, on the lid of the carved chest. "She asked about the ticket. She asked if I knew where it was. I said the ticket was with the man who pledged, and I did not know where he was. And she said, quite softly, Oh, Mr. Niles. He won't be coming. Not this month. Not any month."
The cold room was very quiet.
"She said that," said Lyra. Her voice was barely there. "In May. The week after he missed. Before anybody knew."
"Before I knew," said Niles. "I did not know for certain until today, miss, when I saw his hand on that paper, saying forgive me. But she knew. In May." His bright eye did not move from Lyra's face. "That is why I am telling you this. Rule four or no rule four. Because she knew, and she should not have."
Corin felt the three stones at his hip go cold again. Not cold like a river. Cold like iron in the winter, so cold it seems to burn.
A sister in the country. Doctor Aske, under the Lantern Bridge steps, nine days before a carriage came. Bertram, who missed May. A woman in white with a red mouth who knew that he would not be coming. Before anybody knew.
She did not guess, said Malakor, very quietly. Women like that do not guess. They know because they arrange to know.
"What else did she say?" said Corin.
Niles looked up at the clock.
"She asked to see it," he said. "I let her. It is not against my rules. Anybody may look at a pledge, if they can say what it is, and she said what it was." He nodded at the square of light on the floor. "She stood there. Where Bertram used to stand. Where you are standing now, miss. And she looked at it for a long time. Not at the case. Not at the carving. At the face. At the stones." His voice went lower. "And then she asked me a question I did not expect."
Lyra waited. Corin waited. The clock waited.
"She asked me," said Niles, "whether it had come into my shop with all twelve of its stones."
Thock, said the clock.
"And I told her the truth," said Niles. "Which is that it had not. That when Bertram brought it in, on the back of a handcart, in the rain, two years ago this month, the setting at twelve was already empty. I wrote it in my book. Eleven present, one setting empty. I always write down what is missing as well as what is there. A pledge must come out the way it went in."
"And what did she say?"
The pawnbroker looked at Lyra for a long moment before he answered.
"She laughed," he said. "Not loudly. Just a little. The way you laugh when somebody has told you something you already knew, and you are glad to hear it said out loud. And she said, Of course it didn't. He would never have let it go with the twelfth still in it. He wasn't such a fool as that."
"Who?" said Lyra. "Bertram?"
"I do not think she meant Bertram, miss."
Lyra stared at him.
"Father," she said. Very slowly. "She meant Father."
Niles did not answer. He did not need to.
"And then," he went on, after a moment, "she took a card out of her glove. And she laid it on my counter. And she said, If the ticket should ever turn up, Mr. Niles, send to me at once. At once. Whoever brings it. Before you give them the clock. I'll make it worth your while. And she said, And if it doesn't turn up, I'll be here on the first Tuesday of November. At the third bell after noon. With a hundred gold and a great deal of patience." Niles's mouth went thin. "And she went out, and the big man opened the carriage door for her, and they backed all the way up Gutter Street to Needle Street, with the horses complaining, and the whole street watching from behind their curtains."
"Do you still have the card?"
Niles reached into his waistcoat pocket. He brought out a small square of cream-colored card, thick and stiff, with its edges cut in a little wave like a pie crust and painted with a thin line of silver. He did not give it to her. He held it up, in the light from the skylight, so that she could see.
There was no name on it. Only a direction, in small printed letters: Number 11, Crown Walk.
And under it, in violet ink, by hand, one letter.
A round C, with a hook on top.
Lyra looked at it for a long time. She did not touch it.
"Will you send to her?" she said at last. "If somebody brings the ticket?"
Niles put the card back in his waistcoat pocket.
"Rule two, miss," he said quietly. "Whoever holds the ticket holds the pledge. I do not ask how they came by it." His bright eye held hers. "And I do not send word to anybody else before I give a pledge to the person who has the right to it. No matter what is written on their card." He paused. "That is not one of my four rules. That is just me."
Lyra let out a breath. Her shoulders came down a little.
"Thank you," she said.
"Don't thank me yet, miss." Niles turned and looked up at the great dark clock, with its stars and its stones and its empty claw. "Because on the first Tuesday of November, at the third bell after noon, if nobody has brought me ticket seven seven one four and twenty gold, then the term is out, and the clock is mine to sell. After due notice. And I am a pawnbroker, and not a rich man, and not a young one." He was quiet for a moment. "And she will be standing at my counter with a hundred gold. And I will have no rule left to tell her."
Thock.
The sound went through the cold room like a footstep in an empty church.
Lyra looked up at the clock. At the hands, silver among the painted stars. At the eleven dull stones. At the empty silver claw at twelve.
"Three weeks," she said.
"Three weeks, miss."
"And the first snow."
Niles looked at her, puzzled. Corin did not.
She has given you the same day twice, said Malakor softly. Your lady's aunt. The first snow, for the brooch. The first Tuesday of November, for the clock. Three weeks, either way. A pause. That is not chance, boy. Somebody is closing a net. And they have tied all the ends to the same day.
