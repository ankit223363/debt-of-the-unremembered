Chapter 12: Niles the Pawnbroker, Part 3 of 5: The Pawn Book
The Pawn Book
The page was ruled in columns. Thin red lines, drawn by hand, very straight, from the top of the page to the bottom. And in the columns, the small neat black writing marched down in rows, like soldiers on parade.
Niles had laid a ruler across the page. A long flat ruler of black wood, with brass ends. It lay across the second column from the left, all the way down, so that whatever was written in that column was hidden underneath it.
"Names," said Niles. He tapped the ruler once with one clean finger. "Under there. Not for your eyes." He took his finger away. "The rest you may read."
Lyra bent over the book. Corin bent beside her. The green lamp hissed. The clocks on the back wall said now, now, now in their hundred small voices.
The first column was numbers. Pledge numbers. Four figures each, one under the other, going up by one with every line, the way pledge numbers do. 7711. 7712. 7713.
And 7714.
Niles had put his finger beside that one, on the very edge of the page, without touching the ink. Corin followed the line across, past the black ruler.
The third column said what the pledge was.
Long-case clock. Walnut and brass, glass door. Height seven feet two inches. Face: twelve stones in a ring, set in silver (eleven present, one setting empty). Movement: fine, not English, not Veridian. Maker's plate inside door: E. T. & T. R. Condition: good. Stopped.
The fourth column said what had been lent on it.
Twenty gold.
The fifth column said for how long.
Two years. From the first Tuesday of October.
The sixth column said the interest.
One gold the month. First Tuesday.
And then the seventh column, the widest on the page, was a long row of little marks. Small ticks, one for every month, each with a date beside it in tiny figures. Corin counted them with his eyes. Two ticks in the first winter. Then twelve, a whole year of them. Then the ticks went on.
But they changed.
For the first eighteen ticks, each one was the same. A small neat black tick, and beside it the date, and beside the date a small neat black letter. B. Eighteen times. B. B. B. B. Down and down, month after month, in the very small black hand.
And then, in May, there was a gap. A small blank space in the column. No tick. No date. No B.
And then the ticks began again.
In June. In July. In August. September. And this month, October, the first Tuesday, a week ago. Five more ticks. Every month, without a gap.
But there was no B beside them.
Instead, beside each of the last five ticks, in the same small neat black ink, Niles had drawn a tiny picture. So small that Corin had to bend right down over the counter, until his nose nearly touched the brass grille, to see it.
A little half-mask. Two round eyes. The curve of a nose between them.
Lyra drew in her breath.
Corin straightened up very slowly. He looked at the pawnbroker. Niles was sitting on his tall stool with his clean hands flat on the counter and his one bright eye on Corin's face, waiting.
"You don't write names," said Corin. "In that column. You write a mark."
"I write a mark for each person who pays," said Niles. "So I know at a glance whose money it was. Bertram was B. For his initial. He told me I might." His eye went down to the little drawn masks. "The one who pays now did not give me a name. Or an initial. So I gave them a mark of my own. It is my book, and I may draw in it what I like."
"Why a mask?"
Niles looked at him for a long moment.
"Because that is what was on the seal," he said.
Lyra had not lifted her head from the book. She was looking at the gap. The small blank space in May where there was no tick.
"He missed May," she said. "Bertram. You said he never missed. And then he missed May. And then in June somebody else paid." She looked up. "How did they know? How did anybody know that he'd stopped? Who else knew the clock was here?"
"That," said Niles, "is a very good question, miss. And it is one that I have asked myself on the first Tuesday of every month since June." He folded his hands. "I did not know that anybody else knew. Bertram never brought anyone with him. He never said a word to anyone in my hearing. And yet, on the first Tuesday of June, at the third bell after noon, exactly the hour that Bertram used to come, the door of my shop opened, and the bell rang, and somebody came in and put one gold piece on my counter and said, For seven seven one four, and went away again."
"Who?"
"Rule four, miss."
Lyra closed her mouth.
"I will tell you this much," said Niles, "because it is not a name, and it is not a face, and therefore it is not against my rules. It was not the same person twice."
Corin looked at him.
"Five months," said Niles. "Five times. Five different people. A boy, once. A girl, once. An old woman, once. A man in a fisherman's jersey who smelled of the sea. And once, in August, a woman so heavily veiled that I could not have told you if she were sixteen or sixty." His one eye did not blink. "Every one of them came in at the third bell after noon on the first Tuesday. Every one of them said the same five words. For seven seven one four. And every one of them paid in the same coin."
He reached under the counter. Corin heard a drawer slide open, a small one, on oiled runners. Niles brought something up and laid it on the counter, on the near side of the grille, under the green lamp, and took his hand away.
It was a coin.
It was gold. But it was not like any gold coin Corin had ever seen, in the Ledger or out of it. The Veridian gold piece was round and thick, with the Archon's temple on one side and the closed eye on the other. This coin was not round. It was a shape with many sides. Twelve, he thought, counting quickly with his eyes. A twelve-sided coin, thin and bright, with a hole punched through the middle of it, like a ring. And stamped round the hole, on one side, very small and very fine, there was a pattern. Waves. Little curling waves, going all the way round, one after another, like the edge of a lace collar, like the feet of the Marrowmere cups.
And on the other side, round the hole, there was a word. In letters that were not letters. Lines and curves, small and sharp, cut into the gold.
The same kind of marks that were scratched on the underside of a little white cup on a shelf over a stable. The same kind of marks that were on the back of a silver mask in a dressmaker's pattern book.
"Marrowmere gold," said Niles quietly. "A ring-piece. They thread them on a string, over there, and wear them round the neck. So they cannot be dropped in the canals." He looked at the coin. "I have seen perhaps a dozen in fifty years. And I have taken five of them since June, for the same pledge, from five different hands." He looked up at Lyra. "I keep that one. The first. I put the others in the bank, because I am a pawnbroker and not a fool. But I keep that one in my drawer, because I wanted to be able to look at it, on the first Tuesday of every month, and wonder."
Lyra put out one finger and touched the coin. Just touched it. The way you touch something to make sure it is really there.
"Somebody from Marrowmere," she said slowly, "has been paying for my grandfather's clock. Every month. Since Bertram stopped."
"Somebody who pays in Marrowmere gold," said Niles. "Which is not quite the same thing, miss. Gold travels. But yes."
"Why?"
"I do not know." The pawnbroker shook his head slowly. "I asked the fisherman. In August. I broke my own rule, a little, and I asked him why. And he looked at me as if I were simple, and he said, Because it's owed. And he went away."
Because it's owed.
Corin felt the three stones at his hip go warm again. All at once. As if somebody had laid a hand over them.
Those We Owe, he thought. A list, in violet ink, folded small, falling open on the bare floor of a dressmaker's shop. A lady who was not ill, going to the sea with a footman. A brooch with a true name scratched on the back of it. Cups with names under them. And now a fisherman who smelled of the sea, laying a ring of Marrowmere gold on a pawnbroker's counter and saying because it's owed.
Somebody, said Malakor very softly, is keeping a list. And the clock is on it.
Lyra was looking at the book again. At the first column. At the dates.
"Two years from the first Tuesday of October," she read. "Two years ago. And this is October now." She looked up. "Mr. Niles. When does the term end?"
Niles did not answer at once. He reached out and, with one clean finger, moved the ruler a little lower on the page, so that it still covered the names, and so that the very bottom of the line for 7714 came into the light. There, at the end, in the last column, in red ink, there was one more thing written. A date. And a single word.
"The first Tuesday of November," said Niles. "Three weeks from today. At the third bell after noon." He took his finger away. "That is when the time runs out."
"And then?"
"And then," said Niles, "if the ticket has not been brought to my counter, and the twenty gold has not been paid, and the interest has not been paid, then the pledge is forfeit. And I may sell it." His bright eye did not move from her face. "After due notice. Which I will give. In the window. For seven days. As the sign says."
"But the interest has been paid," said Corin. "Every month. By your fishermen and your veiled women."
"The interest keeps the pledge alive," said Niles. "It does not change the term. Two years is two years. At the end of two years, the ticket must be brought, and the loan repaid, or the clock is mine to sell." He folded his clean hands on the counter. "Unless the ticket holder comes to my counter before the term ends and asks for more time. Which they may do. Once. If they bring the ticket."
"And who has the ticket?" said Lyra.
The clocks ticked. Now. Now. Now.
"Not I," said Niles. "I gave it to the man who pledged the clock. Two years ago. Number seven seven one four, printed on a little card, with my seal, and my signature, and the terms." He looked down at Bertram's note, lying on the counter on Lyra's side of the grille. "And he never brought it back."
Lyra looked down at the note too.
I could not pay to get it out again. Forgive me. Do not let her have it.
"So somewhere," she said slowly, "there's a little card. With seven seven one four on it. And whoever brings it here before the first Tuesday of November, with twenty gold, can take the clock away."
"Yes, miss."
"Anyone."
"Rule two," said Niles gently. "Whoever holds the ticket holds the pledge. I do not ask how they came by it."
Lyra closed her eyes.
And Corin, standing beside her at the counter in his gray coat, with the three stones warm at his hip and the rod cold along his ribs and the Ledger heavy over his heart, thought about a woman in white with a red mouth, sitting at a little white desk in a white room on Crown Walk, with a drawer that locked with a little silver key.
Do not let her have it.
"Mr. Niles," he said. "Has anybody else been here? Asking about the clock? Not to pay. To buy."
The pawnbroker turned his one bright eye on him. It rested on Corin's face for a long time.
"You ask very good questions," said Niles, "for a gray coat."
He did not answer it. Not yet. He slid down off his tall stool, and he was even smaller standing up than sitting down; the top of his shining brown head hardly came to Corin's shoulder. He lifted a hinged flap in the counter, at the end, beside the grille, and stood aside.
"Come," he said. "Look at the clock first. Then ask me again. It is easier to answer some questions in front of the thing they are about."
And from behind the curtain at the back of the shop, the deep slow sound came again. Patient. Far away. Knocking.
Thock.
The Back Room
The curtain at the back of the shop was heavy brown velvet, worn almost bald in the middle where hands had pushed it aside for fifty years. Niles held it back for them. Behind it there was a short dark passage, no longer than a man is tall, with a wooden floor that creaked, and the smell of oil and brass polish and old dust. And at the end of the passage, a door.
It was a strong door. Oak, banded with iron, with two locks, one above the other, and a heavy bar across it on brackets. Niles lifted the bar and leaned it against the wall. He took a ring of keys from his waistcoat pocket, a big iron ring with perhaps thirty keys on it, and found two without looking, by touch, and turned them in the two locks, the top one first, then the bottom.
"The good pledges," he said. "The ones worth stealing. I keep them back here." He pushed the door. It swung inward on oiled hinges without a sound. "Mind the step."
There was a step. Down. Into a room that was colder than the shop, and quieter, and lighter.
The light came from above. There was a window in the ceiling, a skylight, a big square of thick glass set into the roof, gray with grime on the outside and clean on the inside, and the afternoon came down through it in a single soft slant, pale and dusty, like light in a church. It fell on the floor in a square. And around the edges of the square, in the shadow, along all four walls, stood the good pledges.
They were bigger here. Furniture, mostly. A writing desk inlaid with patterns of different colored wood. A harpsichord with its lid shut. A great carved chest with iron corners. A rocking horse, dappled gray, with a real horsehair tail and red leather reins, so fine and so old that it might have been made for a prince. Paintings, leaning against the wall with their faces turned out, in heavy gold frames. A suit of armor, very small, a boy's armor, with its helmet tucked under its arm. A birdcage of gilded wire as tall as a man, empty.
Corin saw none of it, really. Not then. He saw only one thing.
It stood in the middle of the room, in the square of light from the skylight, by itself, the way a tree stands by itself in a field.
The clock.
It was tall. Taller than Corin by a head and more. It rose from the floor on four short carved feet, like the feet of a lion, into a long narrow body, and the body went up and up, and widened at the top into a hood, and the hood ended in a curved crown of carved wood, like the top of a church window, with a small brass rose standing at the very peak of it, open, with five petals.
The wood was walnut. Dark and rich and deep, with a grain in it that ran up the whole length of the case like smoke rising, like water running backward. It had been polished. Recently. Corin could smell the beeswax on it, under the dust of the room. Somebody had stood here, in the square of light, and rubbed this wood with a cloth, carefully, all over, the way you would brush a horse.
Bertram, Corin thought. For a year and a half. Once a month. For exactly as long as it took the big hand to go once round.
But the big hand was not going round.
All over the case, from the lion's feet to the brass rose at the top, there were carvings. Not big. Small and fine and close, so close that from a few steps away they looked like a pattern in the grain. Roses. Hundreds of little roses, open and half-open and in bud, and winding between them, round them, over and under them, a long branch covered in thorns. It went all the way up the clock, from the bottom to the top, the way ivy goes up a wall, and it never once broke. The same thorny branch, winding up and up, holding all the roses together.
"The house of Thorne," said Lyra softly.
She had come down the step and stopped just inside the room, in the shadow, at the edge of the square of light. She was looking up at the clock with her hands folded tightly in front of her.
"I used to sit in front of it," she said. "In the long gallery. On the floor, with my back against the window seat. When I was very small. Before I could read the time." She took a step closer. "I didn't know it was for telling the time. I thought it was for looking at. Like a picture. Like a fire."
She came forward into the light.
Halfway up the case there was a door. A tall narrow door with a pane of glass in it, so that you could see inside the long body of the clock. Behind the glass, in the dark, there hung the pendulum. A long brass rod, and at the bottom of it a round flat disc of brass as big as a dinner plate, polished bright. It was not swinging. It hung quite still, straight down, in the dark, like a held breath.
And above the door, at the top of the case, under the carved hood, there was the face.
Corin had read about it. Twelve stones in the face, one for each hour, and they glowed at night. Gold for a good day. Blue for a sad one. He had imagined it, he supposed, the way you imagine any clock. A white face with black numbers.
It was not like that.
The face was dark. A deep, dark blue, almost black, like the sky an hour after sunset, enameled and smooth and gleaming. And painted on the dark blue, very fine, in thin lines of silver, there were stars. Hundreds of tiny stars, and among them, here and there, larger ones, joined by faint silver lines into the shapes that sailors steer by. A night sky. A whole night sky, on the face of a clock.
And round the edge of it, where the numbers should have been, there was a ring of silver. And set into the ring, at the places where the hours should be, there were stones.
They were round, and smooth, and each as big as the end of Corin's thumb. Each one sat in its own little silver claw, like a jewel in a ring. And each one was a different color. Or had been. They were dull now, all of them, the way gemstones go dull when the light has gone out of them. But you could still see, deep down in each one, the ghost of the color it had been. Rose-red at one o'clock. Amber at two. A green like new leaves at three. A gray-blue like rain at four. Deep violet. Pale gold. A soft milky white. A dark sea-green. Copper. Silver-gray. A blue like the inside of a flame, at eleven.
And at twelve, at the very top of the ring, where the most important stone should have been, there was no stone at all.
Only a little empty claw of silver, open, holding nothing. Like a hand held out for something that had never been put into it.
Corin looked at the empty claw for a long time.
Eleven present, said the line in the pawn book. One setting empty.
"The twelfth is gone," said Lyra. Her voice was strange. "It used to be there. I remember it. It was the brightest of all. It was white. White like—" She stopped. "I don't know what it was white like. I just remember it was white, and it was the last one to go out at night, and the first one to light in the morning."
"When did it go?" said Corin.
"I don't know." She looked at the empty claw. "I was small. One day it was there. And then I was older, and I looked up, and it wasn't, and I didn't think about it. You don't think about things like that when you're a child. You just think the world changed while you weren't looking."
Between the stones, in the middle of the starry dark, there were the hands.
They were silver too. Long and thin and very fine, with points like needles and, near the middle of each one, a little silver rose. The hour hand was short. The minute hand was long. And they were both quite still.
The short hand was pointing just past the gray-blue stone at four o'clock. Not quite halfway to five.
The long hand was pointing at a place just short of the sixth hour. Not on a stone. Between the stones. In the dark, among the painted stars.
Corin looked at them, and something in the back of his mind stirred, very faintly, like a fish turning over in deep water. Then it was gone.
And low down on the starry face, between the hands' center and the bottom of the ring, there was a second, smaller circle. A little dial, no bigger than a coin, with its own tiny silver hand, and around it, very small, painted in silver, the numbers one to seven.
"The days of the week," said Lyra. "Grandfather put it in so that we'd know which day it was without having to ask. Father said it was the only useful thing on the whole clock." She almost smiled. "One for Monday. Seven for Sunday."
The tiny hand on the little dial was pointing at three.
Thock.
Corin flinched. They both did. The sound was so close, so loud in the quiet cold room, that it was like somebody knocking on the inside of his own chest.
It had come from the clock.
He looked at the glass door. At the pendulum, hanging still in the dark. And as he looked, it moved. Just once. Just a little. The bright brass disc at the bottom of it gave a small sudden jerk, sideways, as if something had pushed it from inside, and swung out an inch, and knocked against the side of the case.
Thock.
And swung back, and hung still again.
"It does that," said Niles.
He had come down the step and was standing at the edge of the square of light, by the rocking horse, with his clean hands folded in front of his brown waistcoat. His one bright eye was on the clock.
"Once a minute," he said. "Near enough. Day and night. It has done it since the day it was carried into this room. It will not go. I have looked inside it with my glass, and the movement is perfect, and it is wound, and there is nothing stopping it. And yet it will not go." He looked at the pendulum. "It only does that. Knocks. Once. As if it were trying to start. And then it gives up, and waits, and tries again."
Thock.
"Bertram used to stand there," said Niles. He nodded at the square of light in front of the clock. "Exactly there. With his hat in his hands. And he would wait for the knock. And when it came, every time, he would close his eyes." The pawnbroker was quiet for a moment. "I asked him once what it was. The knocking. And he said: It's waiting, Mr. Niles. And I said, Waiting for what? And he said: For someone to tell it what time it is."
Nobody said anything.
In the cold square of light from the skylight, the great dark clock stood with its eleven dull stones and its one empty claw and its two silver hands pointing at nothing, among the painted stars.
And inside it, in the dark, behind the glass, the brass pendulum hung quite still.
Waiting.
