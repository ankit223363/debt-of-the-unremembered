Chapter 13: The Clock That Ran Backward, Part 4 of 5: Telling the Time
Telling the Time
The minutes went very slowly, and then all at once.
Niles stood by the carved chest with the silver watch open in his clean palm and the lamp beside him, and every minute he said the time, quietly, like a man reading names off a list. Twenty-one. Twenty-two. Twenty-three. Between the names, nobody spoke. The clocks on the other side of the wall ticked very faintly through the brick. The skylight overhead went a shade darker, the soft heavy yellow-gray of the snow sky thickening. And every minute, or nearly, the clock knocked.
Thock.
Lyra stood in front of it. She had taken off her gloves. Her hands hung at her sides, red and raw from the cold and from the roofs, with a long scratch across the back of the left one where a slate had caught it. She did not seem to know the scratch was there. She was looking at the glass door. At the brass pendulum behind it, hanging still. At the place on the glass, at the height of her hand, where Bertram's hand had rested every month, and polished it, so that it was clearer than the rest.
Twenty-five, said Niles.
Corin stood a little behind her and to the left. Two steps behind. He did not decide to. His feet put him there. He had the rod in his bare right hand, pointing down, so cold that his fingers had gone white and stiff around it.
Twenty-six.
"Corin," said Lyra. Without turning round. "If it doesn't work—"
"Then we come back next Wednesday."
"And if it does—"
She did not finish.
Twenty-seven.
Thock.
She flinched.
Twenty-eight, said Niles. And then, after a moment, very quietly: "Get ready, miss."
Lyra lifted her right hand.
She held it in front of the glass, an inch away, the fingers loosely curled, the way a person holds a hand in front of a door before knocking. It was shaking. She looked at it shaking. Then she closed her eyes, and took a breath, and opened them again, and the hand was still.
The pawnbroker's eye was on his watch. On the thin silver second hand, going round.
Corin watched it too, over the small man's shoulder. Round. And round. Past the bottom. Up the side. Toward the top.
"Now," said Niles.
Lyra tapped on the glass.
Four times.
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
A pause. The length of a breath.
Twice.
Tap. Tap.
A pause.
Nine times. Faster. The way her father did it, his hand on the wall, the nine coming quick and close together like rain on a window.
Tap-tap-tap-tap-tap-tap-tap-tap-tap.
A pause.
Three.
Tap. Tap. Tap.
She took her hand away.
Nothing happened.
The pendulum hung still. The silver hands pointed at twenty-nine minutes past four. The eleven stones were dull. The clocks on the other side of the wall went now, now, now. The skylight overhead was gray and heavy and silent.
Nothing.
Corin felt his heart go down in his chest like a stone going down in a well.
Lyra stood with her hand at her side and looked at the glass, and her face did not change, and her shoulders did not move, and Corin knew, standing behind her, that she was going to break. Not cry. Break. The way a cup breaks when it has been held too tightly for too long.
And then, from inside the clock, there was a sound.
Not a knock.
A click.
Small. Sharp. Metal on metal. The sound of a key turning in a lock, a long way down. The sound of a catch being lifted.
And behind the glass door, the brass pendulum moved.
It did not jerk sideways and knock and stop, the way it had for six months. It swung. Slowly. A long, smooth, heavy swing, out to the left, and hung there for a moment at the top, as if it were surprised to find itself so high, and came back down, and swung out to the right.
And from inside the long dark case, deep and slow and steady, there came the sound of a clock that has been asleep for two years waking up.
Tock.
Tick.
Tock.
Lyra put both hands over her mouth.
The hands were moving.
The long silver minute hand, with its little silver rose near the middle. It moved off the place where it had pointed for two years, among the painted stars, just short of the sixth hour. It moved one small mark.
Backward.
Not forward. Back. Toward the fifth hour. Toward the gray-blue stone at four.
Tick. Tock. Another mark. Back.
"It's going backward," whispered Niles. His one eye was enormous. "The movement is perfect. I have looked at every wheel. There is no—it cannot—" He stopped. "It is going backward."
It was. And it was going faster.
The minute hand swept back past the fifth hour. Past the fourth. The short hand began to move too, slowly, the way the short hand of a clock moves, back from the gray-blue stone toward the green at three. The pendulum swung, tick, tock, tick, tock, faster now, the beats coming closer together, like the steps of somebody walking and then running.
And the stones began to light.
The gray-blue stone at four went first. As the short hand left it, going backward, a light came up inside it, deep down, soft and slow, the way a coal glows when you blow on it. Gray-blue, like rain. Like the sky over the sea on a morning when it is going to be wet. It filled the stone and glowed out of it into the starry dark of the face, and the little painted stars nearest to it caught its light and shone.
Then the green at three. As the short hand reached it, going backward. A green like new leaves with the sun behind them. It came up out of the dull stone like spring coming up out of the ground.
Then the amber at two. Warm, and gold, and brown, like honey held up to a candle.
Then the rose-red at one.
The minute hand was going so fast now that it was a blur. A thin silver blur, sweeping round and round the face backward, and the short hand following it, steady, back and back and back, and every stone it passed lit as it passed, one after another, around the ring. Rose-red. And past twelve, past the empty claw, without stopping, round to the other side. The blue like the inside of a flame at eleven. The silver-gray at ten. Copper at nine. The dark sea-green at eight. The milky white at seven. Pale gold at six. Deep violet at five.
And gray-blue at four, again. Brighter now. And the green. And the amber. And the rose. Round and round. Back and back and back.
The little dial below was turning too. Its tiny hand went from three to two to one to seven to six. Days. Going backward. Faster and faster. A week went by in a breath. Then another. Then so many that the tiny hand was only a silver flicker on the little painted dial.
The light in the back room changed.
It came out of the clock's face and filled the cold dim room under the skylight like water filling a bowl. All the colors of the eleven stones, mixed together, moving, turning as the hands turned. Rose and amber and green and blue, gold and violet and white, sliding over the walls, over the gilded birdcage and the boy's suit of armor and the rocking horse with its real horsehair tail, over the painted faces in their gold frames, over Niles's bald brown head and his one wide eye, over Lyra's white face and her hands over her mouth. Round and round. As if the whole room were inside a turning lamp.
And the sound. The pendulum's tick-tock-tick-tock had become a hum. A deep, steady, rising hum, like the hum of a great hive of bees, like the hum of the gemstone lamps in the Bank's long hall, like the hum of a finger run round and round the rim of a glass. It filled the room. It went into the floor and up through Corin's boots and into his bones. It went into the rod in his bare hand and the rod went so cold that it burned.
Steady, said Malakor. His voice was high and thin and strained, the voice of somebody shouting across a wide river in a wind. Steady, boy. Steady. It is nearly there. It is going all the way back. All the way back to the beginning. Hold on.
Back to what?
To the last time all twelve were lit, said the rod.
The hands were going so fast now that Corin could not see them at all. Only the light. The light of the stones, turning, round and round the starry face, faster and faster, until the eleven colors ran together into a single ring of fire, a wheel of every color there was, spinning in the dark at the top of the clock. And in the middle of the wheel, at the top, at twelve, where the most important stone should have been, there was only the empty silver claw. Dark. Holding nothing. The only dark thing in the whole blazing ring.
And then the hands stopped.
All at once. With a sound like a door slamming.
The hum cut off. The pendulum stopped dead at the bottom of its swing, as if a hand had caught it. The wheel of light broke apart into eleven stones again, eleven separate colors in their silver claws, all glowing, all lit, steady and soft and bright.
The two silver hands were pointing straight up.
Together. One on top of the other. At twelve. At the empty claw.
And the tiny hand on the little dial below was pointing at seven.
Nobody breathed.
And from somewhere inside the clock, high up, behind the face, under the carved hood, a bell rang.
One stroke. Clear and high and sweet. Not like the iron bells of the city. Like a glass bell. Like a small silver bell rung once in a quiet house to call someone in from the garden.
It went on and on, that one note, ringing in the cold room, fading and fading, until Corin could not tell anymore whether he was hearing it or only remembering it.
And as it faded, the empty silver claw at twelve o'clock moved.
It turned. Slowly. On a tiny pin, like a key in a lock. A quarter turn. A half. And under it, in the deep blue enamel of the face, a seam appeared. A thin bright line, in the shape of a small square, no bigger than the end of a thumb. And the square slid outward, very slowly, out of the face of the clock, on silver runners, like a drawer.
A tiny drawer. Silver. Hidden for who knew how long under the empty claw.
And on the front of it, very small, engraved in the silver, there were letters.
Not letters Corin knew. Not the scratches of Marrowmere, either, the little lines and curves on the backs of masks and cups. These were different. Square and hard and angular, all straight lines and sharp corners, like letters cut with an axe. Four of them. Or five. He could not tell where one ended and the next began.
Niles drew in his breath.
"Karst," he said.
Lyra turned her head.
"What?"
"That is Karst writing. From the east. From over the sea." The pawnbroker's voice was very low. "I have seen it twice in fifty years. Once on a sailor's knife. Once on a coin that a man brought in to pledge and would not say where he had got." He looked at the tiny silver drawer. "Nobody in this city can read it, miss. Nobody in this country. Not for a hundred and fifty years. It is the writing of the people who came over the sea in the old war and forgot why they had come."
Corin did not know what that meant. He did not remember any old war. But at his ribs, Malakor had gone very, very still.
Lyra put out her hand.
She did not touch the letters. She put two fingers on the edge of the tiny drawer, very gently, and drew it out the rest of the way.
Inside, on a bed of faded blue velvet, there was a stone.
It was small. Round. Smooth. About the size of the others in the ring, the end of a thumb. It hung on a short broken length of fine gold chain, as if it had once been worn round somebody's neck and the chain had been snapped.
And it was blue. A deep clear blue, like the sky at the very top on a winter morning. And it was whole. Not a crack in it. Not a flaw. It lay in the bottom of the little silver drawer and caught the light of the eleven glowing stones around it and gave it back, so bright and so clear that it seemed to have its own light deep inside.
Lyra stared at it.
"Father has one," she said.
Her voice was hardly there.
"Just like this. On a chain. In a little box by his bed. Mrs. Hobb dusts it." She swallowed. "But his is cracked. Straight across. Right through the middle. He's had it since before I was born. I asked him once where he got it and he said his father gave it to him, for luck, when he was a boy, and made him promise to wear it always. And I asked why it was cracked, and he went white, and he wouldn't say." She looked at the whole blue stone in the little drawer. "He never wore it after. Not once. He just kept it in the box."
Edmund made two, said Malakor. Very softly. One for his son to wear. And one to keep. In a drawer, under the twelfth stone, that only opens when the clock is told the time.
Why?
I do not know, said the rod. But I know what a man's face looks like when he puts something away for his child that he hopes his child will never need.
And then the glass door of the clock swung open.
Nobody had touched it. It simply opened. On its own. Slowly. Outward, on its brass hinges, with a long soft creak, the way a door opens in an empty house when the wind gets in. And out of the long dark body of the clock, out of the space behind the still brass pendulum, something came.
Not light. Not smoke. Something between the two. Pale, and gray, and faintly shining, like the breath that comes out of a horse's nostrils on a winter morning. It came out of the case in a long slow coil, and hung in the air in front of the open door, turning, unfolding, uncurling, getting bigger. Fold after fold. The way a letter that has been folded a hundred times grows and grows when you open it, until it is far bigger than you thought it could ever have been.
And it smelled of pipe tobacco, and saddle soap, and beeswax.
Now, said Malakor. Now, boy. Before it goes.
And Lyra, standing right in front of it, with her bare hand still on the edge of the little silver drawer, reached out toward the pale shining coil.
"Bertram," she said.
Bertram's Ash
Corin moved before he thought.
He stepped past Lyra, between her and the open door of the clock, so close that his shoulder knocked hers and she stumbled back a step. Her hand, reaching for the pale shining coil, closed on the gray wool of his sleeve instead.
"Don't touch it," he said. "Lyra. Don't."
"It's him—"
"I know. That's why."
He did not know how he knew. Malakor had told him. Do not let the girl touch the Ash. But it was not only that. His body knew it, the way it knew the rod and the gloves and the way a harvest went. Ash was not a thing you held. Ash was a thing that held you. And if it got into someone who had not been trained to carry it, someone with no rod and no jar and no Ledger to read in the morning, it would not let go.
The coil hung in front of him in the strange still light. Pale and gray and faintly shining. Still unfolding. Fold after fold after fold, opening out into the cold air of the back room, getting bigger, and thinner, and brighter, the way a breath gets thinner as it goes out into a winter morning.
And it was beginning to drift.
Not toward him. Not toward anything. Upward. Toward the skylight. Toward the gray glass and the heavy snow sky beyond it. The way smoke drifts up a chimney. The way Ash goes, Corin knew, when there is nobody there to catch it. Up, and out, and thinner, and thinner, until it is gone.
Now, said Malakor. Now, boy. Or you will lose him.
Corin lifted the rod.
He held it up in his bare right hand, the carved spiral tip pointing at the drifting coil, and he touched the tip to the edge of it. Just touched it. The way you touch the edge of a cobweb.
The Ash stopped drifting.
It hung there for a moment, very still, as if it were thinking. As if it were looking at the silver tip. As if, somewhere in all those pale unfolding folds, something was deciding.
Then it came down.
It came down the rod like water coming down a rope. It wound round the tip, following the carved lines of the spiral, round and round, to the right, the way the lines turned, the way a clock turns, so that it was always going forward. Corin felt it come. Cold. Colder than the rod. Colder than the frost on Tallow Street that morning. It came down the silver and into his hand and up his arm, and his arm went numb from the fingers to the shoulder, and the cold went into his chest and wrapped itself round his heart like a hand.
And it smelled of pipe tobacco.
He lifted the rod to his lips.
Somewhere behind him, a long way off, he heard Lyra say his name. He heard Niles say something sharp. He did not hear what. He closed his eyes.
Breathe, said Malakor. Very gentle. Breathe, scavenger. He has waited a long time.
Corin breathed in.
He was old.
That was the first thing. Not old the way a word is old. Old in the body. Old in the knees and the back and the hands. Old in the way the cold got into his fingers and made them stiff, and the stairs got longer every year, and the light at the end of the day went sooner than it used to. He was sixty-six years old, and he had been the steward of the house of Thorne for twenty-nine years, and his knees hurt.
He was standing in the long gallery.
It was night. Or very nearly morning. The tall windows along one side of the gallery were black, with a gray line at the bottom of them, very faint, where the dawn was thinking about coming. The fire in the grate at the end had gone out hours ago. It was cold. He could see his breath.
And the gallery was full of pictures. That was the strange thing. He knew it, in the part of him that was still Corin, a long way down, like a man at the bottom of a well. The gallery was full of pictures, in gold frames, all along the paneled wall. Faces. Horses. A lake at sunset. Lord Edmund, very stern, with a clock in his hand. Old Lady Agatha, sterner. And Her Ladyship. In her blue dress. With her hoop in her lap and the curl by her left ear and the silver mask on her collar, looking out over the gallery with her gray eyes as if she were listening to something a long way off.
He did not look at Her Ladyship's portrait. He could not. He had not been able to look at it for six years.
He was looking at His Lordship.
Lord Aurelian Thorne was standing in front of the clock.
He was in his shirtsleeves. That was the first thing Bertram saw. His Lordship, who had never once in twenty-nine years been seen outside his own dressing room without a coat. In his shirtsleeves, with the cuffs undone and pushed up past the elbow, and his collar open, and his hair, which was dark and going gray at the sides, standing up on his head as if he had been pushing his hands through it all night. His face was white. Not pale. White, like paper. And his eyes, behind his spectacles, were red.
There was ash on his hands. Paper ash. Black and gray. From the study, Bertram knew. He had been in the study all night with the door locked, burning papers in the grate, one at a time. Bertram had heard him through the door. And he had heard Miss Lyra, at midnight, knocking and knocking, and the door opening, and His Lordship's voice, raised, for the first time in all the years that Bertram had served him. You will never burn anything for me. And then the door closing, and Miss Lyra's feet on the stairs, running, and the sound of her crying in her room.
And now it was nearly dawn, and His Lordship was standing in front of the clock in the long gallery in his shirtsleeves, with ash on his hands.
"Bertram," he said.
"My lord."
"Come here. Stand where I can see you."
Bertram came. His knees hurt. He stood beside His Lordship in front of the tall dark clock, with its carved roses and its thorns going up and up, and its starry face, and its twelve stones. Eleven glowing, soft and steady, gold and green and rose and blue, the way they glowed every night. And at the top, at twelve, the empty silver claw. It had been empty for many years. Since before Miss Lyra could walk. Bertram had never asked why. A steward does not ask why.
The silver hands said twenty-eight minutes past four.
"They're coming in the morning," said His Lordship. "A carriage. From the Ministry. With a Harvester. And a guest." His voice was quite steady. Quite quiet. "They will want to come in. You will let them."
"My lord—"
"You will let them in, Bertram. You will not stand in the door. You will not argue. You will open it and bow and show them up." His Lordship did not look away from the clock. "And before they come, you will send Captain Vance away. On an errand. Any errand. To fetch Doctor Aske, if you like. Tell him Doctor Aske is wanted urgently."
"Doctor Aske is—"
"I know where Doctor Aske is." His Lordship's voice cracked, just for a moment. "I know. Nine days. Under the steps by the Lantern Bridge. I know." He took a breath. "Send Vance for him anyway. It will take him half the morning to find out. And by then it will be over. And he will be alive." He closed his eyes. "If he is here when they come, he will stand in the door. You know he will. He will not let them past. And they will not leave him standing."
Bertram said nothing. His throat had closed.
"And the clock," said His Lordship.
He opened his eyes and looked at it. At the eleven glowing stones. At the empty claw.
"When I am—after—they will sell the house. Everything in it. You know they will. The Bank will have the lot." He put out one ash-black hand and laid it flat on the walnut case, on the carved roses. "Not this. You will take this out of the house before the auction men come. However you can. And you will keep it safe. And you will tell nobody where it is. Not her. Not anybody." A pause. "Not even Lyra. Not until she comes looking. If she comes looking, she will be ready. If she does not, it will be better that she never knew."
"My lord," said Bertram. "I don't understand."
"No. You are not meant to." His Lordship almost smiled. "You are meant to remember. That is all. Remember this."
He lifted his hand from the case. He reached up, to the face of the clock, to the long silver minute hand with its little silver rose. And as the hand moved, by itself, ticking, from twenty-eight minutes to twenty-nine, His Lordship laid one finger on it.
And held it.
The clock stopped.
The pendulum stopped dead at the bottom of its swing. The ticking cut off. The silence in the long gallery was so sudden and so deep that Bertram heard his own heart.
And one by one, the eleven stones went out.
Not all at once. Slowly. One after another, round the ring, like lamps being put out in a house at night. Rose. Amber. Green. Gray-blue. Violet. Gold. White. Sea-green. Copper. Silver-gray. And last of all, the blue like the inside of a flame at eleven. It flickered. It went dim. It went dark.
And the clock stood in the cold gray almost-dawn with its silver hands frozen at twenty-nine minutes past four, and its little dial pointing at three, and every stone dull and dead.
"Four," said His Lordship softly. "Two. Nine. Three."
He took his finger away from the hand.
"Say it back to me, Bertram."
"Four," said Bertram. His voice was not steady. "Two. Nine. Three."
"Again."
"Four. Two. Nine. Three."
"Good." His Lordship turned and looked at him, for the first time. His eyes behind the spectacles were red and wet and very, very tired. "When I can't say it anymore, I'll still be able to tap it. I've made sure of that. It's the one thing I'm keeping. The one thing I'm folding away where they won't find it." He touched his own left wrist, where the shirtsleeve was pushed back. There was something written there. Four small letters. Bertram had seen them before, every morning for twenty years, when he laid out His Lordship's cuffs, and never once asked what they meant. FOLD. "I'll tap it. On the walls. For as long as there's anything left of me. And one day she'll hear. And she'll come to you. And you'll bring her to the clock."
"And then?"
"And then she tells it the time," said His Lordship. "And it tells her the rest."
He turned away from the clock.
He walked down the long gallery, past the faces in their gold frames, past Lord Edmund and old Lady Agatha, and he stopped in front of Her Ladyship's portrait. In the blue dress. With the silver mask on her collar. He stood and looked at it for a long time. Bertram, at the far end of the gallery, by the clock, could not see his face. Only his back. In his shirtsleeves. With his shoulders shaking.
Then His Lordship put his hand into his shirt, at the neck, and drew out a chain. A thin gold chain. And on it, a small round stone. Blue.
Cracked straight across the middle.
He held it in his ash-black hand and looked at it.
"I'm sorry," he said. To the portrait. Very quietly. "I couldn't keep them all. I couldn't keep the little one. I'm so sorry."
And then he closed his hand on the cracked blue stone, and closed his eyes, and burned.
Bertram had seen people burn before. Everyone had. A little flash, a little light, a girl in the market burning the memory of a song to make a stall of apples glow so that people would stop. A soldier burning his mother's face to turn a sword. A flicker. A spark.
This was not a spark.
The light came up out of His Lordship like the sun coming up out of the sea. White. Pure white. So white that it had no color in it at all. It filled the long gallery from end to end. It went into every gold frame and every polished floorboard and every pane of the tall black windows, and turned them all to white, and the white went on and on and on, and there was a sound, high and thin, like a finger run round the rim of a glass, getting higher and higher and higher, and the windows rattled in their frames, and Bertram put his arm over his eyes and still saw it, through his arm, through his eyelids, through his bones.
And then it was gone.
And His Lordship was standing in front of Her Ladyship's portrait in his shirtsleeves, with his hand open, and the cracked blue stone lying in his palm.
He turned round.
He looked at Bertram, at the far end of the gallery, beside the clock. He looked at him for a long time, with his head a little on one side, pleasant and polite and puzzled, the way you look at a man you might have met once at a party and cannot quite place.
Then he smiled.
"Good morning," said Lord Aurelian Thorne. "What a lovely clock."
And outside the tall windows, in the gray dawn, the first swallow of the morning went past, very fast and very close, and His Lordship turned his head to watch it, and his face lit up like a child's.
"Bright," he said.
Corin was on his knees.
He did not know how he had got there. The floor of the back room was hard and cold under him, and his hands were flat on it, and the rod was lying on the boards beside his right hand, and he was breathing in great ragged gulps, the way a man breathes who has been held underwater. His face was wet. He did not know when he had started crying. He did not know if it was him crying, or Bertram.
Somebody was holding his shoulders. Small hard hands. Lyra's.
"Corin. Corin. Look at me. What did you see? What did you see?"
He looked up at her. At her white face in the light of the eleven glowing stones. At her gray eyes. Her mother's eyes. Her father's.
"Your father," he said. His voice came out cracked and strange. Not quite his own. Older. With a stiffness in it, like knees that hurt. "The night he burned. He stopped the clock. With one finger. At twenty-nine past four. And then he said he was sorry. To your mother's picture." He swallowed. "He said, I couldn't keep them all. I couldn't keep the little one."
Lyra's hands went still on his shoulders.
"And then he burned," said Corin. "Everything. Except four numbers. And he turned round and saw Bertram and smiled and said, What a lovely clock."
Nobody said anything.
And then the cold came back into his chest, all at once, so hard that he gasped and doubled over on the floor. Because it was not finished. The Ash was not finished with him. There was more of it, still inside him, still unfolding, still cold, still going round and round in his chest like the grooves of a spiral.
There is more, said Malakor. Hold on, boy. He folded one more thing. Right in the middle. Right at the heart of it. The thing he most wanted you to see.
What?
The last thing, said the rod. Hold on.
