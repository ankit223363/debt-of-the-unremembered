Chapter 13: The Clock That Ran Backward, Part 1 of 5: Wednesday
Debt of the Unremembered · Arc 1
Wednesday
The first thing he read was a number.
It was written on a paper pinned to the very top of the board above his bed, in big black letters, above BOOTS FIRST, above READ THE BOOK BEFORE YOU DO ANYTHING ELSE, above ASK MALAKOR, which had a line through it now and a word written small beside it: asked.
4:29. TODAY. WEDNESDAY.
And under it:
FIND THE TICKET BEFORE THEN.
Corin lay on his back in the gray light with the book on his chest and looked at the number for a long time. Four twenty-nine. It meant nothing to him. A time. Any time. A time when somebody might boil an egg, or catch a tram, or go to sleep.
But his heart had started to beat faster as soon as he saw it. Before he knew his own name. Before he knew anything. His body knew the number, the way a dog knows the sound of its master's step on the stair.
He read the book.
It took a long time. It always did. But this morning he went faster than he should have, he thought, skimming, his eyes jumping down the pages, until he came to the end, to the last crowded pages, and made himself slow down. A pawnbroker with one eye and a brass ball where the other should be. A clock with stars on its face and eleven stones and one empty silver claw. A knock, once a minute, from inside. An old man's Ash, folded small inside the case, trying to get out. Two hands pointing at twenty-nine minutes past four, and a tiny dial pointing at three. A lord at a window, tapping on a wall.
LORD THORNE IS TELLING US THE TIME.
And at twenty-nine minutes past four tomorrow, said a line he had written last of all, very small, at the bottom, it will be exactly the time on that clock. For one minute. Malakor wonders who else knows that.
Corin closed the book.
He lay still for a moment longer. Outside the small high window the sky was a hard pale gray, the color of a knife blade, and the light coming down through it had no warmth in it at all. He could see his breath. The little brass lamp on the crate beside the bed had gone out in the night. The cold had got into the room, and into the blanket, and into the stiff new leather of the book under his hand.
He got up. Boots first. Then the rest. Then the gray coat, with its weight in its different places. The rod. The warm square of stones at the hip. The book over the heart. And under the left arm, the little secret pocket with its folded list and its brass button.
Good morning, said Malakor.
"Do you know who else knows?" said Corin.
The rod was silent for a moment.
You have read the last line, it said. Good. That is the right question for today. A pause. No. I do not know who else knows. But I know who would want to. And I know that she has a very good clock of her own, in a white room on the Hill, and a great deal of patience, and a man with large hands to drive her carriage.
Corin went down the narrow crooked stairs.
In the dark hall at the bottom, by the foot of the stairs, there was a paper waiting for him again, pushed under the old chalk words on the wall, VANE, TOP. Cheap cream paper that smelled of horses. Lyra's hand.
Corin —
I tapped back last night. Four, two, nine, three, on the wall beside his chair, after Mrs. Hobb had gone to bed. He stopped. He turned his head. He looked at the wall where my hand was for a long time. Then he tapped it again, slower. And I tapped it again. We did it eleven times. And then he put his hand flat on the wall, over mine, through the plaster, the way you put your hand on a window when someone is on the other side.
He didn't say anything. But he smiled. Not at the swallows. At the wall.
I will be at the top of Tanner's Rise at the second bell after noon. Wear your gray. Bring your rod. I don't know why I'm writing that, except that I have a feeling we will need it.
— L.
Corin read it twice. Then he folded it small and put it inside the back cover of the Ledger, with the others, and went out.
The street was white.
Not snow. Not yet. Frost. A thin hard frost had come down in the night while the city slept, and laid itself over everything in a fine white fur, every cobble and every railing and every windowsill and every step. The puddles in the gutters of Tallow Street had skins of ice on them, gray and cloudy, with dead leaves frozen inside like flies in amber. The chimney smoke went straight up into the knife-colored sky in long white columns that did not move. A man was scattering ash from a bucket on the step of the chandler's on the corner, so that his customers would not slip, and the ash lay gray on the white frost like a stain.
Corin's boots crunched on the cobbles as he walked. His breath went up in front of him in clouds.
Cold, said Malakor. Early, for the time of year. A pause. The first snow will not be long now.
Corin did not answer. But he thought of a white room on the Hill, and a red mouth smiling over a little glowing cup, and a voice saying: Before the first snow, shall we say? There's no hurry. Snow is weeks away.
He walked faster.
The line outside the Bank on Lantern Quay was shorter than yesterday. The cold had kept people at home, or the cold had made them think that an afternoon was worth more on a morning like this, with the frost on the river and the wind coming off the water like a blade. They stood in a thin gray string along the quay with their shoulders hunched up round their ears and their hands in their armpits, stamping their feet. They did not look at Corin's coat as he passed. They were too cold to look at anything.
Warden Briggs was at his ledger stand by the slag-room stairs. He had a scarf wound round his neck, gray wool, very old, with a darn in it. He looked up.
"Vane."
"Warden."
"Family business." It was not a question.
"Yes."
Briggs grunted. He did not say four glasses. He did not say anything for a while. He looked down at his ledger, and turned a page, and then turned it back again.
"That docker," he said. Without looking up. "Yesterday. That you asked about."
Corin waited.
"Don't ask about him again," said Briggs. His voice was very low and very flat. "Not to me. Not to anybody in this hall. You hear me, Vane?"
"I hear you."
"Good." Briggs picked up his pen. His hand was not quite steady. "Four glasses. Second bell. Go on."
Corin went down the stairs.
He did not remember yesterday. He did not remember the docker. He knew only what the Ledger told him. A young man with a hook. A soft empty smile. Every Tuesday. Eight years. Do you know his name? No. Too fast. And now don't ask about him again.
He wrote it down, in the cold blue light of the slag room, before he started on the first glass. Briggs: "Don't ask about him again. Not to me. Not to anybody." His hand shook. And under it he wrote, small: He knows. He knows exactly who it is.
The glasses were slow that morning. The cold had got into them, the way it had got into everything, and the cloud in them was thick and stiff, like breath frozen on a winter window. The rod drew it out a thread at a time. The scraps that came were cold too. Snow on a roof. Ice on a pond, and somebody's laugh across it. A sled. A red scarf. A dog barking at falling flakes. Winter scraps. As if the glass had chosen them to match the day.
Corin worked with his eyes on the glass and his mind on a clock.
Four twenty-nine.
He cleared the first glass. The bells across the city rang the tenth hour.
He cleared the second. The bells rang the eleventh.
He was halfway through the third when the rod went still in his hand.
Not cold. Not the listening cold of a harvest. Just still. As if it were holding its breath.
Malakor?
Something, said the rod. Very quiet. Upstairs. Do you feel it?
Corin stood in the blue gloom with the silver tip of the rod against the clouded glass and listened.
And from far above, through the brick ceiling, through the floor of the long bright hall, through all the murmuring and clinking and stamping of the morning, there came a sound he did not expect.
Silence.
Not the whole hall. Just a part of it. The way, in a forest full of birdsong, one patch of trees goes quiet when a hawk flies over. Somewhere above him, near the great doors, people had stopped talking.
Then footsteps. Light. Quick. Crossing the white tiles. Coming toward the slag-room stairs.
And a smell came down the stairs ahead of them. Very faint. So faint that he would not have noticed it on any other morning. Cold, and clean, and sweet.
Not lilies.
Something else.
Corin took the rod away from the glass. He turned toward the stairs.
A man came down them. Not Briggs. A young man, slight and neat, in a long gray coat so well cut that it fitted him like his own skin, with a high collar turned up against the cold and a dark scarf, and brown hair, and a pale serious face, and very dark eyes. He came down the last step into the cold blue light of the slag room and stopped, and looked round at the racks of clouded glass, slowly, the way he had looked round the long white and gold drawing room on Crown Walk two nights ago.
Counting.
Then his dark eyes came to Corin, standing by the third rack with the silver rod in his gray-gloved hand.
"Mr. Vane," said Remy Castellan. "Good morning. I am so sorry to interrupt your work."
His voice was quiet and polite and very clear, with the faint careful edges of a boy who has been taught to speak properly by somebody who was not born speaking that way either. He did not come any closer. He stood at the foot of the stairs with his gloved hands folded in front of him.
"Lady Vivienne," he said, "asked me to deliver a message. In person. She said it would be rude to send a card to a man who might not remember receiving it." His face did not change. "She said: Thursday. The Bourse. The kitchen door. Bring your gloves. She said you would know what it meant, because somebody would already have told you." A pause, very slight. "And she said one other thing. She said to tell you that she, too, keeps a very good clock. And that hers says it is a quarter past eleven on a Wednesday morning." The dark eyes did not blink. "And that a man with an errand at half past four in the afternoon would be wise not to be late for it."
Corin did not move.
The young man in gray looked at him for a long moment more, there in the blue light among the clouded glass.
Then he bowed. A short, neat, polite bow.
"Good morning, Mr. Vane," he said. And he turned and went back up the stairs, light and quick, and his footsteps crossed the white tiles above, and the patch of silence in the hall filled up again behind him with murmuring and clinking and the stamp of cold feet.
Corin stood alone in the slag room.
Well, said Malakor, after a long time. Very softly. Now we know who else knows.
Two people, said Corin.
At least two, said the rod. Your lady's aunt, who wants the clock. And a woman with rings on every finger, who wants you to know that she knows.
Why?
Because she wants something from you, said Malakor. On Thursday. And she wants you to be alive on Thursday to give it to her. A pause. That boy was not a threat, Corin. He was a warning. Somebody has just told you to run.
Corin looked down at the third glass, half cleared, the cloud in it torn open like a curtain.
Then he put the rod back in his pocket, and went up the stairs, two at a time, past Briggs at his ledger stand, who looked up with his small tired eyes and opened his mouth and then, seeing Corin's face, shut it again.
"Two and a half," said Corin, as he passed. "I'll make it up."
He did not wait for an answer.
Outside, on the white steps, in the cold, the frost had begun to melt where the low sun touched it, and the steps shone wet and black in the light. But in the shadows, where the sun did not reach, it lay as thick and white as ever. And the sky over the river, far off to the east, beyond the factory chimneys of the far bank, had gone a strange soft yellow-gray color, heavy and low, like the inside of a pillow.
Corin had never seen a sky that color. Or he had, and did not remember.
But his body knew it. His body went cold at the sight of it, all the way down.
Snow sky, said Malakor quietly. By tonight, I would think.
Corin pulled his collar up and went up the Salt Stairs at a run.
Tanner's Rise
Lyra was already there.
She was standing at the top of Tanner's Rise, where the steep little street came up out of the Docks and met the first crooked houses of the Ink Quarter, with her back against a wall and her arms folded and her old brown coat buttoned to the throat. It was not yet noon. The bells across the city had only just begun to ring it, the slow iron voices calling to each other through the cold, one after another, never quite together.
She saw him coming up the hill and pushed herself off the wall.
"You're early," she said.
"So are you."
"I couldn't sit still." Her breath went up in a white cloud. Her cheeks were red with cold and her eyes were very bright, too bright, the way eyes go when someone has not slept. "Father tapped all morning. Faster and faster. Mrs. Hobb came in at breakfast and said he'd been at it since before dawn. She said he wouldn't eat. He just sat there with his hand on the wall, tapping, and every time he got to the end, he'd stop, and wait, and listen. As if he were waiting for someone to tap back." She swallowed. "So I tapped back. Over and over. Until I couldn't stand it. And then I came here."
Corin looked at her.
"Something came to the Bank," he said. "This morning."
He told her. The young man in gray in the slag room. Remy Castellan. Thursday. The Bourse. Bring your gloves. And the clock. A man with an errand at half past four would be wise not to be late.
Lyra's face went still.
"Lady Vivienne," she said.
"Yes."
"How does she know?"
"I don't know."
"How does anybody know?" Lyra's voice rose and then came down again, hard. "We only found out yesterday. In a back room on Gutter Street, with nobody there but Niles. And Niles wouldn't tell. I'd stake my life on that." She shook her head. "So how?"
Because they did not need to find out, said Malakor quietly, from the long pocket at Corin's ribs. They knew already. Before you. Long before. You are the last to know, boy, not the first. That is what the boy in gray came to tell you.
Corin did not say that out loud. He looked down the hill instead.
"We'd better find the room," he said.
Tanner's Rise was the steepest street Corin had ever climbed. Or that he remembered climbing. It went up from the river in one long hard slope, without a single turn, so steep that in places the cobbles gave way to shallow steps, worn into dips by a hundred years of boots, and there was an iron rail fixed into the wall on one side for people to haul themselves up by. Carts did not come this way. Only people, and handcarts, and once, coming slowly down past them, an old gray donkey with a load of hides strapped to its back, picking its way from step to step with its long ears laid flat and its breath smoking.
And the smell. Corin had smelled ink at the top of the Rise, in the Ink Quarter, and fish and tar at the bottom of it, in the Docks. Halfway up, between them, there was a third smell, and it was stronger than either. Thick and brown and sour and animal. Like a stable that had not been cleaned for a month. Like old meat. Like something soaking in a bucket that should have been thrown away.
"The tanneries," said Lyra, wrinkling her nose. "Down there." She pointed over the rail, down a narrow alley, toward a low yard behind a wall. Corin could see the tops of great wooden vats in it, and steam coming off them, and a man in a leather apron stirring something with a long pole. "They soak the hides in that. For weeks. Mr. Pruett says you can smell it on a hot day from the Lantern Bridge."
The tanneries made the leather. And everything else on Tanner's Rise used it. That was the street, Corin saw, as they climbed. Every shop was a leather shop. A cobbler, with boots hanging in the window by their laces. A glover, with a row of wooden hands on a shelf, each wearing a different glove. A bookbinder, with calfskin in rolls. A belt-maker. A man who made leather buckets. A man who made leather bottles. A shop that seemed to sell nothing but straps, hundreds of them, in every width and color, hanging from hooks in the doorway like the tails of a hundred horses.
And saddlers.
There were three. Corin counted them. One at the bottom of the Rise, near the river, big and new and painted red, with a gilded horse's head over the door. One halfway up, small and shabby, with its shutters half closed. And one near the top, a little below where Lyra had been waiting, on a corner where the Rise met a narrow side alley.
"A room over a saddler's," said Lyra. "Only it's not the same saddler, and it's not the same room." She looked from one to the other. "Three. Of course there are three."
"Niles said he was born over one," said Corin. "Sixty-eight years ago. The same street. And he came back to a different one."
"So not the one he was born in."
"No."
"But we don't know which one he was born in."
"No."
Lyra let out a breath that went up white.
"Then we ask," she said. "All three. Starting at the top."
The saddler's at the top of the Rise was called J. TALLIS & DAUGHTER. SADDLES. HARNESS. REPAIRS. The words were painted on a long board over the window in black and gold, and somebody had painted them well, a long time ago, and somebody else had touched up the & DAUGHTER much more recently, in brighter gold, as if it had been added afterward. The window was full of leather. A saddle on a wooden stand, deep brown and gleaming. Bridles hung from hooks, with brass fittings polished bright. A harness for a cart horse, laid out on a cloth with every buckle and strap in its place, like the bones of a horse arranged on a table. And on a little shelf at the front, in a row, horse brasses. Round brass disks, each with a pattern cut into it. A star. A crescent moon. A heart. A wheel.
The door was open. The smell that came out of it was not the sour brown smell of the tanneries. It was a good smell. A warm, rich, clean smell. New leather and saddle soap and beeswax and neat's-foot oil.
Corin stopped on the step and breathed it in, and something moved at the very back of his mind.
Saddle soap. Beeswax. He had smelled them yesterday. On the walnut case of a clock in a cold back room, under a skylight. On the wood where an old man's hand had rested every month for a year and a half.
Here, he thought. It's this one.
He did not know how he knew. He knew.
The shop was long and low, with a beamed ceiling hung with saddles, a dozen of them, hanging upside down from hooks like great brown bats asleep. A long workbench ran down one wall, scarred and stained and covered with tools: awls and knives and mallets and needles as long as a finger, and spools of waxed thread, and pots of dye, and a great iron clamp, shaped like a pair of jaws, that held a strap of leather tight while somebody stitched it. At the end of the bench a stove was burning, and a kettle on it was singing to itself.
And at the bench, on a high stool, with a half-made bridle in her lap and a needle in each hand, there was a woman.
She was big. Not fat. Big the way a cart horse is big. Wide shoulders and thick strong arms, bare to the elbow, brown and freckled, with muscles in them that moved like rope under the skin as she pulled the two needles through the leather from opposite sides, one and then the other, in and through and tight. She had a round red face and a great deal of gray hair piled on top of her head and stuck through with what looked like two more awls. She wore a leather apron, scarred and stained like the bench. She was perhaps fifty.
She looked up when they came in. Her eyes were small and blue and sharp.
"Shut that door," she said. "I'm not heating the whole of Tanner's Rise."
Corin shut the door.
The woman looked them over. Lyra in her brown coat. Corin in his gray. She looked at the gray longest.
"If you're from the Ministry about the dye licence," she said, "I paid it. In September. I've got the stamp. And if you're from the Bank, you can turn round and walk straight back down that hill, because I've nothing to sell you and I wouldn't sell it if I had."
"We're not from the Bank," said Lyra. "Or the Ministry. Not today."
The woman's eyes went to her. And stayed there. Something happened in the round red face. Something slow, like a door opening in a wind.
"Well," she said slowly. "Well, well." She put down her needles. "You'll be the girl."
Lyra did not move.
"What girl?"
"His girl. The one he talked about. Miss Lyra." The woman got down off her stool, slowly, the way a big strong person gets down off something high. She wiped her hands on her leather apron. "He said you had your mother's way of standing. Like you were listening for something a long way off. I'd know it anywhere, he said." She came down the bench toward them, and stopped, and looked at Lyra's face for a long time. "He was right. You do."
"Bertram," said Lyra. Her voice was very small.
"Mr. Ash," said the woman. "That's what he called himself here. Mr. Ash. Two years he lodged upstairs. Paid on the nail. Kept his room like a barracks. Helped me with the harness stitching in the evenings, because his hands were good and my eyes aren't what they were, and I took a little off his rent for it." She folded her big freckled arms. "I'm Jenna Tallis. My father was J. I'm the daughter." She jerked her head at the sign over the window. "I painted that myself, when he died. He never would."
"Mrs. Tallis," said Lyra. "Is he—has he—"
"He's not been back since April." Jenna Tallis's voice did not change. But her arms tightened across her apron. "The last week of April. He came down from his room one morning in his good coat and his hat, with a little bag, and he said, Mrs. Tallis, I'm going to see my sister in the country for a while. And I said, I didn't know you had a sister, Mr. Ash. And he said, Neither did I, and he laughed. A strange sort of laugh." She was quiet for a moment. "And he put a purse on my bench. And he said, That's the rent till the New Year. Keep the room for me. Don't let anybody in. And he went out of that door and down the hill. And I haven't seen him since."
Nobody said anything. The kettle sang on the stove.
"He's dead," said Lyra. "Isn't he."
Jenna Tallis looked at her for a long moment.
"I thought so," she said. "Since about June. But I didn't know. Nobody tells you, round here. People just stop coming." She unfolded her arms. "Who told you?"
"A man on Gutter Street. Who knew him."
"Niles." Jenna Tallis nodded slowly. "He went down there every month. First Tuesday. Always came back quiet." She looked at them both. "So. You'll be wanting to see the room."
"Yes," said Lyra.
"He said not to let anybody in."
"I know."
The saddler looked at Lyra for a long time. Then at Corin, in his gray coat. Then at Lyra again.
"He said that," she said slowly, "in April. And in June, a man came to my door. A big man. As big as a door himself. With a note in a lady's hand, on cream paper, smelling of flowers. He said his mistress was a relation of Mr. Ash's, and was anxious about him, and would like to see his room." Her small blue eyes went hard. "And I said no. Mr. Ash said nobody, and nobody's what he gets. And the big man looked at me for a long time, and then he went away." She paused. "And that night, somebody came in over the yard wall at the back, and broke the lock on the yard door, and went up the back stairs, and was up there in his room for an hour, and went out again the same way."
Lyra closed her eyes.
"I heard them," said Jenna Tallis. "Walking about. Over my head. Opening things. I lay in my bed with my father's hammer in my hand and I didn't go up." Her red face went redder. "I'm not proud of it. But I'm not a fool either. A man that size, in the dark, in a room that small." She shook her head. "In the morning I went up. And it was all—well. You'll see."
She turned and went to a hook by the stove, and took down a key on a loop of leather.
"You're his girl," she said. "He talked about you. Every evening, nearly, over the harness. How you rode Clover round the paddock with your chin up. How you cried when the old dog died. How you threw a hoop across the room once because you couldn't sew." She held out the key. "If he'd let anybody in, it'd be you. I'm sure of that. So I'll let you in. And if he comes back and shouts at me for it, I'll tell him I don't care."
Lyra took the key. Her hand was shaking.
"Thank you," she said.
"Up the back stairs. Through there. The door at the top." Jenna Tallis climbed back up onto her high stool and picked up her two needles and the half-made bridle. She did not look at them. "And Miss. When you find whatever it is the big man didn't find." She pulled the needles through the leather, one and then the other, tight. "You don't need to tell me what it was."
