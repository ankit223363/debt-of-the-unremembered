Chapter 13: The Clock That Ran Backward, Part 5 of 5: The Last Thing He Saw
The Last Thing He Saw
It was dark, and it was cold, and it was wet.
Corin was old again. His knees hurt. He was standing at the top of a flight of stone steps, wide and worn and slimy with river weed, going down into blackness. Below him, at the bottom, he could hear water. Not the still black water of the Cut. Moving water. Slow and heavy and wide, slapping against stone in the dark, sucking and slapping, sucking and slapping, the way a big river moves at night when the tide is coming in.
Above him, the Lantern Bridge.
He knew it. Not from the Ledger. From inside Bertram's eyes. The great gray arches going out over the river into the dark, and the little house-shaped lamps along its walls, every few paces, glowing blue. They were very high up. From the bottom of the steps they looked like a row of small blue stars, hung in the black, far away.
It was nine days before the carriage. He knew that too. The way you know things in a dream.
And there was a letter in his hand.
A thick cream envelope, sealed with dark green wax pressed with the rose and the thorn. His Lordship's seal. His Lordship had given it to him two hours ago, in the study, with the door locked. Take this to Doctor Aske, Bertram. Tonight. By hand. Into his hand. Nobody else's. And His Lordship's face had been white and his hand had been shaking. He'll be at the steps below the Lantern Bridge at the tenth bell. He said he would meet you there. He did not want to be seen coming to the house.
So Bertram had come. And it was the tenth bell. And he was standing at the top of the steps below the Lantern Bridge in the dark, with the letter in his hand and his knees aching and the cold coming up off the river into his old bones.
And there were people at the bottom of the steps.
He heard them before he saw them. Voices. Low. Down by the water, where the steps ended at a little stone landing with iron rings in the wall for tying boats. A man's voice, old and thin and frightened. And another voice, lighter, higher, clear.
Bertram did not go down. He did not know why. Some old part of him, the part that had served in a great house for twenty-nine years and knew when a room was not a room to walk into, made him stop. He stood very still at the top of the steps, in the shadow of the bridge's first great pillar. And then, slowly, he went down three steps, and four, and stopped again, and leaned out, very carefully, round the edge of the pillar, and looked.
There was a lantern on the landing. A small one, set down on the wet stone, with its shutter half closed, so that its light came out low and yellow and lay on the stones and the black water and nothing else. In the light, there were four people.
One was Doctor Aske.
Bertram knew him. A small round man with a fringe of white hair and gold spectacles and a black bag. He had come to the house on Crown Walk once a month for twenty years to listen to His Lordship's chest and look at Her Ladyship's throat and give Miss Lyra and Master Rowan their medicine. He had a soft kind voice and cold hands. He was on his knees on the wet stone of the landing.
He was on his knees because a man was holding him there.
A big man. As big as a door. Standing behind the doctor with one huge hand on each of the doctor's small round shoulders, pressing down. Not hard. He did not need to press hard. His hands were so big that each one covered the doctor's shoulder from the neck to the arm. In the low yellow light his face was broad and flat and red, and he was chewing something, slowly, and his breath came out white in the cold, and even from the steps, even from round the pillar, Bertram could smell it.
Beer. And onions.
In front of the doctor, standing on the landing between him and the black water, there was a woman.
She was tall. She was wrapped in a long dark coat with a fur collar, silver-gray, the color of a winter fox. She wore gloves of some smooth shining dark skin, like a seal's. And over the top half of her face, from her forehead to the tip of her nose, she wore a mask. A white mask. Made of shell, polished until it shone like the inside of a cup, with a faint pink glow deep in it where the lantern light came through. Two long slanting holes for the eyes. Tiny white pearls round the edges, in a pattern like the curl of a wave.
Below the mask, her mouth was bare. And it was red.
Around her neck, over the fur collar, she wore a necklace. Tiny glass vials, a dozen of them, each no bigger than a grain of rice, on a fine gold chain. They glowed. Blue. Faint. Like a row of very small lamps.
And the smell came off her even over the beer and the onions and the river. Thick and sweet and heavy. So strong that Bertram, at the top of the steps, had to put his hand over his mouth.
Lilies.
And the fourth person.
Bertram could not see him well. He stood a little apart from the others, at the very edge of the lantern's light, half in shadow, by the wall where the iron rings were. A tall man. Thin. In a long gray coat with a hood, the hood up, so that his face was only darkness. He did not move. He did not speak. He stood very still with his hands in front of him, and in his hands, held up a little, as if he were offering it to somebody, there was a cup.
A silver cup. Plain. Heavy. About the size of a big egg cup, with a short thick stem and a round foot. Its bowl was wide and shallow. And the inside of the bowl was carved, all the way round and down into the bottom, with fine grooves. A spiral.
The hands that held it wore gloves.
Gray gloves.
Corin, down at the bottom of the well inside Bertram's old eyes, felt his own heart stop.
"Please," Doctor Aske was saying. His soft kind voice was shaking so hard that the words came out in pieces. "Please. I don't know anything. I don't. I never asked. I only ever saw the child twice. Twice, in five years. A fever, and a broken wrist. That's all. I don't even know her name. They never told me her name."
"No," said the woman in the mask. "But you wrote it down."
Her voice was light and high and clear and pleasant. Like a bell. Like a lady at a garden party asking if anyone would like more tea.
"You wrote it in your casebook, Doctor. The Thorne child. Fever. Broken left wrist, set clean. And her age, and her height, and the color of her eyes. You wrote it all down, in your neat little hand, the way doctors do." The red mouth smiled. "And then you kept the casebook. For five years. In a drawer. When she was gone."
"I didn't know it mattered—"
"Everything matters, Doctor. That is the first thing they teach you in my family." The woman tilted her head. "And now somebody has been asking. Somebody from very far away. Somebody who has been writing letters to Aurelian in a hand nobody in this city can read. And Aurelian has been writing back. And sending for you. Twice this month. At night." She paused. "What did he ask you, Doctor?"
The doctor did not answer. He was crying.
The woman sighed.
She turned her masked face toward the tall man in gray, by the wall. She did not say anything. She only lifted one seal-gloved hand, a little.
And the man in gray stepped forward into the light.
He held the silver cup out in front of him, in both his gray-gloved hands, and he lowered it, slowly, until its wide shallow bowl was just above the top of the doctor's bowed head, just above the thin white hair. Not touching. Just above. The way you would hold a bowl under a dripping tap.
And the grooves inside the cup began to glow.
Faint. Blue-white. A thin line of light, running round and round the spiral, from the rim to the center. Round and round.
To the left.
Against the sun. Against the clock. Like water going down a drain on the other side of the world.
And out of the top of Doctor Aske's head, out of the thin white hair, something rose. Thin and pale and shining, like smoke, like breath on a winter morning. It rose up into the bowl of the silver cup and wound into the grooves and went round, and round, and round, to the left, following the light. And the doctor's crying stopped. And his shoulders went loose under the big man's hands. And his head, very slowly, came up.
And he looked at the woman in the mask with a soft, pleasant, puzzled face, the way you look at a stranger on a coach.
"Good evening," said Doctor Aske politely.
The woman in the mask laughed. A light, pretty, tinkling laugh, like little bells.
"Good evening, Doctor," she said. And then, to the big man, in exactly the same light voice: "Put him in the water, Gorse. Gently. He won't mind."
Bertram did not see it. He shut his eyes. He shut them so hard that his old face hurt. He heard it. A shuffle on wet stone. A sound like a sack being lifted. And a splash. Not loud. The river took it, and went on slapping against the steps, sucking and slapping, as if nothing had happened at all.
When he opened his eyes again, the doctor was gone.
The big man was wiping his hands on his coat. The tall man in gray had stepped back to the wall, into the shadow, and was holding the silver cup against his chest, carefully, the way a man holds a cup that is full and must not spill. The grooves inside it had stopped glowing. But they were full of something gray. Bertram could see it, even from the steps. Soft and gray and faintly shining, lying in the spiral like dust in a seashell.
And the woman in the mask was looking out over the black river, with her back to the steps, toward the far bank where the factory chimneys stood up against the sky.
"The tide weighs it," she said softly, to nobody, "and it comes up light."
Then she turned round. And she looked up the steps. Straight up. At the first great pillar of the Lantern Bridge.
At Bertram.
He did not move. He could not. He stood in the shadow of the pillar with the letter in his hand and the cold of the river in his bones, and the woman in the white shell mask looked up at him, and he did not know if she could see him in the dark. He did not know. He would never know.
She looked for a long time.
Then she lifted one seal-gloved hand, very slowly, and put it to the edge of her mask, by her left ear. And she lifted the mask, just a little. Just enough. As if she were adjusting it. As if it pinched.
And under it, in the low yellow lantern light, for one moment, Bertram saw her face.
He had known it for thirty years. He had brought it hot milk when it had a fever, when it was a girl of twelve with plaits, the first winter he came to the house on Crown Walk. He had watched it, at twenty-two, come out of the long gallery past the dressing-room door with tears on it, very fast, in a white dress, and the smell of lilies coming in after.
Miss Cressida, thought Bertram.
And in the bottom of the well, Corin heard it. The name. Not in the woman's voice. In the old man's. Thought so loud and so clear and so full of grief that it was almost a shout.
Miss Cressida.
The mask came down again. The red mouth smiled. And the woman in white turned away from the steps and said, lightly, to the tall man in gray: "Come along. It's cold. And my brother will be wondering where his doctor has got to."
And they went away along the landing, the three of them, into the dark under the bridge, where there must have been a boat, because Corin heard oars. And the lantern went with them. And the steps were black, and the river was black, and the little blue lamps on the bridge high above were very far away.
Bertram stood on the steps in the dark for a long, long time.
Then he went home. To Crown Walk. To the house. Up the stairs, with his knees aching, to the study, where His Lordship was sitting at his desk with the lamp lit, waiting. And he put the letter down on the desk, still sealed. And he told him.
And His Lordship listened, with his white face and his red eyes. And when Bertram had finished, His Lordship did not say anything for a long time.
Then he said: Thank you, Bertram. Go to bed.
And then, as Bertram reached the door: She'll come for me next. In a week or two. With him. With the cup. You know that, don't you?
And Bertram said: Yes, my lord.
And His Lordship said: Then I had better have nothing left for them to take.
And then, at the very end, at the very heart of the fold, there was one more thing.
Not a memory. A voice.
Bertram's voice. Old and tired and careful. Not speaking to anybody in the memory. Speaking to whoever was breathing him in. As if he had folded it in there on purpose, at the very center, the last thing, the thing he most wanted to be found.
Tell Miss Lyra, said Bertram's voice. The little one was not lost. She was sent. Across the water. He sent her, to keep her safe, the winter Miss Lyra turned four, before Master Rowan came. He never told Her Ladyship where. He never told anybody. And Miss Cressida has been looking for her ever since.
A pause. A breath. The smell of pipe tobacco, very faint, fading.
And tell her I'm sorry, said Bertram. I let them in. In the morning. Like he told me to. I bowed, and I showed them up the stairs. Her, in her white, with her mask in her pocket. And the man in gray, with his hood down, and his round spectacles, and his blue fingers. The voice was very faint now. I let them in. Forgive me.
And then there was nothing.
Only cold. And dark. And the smell of saddle soap, and beeswax, and pipe tobacco, going out like a lamp.
Corin came back up out of the well.
He was lying on the floor of the back room, on his side, with his cheek on the cold boards. The light of the eleven stones was moving on the ceiling above him, rose and amber and green and blue. Somebody was holding his hand. Lyra. Somebody else was holding a lamp. Niles. And somebody was standing over all of them, very small, with her cap pulled down over her ears and her face white as paper, and she was shouting.
"—carriage! I said there's a carriage! Coming down Gutter Street! Backwards! With the big ugly man on it! Are you all deaf?"
Juno.
Corin tried to sit up. His arm would not hold him. It was still numb from the fingers to the shoulder, and cold, so cold that it did not feel like his arm at all. Lyra got her hands under him and pulled, and he sat up, and the room swung round him like a lamp on a chain, and came slowly to rest.
Round spectacles, he thought. Blue fingers.
He did not remember who had round spectacles and blue fingers. But it was in the Ledger. Carriage 9. Harvester L. Hale, and one guest. And the Ledger said that Master Hale was the man who had given him his rod.
Malakor, he thought. The man in gray. Under the bridge. With the cup. Was that—
The rod was lying on the floor by his hand, in the moving light of the eleven stones. Silent.
No, said Malakor at last.
Its voice was very quiet. Very old. Very, very tired.
No, boy. The man who came in the morning, in the carriage, with the round spectacles and the blue fingers, was Linus. I would know him anywhere. I was in his hand for thirty years. A pause. But the man under the bridge, with the cup, in the dark, with his hood up, was not Linus.
How do you know?
Because Linus cuts his spiral to the right, said the rod. The way a clock turns. So that it is always going forward. A long pause. And that man's turned left.
The Woman With Lilies
"How far?" said Niles.
He said it very calmly. He had put the lamp down on the carved chest. His one bright eye was on Juno, and his clean hands were folded in front of his brown waistcoat, and his voice was the voice of a man asking how much a customer wanted to borrow on a watch.
"Halfway down," said Juno. "Backing. The horses don't like it. Nobody's helping him. Everybody's gone in and shut their doors." She was breathing hard. "Two minutes. Maybe three."
"Is she in it?"
"Somebody's in it. The blinds are down. I can smell it from the window."
Nobody had to ask what she could smell.
Niles turned and looked at the clock.
It stood in the middle of the cold back room in the strange still light from the skylight, tall and dark, with its glass door hanging open on its brass hinges and its brass pendulum still. Its eleven stones were lit. All of them. Rose and amber and green and gray-blue, violet and gold and milky white, sea-green and copper and silver-gray and the blue like the inside of a flame. They glowed in their silver claws round the starry face, soft and steady and bright, and the light of them lay on the walnut case and made every carved rose and every carved thorn stand out sharp. The silver hands pointed straight up, together, at twelve. And under them, from the dark blue enamel of the face, the tiny silver drawer stood open, with its Karst letters on the front, empty.
The whole blue stone was in Lyra's hand.
She was holding it so tightly that her knuckles were white. Its broken gold chain hung down between her fingers and swung.
"The stone," said Niles.
Lyra looked at him. Her chin came up.
"It isn't in your book," she said. "Eleven present, one setting empty. You said a pledge must come out the way it went in. It didn't go in with this. Nobody knew it was there." Her voice shook and steadied. "It isn't part of the pledge. It's mine. My grandfather made it. For my father. And Father sent it to me through the clock."
The pawnbroker looked at her for a long moment.
"Quite right," he said. "I did not write it. So it is not mine to keep." He turned to Corin. "Can you stand?"
Corin could stand. Just. Lyra pulled him up by his good arm, and he leaned on the carved chest beside the gilded birdcage, and the room swung round him once, slowly, and settled. His right arm hung at his side, numb and cold from the fingers to the shoulder. The rod was in his left hand. He did not remember picking it up.
It was heavy. Heavier than it had ever been. And cold. And full.
He is in here, said Malakor quietly. Bertram. What is left of him. You cannot carry him far like this, boy. Not in me. I am not a jar. He needs to be put somewhere safe. And soon. Before he starts to leak into you.
"I need a jar," said Corin. His voice still did not sound like his own. "Mr. Niles. A glass jar. With a lid. Please."
"Rule three," said Niles. "I do not buy Ash."
"I'm not selling."
The small man looked at him. Then he turned to a shelf by the door, behind the suit of boy's armor, and took down a jar. A plain glass jar, heavy and clear, with a cork stopper and a wire to hold the cork down. He held it out.
"I do not buy Ash," he said. "But I do not charge for jars. Not for an old customer." He pressed it into Corin's left hand, beside the rod. "Put him in it. And take him with you. And keep him somewhere better than I did."
Corin knelt. He had to. He knelt on the cold boards in the light of the eleven stones and set the jar down and pulled the cork out with his teeth, because his right hand would not work, and he held the rod over the open mouth of the jar, the carved spiral tip pointing down. And he let go.
Not of the rod. Of what was in it.
It came out of the silver slowly. A thin pale shining thread. Down. Round and round the spiral, the way it had gone in, and off the tip, and into the jar, where it curled and settled at the bottom, soft and gray and faintly glowing, like breath on a cold window. It did not fill the jar. It hardly covered the bottom. A man's whole last gift. A layer of gray no thicker than a finger.
And as it left the rod, the cold left Corin's arm. All at once. Like water draining out of a bath. His fingers prickled and burned. He could move them.
He pushed the cork back in. He twisted the wire down over it.
There, said Malakor. Very gently. There. Safe now, old man.
Corin took the stub of pencil from the spine of the Ledger, and a scrap of paper from the back, and wrote a label. He did not decide what to write. His hand did it. He licked the back of the paper and stuck it on the glass.
BERTRAM ASHCOMBE. STEWARD. FORGIVE ME.
"One minute," said Juno from the passage. "Less. She's stopping."
Niles moved.
"The clock," he said. "The door. Shut it."
Lyra shut the glass door of the clock. Gently. It clicked. The brass pendulum hung still behind it. The eleven stones went on glowing.
"The drawer."
She pushed the tiny silver drawer back into the face. It slid home on its silver runners with a whisper and a click, and the empty claw at twelve turned back over it, a quarter, a half, all the way, and the seam in the blue enamel was gone. You would never have known.
But the stones were still lit. And the hands still pointed at twelve. And the tiny hand on the little dial below pointed at seven.
"I can't put them out," said Lyra. "I don't know how."
"Then leave them," said Niles. "Let her see them. It is not against any rule for a clock to be lit." He picked up the lamp. "Now. Out. All of you. Into the passage. Behind the curtain. And not a sound." His one eye went to Corin, kneeling on the floor with the jar in his hands. "Especially you. Whatever she says. Whatever happens. She must not see your face, sir. She must not see that rod. And she must not see that jar."
"Why?"
"Because I may be a pawnbroker on Gutter Street," said Niles, "but I am not a fool. And I have seen the face of a man who has just breathed somebody in." He held the oak door open. "So has she, I think."
They went out into the short dark passage. Niles shut the oak door behind them on the glowing clock, and did not lock it, and did not put the bar across. He went through the brown velvet curtain into the shop with his lamp, and let it fall behind him, and they were in the dark.
Corin knelt in the dark on the creaking boards with the jar held against his chest. Lyra knelt beside him. Juno crouched at the curtain, with one eye to the gap at its edge where the velvet had worn thin.
The clocks ticked. A hundred of them, just on the other side of the curtain. Now. Now. Now.
And then the cracked bell on the shop door rang. Twice. And stopped.
And the smell came in.
It came through the curtain like water coming through a cloth. Thick and heavy and sweet. It filled the short dark passage. It got into Corin's nose and his throat and his eyes, and his eyes watered, and he put his sleeve over his mouth.
"Mr. Niles," said a voice. Light and high and clear. "How lovely. You're open."
"I am always open, madam," said Niles, "until the sixth bell."
"So you are." Footsteps. Light and quick. A sharp little tick at each step, like a clock, on the bare boards. Coming down the shop toward the counter. "So you are. I'm sorry to come without warning. I know it isn't the first Tuesday. But I was passing. And I thought I heard something."
"On Gutter Street, madam? From your carriage?"
"A bell." The voice was very pleasant. "A single bell. Very clear. Like a glass bell. It's quite a distinctive sound. I used to hear it every night when I was a girl. At the stroke of twelve. From the long gallery." The footsteps stopped. At the counter, Corin thought. Right on the other side of the curtain. "It woke me up, when I was small. I used to lie in bed and count the stones going out."
Niles said nothing.
"And then," said the voice, "about a quarter of an hour ago, I was sitting in my carriage on Needle Street, waiting for a friend, and I heard it again." A pause. "Isn't that strange?"
"Very strange, madam."
"I thought so too." The little tick of a heel. "May I see it?"
"The pledge?"
"The clock, Mr. Niles."
"Anybody may see a pledge," said Niles, "if they can say what it is. You said what it was, in May. I let you see it then." His voice did not change at all. "I will let you see it now. But I will tell you first, so that you are not surprised. The ticket has been presented."
The silence on the other side of the curtain went on for a long time.
The clocks ticked. Now. Now. Now.
"Presented," said the voice at last. Softly.
"This afternoon. By the person who held it. And the term has been extended, at their request, by rule, to the first Tuesday of February." Niles paused. "So the pledge is not for sale, madam. Not on the first Tuesday of November. Not for a hundred gold. Not for any money."
"By whom?"
"Rule four."
"Oh, Mr. Niles." The voice laughed. The little tinkling bell laugh. "Your rules. Your famous rules. I'd forgotten." The laugh stopped. "Was it a young woman? With short black hair? And a brown coat?"
"Rule four, madam."
"With a man in gray?"
Niles did not answer.
Corin felt Lyra go rigid beside him in the dark. He felt her hand close on his arm. Hard.
And then the voice on the other side of the curtain said, quite lightly, quite pleasantly, the way you call a cat in from the garden:
"Lyra, darling. I know you're there. Your boots have left the most dreadful marks all over Mr. Niles's clean floor. Do come out."
Nobody moved.
"You needn't be afraid," said Cressida Thorne. "I'm not angry. Truly. I'm delighted. You found it. Bertram's ticket. After all this time. I looked and looked." A pause. "Clever girl. He always did like you best."
Lyra let go of Corin's arm.
He felt her stand up beside him in the dark. He reached for her. His fingers found the hem of her old brown coat, and closed on it, and she pulled it out of his hand, gently, and he let her. He could not stop her. He did not think he had the right.
She bent down, very close, so close that her breath touched his ear.
"Stay here," she whispered. "Both of you. Don't come out. Whatever happens. She mustn't see you." A pause. "She mustn't see him."
She meant the jar.
Then she straightened, and stepped past Juno, and put her hand on the brown velvet curtain, and pushed it aside, and went out into the shop.
Juno caught the curtain before it could fall shut. She held it, a crack, an inch, the width of an eye. Corin, on his knees on the boards with the jar against his chest, could see through the gap. Past Juno's small cap. A long low brown shop full of tagged and gleaming things, and a hundred clocks ticking on the wall, and a green lamp on a counter with a brass grille. And Niles, behind the counter, on his tall stool, very small and very still. And Lyra, standing at the end of the counter by the flap, in her filthy brown coat, with her short black hair full of roof dust and her chin up and her hands at her sides.
And in front of the counter, Cressida.
She was not in white. Not all of her. She was wrapped in a long dark coat with a fur collar, silver-gray, the color of a winter fox. Her gloves were some smooth dark shining skin, like a seal's. Her pale gold hair was hidden under a dark fur hat. And at her throat, over the fur collar, on a fine gold chain, there hung a necklace of tiny glass vials, a dozen of them, each no bigger than a grain of rice. Glowing. Faint. Blue.
She was smiling. Her red mouth was smiling.
Corin had seen that coat. That collar. Those gloves. That necklace. An hour ago. On a landing under the Lantern Bridge, in the dark, by the light of a low yellow lantern, standing over a small round man on his knees.
He did not breathe.
"There you are," said Cressida warmly. "Look at you. You're filthy. Have you been climbing roofs? Like a sweep's boy?" She shook her head, smiling. "Your mother used to do that, you know. When she first came. Climb out of the long gallery windows and sit on the roof of the house in the moonlight, and Old Lady Agatha would nearly die of it." Her gray eyes went over Lyra, slowly. "You found the ticket."
"Yes," said Lyra.
"Where was it?"
"Somewhere he thought nobody would look."
Cressida laughed. "How like him. How very like him." She took a step closer. "May I see it, darling?"
"No."
The red mouth went on smiling. But something in the gray eyes went still.
"No?"
"No, Aunt Cressida." Lyra's voice was very quiet and very steady. "It's mine. Rule two. Whoever holds the ticket holds the pledge." She did not look at Niles. "Mr. Niles explained it to me. It's a very good rule. I like it."
Cressida looked at her niece for a long, long moment.
Then she turned her head and looked past Lyra. At the brown velvet curtain at the back of the shop. At the crack in it, where Juno was holding it open the width of an eye.
Corin, on his knees in the dark behind it, with Bertram's jar against his chest, felt those gray eyes on the curtain like the touch of a cold finger on the back of his neck.
She could not see him. He knew she could not. The passage was black and the shop was lit and the crack was tiny. She could not possibly see him.
She looked at the curtain for a long time anyway.
Then she smiled again, and turned back to Lyra.
"Bertram's dead, you know," she said gently. "Poor old Bertram. I'm so sorry, darling. I only found out myself in the spring. He went to the country, to his sister's, and he caught a chill, and that was that. At his age. They wrote to me, because I'm the only Thorne of age." She sighed. "I should have told you. I meant to. But you'd had so much sorrow already."
Lyra did not move. Her hands, at her sides, did not move. Her face did not change at all.
"He didn't have a sister," she said.
The gray eyes rested on her.
"Didn't he?" said Cressida. "How odd. I must have been thinking of somebody else." She drew her fur collar closer round her throat, and the tiny vials on their gold chain swung and glittered. "There are so many people in this city who go to sisters in the country. One loses track."
She turned toward the door.
Then she stopped, with her seal-gloved hand on the latch, and looked back over her shoulder.
"The snow's coming, darling," she said. "Have you seen the sky? It's going to be early this year. Much earlier than anybody thought." The red mouth curved. "Do think about what I said. About the brooch. About your poor father. About houses in the country, with gardens, and nurses day and night." She opened the door. The cracked bell rang. "I'd hurry, if I were you. Before the first snow. That's what we agreed."
And she went out.
The bell rang again as the door swung shut behind her. Twice. And stopped.
Nobody moved for a long time. Lyra stood at the end of the counter with her hands at her sides. Niles sat on his tall stool with his clean hands flat on the counter. The clocks ticked. Now. Now. Now.
Then Juno let the curtain fall.
Corin got up off his knees in the dark. He came out through the curtain with the jar held against his chest under his coat, and stood beside Lyra.
She was shaking. All over. The way a dog shakes in the cold.
"She killed him," she said. Very low. "Didn't she. Bertram. And the doctor. Didn't she."
Corin did not answer. He did not need to.
"And she knows I know," said Lyra. "She looked at me and she knew." She swallowed. "And she smiled."
Through the thick bubbled diamond panes of Niles's window, out on Gutter Street, something was moving. Something small and white. Falling. Slowly. Turning in the air as it fell, the way a feather turns, the way a petal turns, and settling on the cobbles, and on the iron bar with its three brass balls, and on the dark roof of a black carriage backing slowly, slowly, with its horses complaining, all the way up the hill toward Needle Street.
Then another. And another. And another.
Lyra went to the window. She put her hand flat on the cold bubbled glass.
"It's snowing," she said.
On the roof of the bindery opposite, on the far side of Gutter Street, in the falling white, a slight neat figure in a gray coat stood up out of the shadow of a chimney stack, and looked down at Niles's window for a long moment, and then turned and was gone across the roofs, light and quick, like a cat.
And behind them, through the brown velvet curtain, through the short dark passage, through the oak door that nobody had locked, in a cold back room under a skylight that was filling slowly with snow, a tall dark clock stood with its eleven stones glowing and its silver hands pointing straight up at the empty claw at twelve.
It did not knock.
It would never knock again.
That night, in his small gray room at the top of Number 14 Tallow Street, with the snow ticking softly on the slates over his head and a glass jar on the crate beside his bed with a label on it in his own big untidy hand, Corin wrote it all down.
It took him until the fourth bell after midnight. His hand cramped and he had to stop and rub it and start again. He wrote the slag room and the boy in gray. Thursday. The Bourse. He wrote Tanner's Rise and Jenna Tallis and Mr. Ash. He wrote the wrecked room and the slit pocket and the petal. He wrote the third stone. The tobacco tin. The ticket. Forgive me. Bertram's note. Bertram's list. Owed. Her Ladyship. ✓. He wrote the roofs, as much as he could. He wrote Niles and rule two. He wrote twenty-nine minutes past four.
He wrote the clock running backward, and the eleven stones lighting, and the glass bell, and the tiny drawer with the Karst letters, and the whole blue stone.
He wrote the long gallery at dawn, two years ago. His Lordship in his shirtsleeves. You will let them in. Send Vance away. One finger on the minute hand. Four, two, nine, three. Say it back to me. I couldn't keep them all. I couldn't keep the little one. The white light. What a lovely clock. Bright.
He wrote the steps under the Lantern Bridge. The lantern. Doctor Aske on his knees. Gorse. The woman in the white shell mask. The man in gray with his hood up and his gray gloves and his silver cup. The spiral, turning left. Good evening. The splash. The tide weighs it, and it comes up light. The mask, lifted. Miss Cressida.
He wrote: The little one was not lost. She was sent. Across the water. The winter Lyra turned four. Cressida has been looking for her ever since.
He wrote: The man in the carriage in the morning, with the round spectacles and the blue fingers, was Hale. Malakor says so. Hale is the man who gave me my rod.
He wrote: The man under the bridge, with the cup, was NOT Hale. His spiral turns left. Malakor knows who he is. Malakor still will not say.
And last, in letters so big that the pencil tore the paper:
CRESSIDA THORNE KILLED DOCTOR ASKE. GORSE PUT HIM IN THE RIVER. A MAN WITH A LEFT-TURNING CUP TOOK HIM FIRST. BERTRAM SAW. BERTRAM IS DEAD.
SHE KNOWS LYRA KNOWS.
IT IS SNOWING.
He put down the pencil.
He sat on the edge of the narrow iron bed in the dark, with the little brass lamp turned down to a blue bead, and looked at the jar on the crate. At the thin gray layer in the bottom of it, soft and faintly shining, no thicker than a finger.
"Thank you," he said to it. Quietly. "For keeping it. All that time."
The gray in the jar did not move.
But the smell of pipe tobacco, very faint, very sweet, hung in the cold air of the little room for a long time after, as if an old man had been sitting by a fire with his boots off, and had only just got up, and gone.
