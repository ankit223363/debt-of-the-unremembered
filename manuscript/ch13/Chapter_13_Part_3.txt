Chapter 13: The Clock That Ran Backward, Part 3 of 5: Twenty-Nine Past Four
Twenty-Nine Past Four
"Up," said Lyra. "Not down. Gutter Street is up. At the top. Through the Ink Quarter. He's at the bottom."
"Then why is he at the bottom?"
She opened her mouth. Then she shut it again.
Corin went back to the window. He looked down the long steep slope of Tanner's Rise at the black carriage on the corner. At the big man on the box, very still. At the horses breathing white into the cold.
Then he looked the other way. Up. As far as the deep little window would let him, pressing his cheek against the cold glass to see round the edge of the frame.
At the top of the Rise, where it met the first crooked houses of the Ink Quarter, where Lyra had stood waiting with her back against the wall at noon, there was a figure. Standing on the corner. In the shadow of a doorway.
It was dressed in white.
Not a lady. A man. A tall man, very straight, very still, in a white coat and white breeches and white stockings, with his hands behind his back and his powdered white head turned toward the top of the Rise, and his eyes on a spot just above the street. A footman. Standing on a corner in the Ink Quarter in the freezing cold, in a livery that belonged on Crown Walk, as if he had been put there and told not to move.
Ah, said Malakor again. Now that is cleverer.
"There's one at the top too," said Corin. "One of hers. In white."
Lyra came to the window and looked. She did not say anything. Her hands closed on the tobacco tin.
"She doesn't know we've found it," Corin said slowly. "She can't. But she knows we're here. Someone saw us come up the Rise. Someone told her. And she's put a man at each end of the street, and she's waiting to see which way we go." He stepped back from the glass. "If we go down, Gorse will follow us. If we go up, the footman will. And either way, they'll know where we're going. And they'll be there before us."
"Then what do we do?"
"I don't know."
They stood in the wrecked room among the feathers.
There is a third way, said Malakor. There is always a third way, in a city like this. Down is the street. Up is the street. A pause. And the street is not the only thing in a city that goes from one place to another.
And from outside the little window, very close, so close that they both jumped, something went tap.
And then again. Tap. Tap.
Corin turned.
There was a face at the window. Upside down. Hanging down from above, from the roof, with its cap pulled down over its ears and its sharp chin pointing at the sky and its two small eyes glaring in through the dirty bubbled glass. A brass key on a string was swinging from its neck, upside down, tapping against the glass. Tap. Tap.
It mouthed something. Corin could not hear it through the glass. But he did not need to.
Open the window, you idiot.
He opened the window. It stuck, and then gave, with a shriek of old wood, and cold air rushed into the room, and the feathers on the floor lifted and whirled.
Juno's face stayed where it was, upside down, red with cold and with hanging upside down, glaring.
"You're both very stupid," she said. "You know that? Very, very stupid."
"Juno," said Lyra. "What are you—how did you—I said not to—"
"You said not to tell me. Nobody told me." Juno's upside-down face was furious. "Gran told me. Gran found your note in the hall this morning, the one you left for him, and she read it, because she reads everything, and she said, Juno, that girl and that gray coat are going up Tanner's Rise today and they've no more sense between them than a pair of hens. And she said, Go and see they don't get their necks wrung." She sniffed. Upside down, it was a strange sound. "So I've been on this roof since the first bell after noon. Freezing. And watching. And there's a big carriage at the bottom with a big ugly man on it, and there's a white footman at the top who's been standing on that corner since noon like a lamp post and hasn't moved, not even to scratch, and there's another one in white on Needle Street by the Inkwell, and there's a boy in a gray coat on the roof of the bindery across the alley who's been watching this window all afternoon and thinks I haven't seen him." She glared. "I've seen him."
Corin and Lyra looked at each other.
"A boy in a gray coat," said Corin.
"Neat," said Juno. "Brown hair. Dark eyes. Too good a coat for a roof. He climbs like a cat, though. I'll give him that." Her eyes narrowed. "He's not one of hers. He's not watching you. He's watching them."
The young man in gray, said Malakor softly. Remy Castellan. Lady Vivienne's ward. A pause. Well. Now that is interesting.
"Where do you need to go?" said Juno.
Lyra hesitated. Then she said: "Gutter Street. The bottom. By the Cut. The last shop."
"Niles's?"
"Yes."
"When?"
"Before twenty-nine minutes past four."
Juno looked at them both, upside down, for a long moment, with her sharp small face going slowly redder and redder.
Then she said: "Can you climb?"
Lyra could climb. Corin found that out at once. She went out of the window first, without a word, with the tobacco tin buttoned inside her coat, and got her hands on the edge of the roof above, and swung her legs up, and was gone, so fast and so neat that it was like watching a cat go over a wall. Corin went after her, and was not so neat. He hung by his hands from the edge of the slates with his boots scrabbling on the brick below and the long steep drop of the yard under him and the cold air pulling at his coat, and for one long moment he thought he was going to fall, and then a small hard hand took hold of his collar and pulled, and a cracked voice said, Get your knee up, get your KNEE up, and he got his knee up, and he was on the roof.
The roof was steep and gray and slick with frost. He lay on it with his cheek against the cold slates and his heart going like a drum.
"That's twice," said Juno, above him. "That's twice I've caught you."
"Thank you."
"Don't thank me. Get up. Keep low. And don't look at the bindery."
Corin did not look at the bindery. But he saw it anyway, from the edge of his eye, across the narrow alley that ran beside the saddler's: a high narrow building of black brick with a flat roof, and on the roof, in the shadow of a chimney stack, a figure in gray, crouched, very still. Watching the corner where the white footman stood. Not watching them at all.
Juno took them over the roofs of the Ink Quarter.
Corin did not remember any of it afterward. He could not. But he wrote it down that night, as much as he could, with his hands still shaking. Slates. Frost. A gutter full of ice. A plank across a gap between two roofs, three stories up, and Juno running across it as if it were a pavement. A chimney stack to hold on to. A flat roof covered in pigeons, and the pigeons going up all at once in a great gray clatter. Lyra's hand on my arm. Juno's voice: low, keep low, don't look down. The presses clacking underneath us through the roofs, so loud on the flat parts that the slates hummed. The smell of ink coming up through the chimneys. A drainpipe. Down. A wall. Along the top of the wall. Down.
They went the long way. Juno would not go the short way. The short way was along the roofs of Needle Street, and on the corner of Needle Street by the Inkwell there was a man in white, standing very still.
"That one's been there since noon too," said Juno, lying flat on a roof above a narrow lane and looking down over the gutter at the white figure at the far end. "Same as the other. They've got the whole of the bottom of the Ink Quarter tied up like a parcel." She looked at Corin sideways. "What did you do to them?"
"Nothing yet."
"You will," said Juno. "I can tell. You've got that face."
She took them round. West, and then south, along the back of the quarter, where the printing houses gave way to warehouses and the roofs got lower and flatter and farther apart. Down a drainpipe into a yard full of empty barrels. Over a wall into another yard, with a dog in it that barked once and then saw Juno and lay down again. Through a gap in a fence. Along a narrow slimy ledge above black water, with a brick wall on one side and nothing at all on the other, a ledge so narrow that Corin had to turn sideways and press his back to the wall and shuffle, one foot and then the other, with his heart in his throat and his eyes fixed on the back of Lyra's brown coat in front of him.
The black water below them did not move. It lay in its stone trench like a strip of night, straight as a ruler, with a skin of oil on it and ice forming at the edges.
The Cut.
"Here," said Juno.
She had stopped at a place where the ledge widened into a little stone landing, with iron rings set into the wall for tying boats, and a set of slimy steps going up from it into a gap between two buildings. Above them, at the top of the steps, Corin could see the back of a building. Black timber and gray plaster, twisted with age, leaning a little to the left, like a man listening at a door. A small yard. A water butt with ice on it. And a back door, oak, banded with iron.
"Niles's yard," said Juno. "He takes his deliveries off the Cut, from the barges. Nobody uses the front for heavy things. Everybody knows that." She sniffed. "Well. Everybody who lives on roofs."
Lyra had gone up the slimy steps two at a time. She was at the back door. She was knocking. Hard.
Far off, across the roofs, the bells began to ring.
Corin stood on the little stone landing above the black water with Juno beside him and counted.
One. Two. Three. Four.
The fourth bell after noon.
Twenty-nine minutes, said Malakor.
The back door opened a crack. A single bright eye looked out. Then the door opened wide, and Niles was standing there in his brown waistcoat with his sleeves held up by black garters and a lamp in his hand, staring at Lyra on his step, and at Corin and Juno coming up the slimy steps behind her, filthy and frozen and out of breath.
"Miss Thorne," he said. "What in the name of—"
Lyra held out the tobacco tin.
She did not say anything. She could not. She had no breath left. She just held out the tin in both shaking hands, with its worn-away lighthouse on the lid, and opened it, and showed him what was inside.
Niles looked down at the ticket.
For a long moment he did not move at all.
Then he lifted the lamp, and looked at Lyra's face in its light, and something happened in his narrow lined face that Corin had not seen there before. It was not quite a smile. It was the look of a man who has been standing at a window for a long time, watching a road, and has finally seen someone coming up it.
"Come in," said Niles. "Quickly. All of you. And bolt the door behind you."
And behind him, from the dark of the house, from the cold back room under the skylight, patient and far away, came the knock.
Thock.
Rule Two
Niles did not take them through the back room. He took them round it, through a narrow passage that smelled of oil and damp, and out through the brown velvet curtain into the shop, where the green lamp burned on the counter and a hundred clocks ticked on the back wall in the brown dark, now, now, now.
He went behind his counter and climbed up onto his tall stool. He put the lamp down beside the great brown ledger. He held out one clean hand, palm up, through the little arched gap in the brass grille.
"The ticket, miss," he said. "If you please."
Lyra took it out of the tobacco tin. Her fingers were stiff with cold and filthy from the roofs, and she fumbled it, and nearly dropped it, and caught it again. She laid it on the pawnbroker's palm.
Niles drew it back through the grille. He laid it on the counter, under the green lamp, flat. He took the jeweler's glass from the ribbon round his neck and screwed it into his one eye. And he bent over the little yellowed card, very close, the way he had bent over Bertram's note yesterday, and looked at it.
He looked at it for a long time.
At the printing. At the number, 7714, in his own small hand. At the name. At the seal at the bottom, the circle with three small balls. He turned it over and looked at the back, at Forgive me, pressed hard into the card in brown ink gone pale, and Corin saw the small man's thin mouth tighten. Then he turned it face up again. He opened the great brown ledger at the page with the black ruler across it, and laid the ticket beside the line for 7714, and looked from one to the other. The number. The hand. The date.
Then he took the glass out of his eye and let it fall on its ribbon, and sat back on his stool.
"It is mine," he said. "My ticket. My hand. My seal. Issued on the first Tuesday of October, two years ago, against pledge seven seven one four, to Bertram Ashcombe." He looked up at Lyra. "It is good."
Lyra let out a breath that seemed to come from the bottom of her boots.
Behind her, by the door, Juno had climbed up onto a carved chest beside the window, and was kneeling on it with her nose almost touching the thick bubbled diamond panes, looking out at Gutter Street. She did not turn round. But Corin saw her small shoulders go down a little, as if she, too, had been holding her breath.
"Rule two," said Niles. "Whoever holds the ticket holds the pledge. I do not ask how they came by it." He laid one clean finger on the yellowed card. "So I will not ask, miss. I will only say that I know where it has been, because I can smell it. Pipe tobacco. Mr. Ashcombe's tobacco. And ash. Old hearth ash." His bright eye did not move from her face. "And I will say that I am glad it was you that found it."
"Thank you," said Lyra.
"Now." Niles folded his hands on the counter. "You hold the ticket. You may do one of three things. You may pay me twenty gold, today, and take the clock away. Do you have twenty gold?"
"No."
"I did not think so. Then you may leave the ticket with me, and go away, and come back with twenty gold before the first Tuesday of November, and take it then." He paused. "Or you may ask me for more time. Which you may do once, and only once, by rule, if you hold the ticket and the interest has been kept." He looked down at the ledger, at the long row of ticks. B. B. B. And then the five little masks. "And the interest has been kept."
"How much more time?"
"Three months," said Niles. "From the end of the term. To the first Tuesday of February." He looked at her. "At the same rate. One gold the month, on the first Tuesday."
"I don't have one gold a month."
"No," said Niles. "But somebody does. And somebody has been paying it, in Marrowmere gold, without being asked, since June." His mouth moved at one corner. "I do not suppose they will stop now. Do you?"
Lyra looked at him for a long moment.
"More time," she said. "Please. I ask for more time."
Niles picked up his pen. He dipped it in the inkwell on the counter. He drew the great ledger toward him, and at the end of the line for 7714, in the last column, beside the date in red ink, he wrote, in the same small neat black hand, a new line. Corin leaned to see it through the grille. Term extended three months at the request of the ticket holder. To the first Tuesday of February. And the date. Today's.
And then, in the column with the black ruler across it, the column of names, he lifted the ruler just enough to write, and wrote something, and put the ruler back.
Lyra saw him do it.
"You wrote my name," she said.
"I wrote that the ticket was presented," said Niles. "And by whom. I write it every time. It is the rule." He looked up at her. "My book does not talk while I am alive, miss. I told you so."
"And if you're not alive?"
The pawnbroker was quiet for a moment.
"Then," he said, "I expect it will talk a great deal. To anybody who cares to read it. And they will find, on that page, that a Thorne came for a Thorne clock, with a ticket that was hers by right, and asked for time, and was given it." He blotted the page with a little rocker of blotting paper. "I think that is a thing worth writing down."
He slid the ticket back to her through the grille.
"Keep it," he said. "Keep it very safe. Safer than he did. Under more than one stone." His eye went to the window, to Juno kneeling on the chest with her nose to the glass. "Somebody will want it very badly, before February."
Lyra took the ticket. She did not put it in the tobacco tin. She put it inside the collar of her dress, with everything else, close against her skin.
On the back wall, among the hundred ticking clocks, the cuckoo clock with the little wooden bird frozen outside its door made a small whirring sound, like something clearing its throat. Corin looked at it. Its hands said a quarter past four.
He looked at the others. A carriage clock on a bracket: sixteen minutes past. A tall clock in the corner shaped like a cathedral: fourteen minutes past. A little round clock with a painted face: seventeen.
They all disagree, said Malakor dryly. They always do. Ask the little man which one is right.
"Mr. Niles," said Corin. "What time is it?"
Niles took out a watch. A fat silver watch, on a chain, from his waistcoat pocket. He opened its lid with his thumb and looked at it and closed it again.
"Sixteen minutes past four," he said. "By the Archon's temple bell, which I set this by every morning, and which has not been wrong in fifty years." He put the watch away. He looked at Corin. Then at Lyra. Then at the brown velvet curtain at the back of the shop. "You have thirteen minutes. If you mean to do what I think you mean to do."
Lyra went still.
"How do you know what we mean to do?"
"Because I watched your face yesterday, miss, when he read you the time off the face of that clock. And I watched his, when he went back down the step. And I said no." Niles slid down off his stool. "I said no because it was not yours. Not by right. Not yet." He lifted the flap in the counter. "It is yours now. By rule two. You may do with your own pledge what you please, short of carrying it out of my door without paying." He held the flap up and stood beside it, small and neat in his brown waistcoat, with his one bright eye and the brass ball sewn where the other should be. "I will not stop you this time."
Lyra looked at him.
"Why?" she said. "Why are you helping us? You don't know us. You've known us for one day."
Niles was quiet for a moment.
"I have known Bertram Ashcombe for two years," he said. "Once a month. On a Tuesday. For exactly as long as it takes the big hand to go once round." He looked at the curtain. "And for two years I have watched him stand in front of that clock with his hat in his hands and wait for something. And I have never known what." His voice was very quiet. "I would like to know what, miss. Before I die. I think I have earned that much."
They went through the curtain.
Juno stayed at the window. Corin saw her as he passed, kneeling on the carved chest with her face pressed to the bubbled glass and her hand closed on the brass key at her neck.
"Juno," he said.
"I'm watching," she said. Without turning round. "Go on. If anything comes down the street, I'll shout." She paused. "And if it's the big ugly man, I'll shout louder."
The short dark passage. The oak door banded with iron. Niles took the ring of keys from his waistcoat and found the two without looking, and turned them in the locks, the top one and then the bottom, and lifted the heavy bar away and leaned it against the wall. He pushed the door open.
The cold came out to meet them.
The back room was darker than yesterday. The skylight in the ceiling had gone the color of a bruise, the heavy soft yellow-gray of the sky outside, and the light that came down through it was thin and strange and very still, like the light before a storm. It lay on the floor in its square. And in the middle of the square, by itself, the way a tree stands by itself in a field, the clock.
Tall and dark. Its carved roses and thorns going up and up. Its brass rose open at the very top. Its starry face, deep blue, almost black, with the eleven dull stones in their silver claws and the one empty claw at twelve. Its silver hands, pointing at twenty-nine minutes past four, among the painted stars. Its little dial, pointing at three.
And behind the glass door in its long dark body, the brass pendulum, hanging still.
Waiting.
Thock.
Niles lit a lamp. A small oil lamp, from a shelf by the door. He set it on the lid of the carved chest, beside the gilded birdcage, and turned the wick up, and its yellow light came out across the floor and lay on the walnut case and made the carved roses glow.
Then he took out his fat silver watch again, and opened it, and held it in his hand where they could all see it.
"Eighteen minutes past," he said quietly.
Lyra stood in front of the clock. In the square of strange still light. Where Bertram used to stand. Where the woman in white had stood, in May, and asked if it had come with all its stones.
She was shaking. Corin could see it. Her whole body, under the old brown coat, was shaking, the way a dog shakes in the cold. She did not seem to know it.
"What if it doesn't work?" she said. Very low. "What if Bertram was wrong? What if Father was—what if it's only tapping? Only a man at a window, tapping, because his hand needs something to do?"
"Then nothing will happen," said Corin. "And we'll still have the ticket. And we'll have three months."
She looked at him.
"And if it does work?"
Corin did not answer that. He did not know the answer. He took off his right glove, the gray one, and folded it, and put it in his pocket. He took the rod out of its long pocket, slowly, and held it in his bare right hand, with the carved spiral tip pointing down at the floor.
It was cold. Colder than it had ever been. So cold that it hurt his fingers.
Whatever comes out of that clock, said Malakor, very quietly, it will come fast, boy. It has been folded for six months. When it unfolds, it will not unfold gently. A pause. Be ready. And do not let her touch it. Whatever happens. Do not let the girl touch the Ash.
"Twenty minutes past," said Niles.
The clocks on the other side of the wall ticked. A hundred small voices, very faint, through the brick. Now. Now. Now.
And in the middle of the cold strange light, the great dark clock stood with its silver hands frozen among the stars, at a time that was nine minutes away, and waited for someone to tell it that the time had come.
