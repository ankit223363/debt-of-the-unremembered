Chapter 13: The Clock That Ran Backward, Part 2 of 5: The Room Over the Saddler's
The Room Over the Saddler's
The back stairs went up from a little yard behind the shop, where the frost lay white on a pile of old harness and a rain barrel had a skin of ice on it as thick as a plate. The yard door had a new lock on it, very bright, and beside the new lock, in the old gray wood, there were the splintered marks of the old one, where somebody had put a bar into the gap and pulled.
The stairs themselves were outside the house, against the back wall, wooden, steep, with a thin rail. They creaked. At the top there was a small landing with a door, painted brown, with a brass number on it that had been taken off and left only four small holes and a pale shape.
Lyra stood in front of the door with the key on its leather loop in her hand.
"He took the number off," she said.
"Yes."
"He always did that. At the house. When a room was empty. He'd take the name card off the door and put it in a drawer, so the house wouldn't remember who'd been there." She looked at the four small holes. "He'd have done it the morning he left. So the room wouldn't remember him."
She put the key in the lock and turned it.
The room was small. It was the size of the little dressing room off the long gallery at Number 3, perhaps a little bigger, with a sloping ceiling that came down low on one side and a single window on the other, set deep in the wall, looking out over the yard and down the whole long steep length of Tanner's Rise to the river. The light that came through it was gray and cold. It lay on the floor in a long pale shape like a sheet.
And the floor was a mess.
Corin had read about rooms like this in the Ledger. He did not remember them. But he knew them, the way he knew the smell of Ash, the way his hand knew how to hold the rod. Rooms where somebody had come looking for something, in the dark, in a hurry, and had not found it, and had grown angry.
The bed was against the wall under the slope of the roof. A narrow iron bed, like his own. Its mattress had been dragged half off it, and slit open from end to end with a knife, and the straw and horsehair inside it pulled out in great handfuls and thrown on the floor. The pillow had been slit too. Feathers lay everywhere, gray and soft, in drifts against the walls, and when the cold air came in through the open door behind them the feathers lifted and turned and settled again, like snow that could not decide where to land.
There was a chest of drawers by the window. Every drawer had been pulled out and turned upside down and dropped. Shirts lay on the floor. Collars. Neckcloths, very white, very carefully folded once, now trodden on. Socks, rolled in pairs. A box of shoe polish, tipped over, with black polish smeared across the boards in a long streak where a boot had stepped in it. A little leather case that had held razors, its velvet lining torn out. A shaving brush. A mug.
There was a small table with one chair, and the chair was on its side, and one of its legs had been twisted off. The table drawer had been pulled out and its bottom kicked in. There was a shelf over the table, and the books that had been on it were on the floor now, a dozen of them, with their spines broken and their pages torn out and scattered. Corin saw the title of one on a loose page by his boot. The Duties of a House Steward. With Remarks Upon the Management of Servants, Plate, Linen, and Cellars.
In the corner by the door, on a hook, there hung a coat. A long black coat, very plain, very good, the coat of a man who had been the steward of a great house for twenty-nine years. Somebody had taken it down off the hook, and gone through it, and hung it back up again, crooked. The lining was hanging out of it in strips.
Corin went to it. He did not touch it. He looked.
The lining had been cut. Not torn. Cut, neatly, with a sharp knife, all round the inside of the coat, down every seam. And on the left side, under the arm, close to where a man's heart would be, there was a small flat pocket. A little secret one, of black silk, sewn so neatly between the lining and the wool that you would never have known it was there. Unless you had known to look.
It had been slit open.
It was empty.
"Madame Hesper's," said Lyra behind him. Her voice was very low. "She made every coat in the house. She put a pocket in every one. Keep sewing pockets." She looked at the slit black silk. "And whoever came here knew it."
Your lady's aunt, said Malakor quietly, wore Madame Hesper's dresses until she was twenty-two. She would know about the pockets. She would know exactly where to cut.
Corin looked at the empty pocket for a long time.
Then he turned round and looked at the rest of the room. Slowly. Not at the mess. Through it. The way the tall woman had taught him to look at a room, letting it come to him from the edges.
The feathers. The straw. The torn pages. The clothes. The polish. The broken chair.
And, on the bare boards near the window, half hidden under a fallen collar, something small and pale and curled.
He crouched and moved the collar with one finger.
It was a petal. Long and narrow and curling, gone brown and papery and brittle, like a scrap of old parchment. It had lain there since June, he thought, in this cold room, drying. It had lost almost all its smell. But not quite. When he bent close, there was still something left in it. Very faint. Very sweet.
He did not pick it up.
"She didn't come herself," said Lyra. She was standing by the window, looking down at the petal. "She sent him. Gorse. In the dark. Over the wall." Her mouth was hard. "But she gave him a list. Where to look. Under the mattress. In the drawers. In the books. In the pocket in the coat." She looked round the wrecked room. "And the petal came off her note. Or off his coat, from standing in her house. And he didn't even notice."
"And he didn't find it," said Corin.
Lyra looked at him.
"The ticket," said Corin. "He didn't find it. If he had, she'd have gone to Niles in June with the ticket in her hand, and paid her twenty gold, and taken the clock away." He stood up. "She didn't. She went in May, without a ticket, and offered a hundred gold, and Niles said no. And in June she sent Gorse here, and Gorse tore the room apart, and found nothing. And she's going back to Niles on the first Tuesday of November with a hundred gold and no ticket, because she still hasn't got it."
Lyra's eyes went wide.
"So it's still here," she said.
"Or it was never here at all."
They looked at each other.
He was that kind of man, Niles had said. It will be somewhere he thought nobody would look.
Corin looked round the room again. More slowly this time. Not for the mess. For the man.
Bertram Ashcombe. Who had kept his room like a barracks. Whose collars were folded in exact squares, and whose socks were rolled in exact pairs, and whose books were about the management of plate and linen. Who took the name cards off the doors of empty rooms so that the house would not remember who had been there. Who had polished a clock every month for a year and a half for exactly as long as it took the big hand to go once round. Who had hidden a garden key under the third stone of a step, and told a girl in a kitchen at night exactly where it was. They will never think to look.
A careful man. A man who did things the same way every time. Because the same way was the right way, and the right way was the only way he knew.
Corin's eyes went to the fireplace.
It was a small fireplace. Tiny. Hardly bigger than a bread oven, set into the wall opposite the window, with a narrow iron grate and a little iron fender in front of it. Gorse had been at it, too. The ash had been raked out of the grate onto the hearth, gray and cold and old, and stirred, and kicked. The iron fender had been pulled out from the wall and thrown aside. Somebody had put a hand up the chimney, too; there was soot on the wall beside it, in the shape of a big palm with thick fingers, and soot smeared on the floor where the hand had wiped itself.
But the hearth was not touched.
The hearth itself. The flat stones in front of the grate, where the fender had stood. They were old stones, gray and smooth and worn. Five of them, laid side by side in a row, each about the size of a large book. The ash lay on them, and the soot, and a few gray feathers. But nobody had lifted them. Why would they? You could see they were stones. You could see they were set into the floor. You could see there was nothing on them but ash.
Corin went and crouched in front of them.
He counted them. From the left. One. Two. Three. Four. Five.
Then from the right. One. Two. Three.
The middle one. The third from either end.
It looked like the others. Gray. Smooth. Old. Ash on it. But when he bent very close, so close that his cheek was nearly on the cold floor, and looked along the edge of it with one eye shut, the way a carpenter looks along a plank to see if it is straight, he saw something that he would never have seen if he had not been looking for it.
The gap between the third stone and the fourth was a hair wider than the gap between the fourth and the fifth.
Just a hair. The width of a thread. The width of a sheet of paper.
And there was no ash in it.
The ash lay over all the stones, in the cracks between them, gray and fine. But in that one crack, the one along the edge of the third stone, the ash had fallen through. As if there were nothing underneath to stop it.
"Lyra," said Corin. He did not look up. "How did Bertram hide the garden key?"
There was a long silence behind him.
"Under the third stone," said Lyra.
Her voice was a whisper.
"Of the step," she said. "The garden step. Miss Lyra, the key is under the third stone of the step. He said it in the kitchen. He made me say it back to him." She came across the room through the feathers and the straw and knelt beside him on the cold floor in her old brown coat, and looked at the five gray stones of the hearth, and at the middle one. "He always did things the same way. Always. He said if you do a thing the same way every time, you never have to remember how you did it. You only have to remember that you did."
She put out her hand toward the third stone. It was shaking.
Then she stopped, and looked at Corin.
"You do it," she said. "I can't. My hands won't."
Corin took off his gray gloves. He folded them and put them in his pocket. He put his bare fingers on the edges of the third stone, where the gap was a hair too wide, and pressed.
It moved.
Not much. A tiny shift. A sliding feeling under his fingertips, as if the stone were sitting on something smooth. He pressed harder on the near edge. The far edge lifted, just a little, out of the floor, with a small dry scrape.
And from the long pocket at his ribs, very softly, Malakor said: Oh, well done, old man. Well done.
The Third Stone
The stone came up with a sound like a breath let out.
Corin lifted it by its near edge and tipped it back, slowly, until it leaned against the iron grate, and the cold gray light from the window fell into the place where it had been.
There was a hollow underneath. Small and square and neat, no deeper than his hand was long, dug out of the old mortar and lined with thin flat pieces of slate, the way the hollow under the garden step at Number 3 had been lined. Bertram had done it the same way. Of course he had. A thing done the same way every time never has to be remembered.
And in the hollow, there was a package.
Oilcloth. Black and waxy. Tied with tarred string, in a knot that Corin did not know the name of, but that his hands seemed to know how to untie. It was flat, and a little longer than his palm, and when he lifted it out it was heavier than it looked, and something inside it shifted with a small hard clink.
He did not open it. He held it out to Lyra.
She took it in both hands. She knelt on the cold floor among the feathers and the straw and the torn pages of The Duties of a House Steward, with the wrecked room all around her and the gray light on her face, and she looked down at the black oilcloth in her lap for a long time.
Then she untied the string.
Inside the oilcloth there was a tin. A flat tin box, dented at one corner, with a hinged lid. The lid had once been painted, a long time ago, with a picture of a ship under sail on a blue sea and a lighthouse on a rock, but most of the paint had worn away under a thumb, and only the lighthouse was left, and part of the sea. A tobacco tin. The kind old men keep in the pocket of their coat and take out in the evening by the fire.
It smelled of pipe tobacco when Lyra opened it. Old and sweet and brown. The same smell Corin had found yesterday under the beeswax on the walnut case of a clock.
There were three things inside.
The first was a card.
It was small and square and stiff, cream-colored once, gone yellow at the edges. Printed at the top: N. NILES. And three brass balls. And a line, and on the line, in very small, very neat black ink, the hand Corin had seen on a hundred little brown paper tags in a dark shop on Gutter Street: 7714. And under that, another line: B. Ashcombe. And under that, the terms, in very small print. And at the bottom, pressed into the card, a seal. A circle, with three small balls inside it.
The ticket.
Lyra held it in her fingers. Her hand was shaking so hard that the card shook too.
"It's here," she said. "It was here all the time. Under the floor. Under the ash." She laughed, a small cracked breathless laugh. "Gorse kicked the ash all over it. He stood on it. He put his hand up the chimney and wiped it on the floor right next to it. And he never once—"
She stopped. She turned the ticket over.
On the back, in a different hand, an old man's careful upright hand, in brown ink gone pale, there were two words. Small. Pressed hard into the card, so that you could feel them with your thumb from the front.
Forgive me.
Lyra looked at them for a long time.
"Niles said," she whispered. "He said Bertram wrote forgive me on the back of his first ticket. Under his name. And Niles asked what it meant, and Bertram said it was none of his business." She swallowed. "Forgive him for what? For what?"
Corin did not know. He looked at the second thing in the tin.
It was a paper. Folded small. Very small, into a tight little square, the way Lyra's mother had folded her list, the way Corin had folded his. Lyra put the ticket down very carefully on the oilcloth in her lap, and unfolded the paper with fingers that would hardly obey her.
It was a note. The same careful upright hand. Short. Written, Corin thought, quickly, but not carelessly. Bertram did nothing carelessly. Written by a man who knew that he did not have long, and wanted to use the time he had exactly right.
For whoever finds this —
If you have found this, you are either Miss Lyra, or you are someone she trusts. Nobody else knows the third stone. Nobody else would think to look under it.
The clock stopped at twenty-nine minutes past four, on a Wednesday. His Lordship stopped it. With his own hand. On the last night. And then he told me what I must do, and I have done it, every month, as long as I could.
He said the clock will only listen on its own day, and in its own minute. It will not hear you at any other time. You must bring the ticket to Mr. Niles first, so that it is yours by right. Then stand in front of the clock on a Wednesday, at twenty-nine minutes past four, and tell it the time. On the glass. With your hand. Four, two, nine, three. As His Lordship taps it.
Then it will run.
I have put something of my own inside it, to keep it company while it waits, and to tell you what I saw. It is all I had left to give. It is not much. But it is true.
Do not let her be there when it runs.
— B.
Lyra read it once to herself. Then out loud, very slowly, to Corin. Then she sat on the cold floor of the wrecked room with the note in one hand and the ticket in her lap and did not say anything at all.
His Lordship stopped it. With his own hand.
He said the clock will only listen on its own day, and in its own minute.
Do not let her be there when it runs.
Corin looked at the little window. At the gray sky beyond it, heavy and low and soft, the color of the inside of a pillow. He did not know what time it was. He had not heard the bells for a while. He thought it must be past one.
Four twenty-nine. On a Wednesday.
Today.
If you miss it, said Malakor very quietly, it is a week. Seven days. Until the next Wednesday. A pause. And next Wednesday is still before the first Tuesday of November. Still inside the term. You would still have the ticket.
So we could wait.
You could. If you were sure that nobody else knew. If you were sure that a woman in white with a hundred gold, who has been watching this clock since May, did not also know the day and the minute. The rod's voice was very low. A young man in gray came to the Bank this morning to tell you not to be late. Do you think he came to tell you because it does not matter?
Corin looked down at the third thing in the tin.
Lyra had not touched it yet. It lay in the bottom of the tobacco tin, where the ticket and the note had covered it. A small square of paper. Not folded. Flat. With a few lines of writing on it, in brown ink, in the same careful hand. A short list. A column of names, one under the other, with a little mark beside some of them.
Corin did not need to be told what it was. He had one himself. Folded small, under his left arm, close to his heart.
At the top, Bertram had written: Owed.
And under it:
Her Ladyship. ✓
His Lordship. ✓
Miss Lyra.
Master Rowan.
Abel. ✗
Dr. Aske. ✗
Capt. Vance.
Madame Hesper.
The little one.
The last line had no mark beside it at all. Only a small question mark, very faint, in pencil, as if it had been added later and then almost rubbed out.
Lyra had seen it. She was looking at it now, over his shoulder, very still.
"The little one," she said.
Corin did not answer. He thought of a nursery door frame in an empty house on the Hill, with five small pencil lines on the outside edge and the name beside them cut away with a knife. He thought of a bell on a kitchen board with its label painted over. He thought of an empty chair at a Midwinter dinner, set with a plate and a cup, for someone who couldn't come.
He thought of the rubbing in his own secret pocket. One letter. W. And a leaf.
"The ticks," said Lyra. Her voice was very thin. "Mother has a tick. And Father. And Abel has a cross. And Doctor Aske." She looked at the list. "Madame Hesper said Mother's list had marks too. Some ticks and some crosses. And she didn't know what they meant."
"Abel went to the coast with your mother," said Corin slowly. "And didn't come back. And Doctor Aske was found under the Lantern Bridge steps."
Lyra's face went white.
"A cross means dead," she said.
"I think so."
"And a tick—" She stopped. She looked at the two ticks. Her Ladyship. His Lordship. "A tick means—what? Paid? Safe? Done?" Her voice was rising. "Father isn't safe. He sits at a window and doesn't know his own name. How is that a tick? How is that safe?"
Corin did not know.
But he looked at the tick beside Her Ladyship. Small, and neat, and black, and certain. Not a cross. A tick. The same mark Bertram had put beside a man who was alive, and sitting at a window, and tapping on a wall.
Not a cross.
Healthy as a horse, he thought. Why the sea?
He did not say it out loud. He did not need to. He saw Lyra see it too. He saw it go into her face, slowly, the way light goes into a dark room when somebody opens a shutter a crack, and she put her hand over her mouth.
"No," she said through her fingers. "No. Don't. Don't make me hope that. Not today. I can't. Not today."
"I'm not making you hope anything," said Corin quietly. "I don't know what it means. It's only a mark on a paper."
"Then put it away."
He put it away. He folded Bertram's little list very small, and Lyra took it from him without looking at it, and put it inside the collar of her dress, with the iron key and her mother's handkerchief and Rowan's note and Niles's blank ticket and Bertram's note from the garden step. She would rattle, soon, Corin thought, if she went on like this. She would have to go to Madame Hesper and ask for a bigger pocket.
She put the real ticket, 7714, B. Ashcombe, Forgive me, into the tobacco tin on its own, and closed the lid on the worn-away lighthouse, and held it in both hands against her chest.
Then she stood up.
"What time is it?" she said.
As if in answer, far off, across the roofs of the Ink Quarter, a bell began to ring. Then another. Then all of them. The slow iron voices of the whole city, calling to each other through the cold gray afternoon, never quite together.
Corin counted.
One. Two.
The second bell after noon.
"Two hours," said Lyra. "And a half. And Gutter Street is a quarter of an hour from here. Less. Down Needle Street and past the Inkwell." She let out a breath. "We have time. We have more than enough time."
Then why, said Malakor softly, did a young man in gray walk into the Third Basement this morning to tell you not to be late?
Corin went to the window.
It was small and deep-set and dirty, and the glass in it was old and bubbled, like the glass in Niles's shop. But it looked straight down Tanner's Rise. All the long steep length of it. The leather shops. The cobbler and the glover and the strap-seller. The iron rail. The shallow worn steps. The old gray donkey with its load of hides, going down. All the way to the bottom, where the Rise met the Docks road along the river.
And at the bottom, at the corner, where the steps ended and the cobbles began, a closed black carriage was standing.
It had no crest on its door. Its horses were very good. Their breath went up in white clouds in the cold. And on the box, wrapped in a heavy gray coachman's coat with three short capes over the shoulders, sat a man so big that the carriage leaned a little toward his side, on its springs.
He was not looking up the Rise. He was not looking at anything. He was sitting very still on the box with the reins in his huge hands, the way a cat sits by a mousehole. Waiting.
Ah, said Malakor.
Corin stepped back from the window.
"Lyra," he said. "Is there another way down?"
