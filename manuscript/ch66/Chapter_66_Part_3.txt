Chapter 66: Saint Clement's, Part 3 of 5: The Service
The Service
The priest was old.
He came out of the little door beside the altar in a white robe with a black band round the hem, very slowly, leaning on a carved staff, with a boy in a white shirt walking beside him carrying a book. He was so old that his head shook a little all the time, gently, like a leaf on a still day. His hair was thin and white and stood up round his head like the fluff on a dandelion. But his voice, when he began, was big and deep and slow, much bigger than he was, and it filled the low cold church from end to end and came back off the stone.
Rowan did not hear most of it.
He tried. He sat very straight in the cold pew with his hands on his knees and his eyes on the old man's face and tried to listen. But the words went past him. They went past like carts on a road, one after another, and he watched them go and could not have said what was in them.
The Saints keep him. The Saints keep all who sleep. Here we lay down the one we have loved. Here we give him back.
There was singing. Rowan stood up when the others stood up. Aunt Cressida held the hymn book open between them in her black-gloved hands, and the words were there on the page, and the tune was one he knew, one he had sung here every turn of the year of his life, standing between his father and his sister. He opened his mouth.
Nothing came out.
He stood there with his mouth open and the book in front of him and the thin, ragged singing of thirty people going on round him in the cold, and his throat would not work. It had closed. Like a fist. He could breathe through it, just. But nothing would come out of it.
He shut his mouth again and stared at the page.
Beside him, Aunt Cressida was singing in a high, sweet, trembling voice, very correct, every note in its place.
And from halfway down the church on the left, there came another voice.
It was loud. It was rough and cracked and a little flat, and it was much too loud for a church, and it was not trying to be correct at all. It came up out of the pews like a goose coming up out of a pond. And it sang every word. Every word of every verse, out loud, with all its strength, as if it had been told that the man in the box could hear it and it wanted to be sure he did.
Rowan turned his head.
It was the old woman with the basket of candles. Mrs. Doyle, someone in the pew behind him whispered. She was standing up very straight with her red hands holding the back of the pew in front of her, and her broad red face tipped up toward the roof, and her eyes shut, and she was singing. Not looking at the book. She did not need the book. She knew the words. She sang them as if she had sung them every Sunday of her life, which, Rowan thought, she probably had.
And she was singing loud enough for two.
He understood that, suddenly. Standing there with his shut throat and his open book. She had seen him. She had seen him open his mouth and nothing come out, from where she stood halfway down the church. And she was singing for him. His part, and hers. Loud. So that the song would still be the right size. So that the man in the box would not notice there was one voice missing from the front pew.
Rowan looked back at his book. The words swam.
He did not sing. He could not. But he stood, and he held his side of the book, and he listened to the old woman singing for him, and it got him through to the end of the hymn.
Then they sat down, and the priest spoke again.
And this time Rowan heard it. All of it. Every word. He did not know why. Perhaps because of the singing. Perhaps because the old man's voice had changed, and gone softer, and slower, the way a voice goes when it is saying something it believes.
"We ask the Saints," said the priest, "to take Aurelian Thorne into the long room."
The church was very quiet. The candles stood straight in the cold air.
"The long room where nothing is forgotten," said the priest. "Where every day of a life is kept. Every face. Every word. Every kindness done and every kindness left undone. Where nothing that was ever given away is lost, and nothing that was ever taken is missing. We ask that he be put there, among all that he was, and that he be allowed to walk in it, and know it all again, and be whole."
His old head shook gently.
"He lost a great deal," said the priest. "In his last years. We all know it. We do not need to say how. He lost his days, and his words, and his house, and, at the end, the faces of the people who loved him." He paused. "But in the long room, nothing is lost. In the long room, he will find his wife's face. And his children's faces. And his own name. Waiting for him. Where he left them."
Rowan did not move.
"And we who are left," said the priest, "we ask the Saints to help us carry what he cannot carry now. His name. His house. His children. Until we too go into the long room, and give it all back to him."
He lifted one thin old hand over the coffin.
"Rest, Aurelian," he said. "Rest. It's all still there."
And Rowan thought, very clearly, the way you think a thing in a dream: I don't believe you.
And then, a moment after: I want to.
He sat very still in the cold pew, with his hand pressed flat on his chest over the little hard square of the letter, and did not cry. A Thorne did not cry in church.
At the back of the church, in the corner, under the little painted head with no body, Master Kross had bowed his head. Rowan did not see it. He was not looking.
The Place Beside Him
There was a space on his left.
He had felt it all through the service. Aunt Cressida was on his right, close, in her black silk and her lily smell, with her arm against his. On his left there was nobody. The pew went on, empty, polished, dark, to the little latched door at the end by the aisle. There was room on it for four more people. There was room for one.
She should have been there.
Not because she was the eldest, though she was. Not because it was proper, though it was. Because she always had been. Every turn of the year, every year of his life, in this pew, in this church, under these saints. Father on the end by the aisle, because he had to get up and read. Lyra next to him. Then Rowan. Lyra in her new coat, fidgeting, counting the saints under her breath. Rowan beside her, being good, because one of them had to be. And her elbow in his ribs, at the dull parts, and her whisper, look at the one with the eyes on a dish, Ro, he looks like Mr. Pallister.
And he would laugh, and Father would turn his head and look at them both, and they would stop. And then, a moment later, Father's mouth would twitch at one corner, very slightly, and they would know he was not angry.
The space on his left was cold.
He did not look at it. He looked at the box.
Where was she? He did not know. North, the letter said. Somewhere north. Somewhere she could not tell him, because someone might make him tell. With a harvester with gray hands who was to be held on sight. Was she warm? Was she eating? Was she, right now, at this moment, at the second bell after noon, thinking about this church?
He thought she was. He did not know how he knew. But he thought, wherever she was, she knew what time it was. She always knew what time it was. She had learned to tell it by the sun, from Miss Penhallow, out of spite, so that she could prove Miss Penhallow wrong, and then she had never been able to stop.
She would be facing south, he thought. Toward here.
He sat up straighter in the pew. As if she could see him.
The priest had finished. He stood by the coffin with his staff, his head shaking gently, and the boy beside him closed the book. And from the side of the church, from the little door by the altar, six men came out.
They were not cadets. They were not the men from the Ministry who had carried the box out of Master Kross's house on Friday. They were older. Big, slow, broad men in black coats that did not fit them very well, as if the coats had been borrowed for the day. Two of them had beards. One of them had only half his left ear. And they walked the way soldiers walk, all six together, in step, without anyone giving the step.
Rowan did not know who they were.
Then he saw Bertram, in the pew across the aisle, looking at them. And he saw the old butler's face, and the way his hand had tightened on the head of his stick, and he understood.
The guard. The house guard from Crown Walk. The men his father had paid, for years, to stand at the doors and walk the garden at night. Men Oren Vance had drilled in the yard every morning while Lyra watched from the schoolroom window instead of doing her sums. When Crown Walk was shut up, they had been let go. All of them. With a month's pay and no character, because there had been no one left to write one.
They had come back.
Rowan did not know who had found them. Bertram, perhaps. Or they had read the notice in the paper and come on their own. But they were here. Six of them. In borrowed coats. To carry him.
They went to the coffin and stood round it, three on each side, and bent, and put their hands under it. And lifted. All together. Slowly. Without a sound. And put it up on their shoulders, and stood there for a moment with it, steady, the way men stand with a weight they know how to carry.
The heather wreath on the black cloth trembled and was still.
Then they turned, and began to walk.
Not down the aisle, toward the door. The other way. Past the altar, to the right, toward a low dark arch in the corner of the church where there was a door of old black wood, standing open. And beyond the door, steps. Going down.
The priest went first, with his staff, and the boy beside him with a lantern. Then the six men with the box. Then, after a moment, Aunt Cressida stood up, and so Rowan stood up too.
He went out of the pew. Past the cold space on his left, where nobody was.
And as he went, very quietly, without moving his lips, so that nobody could see, he counted the saints on the wall as he passed them. One. Two. Three. All the way to twenty-two. And then, at the door of the crypt, he turned his head and looked back up the long gold-lit church, past the scattered people standing in the pews, past the Duke with his white hands and the old woman with her candles and the round bald man with his hat, to the little painted head over the far door, with no body, looking down.
Twenty-three, he thought.
And went down the stair.
The Vault
The stair went down in a tight curve, turning to the left, round and round a thick stone post in the middle. The steps were narrow at the inside and wide at the outside, and very worn, each one hollowed in the middle like a spoon, so that you had to put your foot on the outside edge to be sure of it. There was no rail. There was only the curved stone wall on the right, rough and cold and damp, and Rowan kept his right hand flat on it as he went down, the way you would on a ship's ladder.
Ahead of him the lantern went round the curve and the light went with it, yellow and swinging, throwing the shadows of the six men and the box up onto the wall and down again. He could hear their breathing. Slow, heavy, careful. He could hear the scrape of their boots on the stone, all together, in step. Once one of them said something very low, a single word, and they all stopped, and shifted the weight, and went on.
Behind him came Aunt Cressida, holding his shoulder, and her breath was quick and shallow, and her lily smell came down the stair with her. Behind her, the others. He did not look back to see who.
It got colder as they went down. And darker. And the smell changed.
Upstairs it had been wax and wool and dust. Down here it was stone. Wet stone and cold earth and something under that, dry and faint and sweetish, like old paper, like the inside of a drawer that has not been opened for a hundred years. Rowan breathed it in and felt it go down into his chest and stay there.
He counted the steps. He could not help it. Lyra would have.
Thirty-one.
At the bottom there was a low passage, so low that the six men had to bend their knees and lower the box from their shoulders to their hands to get it through. The roof was a curve of old brick, black with lamp smoke. The floor was beaten earth, dry and hard. And on either side, as the lantern went past, there were openings in the walls. Low dark arches, one after another, each with a name cut over it in the stone.
ASHCOMBE. FELL. OST. LANGLEY. MARROW.
The old families of the Hill. All of them down here, in the dark, under the church, each family in its own room, the way they had each had their own house up above. Rowan saw the arches go by in the swinging light, and the black openings under them, and did not look in.
The passage ended in a door.
It was not like the other openings. It was a real door, of oak, very thick and very old, black with age, with great iron hinges shaped like leaves and a ring of iron for a handle. It was standing open. Someone had come down earlier and opened it, and set lanterns inside, so that a soft yellow light came out of the doorway and lay across the earth floor of the passage.
And over the door, cut deep into the stone of the arch, there was the tree.
Rowan knew it. He had known it all his life. It was on the seal his father wore on his right hand. It was carved over the fireplace in the hall at Crown Walk. It was on the gate. It was stitched on the corner of every napkin in the house. A tree, with a straight trunk and round spreading branches, and in the branches, as if it had grown there, a crown.
THORNE.
The priest went in first. Then the six men, bending low under the arch with the box between them. Then Rowan.
The vault was small.
He had thought it would be bigger. He had been here once before, when he was very small, so small that he did not remember anything of it except the dark and his father's hand. In his mind it had been huge. A great dark hall. It was not. It was a low square room, perhaps twelve paces across, with a roof of four low arches meeting in the middle on a short thick stone pillar. The walls were rough stone. And all round the walls, from the floor to the roof, there were shelves.
Stone shelves. Deep ones. Four rows of them, one above the other, cut into the walls or built out from them, like the shelves in a larder. And on the shelves, lying end to end, there were the boxes.
Long boxes. Dozens of them. Some were dark wood, like his father's. Some were stone, with carved lids. Some were lead, gray and dull, gone white and powdery at the corners. The oldest ones, on the bottom shelf and in the far corners, were so old that the wood had gone black and soft, and one had split along its side, and Rowan did not look at what was in the split. Each box had a plate on it, at the end that faced the room. Brass, most of them. Some were so green you could not read them. Some had been polished, not long ago, and shone in the lantern light.
The Thornes. All of them. His grandfather, and his grandfather's father, and their wives and their brothers and their children who had died small. Two hundred years of Thornes, lying on shelves in the dark under Saint Clement's, with their names on brass plates at their feet.
The lanterns had been set on the floor, along the walls, and one on the stone pillar in the middle, and the light came up from below, yellow and soft, and made the shadows of the shelves go up the walls and across the low curved roof in long black stripes.
It was very quiet. Quieter than any place Rowan had ever been. The sound of the six men's breathing was very loud.
On the right-hand wall, the second shelf up, at about the height of a man's chest, there was a space.
An empty space. Long and dark. Exactly the length of a box. The stone at the back of it was clean, where the dust had been swept away. Somebody had come down and swept it. For him.
And beside the empty space, on the same shelf, close, so close that they would be almost touching, there was another box.
It was newer than the rest. Not new. But newer. It was a dark polished wood, like his father's, but smaller. Narrower. There was no dust on it. Its brass plate shone, as if it had been polished that morning.
Rowan knew whose box it was before he could read the plate. He had always known it would be there. He had only been too small, the last time, to look.
His mother's.
Too Light
The priest said the words.
They were short, down here. Not like upstairs. A few lines only, in the big deep voice gone soft, with the lantern held up by the boy so that the old man could read from the book. Rowan did not hear them. He was looking at his mother's box.
He had not thought about her in a long time. Not really. He had been five when she went. He had a few things of her. A smell, of some flower, not lilies, something sharper and greener. A voice, on the stairs, at night, singing. A hand on his head. Her saying, once, when he was frightened of the dark in the nursery, don't be frightened, the dark is only the day with its eyes shut, it's all still there. That was all. He had kept those few things very carefully, the way you keep a few coins in a box, and taken them out sometimes, and looked at them, and put them back.
And here she was. On a shelf. In a box.
It did not feel like her. It felt like a box.
The priest stopped speaking. He closed the book. He looked at the six men and nodded.
They came forward with the coffin. Slowly. Carefully. The vault was small and there was not much room to turn. They brought it to the right-hand wall, to the second shelf, to the empty space beside Rowan's mother, and stood there with it at chest height, the six of them, three on each side, holding it level. Then the two at the front set the head end of it on the edge of the shelf. And began to slide it in.
It was a tight fit. The space was exactly the size of the box, and the box was heavy, and the stone was rough. It went in a little way and stuck. The men at the back pushed. It moved, and scraped, and stuck again. One of the bearded men said something under his breath. The man with half an ear put his shoulder to the foot of it.
And Rowan saw the trouble.
The edge of his father's box had caught against the edge of his mother's. The two boxes were so close on the shelf that the new one could not go in without pushing the old one aside a little. Just a little. A finger's width. But his mother's box was in the way, and it would not move on its own, and the men could not reach it to move it, because their hands were full.
"Wait," said Rowan.
The men stopped.
He stepped forward. He did not think about it. He did not ask anyone. He went to the shelf, beside the men, and put his two hands on the corner of his mother's box. The near corner. The foot. Where the brass plate was.
The wood was cold. Smooth and cold and polished, under his palms, through his gloves. He gripped the corner and lifted it, a little, just enough to take the weight off the stone, so that he could move it sideways, that finger's width, out of the way.
And it came up in his hands.
It came up so easily that he almost dropped it.
He had braced for weight. He had braced his legs and his back and set his shoulders, the way you do before you lift something heavy, a sack of meal, a man's body, the way the cadets were taught on the drill ground. He had expected it to be heavy. A box of oak, with a woman in it. He had expected to have to strain.
It was not heavy.
It came up off the stone shelf in his gloved hands like a box with nothing in it. Like a drawer pulled out of a desk. Like the lid of a case. Light. So light that the corner rose almost to the level of his chin before he could stop it, and the whole box tilted on the shelf, and the far end of it knocked against the back wall with a hollow wooden sound.
A hollow sound.
The sound an empty box makes when you knock on it.
Rowan froze.
He stood there with the corner of his mother's coffin held up in his two hands at the height of his chin, and his arms locked, and did not move. He could not move. Everything in him had stopped. His heart. His breath. His thoughts. For a long moment there was nothing in his head at all except the feel of it in his hands. The lightness of it. The way it had come up.
Nobody else had seen. He was sure of that, in the part of his mind that was still working. The six men were behind him and to the side, holding his father's box, waiting, looking at the shelf. The priest was behind them, and the boy with the lantern. Aunt Cressida was by the door, with her face in her black-gloved hands.
Nobody had seen.
He set the corner down.
Carefully. Gently. A finger's width to the left, the way he had meant to. As if it were heavy. As if it took all his strength. He let it down onto the stone shelf, slowly, and took his hands off it, and stepped back.
"There," he said. His voice sounded strange to him. Quite steady. Quite ordinary. A cadet's voice. "It should go now."
The men pushed. His father's box slid into the space beside his mother's, scraping on the stone, and went all the way in, and stopped. The heather wreath on top of it brushed the shelf above and a few small dry purple flowers fell off and lay on the stone.
The men stepped back. They stood in a line, the six of them, in their borrowed coats, and bowed their heads.
And Rowan, standing among them with his hands at his sides and his face quite still, lifted his eyes, and looked past the priest and the boy and the lantern, to the door of the vault.
There was someone on the stair.
Just outside the door. On the last step of the curving stair, where the passage began, half in the light that came out of the vault and half in the dark. A thin gray man in a long gray coat, with his hat in his left hand and his right hand bare. Standing very still.
Master Kross had come down after them. Rowan had not heard him come. Nobody ever heard him come.
And he was looking at Rowan.
Not at the coffins. Not at the priest. At Rowan. And at Rowan's hands. And at the corner of the box on the shelf, the near corner, the foot, where Rowan's hands had been.
He had seen.
Rowan knew it the way you know you have been seen taking something from a table. All at once, cold, in the stomach. Master Kross had been standing on the stair, in the dark, watching, the whole time. He had seen the corner come up too fast. He had seen Rowan freeze. He had seen him set it down again as if it were heavy.
He had seen Rowan notice.
For a long moment they looked at each other across the low lantern-lit vault, over the bowed heads of the six men. Master Kross's face did not change at all. It never did. His tired gray eyes were steady.
Then, very slightly, so slightly that nobody else could have seen it, he shook his head.
Once.
And turned, and went back up the stair, without a sound, into the dark.
