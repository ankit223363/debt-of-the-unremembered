Chapter 66: Saint Clement's, Part 2 of 5: Down the Hill
Down the Hill
They walked.
Aunt Cressida had wanted the carriage. It was only a quarter of a mile to Saint Clement's, along the top of the Hill and down a little and round the corner of the Crescent, but there was snow, and she had her good shoes on, and a lady did not walk to a funeral. Rowan had said, very politely, that his father would have walked. That his father had walked to Saint Clement's every year of his life, at the turn of the year, with his children in front of him, and had never once taken the carriage, because he said a man ought to arrive at a church on his own feet.
Aunt Cressida had looked at him for a long moment. Then she had said, "Yes. He would. Very well," and sent the carriage on ahead, empty, to wait at the church gate for after.
So they walked. Rowan in his cadet black with his hands at his sides. Aunt Cressida beside him on his arm, in her black silk and her black fur cloak and a black hat with a short black veil, picking her way through the snow in her good shoes with small quick steps. Pruett behind them with an umbrella in case it snowed again. Two maids behind Pruett.
The Hill was very quiet.
It always was on a Sunday morning. The great houses stood back from the road behind their railings and their little front gardens, tall and pale and handsome, with their shutters closed. Snow lay on the railings, on the steps, on the stone urns by the doors, on the bare branches of the plane trees along the pavement. It had been swept from the pavement in front of most of the houses, in neat brown paths, but not from the road, and the road was white and smooth and empty, with only one line of cart tracks down the middle of it. A milk cart, Rowan thought. Early.
Their breath smoked. The snow squeaked under their feet. Somewhere a dog barked, once, behind a wall.
And from the top of the Hill, where the road bent round toward the Crescent, you could see the city.
Rowan stopped. He did not mean to. His feet did it.
It lay below them, all of it, spread out under the white sky. The river first, wide and gray and slow, with ice along its edges and the bridges over it, one after another, black against the gray. Lantern Bridge. The Old Bridge. The Toll Bridge with its two towers. Then the roofs, on both sides of the river, roofs and roofs and roofs, gray slate and red tile, all white-topped with snow, going away into the haze. Chimneys. Thousands of chimneys, every one with a thread of smoke going up from it, straight, into the still air. Church towers. The great dome of the Bank, dark and heavy. The Spire, far off to the left, very tall and thin and black, like a needle stuck into the sky.
And the lamps.
That was what had stopped him.
It was daytime. Gray, cold, cloudy daytime, but daytime. And all along the river, on the quays, the lamps were lit.
Not the ordinary lamps. The Ash lamps. The tall iron posts with the glass globes on top, that had stood along Lantern Quay and the river walks for longer than Rowan had been alive, and burned at night with a soft white light. They were burning now. In the day. And the light was not white.
It was blue.
A pale cold blue, like the blue at the bottom of a candle flame. All along the river, a long line of small blue lights, on both sides, from the Old Bridge down to the docks, burning in the gray morning when no lamp should be burning at all.
They had been like that for six days. Since Midwinter. Since the night of the fire.
Nobody knew why. Or nobody would say. The Ministry had put up notices saying the lamps were being repaired, and that the public was not to touch them, and that anyone found tampering with them would be held. But nobody came to repair them. They only burned, blue, day and night, and people walked past them on the quays without looking at them, the way you walk past a man who is talking to himself in the street.
"Come along, darling," said Aunt Cressida. "We'll be late."
He came along.
But as they went down the slope toward the Crescent, he could not stop thinking about it. About that night. Midwinter. He had not been there. He had been on duty at the Long Office, at his desk, copying, while the masquerade went on at the Bourse across the river. He had heard the bells, at midnight. And then, an hour after, he had heard something else. A sound like nothing he had ever heard. A long deep groan, like the earth turning over in its sleep, and every window in the Long Office had shaken in its frame. And then the shouting in the street. And the glow in the sky, over the river, orange and then white and then a color he had no name for.
In the morning, people said a man had held the roof up.
That was what they said. In the Long Office, in the cadets' mess, in the street, in the queue at the chophouse in Mercer Street where Master Kross sent him every noon for a pork pie. A man had held the roof up. The great glass dome of the Bourse had been coming down, burning, on all the people underneath, the lords and ladies in their masks and the servants and the musicians, and a man had stood in the middle of the floor with his hands up over his head, and held it. Held it up. Until they were all out. Nobody knew how. Nobody knew who. Some said he had gray hands. Some said he had burned so much to do it that there was nothing left of him, and he had walked out of the fire with no face. Some said he was a harvester. Some said he was a saint.
And on the wall of the chophouse, the morning after, there had been a new notice. Pasted over the old ones, still wet.
VANE, CORIN. HARVESTER, FORMERLY OF THIS MINISTRY. LAME IN THE LEFT LEG. GRAY TO THE SHOULDER. TO BE HELD ON SIGHT.
By order of the Inquisitor.
S. K.
Rowan had stood in front of it with his pork pie in his hand for a long time.
He knew who Corin Vane was. He knew because Lyra was with him. He knew because he had stood at a grating in a cellar under the city, weeks ago now, and lied to Master Kross's face about it, because Corin Vane was with his sister and he had not known yet whether he wanted her found.
He still did not know.
They came round the corner of the Crescent.
The Crescent was a long curve of tall white houses, all exactly the same, with black railings and black doors and fanlights over the doors like half suns. It made a half-circle round a small garden of bare trees and snow, with railings round it, and a gate. At the end of the Crescent, where the curve finished, the road went two ways. Right, back along the side of the Hill, toward Aunt Cressida's house and home. Left, down, steeply, toward the river and the old town and the Spire.
And straight ahead, at the end of a short lane between two high garden walls, there was Saint Clement's.
The bell was ringing.
One stroke. Slow and deep and heavy, so that Rowan felt it in his chest. Then a long silence. Then another.
Aunt Cressida's hand tightened on his arm.
Saint Clement's
Saint Clement's was the oldest church on the Hill, and the smallest, and the coldest.
It sat at the end of its short lane between the two garden walls as if it had been there first, before the walls, before the Hill had houses on it, before the city had come up the slope from the river and wrapped itself round. Which it had. It was built of white stone, gone gray and soft with age, the corners rounded off by three hundred years of rain. It had a squat square tower at the west end, not tall, with a little lead roof like a cap and a gilded weathercock on top in the shape of a fish. The fish had pointed south for as long as anyone could remember. The wind had stopped turning it years ago.
The windows were narrow and pointed and very old, filled with small diamond panes of greenish glass in lead, so that from outside you could see nothing through them, only the dim green shine. Over the door there was a carving of the saint himself, Clement, with an anchor in his hand, worn so smooth by the weather that his face was gone and the anchor looked like a cross.
There was snow on everything. On the roof, thick and white and smooth. On the tower cap. On the fish. On the sills of the narrow windows. On the stone wall round the churchyard, and on the old gravestones inside the wall, leaning this way and that, each one with a white cap. On the yew tree by the gate, its dark branches weighed down so low that they almost touched the path.
And on the steps.
There were five of them, up to the door, broad and shallow and worn hollow in the middle by feet. And the snow on them had been swept, but not well. Aunt Cressida had been right. Somebody had pushed a broom across them, once, quickly, and left a thin hard crust of trodden snow and ice in every hollow, gray and shining and treacherous.
"You see," said Aunt Cressida, under her breath. "I told him. I told him last year."
Rowan held her arm tighter, and they went up.
The bell was still ringing. It was right over their heads now, in the tower. Each stroke came down through the stone and into the soles of Rowan's boots. Then the long wait. Then the next.
The door was open. Both leaves of it, the great old oak doors studded with black iron nails. And as they came up onto the top step, the cold came out of the church to meet them.
It was colder inside than out. It always was, at Saint Clement's. Rowan remembered that from every turn of the year of his life. You came in out of the snow, stamping your feet, and the air inside was colder still. Old cold. Stone cold. The cold of a place that has not been warm for three hundred years and has given up trying. It went through his coat and his shirt as if they were not there, and settled on his skin.
And the smell.
Cold wax, first. Hundreds of candles, burned and put out and burned again, year after year, so that the smell of them was in the stone itself. Then wet wool, from the coats of the people already inside, steaming faintly in the cold. Then dust. Then something old and sweet and dry, like the inside of a very old book, that Rowan thought might be the smell of the dead under the floor.
And under it all, faint, from somewhere at the front, lilies.
Aunt Cressida's, he thought. She must have sent them ahead.
The church was long and narrow and low. A single aisle down the middle, paved with old flat stones, some of them carved with names and dates so worn they could hardly be read. Wooden pews on either side, dark and high-backed, with little doors at the ends that latched. Pillars of white stone, thick and round, holding up low arches. And over the arches, the roof, of dark old wood, with carved beams going across, and on the ends of the beams, carved angels with their wings folded, looking down.
And on the walls, the saints.
They were painted straight onto the plaster, between the windows, all the way down both sides of the church. Tall thin figures in long robes, red and blue and green, all faded now, the colors gone soft and chalky like colors in a dream. Each one stood with one hand raised and the other holding the thing that showed who they were. A key. A wheel. A lamb. A loaf. A pair of eyes on a dish. And round each head, a ring of gold. Faded gold, rubbed and flaking, but gold, so that when the candles were lit, the rings caught the light and shone, all down the walls, like a row of small dim moons.
Twenty-two of them. Twenty-three, if you counted the little head over the inside of the door, which was only a head, with no body, looking down at you as you came in.
Lyra had always counted it. Rowan never had.
He looked up at it now, as he came in under it. A small round face, very faded, with a ring of gold. And no body.
Twenty-three, he thought. For her.
The candles were lit. Not all of them. There were too many for that. But a great many, in the iron stands along the aisle and on the altar at the far end, small yellow flames standing very straight and still in the cold air, so that the church was full of a low gold light, and the faded gold rings round the saints' heads shone. And in front of the altar, on a low wooden frame draped with black cloth, there was the coffin.
Rowan stopped in the doorway.
He had seen it before. He had seen it come out of Master Kross's house on Steeple Lane, at dawn, on Friday, on the shoulders of six men, under a black cloth. He had stood on the steps and watched it go down the street toward the Hill in the snow. He had known it was there. In the church. Waiting. Since Friday.
But he had not seen it here.
It was a plain box. Long and dark and plain, of old oak, with brass handles. The black cloth lay over it, a heavy velvet, falling to the floor on both sides. And on top of the cloth, at the head end, there was a wreath. Not lilies. Something else. Something small and green and dark, woven in a ring.
Heather, Rowan thought, when he looked closer. Dry heather, brown and purple, the kind that grew on high moors far to the north and nowhere near the city. He did not know who could have sent it. There was a card tucked into it, very small, and on the card there was no name. Only a little drawing, in brown ink, of a sun.
He only stood in the doorway of Saint Clement's in his cadet black, with his aunt's hand on his arm and the cold coming up out of the stone into his feet, and looked down the long gold-lit aisle at his father's box.
And the bell stopped.
Who Came
There were not many.
Rowan had known there would not be. He had told himself so, all week, lying awake in the green room with the gold baskets on the walls. His father had been taken from his study two years ago and put in a bed in a house in the country where nobody came. Two years. In two years, people forget. In two years, the people who had come to Crown Walk every Midwinter, and drunk his father's wine, and played cards in the long drawing room, and told him and Lyra what tall children they were, had found other houses to go to at Midwinter. Other wine. And then the newspapers had said what they said about the Thorne name, after Lyra. And after that, nobody wanted to be seen in a church with it.
He had known. He had told himself.
It did not help.
The church held two hundred, perhaps, if they sat close. There were fewer than thirty. They were scattered through the pews in ones and twos, with great gaps between them, like the last leaves on a tree in winter. They had their coats on. Their breath smoked in the candlelight. When Rowan came down the aisle with his aunt on his arm, they turned their heads to look, and then turned back, quickly, and looked at their hands.
He did not look at them. He looked straight ahead, at the box, the way a cadet looks straight ahead on parade. But he saw them anyway. Master Kross had trained him to see a room without looking at it.
He saw the Duke of Marrow.
The Duke was in the third pew on the right, alone, at the end by the aisle. He was a big man, broad and heavy, with a red face and thick white hair brushed straight back. He was dressed in black, very fine, with a black silk stock. And his hands were in bandages. Both of them. White linen, wound thick and clean round each hand from the fingers to the wrist and up inside the sleeves, so that his hands lay on the back of the pew in front of him like two white paws. He could not have held a hymn book. He could not have held anything. He sat very straight and looked at the coffin, and his red face was wet.
The fire, Rowan thought. The Bourse. He was there.
He saw a short round man in a caped coat, three pews behind the Duke, holding a wide hat in both hands on his knees. Bald, with a fringe of gray hair round the back of his head. He did not know the man's name. He had seen him before, somewhere. At the Long Office, perhaps. Or in the street outside Master Kross's house. The man saw him looking and gave him a small, sad nod, and Rowan, without knowing why, nodded back.
Tibbs. The name came up out of somewhere at the back of his mind. Tibbs. A man who came and went with parcels and messages for people who did not trust the post, and was never quite seen arriving.
He saw an old woman in a rusty black shawl, sitting very upright in a pew halfway down on the left, with a big basket on the seat beside her. A basket full of candles. Plain tallow candles, the cheap kind, dozens of them, tied in bundles with string. She had a broad red face and red raw hands and she had walked, Rowan could see, a long way through the snow, because the hem of her skirt was wet and dark to the knee. He did not know her at all. She did not look like anyone who belonged on the Hill. She looked like someone who scrubbed the steps of a church, not someone who sat in one. But she sat there as if she had every right, with her red hands folded on her knees, and looked at his father's box with a face so full of plain kind sorrow that Rowan had to look away from it.
He saw Bertram.
Old Bertram, his father's butler, in the front pew on the left, the family pew, at the very end, as far from the aisle as he could get. As if he did not think he had a right to be in it at all. He was very small. He had always been small, but he was smaller now, shrunk inside his black coat like a nut in a shell. His white side-whiskers were very neat. His hands were folded on top of a black stick. When Rowan came level with him, the old man looked up, and his eyes filled, and he stood up, slowly, on the stick, and bowed. Low. The way he had bowed to Rowan's father at the door of Crown Walk every evening of Rowan's life.
Rowan's throat closed.
He stopped. He let go of his aunt's arm. And he bowed back to Bertram. Not a cadet's bow. The bow his father had taught him, for the people who had served the house. A short, deep, plain bow, from the waist, with his eyes down.
Bertram sat down again, slowly, and put his face in his hands.
Aunt Cressida took Rowan's arm again, very tight, and led him into the front pew on the right. The other family pew. They sat.
The pew was very cold. The wood was hard and polished and the cold came up out of it through his breeches. In front of him, close, so close he could have reached out and touched it, was the black cloth over the box, and the heather wreath, and the little card with the sun on it.
He did not look round again. But he knew who else was there. He had seen him as they came in.
At the back. In the very last pew, in the corner, under the little painted head with no body. Alone. No cadets with him. No Wren in her white coat. Only a thin gray man in a long gray coat with his hat on the seat beside him, sitting very still, with his gloved hands folded in his lap.
Master Kross.
He had not looked at Rowan when Rowan came in. He had been looking at the coffin. But Rowan had seen, as he passed, that Master Kross had taken his right glove off, and was holding it in his left hand.
He only did that for things that mattered.
