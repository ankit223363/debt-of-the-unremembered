Chapter 66: Saint Clement's, Part 5 of 5: The Railings
The Railings
Master Kross was waiting by the railings.
Rowan saw him as soon as he turned from the steps. He was standing a little way along the churchyard wall, outside it, on the pavement of the lane, where a length of black iron railing ran between the end of the wall and the corner of the next garden. Alone. In his long gray coat, buttoned to the throat, with his gray hat on. There was snow on the brim of the hat. It had begun to snow again, lightly, while they were in the vault. Small dry flakes, coming down very slowly out of the white sky, not settling, only drifting, like ash.
He was not looking at the church. He was looking down the lane, toward the Crescent. His gloved hands were folded on the top of the railing in front of him.
Both gloves were on again.
Rowan stood at the bottom of the steps. Aunt Cressida was not in the lane. He looked for her and could not see her. Perhaps she had gone to the vestry. Perhaps to the carriage. The carriage was at the gate, at the other end of the lane, black, with its curtains drawn and its two gray horses standing with their heads down in the falling snow.
He should go and find her. He had promised.
And instead of turning toward the gate, he turned the other way.
He did not decide to. His feet did it.
He walked along the lane, in the falling snow, to the railings, and stopped beside Master Kross. Not close. An arm's length away. The way a cadet stands beside his master on parade. He put his hands behind his back.
Master Kross did not look at him.
For a long time neither of them said anything. The snow came down between them, slow and dry. Down the lane, at the Crescent, a cart went by, muffled, its wheels quiet in the snow. The bell in the tower of Saint Clement's had stopped. Everything had stopped. It was very quiet.
Rowan's heart was going very hard.
He thought about the vault. About Master Kross on the stair in the dark, watching. About the slight shake of the head. Once.
What had it meant? Don't. Don't say. Don't ask. Don't think it. Or something else. Something he did not know.
He did not ask about it.
He asked about the other thing instead. The thing he had been not-asking for two weeks. Since the grating in the cellar. Since the letter. Since the night of the fire.
"Where is she?" he said.
His voice came out quite steady. He was pleased with that. A cadet's voice.
Master Kross did not answer at once. He stood with his hands folded on the railing and looked down the lane at the snow. Then he said, in his quiet exact voice, the voice he used in the Long Office for reading out the Thursday list:
"She went north."
Rowan waited.
"By the dyke road," said Master Kross. "On Thursday night. In the snow. With the harvester, and Captain Vance." A pause. "They took a punt through the fens. By now they are in the hills. Past them, perhaps."
North.
The letter had said north. It had not said the dyke road. It had not said Thursday. It had not said Captain Vance, though Rowan had guessed. Oren. Old Oren, who had taught him to sit a horse.
He stood very straight with his hands behind his back.
"How do you know?" he said.
And Master Kross turned his head, at last, and looked at him.
His eyes were gray. Tired gray, the gray of old pewter, with heavy lids. Rowan had looked into them every day for two years across the desk in the glass office at the end of the Long Office, and he had never once been able to tell what was behind them. He could not tell now.
"Because it is my business to know," said Master Kross. "It is the only thing I am good at."
Rowan held his eyes.
And then he asked the real question. The one under the other one. He did not know he was going to ask it until it was out.
"Did you let her?"
The snow came down.
Master Kross did not look away. He did not move at all. He stood with his gloved hands on the railing and his tired gray eyes on Rowan's face, and he did not answer. The silence went on. It went on so long that Rowan thought he was not going to answer at all. That he was going to do what he always did. Turn away. Say, that will do, Cadet. Go back to your desk.
It went on.
Then Master Kross said:
"Yes."
One word. Very quiet. Exactly the same voice as the rest. As if he had said, it is snowing, or the list is on the board.
Yes.
Rowan did not know what he felt.
It came up in him all at once, everything, so fast that he could not sort it. He wanted to hit him. He wanted to hit him in his tired gray face, with his fist, as hard as he could, for letting her go. For letting her walk into the snow with a man who was to be held on sight. Into the north. Where Rowan could not follow. Where he could not see her or help her or know if she was cold.
And he wanted to thank him.
He wanted to thank him so badly that his eyes stung. For letting her go. For not stopping her. For not putting her on the list, or in a cell, or in one of the black closed carts that went out of the Records gate at night. For standing aside. For saying yes.
He did not do either.
He stood there with his hands behind his back, clenched, so hard his nails cut his palms through his gloves, and his face quite still, and looked at Master Kross. And Master Kross looked back.
And Rowan did not know whether the man in front of him had done it to save her, or for some other reason that Rowan could not see.
"Thank you, sir," he said at last.
He did not know which thing he meant it for. Perhaps for answering. Perhaps for the truth.
Master Kross looked at him a moment longer. Then something moved in his face. Very small. At the corner of the mouth. Gone almost before it came.
"Go to your aunt, Cadet," he said. "She is in the vestry. She is waiting for you." He turned back to the railing and looked down the lane again, toward the Crescent, where the snow was falling on the white houses. "Stay with her until she is in the carriage. You promised her that, I think."
Rowan stared at him. He had not told anyone.
"Go on," said Master Kross.
Rowan went.
But at the gate of the churchyard he looked back. And Master Kross was still standing at the railings in the falling snow, alone, with his hands folded on the iron, watching the lane. Not the church. Not the carriage. The lane. As if he were waiting for something to come up it.
Or someone.
The Vestry
The vestry was a small cold room at the back of the church, behind the altar, through a low door that Rowan had never been through in his life.
It was a plain room. Whitewashed walls, gone gray and stained with damp in the corners. A stone floor. One narrow window, high up, with plain glass in it, through which he could see the snow coming down past the bare black branches of a tree. A wooden press against one wall, with its doors open and white robes hanging inside on pegs, and the smell of them, starch and old sweat. A table, scrubbed pale, with a brass candlestick on it and a book and a jug of water with ice on the top. A wooden chair. And on a hook by the door, the old priest's black cloak.
It was colder in here than in the church. There was no fire. There never had been, by the look of the grate, which was swept and empty and had a dead spider in it.
Aunt Cressida was standing by the table.
She was alone. She had taken off her hat and the short black veil, and they lay on the table beside the candlestick, and her face without the veil was very white and very tired and much older than it had been at breakfast. The bright smile was gone. All of it. As if she had taken it off with the hat.
She had something in her hands.
She was holding it against her chest, in both her black-gloved hands, the way you might hold a cat that wanted to get down. A packet. Flat and square, about the size of a large book, wrapped in oilcloth, the dark shiny kind that sailors use, and tied round twice with tarred string, and the knots sealed with a blob of dark red wax.
"Shut the door, darling," she said.
He shut it.
She did not say anything for a moment. She stood there with the packet held against her chest, looking at him. He did not know what was in her face. Not fear, now. Or not only fear. Something more like the look of a person standing at the edge of very cold water, deciding to go in.
"Rowan," she said. "Come here."
He went to her.
She held the packet out to him. In both hands. The way you hand someone a baby.
He did not take it.
"What is it, Aunt?"
"Papers," she said. "Take it."
He took it. It was heavier than it looked. Stiff and cold and smelling of tar.
"The deeds," said Aunt Cressida. Her voice was low and quick. "To the Thorne lands. The ones that are left. The farms at Wexley, and the mill, and the house in Linden Street. Your father signed them over to me when they took him, so that the Bank couldn't have them. They're yours now. They were always meant to be yours." She drew a breath. "And the Council papers. The old ones. Your grandfather's seat, on the Council. The writ for it. It hasn't been used in two years. Nobody's sat in it. But it's still yours. It's still a Thorne seat. Nobody can take it away unless you give it up, or you're dead."
Rowan stared at her.
"And a letter," she said. "From me. In my own hand. In the purple ink, so you'll know it's mine." She tried to smile and could not. "Don't read it now. Read it later. If you need to."
"If I need to?"
She put her black-gloved hand on his arm. She held it very tight.
"Listen to me, darling. Listen. This is in case anything happens to me."
The snow came down past the high window.
"Nothing's going to happen to you, Aunt," said Rowan.
"No. Of course not. But in case." Her fingers dug into his arm through the black cloth. "In case. You keep that. You keep it on you, or you keep it somewhere nobody knows. Not in my house. Not at the Ministry. Not in your desk. Somewhere of your own." She was looking at him very hard. "And you don't give it to anyone. Do you understand me? Anyone. Whoever asks. Whatever they say. Whatever papers they show you with seals on."
"Not even Master Kross?"
She was quiet for a moment.
"Not to anyone," she said, "with a circle by their name."
Rowan did not understand.
"What circle?" he said. "Aunt, what do you mean? What circle? Whose name?"
She shook her head. Quickly. Once. And her lips pressed together, so hard they went white, and he saw that she was not going to say it. That she could not. That whatever it was, it was too big, or too frightening, or too near, to say out loud in a cold room at the back of a church with the door shut.
"Just remember it," she said. "A circle. If you ever see a list, darling, and there are names on it, and some of them have a little circle drawn beside them. In ink. Like this." She lifted her free hand and drew it in the air between them with one black-gloved finger. A small round shape. Closed. "Those are the ones you don't give it to. And those are the ones you don't trust. And if you ever see..."
She stopped.
"If I ever see what?"
She did not finish. She let go of his arm. She picked up her hat from the table and put it on, and pulled the short black veil down over her face, so that he could not see it anymore.
"Put it inside your coat," she said. Her voice was steadier now. "Inside. Against your shirt. Button it over. There. Like that. Nobody need see."
He put it inside his coat. It lay flat and stiff against his chest, cold through his shirt. On the left side. Over the little hard square of the letter. He buttoned the coat over it.
Aunt Cressida looked at him. Through the veil he could see her eyes, wet and bright.
"Your mother would have been so proud of you," she said. "I don't think I ever told you that. She would. She was always so..." She stopped again. "Never mind. Come. The carriage is waiting. You said you'd see me to it."
"Yes, Aunt."
She took his arm. Lightly, this time. Not tight. And they went out of the cold little room together, and through the empty church, past the twenty-two saints and the twenty-third with no body, and out through the porch into the snow.
The Glove
The snow was coming down more thickly now.
It had been dry and slow when he stood at the railings. Now the flakes were bigger and softer and wetter, and they came down in a steady slanting curtain, so that the far end of the lane was blurred and the white houses of the Crescent beyond it were only pale shapes. It settled on Rowan's shoulders and on the brim of Aunt Cressida's hat. It settled on the five worn steps, over the trodden ice, and made them look safe, which they were not.
They went down carefully. Rowan on the outside, Aunt Cressida on his arm, her free hand holding up the hem of her black silk out of the wet. Pruett was at the bottom with the umbrella, and he put it up over her head as she came down, and the snow drummed softly on the black cloth.
The others had gone. The lane was empty except for the carriage.
It stood at the churchyard gate, by the yew tree, where it had stood all through the service. A big closed carriage, black, glossy, with the Thorne tree-and-crown painted small on the door in faded gold, under a newer, larger painting of Aunt Cressida's own crest, a heron standing in reeds. Two gray horses in the shafts. They had been standing a long time in the cold, and they had blankets over their backs, and the snow lay white on the blankets and on their manes. They stamped. Their breath went up in clouds. The coachman sat on the box above them, huddled in a caped coat, with snow on his hat and his whip upright in its holder.
The curtains of the carriage were drawn.
Rowan noticed that. He did not know why. Aunt Cressida always had the curtains open when she drove. She liked to see and be seen. She liked to lean forward at the window as she went down the Hill and nod to people. The curtains were heavy gray velvet, and today they were drawn shut, tight, on both windows, so that you could not see in at all.
Well, he thought. It was a funeral. People drew the curtains for a funeral.
A footman got down from the back of the carriage as they came up. One of Aunt Cressida's. A tall young man in her heron livery, gray and green. He went to the door and put his hand on the handle and waited, his head bowed, the snow on his hat.
They came to the gate.
Aunt Cressida stopped.
She stopped quite suddenly, a few steps from the carriage, on the path under the yew tree, so that Rowan, who had been walking on, was pulled up short by her hand on his arm. He looked at her. Her face behind the short black veil was turned toward the carriage door. Very still.
"Aunt?"
She did not answer.
The footman, seeing them stop, lifted his head. And then, because it was his job, and because she had come to the gate and he had been told to open the door when she came to the gate, he turned the handle, and opened it.
The door swung out, toward them.
And inside the carriage, in the dim gray light behind the drawn curtains, something moved.
Rowan saw it. He was looking straight at it. He could not have helped seeing it.
A hand.
It was in a glove. A pale gray glove. Very pale, almost white, the color of a dove's breast, of fine soft kid, fitted close. A long hand. Long and thin. The fingers very long. It lifted, out of the shadow inside the carriage, from somewhere on the far seat, and rose a little into the gray light from the open door. Slowly. Gracefully. The way a lady lifts her hand to a friend across a ballroom.
And it waved.
A small wave. Only the fingers. They bent, once, all together, and straightened. Like a greeting. Like hello. Like, there you are, my dear, I have been waiting.
Then the hand went down again, into the shadow, and was gone.
Rowan blinked.
Her maid, he thought. Aunt Cressida's maid. Thursby. She often rode with Aunt Cressida in the carriage. She must have got in to wait out of the snow. Though Thursby was not tall, and her hands were short and red. And Thursby had walked to the church behind them, with the other maid. He had seen her. In the porch.
Well. Somebody's maid, then.
He turned to Aunt Cressida to say something. He did not know what.
And she was standing quite still on the path, with her hand on his arm, and her face behind the veil turned to the open carriage door, and she was not breathing.
One breath. He felt it. Or felt the lack of it. Her hand on his arm, gone rigid. Her whole body, beside him, held. Not moving. Not breathing. For one long moment, in the falling snow, under the yew tree, at the gate of Saint Clement's.
One breath.
Then she let it go.
And she took her hand off his arm. Quite gently. And turned to him, and he could see through the veil that she was smiling. A small smile. Not the bright one. A different one. A tired, quiet, almost kind smile, like the smile of somebody saying goodbye in the yard of a coaching inn.
"Thank you, darling," she said. "You were very good. Your father would have been so proud."
"Aunt..."
"Go home," she said. "Go home and get warm. Have Pruett light the fire in your room. I'll see you at dinner."
And she turned, and took the footman's hand, and put her foot on the little iron step, and got into the carriage.
Rowan saw her go into the shadow. He saw the black silk of her skirt go in after her, and the last of it pulled in out of the snow. He saw her sit down, on the near seat, facing backward. Facing the far seat.
He did not see what was on the far seat. It was too dark.
The footman shut the door.
The gray curtain on the near window moved, very slightly, and was still.
Left at the Crescent
The footman climbed up onto the back. The coachman lifted his whip from its holder and touched the gray horses on their snowy backs, not hard, and said something to them, and they leaned into their collars and the carriage moved.
Its wheels crunched in the new snow. It went slowly at first, out of the lane between the garden walls, the two gray horses stepping high and careful, their breath going up in clouds, the carriage rocking a little on its springs behind them. The snow fell on its black roof and slid off.
Rowan stood at the gate of Saint Clement's under the yew tree and watched it go.
Pruett was beside him with the umbrella. Rowan did not want the umbrella. He stood out from under it, in the snow, with the flakes settling on his bare cropped head and his shoulders and running down inside his collar, cold, and did not notice.
Inside his coat, against his shirt, the oilcloth packet lay flat and stiff and cold. Under it, over his heart, the small hard square of the letter.
He put his hand flat on his chest. On both of them.
The carriage came to the end of the lane, where it opened out onto the Crescent. It slowed there. The coachman leaned to look both ways, the way you do, though there was nothing on the road in the snow, nothing at all, only the long white curve of the Crescent and the bare trees in its garden and the tall white houses with their black doors.
Rowan watched.
At the end of the Crescent the road went two ways. He knew it the way he knew the stairs at Crown Walk. Right, back along the side of the Hill, past the big houses and the plane trees, to Aunt Cressida's gate and her lilies and her fire that was always lit. Home. Left, down. Steeply down, off the Hill, round the bend and down the long slope of the old road toward the river and the bridges and the old town, and beyond the old town, the Spire.
The carriage would go right. Of course it would. She had said, I'll see you at dinner. She was going home.
The carriage moved off from the end of the lane, and went along the Crescent, slowly, the gray horses blurred in the snow. Along the white curve. Past the black doors. Past the little garden with its bare trees. To the far end, where the road divided.
And turned left.
Rowan stood still.
He watched it turn. He watched the gray horses swing their heads round to the left and the carriage follow them, rocking, round the corner of the last white house, onto the old road. Down. Off the Hill. Toward the river. He watched the black roof of it go down below the line of the garden walls, and the last of it, the top of the coachman's snowy hat, go down after it.
And then it was gone.
Toward the Spire.
Not right. Not home. Left.
"Sir?" said Pruett, beside him, very quietly. "Master Rowan? Shall we walk back, sir? You'll catch your death."
Rowan did not answer.
He stood in the falling snow at the gate of the church where his father had just been put on a shelf in the dark beside a box with nothing in it, and looked at the empty end of the Crescent where the carriage had gone, and the snow came down and began, already, to fill in the tracks of its wheels.
She had said dinner.
She had said, I'll see you at dinner. She had said, in case anything happens to me. She had stopped on the path and not breathed, for one breath, looking into the dark of the carriage at a pale gray glove. And then she had smiled, and got in.
And turned left.
He thought of the circle she had drawn in the air between them, with one black-gloved finger. Small and round and closed.
He thought of Master Kross, at the railings, watching the lane. Not the church. The lane.
He turned his head.
The railings were empty. There was nobody there. Only the black iron bars going along the end of the churchyard wall, with snow piled on top of each one in a small white cap, and the place on the pavement in front of them where someone had stood for a long time, the snow trodden flat in the shape of two feet, already beginning to fill in.
Master Kross had gone.
Rowan did not know when. He had not seen him go.
He stood a moment longer. Then he put his hand inside his coat and touched the oilcloth packet, the cold stiff edge of it. And the hard little square beneath it.
"Yes, Pruett," he said. His voice was quite steady. "Let's walk back."
They walked. Along the lane, out onto the Crescent, along the white curve in the snow, Pruett with the umbrella and Rowan bareheaded beside him. And at the end of the Crescent, where the road divided, Rowan stopped, and looked down the left-hand way. The steep old road going down off the Hill, white and empty, with the fresh wheel tracks in it, two dark lines in the snow, going down and down and round the bend.
Far off, at the bottom of everything, through the falling snow, he could just see the top of the Spire. Tall and thin and black, like a needle stuck into the white sky.
Then he turned right, and went home.
That night at dinner, Aunt Cressida's place at the head of the table was laid, as it always was. The silver. The crystal. The white napkin folded in the shape of a lily.
Nobody sat in it.
And Rowan sat alone at the long table among the lilies, with the oilcloth packet inside his coat, and did not eat, and watched the door until midnight, and folded his napkin, without knowing he was doing it, eleven times.
