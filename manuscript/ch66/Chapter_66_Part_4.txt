Chapter 66: Saint Clement's, Part 4 of 5: The Plate
The Plate
They went up, after. All of them. The priest first, and the boy with the lantern, and then the six men, slowly, bending under the low arch, and then Aunt Cressida, who did not look back.
Rowan said, "I'll come in a moment."
Aunt Cressida stopped on the first step and looked down at him. Her face behind the short black veil was white and wet. For a moment he thought she would say no. That she would say, darling, come up, it's cold, you'll catch your death. That she would remind him he had promised to stay with her.
She did not. She looked at him for a long moment, and then she said, very low, "Don't be long," and went on up the stair, and the lily smell went with her, round the curve, and was gone.
And Rowan was alone in the vault with the dead.
The lanterns were still burning. Four of them along the walls, and one on the stone pillar in the middle. The light was soft and yellow and very still. It lay on the stone shelves and the long boxes and the brass plates, and made the shadows go up the walls in black stripes. Somewhere, far off, up the stair, a door closed. Then there was nothing. No sound at all. Not a drip. Not a breath. Only the faint hiss of the lantern wicks, and his own heart.
He took the lantern off the pillar.
It was heavy, iron and glass, with a ring on top. He held it up by the ring, and went to the shelf. To the second shelf on the right. To the two boxes lying side by side, so close they touched. His father's, new and dark, with the heather on it and the little dry purple flowers fallen on the stone. And his mother's.
He did not touch it again. He did not need to.
He held the lantern close to the end of it, the foot, and looked at the plate.
It was brass. A small oblong of it, about the size of a lady's hand, fixed to the wood with four little brass pins, one at each corner. It had been polished. Recently. The brass shone a soft deep gold in the lantern light, and there were the marks of the cloth on it, very faint, fine curved lines, where someone had rubbed it. Someone had come down here. Not long ago. And polished his mother's plate. And nobody else's.
There was writing on it. Cut into the brass in fine, sharp, upright letters, the way plates are always cut.
ISOLDE THORNE
That was all.
Rowan held the lantern closer.
That was all. Her name. Nothing else. No dates. No born in, no died in. No beloved wife of. No mother of. No words from the Saints, the way the other plates had, the ones he had passed on the shelves coming in, which all said something. Here lies. At rest. Gone before. Not here. Only her name, in the middle of the plate, and around it nothing. Smooth bright empty brass, on every side.
As if whoever made it had not known what else to put.
Or had known, and had not been allowed to.
He stood and looked at it for a long time.
Then he saw the mark.
It was in the corner. The bottom right-hand corner, close to the little brass pin, so close that the head of the pin almost covered it. Very small. Smaller than his smallest fingernail. Cut into the brass so fine and so shallow that he would not have seen it at all if the lantern had not been held exactly where it was, so that the light fell across it sideways and made the edges of the cut throw a tiny shadow.
A square.
No. Not quite a square. He bent closer, until his breath clouded the brass, and then waited for the cloud to clear, and looked again.
It was a square with lines inside it. Fine straight lines, crossing it. One from corner to corner. Then another, the other way. Then a line across the middle. And another. And the corners turned in, very slightly, toward the center, the way the corners of a piece of paper turn in when you fold it.
A folded square.
A square of paper, folded small. Folded over and over. He could not count the folds. They were too small, too fine. But he knew what it was. He knew it the way he knew his own hands.
He had folded a thing like that every day for two years. He had one in his breast pocket now, over his heart. Hard and small and creased soft at the edges. He had watched Master Kross fold them, at his desk, every day, every scrap of paper, before he put it away. He had seen it in other places too. He did not remember where. Somewhere. On something. A long time ago. A small folded square, drawn very small.
Rowan straightened up.
He stood there in the cold silent vault with the lantern in his hand, and looked at his mother's plate. At her name with no dates. At the bright empty brass around it. At the tiny folded square in the corner, nearly hidden by the pin.
And he thought about the box. How it had come up in his hands. How light it had been. The hollow knock of it against the wall.
He did not let himself think the next thing.
He did not let himself think it at all. He took it, whole, before it could form, and folded it over, and over, and over, small, and smaller, and put it away somewhere deep inside himself, where it would not be seen. Eleven times. The way he had been taught.
Then he put the lantern back on the pillar, exactly where it had been, and turned it so that the handle faced the same way.
And went to the door, and stopped there, and looked back once more. At the two boxes, side by side, on the second shelf. One heavy. One not.
"Goodbye, Father," he said.
He did not say anything to the other one.
He did not know what to say to it.
Bertram
Bertram was waiting at the top of the crypt stair.
Rowan came up round the last turn of it, with his hand on the cold curved wall, and there was the old man, standing just inside the low black door in the corner of the church, leaning on his stick. He had not gone out with the others. He had waited. In the dim gray light from the green windows, with the smoke of the put-out candles drifting over his head, he looked smaller than ever, and older, and very tired.
"Master Rowan," he said.
He said it the way he had always said it. At the door of Crown Walk, when Rowan came home from his lessons. On the stairs, at night, with a candle, when Rowan could not sleep. Master Rowan. Two words, in a dry careful old voice, with a little bow of the head between them.
"Bertram."
They stood there. Rowan did not know what to say. He had not seen the old man for two years. Not since the morning Crown Walk was shut up, and the servants were let go, and Bertram had stood in the hall in his black coat with his box of things at his feet and shaken Rowan's hand, very formally, and said, it has been an honor, Master Rowan, and gone out of the front door for the last time.
"Where have you been?" Rowan said at last. "All this time. I asked. Nobody knew."
"With my sister, Master Rowan. In Tanner's Row. She keeps a lodging house." The old man's mouth moved a little. "I help with the books. And the boots."
The boots. Bertram, who had run the whole of Crown Walk for forty years, the cellars and the silver and the twenty servants and the guest lists for the Midwinter ball, cleaning lodgers' boots in Tanner's Row.
Rowan's throat went tight again.
"I'm sorry," he said.
"No need, Master Rowan. It is honest work. And she feeds me." Bertram shifted his weight on his stick. Then he looked down the dark stair, the way Rowan had come up, and his old face changed a little. "It went well, below? He is... settled?"
"Yes," said Rowan. "Beside her."
"Ah." The old man nodded slowly. "Good. That is good. That is where he wanted to be. He said so. Many times."
Rowan looked at him.
He should not ask. He knew he should not. He had folded the thought up eleven times and put it away, deep, where it could not be seen. He had promised himself.
But Bertram had been there. Eleven years ago. When it happened. Bertram had been there for everything.
"Bertram," he said. "When my mother died."
The old man went still.
"I was five," said Rowan. "I don't remember it. I don't remember the funeral. Was I there? Did I come down here? I don't remember anything about it." He kept his voice very level. "Was it... was it like today?"
Bertram did not answer at once. He stood leaning on his stick, looking at Rowan, with his white side-whiskers and his tired red-rimmed eyes. Then he looked away. At the dark stair. At the green windows. At the floor.
"No, Master Rowan," he said. "It was not like today."
Rowan waited.
"Her ladyship died away from home," said Bertram slowly. "You will remember that much, perhaps. She went over the water. Over the Narrow Sea, in the middle of winter, in a great hurry, in her heavy traveling clothes. To her own people, we always supposed, though she never said. You and Miss Lyra stayed at Crown Walk with your father. And she did not come back." He was quiet for a moment. "A letter came. And then, a month after, the box. By sea. Sealed. With the seals of her own country on it, which none of us could read."
The smoke from the candles drifted.
"His lordship had it brought here," said Bertram. "At night. Very quietly. Only him, and me, and the old priest that was here then, and four men to carry. No service. No hymns. No one from the Hill. You children were asleep." He paused. "He said she would not have wanted a fuss. He said she always hated a fuss."
"Did he open it?"
The words were out before Rowan could stop them.
Bertram looked at him. And for a long moment, in the dim church, the old man's face was perfectly blank. The face of a good servant, who has heard a question he must not answer, and is deciding whether to hear it.
"No, Master Rowan," he said at last. "He did not open it. I asked him, once, if he wished it. Down there, on the shelf, by lantern light. And he said..." Bertram stopped. Then he went on, in a voice that was very low and very careful, as if he were carrying something that might spill. "He said, no, Bertram. I would rather remember her laughing."
They stood there.
"And then he polished the plate," said Bertram. "Himself. With his own handkerchief. He did it every year after, on the night she died. He came down here alone, and polished it, and came back up." The old man's eyes had filled. "I have done it the last two years. Since he could not. I came down on the night, with a cloth. I hope that was not wrong."
Rowan thought of the bright brass. The fine curved marks of the cloth on it.
"No," he said. "It wasn't wrong."
"Thank you, Master Rowan."
Bertram took out a large white handkerchief and blew his nose, very quietly, and put it away again. Then he straightened up as far as his old back would let him, and lifted his chin, and became, all at once, the butler of Crown Walk again. Correct. Composed. Nothing showing.
"Your aunt is in the vestry, Master Rowan," he said. "She asked for you. I would not keep her waiting."
"No."
Rowan turned to go. Then he turned back.
"Bertram," he said. "The house in Tanner's Row. What number?"
The old man blinked. "Number nine, Master Rowan. The green door. But there is no need..."
"Number nine," said Rowan. "The green door." He said it twice, so that he would keep it. "I'll come. One Sunday."
And for a moment, before he could stop it, something broke through the butler's face. Something that was not correct at all. And Rowan saw, quite clearly, the old man who used to bring him warm milk on the stairs at night, when he was small and could not sleep, and sit on the step below him, and tell him the names of the stars through the landing window until his eyes closed.
Then it was gone again, and Bertram bowed.
"Very good, Master Rowan," he said.
The Porch
The church was nearly empty when he came up.
The candles had been put out, most of them. Thin gray threads of smoke went up from the black wicks and hung under the low roof, and the smell of hot wax was very strong. The gold rings round the saints' heads had gone dim. The light through the narrow green windows was the color of pond water.
The people had gone out. He could hear them in the porch and on the steps, a low murmur of voices, and the stamp of boots on stone, and someone coughing. The cold came in through the open doors in a long steady breath down the aisle.
He walked down the aisle toward it. Past the empty pews. Past the place where the old woman with the candles had sat. She had left one of her bundles of candles on the seat, tied with string, he saw, as if she meant it to be found. He did not know what for.
The porch was a small square room of stone under the tower, with stone benches along both walls and the great oak doors open at the end onto the snow. It was full of people and gray light and cold. They were putting on their gloves and their hats, turning up their collars, saying quiet things to each other. They stopped when Rowan came in.
He did not want to talk to any of them. He looked for his aunt. She was not there. Out on the steps, perhaps. Or already at the carriage.
"Thorne."
The voice was deep and rough and a little hoarse, like a voice that has shouted a great deal once and does not shout anymore.
Rowan turned.
The Duke of Marrow was sitting on the stone bench by the door, with his back against the wall and his bandaged hands lying in his lap. He was alone. The others had drawn back from him a little, the way people draw back from a big dog, not because it is dangerous but because it is big. Close to, his face was redder than it had looked in the church, and there were burn marks on it. Small ones. On his forehead, on one cheek, on the side of his thick neck above the black stock. Little shiny pink patches of new skin, the size of coins. And his white eyebrows had been singed short.
He was looking up at Rowan with small, bright, sad blue eyes.
"Sit down a moment, boy," he said. "I can't get up without a hand, and I'd rather not ask for one."
Rowan sat down beside him on the cold stone bench.
For a while the Duke did not say anything. He sat and looked out of the open doors at the snow on the steps and the yew tree by the gate, bent down under its white weight. His breath smoked.
"I knew your father forty years," he said at last. "Since we were boys. At school together, out at Harrowfield. He was the cleverest boy in the school and the worst at games. I was the stupidest and the best. We made a very good pair." A little rough sound in his throat that might have been a laugh. "He did my sums. I stopped the big boys hitting him. That was how it went. For forty years."
Rowan said nothing.
"I have the house," said the Duke. "Crown Walk. You know that."
"Yes, sir."
"I bought it at the sale. With the furniture. Your aunt bid against me." He did not look at Rowan. "Your mother asked me to. Years ago. If they ever sell this house, Ansel, buy it. I never knew why she thought they would. But I did as I was told." He moved one of his white bandaged hands a little, in his lap. "Nobody lives there. I keep the key. I go once a week. I walk round. I don't touch anything." A pause. "I wind the clock in his study."
Rowan turned his head.
"The tall one," said the Duke. "With the moon. I wind it every Sunday. I don't know why. Nobody hears it." He was quiet. "I didn't go today. My hands." He looked down at them. "It'll have run down by now, I suppose. I'll get somebody to do it."
Rowan could not speak.
The Duke sat with his burned face turned to the snow. Then he said, in a different voice, lower, so that the people at the other end of the porch could not hear:
"Your sister was in the fire."
Rowan went still.
"At the Bourse," said the Duke. "On Midwinter night. I took her in. As my guest. On my card. She asked me and I said yes, and the Saints know I've been sorry since, and the Saints know I'd do it again." His bright small eyes came round to Rowan's face at last. "When the roof came down. When the fire came down through the dome. There were people under it. A great many. Old Lady Ashcombe. The Pellow girls. Half the Hill. Servants. The musicians, poor devils, in their gallery." He swallowed. "She went back in."
The porch was very quiet.
"She went back in," said the Duke. "I saw her. I was on the steps, outside, with my coat on fire, and somebody was beating it out, and I saw her go back in. Through the doors. Into it. And come out again with an old woman over her shoulder. And go back in. And come out with a boy. And go back in." His voice had gone rough. "Four times. I counted. While the roof was coming down. While the man in the middle was holding it up with his hands, and you could see him, through the doors, in the fire, with his hands up over his head and his arms gone gray, and the whole dome of glass sitting on him like a lid on a pot. She went in four times. She got them out."
He looked at Rowan.
"You should know that," he said. "Whatever else they tell you about her. Whatever they write in the papers. Whatever's in that letter you keep your hand on. You should know that."
Rowan had not known his hand was on his chest. It was. Pressed flat over the little hard square in his breast pocket.
He said nothing.
He could not have said anything if his life had depended on it. His throat had closed again, the way it had closed in the hymn. He sat on the cold stone bench beside the burned old man with his hand on his chest and his face very still and his eyes on the snow, and said nothing at all.
The Duke looked at him for a long moment.
Then he smiled. A small, sad, crooked smile, in his red burned face.
"No," he said gently. "No. Of course."
He looked away again, at the snow.
"You're a Thorne," he said. "Thornes don't. Your father never did either. He'd sit there with his face like a shut door and you'd think he'd heard nothing, and then three weeks later you'd find he'd sold half his shares to pay your debts and never told you." The rough sound again. "Go on. Your aunt's waiting. Help me up first."
Rowan stood, and put his hands under the old man's arm, and lifted. The Duke came up off the bench heavily, grunting, and stood swaying a little, and then steadied.
"Thank you," he said. And then, as Rowan let go: "Come and see the clock, one Sunday. If you like. I'll leave the gate open."
And he went out through the great doors and down the treacherous steps into the snow, slowly, on his own, with his white hands held out a little in front of him for balance, like a man walking in the dark.
Mrs. Doyle
The old woman with the candles was on the steps.
She was standing at the bottom of them, by the yew tree, with her big basket on her arm, as if she had been waiting for someone and had not quite decided to give up. The snow was beginning again, lightly, and it settled on her rusty black shawl and on her broad red face and on the bundles of candles in the basket. She did not seem to notice. She was watching the Duke go away down the lane, slowly, with his white hands held out in front of him, and she was shaking her head.
Then she turned and saw Rowan at the top of the steps, and her face changed.
She did not smile, exactly. She looked at him the way you look at a child who has fallen down in the street and has not yet decided whether to cry.
"There you are, love," she said.
Nobody had called Rowan love in his life. Not his mother, that he remembered. Not his aunt, who called him darling. Not the cadets, certainly. Not Master Kross. He stood at the top of the five steps in his cadet black and did not know what to do with it.
"Mind the steps," said the old woman. "They're a disgrace. I'd have them done in a quarter of an hour, give me a broom and a bucket of ash. Come down careful. Edges."
He came down careful. Edges. At the bottom she looked up at him, a long way up, because she was small, and he was not.
"Doyle," she said. "Mrs. Doyle. Saint Bride's, down by the river. You'll not know it. It's not a church for the Hill." She hitched the basket higher on her arm. "I keep it. Since my eyes got too bad for the sewing. Sweep it and scrub it and light the candles and turn out the drunks of a Sunday morning."
"I..." Rowan did not know what he was going to say. "Thank you for coming, Mrs. Doyle."
"Don't thank me. I come to light his candles." She looked past him, at the door of the church. "I left them a bundle. On the seat. For the verger to put out, every Sunday, for the year. Though I doubt he will. I'll come up and see." She looked back at Rowan. "One for every year, see. That's my custom. I light one for every year a person lived. Your father, that's fifty-two. Fifty-two candles. I did it in my own church, too, the night he died. All of them at once. You've never seen a church so bright."
Rowan stared at her.
"The night he died," he said. "You... you were there?"
The old woman looked at him for a moment. Her red face went very kind.
"Not when he went, love. No. He went somewhere else, with your sister holding his hand. But after. She came to my church after. Your sister. In the middle of the night, in the snow, with the harvester and the big captain and the rest of them. And I lit his candles, all fifty-two of them, and she stood in the middle of them and looked at them." A pause. "And then she came and put her arms round me. Without a word. Just held on. Like this." She put her free arm round herself, in the shawl, and held tight, to show him. "For a long time."
The snow came down between them.
Rowan could not speak.
"I thought you'd want to know that," said Mrs. Doyle. "That she wasn't by herself. After. That somebody was there with a light."
She looked at him a moment longer. Then she reached up, a long way, and patted his arm. Twice. Through the black cloth. The way you pat a horse.
"You sing next time," she said. "Do you hear me? Next time. Even if it comes out like a crow. He'll not mind. They never mind."
And she turned and went off down the lane in the snow with her basket on her arm, a small black stout figure, walking fast, not looking back, toward the Crescent and the steep road and the long way down to the river.
Rowan stood at the bottom of the steps and watched her go.
Saint Bride's, he thought. Down by the river. Forty years.
He put it with the other thing. Number nine, Tanner's Row. The green door. He put them together, carefully, in the place where he kept things he meant to keep. And then, because he did not trust that place anymore, not since the letter, he took out his cadet's notebook from his breast pocket, and his pencil, and wrote them both down. Small. In his own hand, with the hooked R that his writing master had never been able to cure.
ST BRIDE'S. MRS DOYLE. BY THE RIVER.
TANNER'S ROW 9. GREEN DOOR. BERTRAM.
Then he put the notebook away, and looked for his aunt.
