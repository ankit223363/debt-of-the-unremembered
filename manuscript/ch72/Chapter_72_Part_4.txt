Chapter 72: The Trench, Part 4 of 5: Locked
Locked
He did it two legs later. He had been thinking about it since the first soldier on the lip. He knew he should not. He did it anyway.
The others were ahead. Not far. At the next corner, all of them, waiting while Kestrel crouched with her palm flat on the floor and listened for the ticking. Corin was behind, as he always was now, on the crutch. And there was a soldier by the south wall, alone, between one corner and the next. An ordinary one. A man of middle height in a long coat and a sun badge, with his face turned up to the north and his hands open at his sides.
Corin stopped in front of him.
He looked along the Trench at the others. Nobody was looking back.
He took his left glove off. Slowly. Finger by finger, the way Lyra had taken hers off by the grit. And put it in his pocket. And looked at his hand.
Gray. Gray from the black tips of the fingers to the wrist, and on up under the sleeve, to a hand's width below the shoulder. The color of old ash. Dead and smooth and cold. The hand that had held the dome. The hand that had felt Vivienne go out of the world, and taken in all of her. The hand that had walked into ten thousand jars in the Harvesting Section and come out with faces and rooms and songs.
A harvester's hand.
That was all it had ever been for. To put on burnt-out things and feel what was left inside them. To find the Ash. To pull.
He had not pulled. Not since the Edge. Not once. The Glass did not allow it, Kestrel said, and he believed her. He had tried nothing. He had laid his hand on the Glass in the night and a light had come up to it, but he had not pulled. He had not even reached.
But he had wanted to.
He could admit that, here, alone, with the glass man in front of him. He had wanted to since the first one on the lip. Since he saw the dark bones inside the green and the almost-face and the hand up to shade the eyes. Wanted to know. What the man had seen. What he had been thinking. Who he was. Whether he had a name, and what it was, and who had called him by it.
That was what Corin was. A man who wanted to know what was inside other people. He had been that for longer than he had been anything else.
And there were so many of them here. Standing in rows. Waiting. Every one with a whole life in him, held in the glass, sixty years, with nobody to take it.
Nobody to take it.
He put his bare gray palm flat on the soldier's chest.
On the coat, under the sun badge. Over the place where the heart would be. He pressed. Not hard. The way he had pressed a thousand times on a thousand jars, at his desk at Intake, with the lamp lit and the clerks gone home.
And he reached.
It was the oldest thing he knew how to do. Older than walking, almost. He did not have to think about it. He opened the part of himself that opened, the way you open your hand. And reached in, through the palm, through the gray, into what was under it. Feeling for the cold that is not cold. For the pull, like a current underwater. For the first thin edge of somebody else.
There was nothing.
Not nothing as in empty. Not the hollow, scraped feeling of a jar that has been walked too many times, or a mage who has burned everything. He knew that feeling. This was not that.
This was like pressing his hand to a door that had been bricked up from the other side.
The glass was there. Cool. Smooth. Under his palm. And behind the glass, he knew, there was something. There had to be. A man had stood here. A whole man, with a whole life. But between Corin's hand and that man there was the glass, and the glass did not open. It did not give. It did not let him in by so much as a hair. It was not resisting him. Resisting would have been something. It was simply shut. The way a stone is shut. The way the sky is shut. Completely, and forever, and without any interest in him at all.
He held his hand there for a long time.
Nothing came.
No cold. No pull. No voice. No face. No smell of a kitchen, or a field, or a wife's hair. No last thing seen. Nothing. Only the cool smooth glass under his palm, and his own breathing, and from far away behind the wall, faint and steady, the ticking.
The Glass holds them, Kestrel had said. It does not let anyone in. It does not let anything out.
He took his hand away.
And as he did, something went through him. Not from the glass. From himself. A hot sick wave, starting in his stomach and going up into his face, so that his cheeks burned in the cool green air of the Trench.
Shame.
He stood there with his gray hand held up in front of him, bare, and looked at it. And then at the soldier. At the open empty hands, and the lifted face, and the pike behind him against the wall. At a farmer who had come down off his hill with the pike off his barn wall and stood in a ditch and turned round to see the light and been made into glass, sixty years ago, and stood here ever since, waiting for someone to tell him he could put his hands down.
And Corin had come along and put his hand on him. Not to tell him anything. Not to sing to him. To take.
To see what he could get.
He pulled his glove back on. His fingers were clumsy with it. He could not get the thumb in.
When he looked up, Lyra was there.
She had come back along the Trench. He did not know when. She was standing a few steps off, by the north wall, with her arms folded. Looking at him. Not at his face. At his hand, in its half-pulled glove.
She had seen.
He did not try to pretend she had not.
"I tried," he said. His voice came out very small in the dead air. "I put my hand on him. I tried to take him. To see."
She did not say anything.
"There was nothing there," said Corin. "Kestrel was right. There's nothing to get. It's shut." He got the thumb into the glove at last. "And I'm glad. I'm glad it's shut. Because I shouldn't have. I'm ashamed I tried."
He made himself look at her when he said it.
What You Are
Lyra did not answer at once.
She unfolded her arms. She came the few steps across the Trench to where he stood. And she looked at the soldier. For a long moment. At the place on his chest, under the sun badge, where Corin's hand had been.
"I tried too," she said.
Corin looked at her.
"Last night. Before we set off." She did not look away from the soldier. "I went out on the Glass a little way, where nobody would see. And I tried to burn. Something small. The taste of salt." Her mouth twisted, very slightly. "To make a light. To see if I could."
"Did it—"
"No. Nothing. Like pushing on a door that's painted on a wall." She said it quite flatly. "And then I sat down on the Glass and cried like a baby. Because I was so glad."
She did not say anything for a moment.
"So I don't think you should be ashamed of trying," she said. "Not really. We all tried. Everyone tries, the first time. Oren would have tried, if he had anything left that the Glass would want. Vespera's probably tried with her needles." A pause. "Kestrel didn't tell us not to try. She told us we couldn't. It's not the same."
"I wanted to take him," said Corin.
"I know."
"Not to help him. Not to sing. To take. To see what was in him. Because I wanted to know." He held up his gloved hand. "That's what I am. That's all I am. Somebody who goes into other people and takes what's left. I've been that so long I don't know how to be anything else. I came down here and saw them all standing there, waiting, and the first thing I thought was not poor men. It was what's in them."
"Was it?" said Lyra.
"What?"
"The first thing." She turned and looked at him, at last. Her face, under the hide, was very serious. "Was it really the first thing? Up there. At the lip. When you saw the first one with his hand up."
Corin thought about it.
He thought about the man on the lip. The green glass. The bones. The almost-face. The hand up to shade the eyes, looking north, at something coming. And what he had felt. Standing in front of him on the crutch, in the noon glare.
It had not been what's in him.
It had been what did he see. And then, very quickly after, before he could stop it: and he did not put his hand down again. And something in his throat.
"No," he said slowly. "No. It wasn't the first thing."
"Then it isn't all you are," said Lyra.
She said it simply. The way she might have said the sky was blue.
"You've done that for a long time," she said. "Gone into people. And you're good at it. And you want to know things. That's not wrong. That's you. I've watched you want to know things since—" She stopped. Started again. "Since before you can remember. You always wanted to know. What a person was. What they'd seen. It's why you were good at your work. It's why you're good at this. At remembering me, now. One thing a day."
She looked at the soldier again.
"But you came down here and you stood in front of the boy with Oren, and you didn't try to take him," she said. "And you stood in front of the tall one with the badge, and you didn't. And you knelt by the doll and you didn't. You waited till you were alone, with an ordinary one, who you didn't know, so it wouldn't hurt so much if it worked." She paused. "That's not a man who only wants to take. That's a man who's ashamed of wanting to, and does it anyway, a bit, the way you pick at a scab. And then tells somebody."
Corin did not say anything.
"You told me," said Lyra. "You didn't have to. I'd have said nothing. But you told me." She shrugged. "So I told you about the salt. Now we're even."
She put out her hand. Not to him. To the soldier. And laid it, very lightly, on the glass of the open hand that hung at his side. Not on the chest. Not over the heart. On the hand. Palm to palm, the way you hold someone's hand to help them up.
She did not reach. Corin could see that. There was no pull in it. She just held the glass hand for a moment. Then let it go.
"Sorry," she said to the soldier. Quietly. "He didn't mean anything by it. He's like that. He'd have liked you, I think."
And she turned and went back along the Trench toward the others.
Corin stood there.
He looked at the soldier. At the glass hand Lyra had held.
Then, slowly, he touched two fingers of his gloved hand to his own forehead, and drew them down, to the tip of his nose. The way the Ashborn did. The way Kestrel had done to the short broad soldier at the foot of the slope.
He did not know if it meant anything, from him. He did it anyway.
Then he went after her.
Written Down
At the next corner, while Kestrel crouched to listen, he wrote.
He had not meant to. There was no time. But his hands were shaking, a little, from the soldier and from Lyra and from Marrit's red thread in his pocket. And the only thing he knew that stopped them shaking was the book.
So he leaned his back against the angle of the walls, and got the green book out of his pack, and the pencil stub out of its spine, and opened it flat against the cool glass of the wall, at the height of his chest, the way a clerk writes standing at a high desk.
The page was half full already. THURSDAY. ON THE GLASS. SHE SLEPT. And under it, the heron. SHE STOOD ON ONE LEG ON A GARDEN WALL. And under that, nothing yet.
He wrote.
THE TRENCH. MEN OF GLASS. GREEN. BONES INSIDE.
He stopped. Looked at it. The letters were square and black and leaned the way his letters always leaned. He did not know why they leaned. Lyra said they always had.
THEY WERE FACING SOUTH WITH PIKES. THEN THEY TURNED ROUND TO SEE HER. THEY PUT THE PIKES DOWN. NOT AFRAID.
He wrote it slowly. Pressing hard, so that the letters went deep into the paper. So that if the pencil faded, the dents would still be there.
FARMERS WITH PIKES. OREN SAID. LIKE A TITLE.
A DRUM BOY. A DOLL IN HIS PACK. RED. ONE EYE. STITCHES LEAN BACK.
KESTREL: THE VALLEY MOTHERS SEWED THEM FOR THE CHILDREN WHO MARCHED. EVERY STITCH IS LOOKING HOME. FOLLOW IT.
He stopped again. His hand had stopped. He looked at the word he had just written. HOME. In his own square capitals. It looked strange to him. Like a word in another language.
He went on.
OREN PUT HIS HAND ON THE BOY. HE SAID YOU CAN PUT IT DOWN NOW. HIS HAND SHOOK.
LYRA: ROBIN'S DOLL IS THE SAME. THE LADY IN THE BLUE CLOAK.
VESPERA WAS TAUGHT THE STITCH. SHE WILL NOT SAY BY WHO. SHE HAS NEVER USED IT. IT IS FOR THE ONES WHO GO AWAY.
He did not write what Vespera had said about the tree with a crown, behind a baby's ear. It was already in the book. Further back. In his own hand. A TREE WITH A CROWN. He did not need it twice.
He did not think of the tall soldier with his hand on his heart.
He wrote:
I TRIED TO TAKE ONE. I PUT MY HAND ON HIS CHEST. IT WAS SHUT. LIKE A STONE.
I AM ASHAMED. I AM GLAD IT WAS SHUT.
LYRA SAYS IT IS NOT ALL I AM.
He looked at that line for a long time.
Then, under it:
MARRIT: THE LIGHTS COME UP TO ANYONE. THEY ONLY STAY FOR THEIR OWN.
He did not write anything after that. He held the pencil over the page, and the point of it shook, and he did not write anything at all.
Somewhere along the Trench, someone was humming.
He lifted his head.
It was Sef. Corin could see him, a little way back along the leg they had just come down, standing in front of one of the glass soldiers. Not touching it. Just standing. With his thin shoulders up and his head bent. And humming. Very low. So low the walls almost took it.
The song for the dead. The one Kestrel had sung at the Edge, with her back to the fire, while Last Thorn burned. Corin knew it. Slow and low and going down at the end of every line, like someone setting something heavy down, gently, on the ground.
Kestrel had heard it too. She lifted her head from the floor where she had been listening. And looked at Sef. And Corin thought for a moment she would tell him to stop. They had no time. The ticking was close.
She did not tell him to stop.
She stood up. And went back along the Trench to the next soldier along from Sef. And stood in front of him. And began to hum too.
And then Arran. And Brin, his low slow voice like a big dog grumbling under the others. And Oda. And Marrit, very old and very cracked, a little behind the beat.
Not loud. Not singing. Humming. The Ashborn, each in front of a different glass man, humming the song for the dead, very low, in the green dimness of the Trench, with the ticking coming through the walls.
Not the three nights. Not every one. There was no time for that. Only a few. Only the ones they were passing.
But something.
Corin looked down at the open book against the wall.
Then he wrote one more line, quickly, before he could think about it.
THEY HUMMED TO THEM. THAT IS ALL THEY GET. IT IS GIVING. THE GLASS DOES NOT MIND.
He closed the book. Put it in his pack. Got the crutch under his arm.
And as he went past the soldier nearest him, the ordinary one, the one he had put his hand on, he found that he was humming too. Very low. Under his breath. Badly, probably. He did not know the tune well.
But he knew it well enough.
Close
The ticking was louder at the next corner. Corin heard it before he reached the others. Not through the wall now. Through the air. Coming down into the Trench from the strip of sky above, from the open Glass to the south, as clear as a clock in the next room.
Tick. Tick. Tick.
Kestrel was on her feet. All of them were. Standing close in the angle of the corner, under the north wall, with their faces turned up toward the south lip. Oren had his sword out. Not raised. Held down by his leg, point to the floor. Oda had her spear. Brin had his good hand on the hilt of his bone knife.
Nobody spoke.
Corin came up among them on the crutch, as quietly as he could, which was not very. The felt foot of the crutch caught on a ridge and scraped, and Kestrel's head turned, sharp, and he stopped.
They listened.
Tick. Tick. Tick.
It was not right above them. Corin could tell that much. It was off to the south and the west. Behind them. But it was not the far small tapping of the morning, like a fingernail on a window three rooms away. It was nearer than that. Much nearer. It had weight in it now. He could hear, under the ticks, something else. A low grinding hum. Like a mill wheel turning a long way off. And under that, every so often, a dull heavy knock. Knock. And a pause. Knock.
Feet.
Iron feet, on the Glass.
"How far?" said Vespera. Barely a breath.
Oren did not answer at once. He had his eyes shut again. His lips were moving in his beard. Counting the space between the knocks, Corin thought. The way soldiers count.
Then he opened his eyes.
He did not hold up his fingers this time. He looked at Corin, and Corin saw his face, and did not need the fingers.
"An hour," said Oren. "Less. They've gained on the corners. They've been coming straight across the top while we went round every bend in this ditch." He looked up at the strip of sky. The light was gold now, deep gold, going to red at the edge. "How long to dusk?"
"Longer than that," said Kestrel.
She said it very evenly.
Nobody said anything for a moment.
"Can they see down in here?" said Lyra. "If they come to the edge. Can they see us?"
"They don't see," said Kestrel. "Not the way you see. They go to him." She did not look at Corin. She did not need to. "Wherever he is, they know it. The way a compass knows north. They will come to the lip above wherever he is, and come down. If there's a way down, they'll find it. If there isn't, they'll jump."
"Into the Trench."
"Yes."
Corin looked at the walls. Six feet of smooth glass on either side. Narrow. Two men wide. If three iron men came down into it from above, at this end or that, there would be nowhere to go. Nowhere to run, nowhere to get round them. Only the long straight green legs of the Trench and the sharp corners and the glass soldiers standing along the walls in rows.
The Erasers would not care about the glass soldiers. They would go through them, the way they had gone through the tents at Last Thorn. They would push them aside like thorns. And the soldiers would fall, at last, after sixty years. And break.
"How far to the end?" said Oren.
"Four legs," said Kestrel. "Five. Then the wall is broken again, the way it was where we came in. There is a way up." She looked at Corin. At his crutch. At his stiff leg. "It is steep."
"I'll carry him," said Oren.
"You carried him down."
"I'll carry him up."
Kestrel looked at Oren for a long moment. Then she nodded, once. And turned.
"Now," she said. "Fast. All of us. Do not stop for anything. Do not touch them. Do not look at them. Walk."
They walked.
Fast. Faster than they had walked all day. Corin swung the crutch and his good leg and his bad one, pat, step-step, pat, step-step, as fast as he could make them go, and it was not fast enough, and he knew it was not. Arran dropped back beside him without being asked and took his other arm, the free one, and pulled. Lyra was at his back. He could hear her breathing.
The glass soldiers went past on either side. He did not look at them. He tried not to. Green shapes in the corners of his eyes, one after another, faces lifted, hands open. Like people standing along a street to watch a procession go by. A slow sad procession. A funeral.
The corners came and went. One. Two. At each one, Kestrel stopped for a breath and put her hand flat on the floor. And then went on, faster.
Tick. Tick. Tick.
Knock.
The light in the strip of sky over their heads went from gold to red. The green of the glass soldiers went dark, and then darker, and the shadows in the Trench crept up the walls.
Three.
Corin's good leg was burning. His arm, under the crutch, was rubbed raw at the armpit through the coat. His breath was coming in great dry rasps that hurt his throat. He did not slow down. He could not. Arran would not let him.
Four.
Knock. Knock.
Louder.
Near.
"There," said Kestrel. Ahead. "There. The wall."
And Corin looked up, through the sweat and the red light and the slits of the eye hide, and saw it: the end of the Trench. A last short leg, and then the wall going up, broken and slumped, in a rough glassy slope like the one they had come down at noon.
And above it, the sky. Red. Deep red. Going purple at the top.
The sun was going down.
But it had not gone yet.
