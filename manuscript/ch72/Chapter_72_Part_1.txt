Chapter 72: The Trench, Part 1 of 5: The First One
The First One
Corin saw the Trench before he saw the man.
He came up the long slope of the swell behind Kestrel and Sef, slower than all of them, the crutch slipping a little on the hot Glass at every step even with the felt wound round its foot. The sun was straight overhead. It pressed down on the top of his head and the backs of his hands like a flat iron. Under him the Glass threw it back up, so that the air between was thick and white and shaking, and everything he looked at swam.
He came over the top.
And there, below him, a long way off, the shine was broken.
A line. A dark line, drawn across the white floor of the world from left to right, as far as he could see in both directions. Not straight. It went in a zigzag. A short way one way, then a sharp corner, then a short way the other. Like a line of stitching done by someone in a hurry. Or like the line lightning makes, in old pictures. It ran away east and west into the glare until it was lost.
It was dark because it did not shine. Everything else out here shone. That line did not. It was a cut. A gash in the Glass, deep enough that the sun could not get down into the bottom of it, so that it lay across the white shine like a crack across a plate.
The Trench.
He heard the others come up behind him and stop. Nobody said anything. Sef was breathing hard beside Kestrel, still, from his run. His palms were wrapped now in strips of Vespera's linen.
Then Corin saw the man.
He was standing on the near lip of the Trench. Right on the edge. A little to the left of where they would come down to it, if they walked straight on.
At first Corin thought he was one of them. One of their own. Somebody who had gone on ahead. He was the right size. He was standing the way a man stands. Upright, with his feet apart, a little turned. And he had one hand up. Up to his forehead, flat, palm down. The way you hold your hand over your eyes when you are looking into the sun. Looking out. Looking a long way.
Then Corin saw that the sun went through him.
They went down toward him. Slowly. All of them. Not in a line now. Together, in a knot, close, the way people walk at night past a house they think is haunted. Corin's crutch went down on the Glass, pat, and his good leg came after, and his bad one, and every step the man got clearer.
He was glass.
All of him. The same glass as the floor they were walking on. But not black. Not smoke-colored, like the Glass in the day. He was green. A pale clouded green, like the green of an old bottle that has been lying a long time in a river, scoured by sand, gone milky on the outside. The light came into him and stopped, a little way in, and lit him from inside. He glowed. Faintly. Green, in the white glare.
And inside him there were bones.
Corin saw them as he came close. Dark shapes, in the green. Not clear. Blurred, the way a stone looks at the bottom of a cloudy pond. But there. The long dark shapes of the bones of the legs, going down inside the green legs. The darker knobs of the knees. The curve of the ribs, like the ribs of a boat, under the green chest. The arm that was raised: the two long bones of the forearm, side by side, and the knob of the elbow. The hand at the forehead: a fan of small dark bones, spread, inside the green fingers.
And the skull.
Corin stopped walking.
He was close now. They had come up behind the man, and Corin had gone round him, slowly, along the very lip of the Trench, with the drop at his left side, until he could see the front of him. Close enough to touch. He did not touch. He stood on the Glass with the crutch under his arm and looked up at the face.
It was almost a face.
That was the worst of it. If it had been only a skull inside glass, he thought he could have borne it. He had seen skulls. But the glass had kept the shape of the face round the skull. Not clearly. Smoothed, as if somebody had made a face out of wax and then held it near a fire. But the shape was there. A forehead, under the shading hand. A nose, long, a little crooked. The line of a jaw. A mouth, closed. The eyes were only two shallow hollows in the green. But they were looking out. Out over Corin's head. Out to the north. A long way.
And round the shape of the man, the shape of what he had been wearing. The glass had kept that too. A long coat, down past the knees, with a hem that flared a little, as if the wind had been blowing it. A wide belt round the middle, with a buckle; Corin could see the dark shape of the buckle inside the green, a square of something that had been iron. A pouch on the belt. Boots. You could see the line where the boot tops ended and the leggings began. You could see, on the front of the coat, high on the left side, a small round shape. A badge, perhaps. A button.
He stood on the very edge of the Trench, with one foot a little forward and his weight on it, as if he had just climbed up out of it to look. And his hand up to shade his eyes.
Looking at something.
Corin turned his head. Slowly. And looked where the man was looking.
North. Across the dark gash of the Trench and its far lip. Over the white shaking glare beyond, swell after swell. Toward the middle of the Glass, where none of them had been yet.
Toward the Eye.
There was nothing there. Only the shine. And the sky. White, and empty, and very far.
But the man had seen something there. Sixty years ago. Something so bright that he had put up his hand to shade his eyes from it. And had stood like that, on the edge of his ditch, looking.
And had not put his hand down again.
Down
Kestrel took them down into the Trench a little way east of the man, where the wall had slumped.
That was the only word Corin could find for it. Slumped. Most of the wall, as far as he could see along it, went straight down from the lip like the side of a well. But here, in one place, it had sagged. As if, sixty years ago, when it was soft, it had begun to slide and then set hard before it got far. It made a rough slope, steep and glassy, with ridges in it like the ridges of a melted candle. You could get down it. Just.
The Ashborn went down it the way they did everything on the Glass. Sitting. On their backsides, with their spears held up out of the way and their felt feet out in front, sliding slowly, braking with their heels and the flats of their hands. Arran went first and stood at the bottom with his long arms up. Then Oda, wincing on her bad feet. Then the others.
Corin could not sit and slide. Not with the knee. It would not bend enough. He stood at the top with the crutch and looked at the slope and did not know what to do.
Oren solved it. He did not ask. He came up beside Corin, and turned round, and bent his back, and said over his shoulder, "On."
"Oren, I'm too—"
"You're a bag of bones, lad. I've carried pikes that weighed more. On."
So Corin got on. Arms round Oren's thick neck, bad leg sticking out stiff to one side, crutch handed down to Arran. And Oren went down the slope backwards, crouched low, one hand on the Glass, his boots finding the ridges one by one, breathing hard through his nose. And at the bottom he crouched lower, and let Corin slide off, and straightened up with a grunt, and his back cracked like a stick.
"Saints," said Oren. "Bag of bones. Heavy bones."
Then Corin looked round, and forgot about his leg.
The Trench was deep. Six feet, or a little more. The top of the wall was a hand's width above Oren's head, and Oren was the tallest of them but for Kestrel. And it was narrow. Two men could have walked down it side by side. Not three. The walls went up on either side close and straight and smooth.
So smooth.
He put out his hand to the nearest wall, and laid his gray palm on it, without thinking. And it was like touching the inside of a bottle. Cool. Cool, in the noon, because the sun did not reach down in here, not this high. Cool and slick and perfectly smooth, without a single crack or a single ridge or a single grain of grit. Dark green-black. And when he took his hand away there was no mark at all. Not even the mist of his breath.
The floor was different.
The floor was ridged. Little ridges, low and rounded, running across the bottom of the Trench in rough rows, a step apart. He looked at them for a long time before he understood. They were where feet had stood. Boots. More boots than he could feel, standing in mud, in rows, shifting, stamping, waiting. And the mud had melted, and the boot-holes had melted, and the edges of them had slumped and run together and set into these soft low ridges, like the ridges the tide leaves in sand.
And along the foot of the wall, on the side toward the south, lay the pikes.
He had not seen them from above. They had been lying in the shadow. Now he saw them. Long dark shapes, leaning against the wall, or lying along the bottom of it, half sunk into the floor. Row on row. More than he could feel. The shafts had been wood. The wood was gone. The glass had taken it, or the fire, and where each shaft had been there was only a long thin hollow in the floor, a groove, perfectly round, as if somebody had pressed a broom handle into soft wax.
But the heads were still there.
Iron. Or what had been iron. Red now. Rusted through. Rusted so far that they were not solid anymore but lace. Red lace. Fine and fragile and full of holes, so that you could see the green-black of the Glass through them, the way you see a tablecloth through a doily. They still had their shape. The long point, the hook on one side, the socket where the shaft had gone. But Corin thought that if he breathed on one too hard, it would fall to red dust.
He did not breathe on one.
Nobody said anything.
That was the next thing he noticed. The quiet.
It was quieter in the Trench than on the Glass. He had not thought anything could be quieter than the Glass. But up there, there had been the wind. The small dry wind, always moving, always just there at the edge of hearing. Down here there was no wind. The walls cut it off. And the walls took sound, too. When Oren grunted, setting him down, the sound had gone into the wall and not come back. When Arran handed him his crutch, the knock of it on the floor was small and flat and dead, like a sound in a room hung with heavy cloth.
All of them stood at the bottom of the slope, close together, and did not speak.
And all along the Trench, both ways, as far as Corin could see before it turned its first sharp corner, they stood.
The soldiers.
Glass men. Green, and clouded, and glowing faintly where the light came down into the Trench from above and touched them. One. A few steps along, another. Then another. Every few yards, all down the Trench, a glass man standing at his place. Not in a row, exactly. Some close to the north wall. Some in the middle of the floor. One sitting, with his back against the south wall and his knees drawn up, as if he had been resting. One bending, with his hand down, as if he had been reaching for something he had dropped. Most standing.
All of them quite still.
All of them facing the same way.
The Turn
North.
Corin looked from one to the next, and the next, down the long green-lit gut of the Trench, and they were all facing north.
Every one. The standing ones, with their faces turned up a little toward the top of the north wall. The sitting one, with his back to the south wall and his chin lifted. The one bending for whatever he had dropped, with his head twisted round on his neck, up and to the north, as if he had heard his name. All of them. Looking the same way. Toward the middle of the Glass. Toward where the light had come from.
And the pikes were on the other side.
That was what made his skin go cold, in the cool of the Trench, in the noon. The pikes were along the south wall. The wall the soldiers had their backs to. Leaning there, lying there, in rows, where they had been set down. Or dropped.
They had been facing south. With their pikes. Facing the way the gray men would come.
And then they had turned round.
He looked at Arran.
Arran was standing very still, a little apart from the others, with his back against the north wall and his long arms hanging at his sides. Looking down the Trench. Not smiling. Corin had never seen his face so still.
"You said," said Corin. Very low. The walls took his voice and made it small. "In the story. You said they were facing south. Waiting for the gray men."
"They were," said Arran.
"They're not."
"No." Arran's voice was as low as his. "They turned."
"When?"
Arran did not answer at once. He looked at the nearest soldier. A short broad man, green in the green light, with his feet planted wide on the ridged floor and his head back and his face lifted to the north wall. His hands were down at his sides. Open. Empty. The pike that had been in them lay behind him, against the south wall, a long round groove in the floor and a head of red lace.
"Old Hask said," said Arran, "that the soldiers heard her. Before the sun came. They heard her. She was a long way off, in the middle, where the Eye is now. Too far to hear a voice. But they heard her anyway. Every one of them." He swallowed. "And they put down their pikes, and turned round. To see."
"Heard what?"
"Old Hask did not know. Nobody knows. Old Hask said maybe she called them. Maybe she said goodbye. Maybe she just shouted, the way she always shouted, and they knew her voice, the way you know your mother's voice across a whole town." He looked down at his own hands. "And they turned round to see what she was doing. And she was making the sun. And the sun came out to meet them."
Corin did not say anything.
He looked at the short broad soldier with his face lifted. He looked at the one by the lip, outside, with his hand up to shade his eyes. He understood now. The man had not been looking at something far away. He had been looking at something coming.
"That's not how you told it," said Lyra. Behind him. Her voice was very quiet. "This morning. You made it a joke."
"It is a joke," said Arran. "That's how we tell it to the children. Sola shouted, and the sun came out of her mouth, and that's why you never make a Vance angry. Ha ha." He did not laugh. "You tell it to the children like that, so they're not afraid. And then one day they're old enough to walk the Glass, and you bring them here. And they see." He pushed himself off the wall. "And then they know why we tell it as a joke."
He walked away down the Trench. Past the short broad soldier. Not looking at him. Past the next one.
Corin watched him go.
And then, because he could not help it, because he was what he was and had been for longer than he could remember, he looked at the soldiers' faces. One after another. The smooth green almost-faces with their dark bone shadows. Looking for what was in them. The way he had looked into ten thousand faces in the Harvesting Section, in the jars, in the burnt-out heads of mages, looking for the last thing they saw.
And he thought he could see it.
Not in any one face. In all of them. In the way they stood. The heads up. The hands open. The pikes put down behind them, not thrown. Set down. Leaned against the wall, some of them, quite neatly, by men who had expected to pick them up again.
They had not been afraid.
That was what he saw. That was what was in the shape of them, standing there in their rows with their backs to the enemy and their faces to the light. They had not been running. They had not been cowering. They had not been throwing up their arms. They had turned round, every one of them, and put down what they held, and lifted their faces, the way you lift your face when you hear someone you love calling your name across a field.
And the sun had come to meet them.
Corin stood in the cool green quiet with his hand on his crutch and found that he could not see very well.
He did not wipe his eyes. Out here, Kestrel had said, you did not waste water.
He let it dry.
What They Get
Kestrel did not walk on at once. She stood at the foot of the slumped slope with her spear grounded and her eye hide pushed up on her forehead, looking at the short broad soldier. And then she went to him.
She did not touch him. She stood in front of him, close, on the ridged floor, and looked up into the smooth green almost-face with its lifted chin. She was a little taller than he was. She had to bend her head.
Then she touched her own face. The gray line. Two fingers, forehead to nose. Slowly.
And stepped back.
Corin had seen her greet the living that way. In the tent of hands. At the Edge. To Oren, at the cairn. He had not seen her greet the dead.
"Do you breathe them?" said Lyra. She had come up beside Corin. She was looking at the soldier too. "The way you breathed Old Hask. Do you—is that why you come here?"
"No," said Kestrel.
She turned round. Her tea-colored eyes went over the four from the south, one after another. Then back to the soldier.
"We do not breathe these," she said. "We cannot. There is nothing to breathe. The breath went out of them sixty years ago, all at once, with nobody there to take it. And the Glass closed over them." She looked down the Trench. At the next soldier, and the next. "The Glass holds them. The way it holds everything. It does not let anyone in. It does not let anything out. Not their breath. Not their Ash. Not their names."
"Then why do you come?"
"We come once a year," said Kestrel. "At the end of summer, when the Glass is not too hot by night and not too cold. A few of us. The ones who are walking out anyway. We come down into the Trench, here, where the wall is broken. And we walk the length of it. And we sing to them."
"Sing what?"
"The song for the dead. The one we sang at Last Thorn, when the tents burned. The same one." Kestrel's voice did not change. "We sing it to every one of them. One by one. We stand in front of each one, the way this one stood in front of him. And sing it once. And touch the line. And go on to the next." She paused. "It takes three nights to walk the whole Trench and sing to every one."
Nobody said anything.
Corin thought about it. Three nights. Walking along this dark green gut in the cold, from one glass man to the next, singing the same slow low song over and over, the way she had sung it at the Edge with her back to the fire. Every year. For sixty years. To men who could not hear it.
"That's all they get," said Kestrel. "A song. Once a year. From people who did not know them." She touched the gray line again. "And we think it is all they want."
"How do you know?" said Oren.
He said it roughly. Not unkindly. The way a soldier asks a question he needs the answer to.
Kestrel looked at him. For a long time.
"Because they are still standing," she said. "They could have fallen. In sixty years, with the Glass moving under them in the summers, the way it does, a little. Some of the walls have slumped. Some of the floor has cracked. They could have fallen down, and lain down, and been done." She looked at the short broad soldier. "But they are still standing. Every one. Still looking. As if they are waiting for something." Her voice went very low. "We think they are waiting for someone to come and tell them it is over. That they can put their hands down now. That they can sit down. And we do not know how to tell them. Nobody knows the words. So we do the only thing we can. We come, and we sing. So that at least they know, once a year, that someone remembers they are here."
Oren did not say anything.
He was looking at the soldier too. At the open empty hands. At the pike behind him against the wall, red lace on a round groove.
Then he did something Corin did not expect.
He took off his eye hide. And pulled the cap off his head, the old knitted cap he had worn since Thistle Ford, so that his short gray hair stood up in tufts. And held it against his chest. Both hands. The way the rider on the Lip had held his hat, that morning, over the smoke of Last Thorn.
And stood there.
Corin found that his own hand had gone to his head. He had no cap. He had nothing to take off. He let his hand drop.
Lyra had nothing either. Nor Vespera. But one by one, without a word, the four from the south bowed their heads. And stood there in the cool green quiet in front of the short broad soldier, for as long as it would take to say a name.
Then Oren put his cap back on.
"Right," he said. Rough again. "Right. Which way?"
"East," said Kestrel. "Along the Trench. In the shade." She looked up at the strip of white sky over their heads, between the two lips of the walls. "We do not like to walk in here. But it is noon, and the walls make shade, and the ticking is close. So today we walk in it." She settled her spear on her shoulder. "Do not touch them. Do not speak loud. Do not stop unless we stop."
She turned and went east, into the green dimness, past the short broad soldier and the next.
They followed her. One by one. Into the Trench.
