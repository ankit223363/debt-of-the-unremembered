Chapter 72: The Trench, Part 5 of 5: Out
Out
Oren carried him up.
He did not say anything. He did not need to. He came back down the last short leg of the Trench at a run, and turned his back, and crouched, and Corin got on, the way he had at noon. Arms round the thick neck. Bad leg out stiff. Crutch thrown up the slope to Arran, who was already at the top.
And Oren went up.
Not backwards, this time. Forwards. On all fours, almost, like a bear going up a rock, with Corin on his back, his big hands slapping onto the glassy ridges of the slope one after another and his boots shoving and slipping and shoving again. Corin could feel every breath go in and out of him. Great heaving breaths, like a bellows. Could feel the muscles of his back bunch and stretch under the coat. Could hear him grunting, low, at every push, a word that was not quite a word.
Halfway up, Oren's boot slid.
They went back. A hand's width. Two. Corin felt them start to go, felt his stomach drop, felt the slope tip under them. And then Oren's hand found a ridge and locked on it, and his whole body went rigid, and they hung there. Not moving. Corin could hear the knocking now, very loud, very close, somewhere to the south and above. Knock. And the ticking. And the grinding hum.
Then hands came down from the top. Arran's. Kestrel's. Lyra's. Grabbing Oren's coat, his arms, his collar. And pulled.
And Oren came up over the lip of the Trench in a heap, on his face, with Corin on his back, and lay there on the Glass with his arms spread and his chest going like a forge, and said, into the black, "Saints. Saints. Saints."
Corin rolled off him.
And lay on his back on the Glass, at the edge of the Trench, and looked up at the sky.
It was red. All of it. The whole bowl of the sky, from one edge of the world to the other, red and gold and purple, the way it had been the night before. The sun was down on the rim in the west, a fat red coal, touching its twin in the Glass. Going.
He rolled over onto his front, and got his hands under him, and lifted his head.
And saw the Fold Hills.
They were ahead. North and a little east. Not far. A mile, perhaps. Two. Rising up out of the flat shine of the Glass like nothing he had ever seen.
They were waves.
That was all he could think. They were waves. The kind of waves you see in a storm at sea, in pictures of shipwrecks. Great steep heaving waves, piled up one behind another, going away north and east as far as he could see in the red light. Taller than houses. Taller than the tower at the Ministry. With steep sides and sharp crests that curled over at the top, and deep dark troughs between them.
And they were not moving.
They were glass. The same black glass as the floor of the world. But here the floor had risen up. Had heaved, and piled, and curled over, the way water does when the wind drives it. And then, at the very moment of curling, of falling, had stopped. Had set. Had frozen like that, in the middle of the storm, forever, with their crests hanging over their troughs and never coming down.
The red light lay along the tops of them. Along every crest, a line of fire. And the troughs between were black. Pitch black. Darker than anything he had seen on the Glass. So dark they did not look like shadow. They looked like holes.
The Fold Hills. Black against the red sky. Frozen in the act of breaking.
Corin could not stop looking at them.
He had seen the sea. He thought he had. Somewhere in him there was the harbor at the bottom of the city, gray and choppy under a gray sky, with the gulls and the smell of tar and fish. And once, perhaps, a storm. Waves coming in over the sea wall at the end of the Fish Stairs, white and huge, and people running. He did not know whose memory that was. It did not matter.
The Fold Hills were that storm. That storm, at its worst. At the moment when the biggest wave of all reared up over the wall and hung there, curling, at the very top, before it fell.
And had never fallen.
He could see, now, in the last of the red light, that the waves were not smooth like the floor of the Glass. They were folded. That was why the Ashborn called them that. The black glass on their sides was creased and pleated and doubled over on itself, again and again, in long ridges, like cloth that has been pushed together on a table. Like the folds of a heavy curtain. Or the skin on the back of an old man's hand. Every fold catching the light along its edge, and every fold with its own small black shadow under it, so that the sides of the great waves were striped with red and black, red and black, all the way up to the curling crests.
And in some places the waves had broken through each other. Where one fold had pushed up under the next, the glass had cracked, and split, and left gaps. Dark slits, going in. He could see a few of them from here. Black, narrow, deep. Like doorways into nothing.
The old ways, Kestrel had said. Cracks and passes. Places where you could go under.
"Arran," said Lyra. Very quietly. "Have you been in there?"
Arran was sitting on the Glass with his long legs drawn up and his arms round his knees, looking at the hills.
"Once," he said. "A little way. With Old Hask. When I was small." He did not smile. "He took me to the first fold, and showed me where the way in was, and told me never to go further without him. And then he made me walk out again backwards. Facing it. So it would know I was not running away."
"Did it?"
"I don't know," said Arran. "I came out."
"Saints," said Vespera. Very quietly. She was standing beside him, small, with her hand at her throat. "Saints and all their mothers."
"That's where we go," said Kestrel.
She was looking at the hills, not at them. Her face was red with the light.
"In there," she said. "Through them. There are ways through. Old ways. Cracks, and passes, and places where the waves broke and you can go under. The Ashborn know some. Not all." She looked at the sun, going. "They cannot follow us in there at night. And in the day, in there, they will be slow. There is no straight line in the Fold Hills."
"Good," said Oren, from the Glass, without lifting his head.
Corin looked back.
Back over the Trench, across its dark zigzag, south, the way they had come. Across the long low swells of the open Glass, going purple now in the dusk.
Three shapes.
On the top of the nearest swell. Not far. Not far at all. Tall and dark against the last of the light. Standing in a row.
As he watched, the sun went down.
The last red sliver of it touched its twin in the Glass, and went in, and was gone. And the color went out of the sky all at once, like a lamp being blown out. And the cold came down.
And the three shapes on the swell came on. Down the near side of it, toward the Trench. Slower now. Much slower. Corin lay on the Glass and watched them come, and felt the cold come up into his palms from the black, the top of the Glass giving its heat back to the sky the way Kestrel had said it would. A little colder with every breath. And the three shapes slower with every step. Like clocks running down.
Then they stopped.
Corin saw them stop. Saw the one in front lift its foot for one more step. And hold it there. Hold it, a hand's width above the Glass, not moving. And then, very slowly, the foot came down. And touched. And stayed.
And the ticking stopped.
Not faded. Stopped. In the middle of a tick. As if somebody had laid a hand on the clock.
The silence came down over the Glass like snow.
Nobody moved for a long time.
Then Oren rolled over onto his back on the Glass beside Corin, and lay there looking up at the first stars coming out, and began, very softly, to laugh.
"Two fingers," he said. "Saints. Two fingers ahead." He wiped his face. "Didn't I say? That's all a march is."
Every Rest After
Lyra was the one who got up first.
Corin watched her do it, from where he lay on the cold Glass. She sat up. Pulled her eye hide off and stuffed it in her coat. Stood. Swayed, once, and caught herself. And then walked over to where Oren lay laughing on his back, and stood over him, and drew her sword.
Oren stopped laughing.
"You said," said Lyra. "Every rest after."
"Girl. I've just carried a grown man up a glass cliff."
"You said at noon, too. And we didn't. Because of the water." She set her feet. Flat. Heels down. Knees bent. Corin saw her do it, carefully, the way Oren had shown her, like someone remembering the steps of a hard sum. "So that's two you owe me."
Oren looked up at her for a long moment, from the Glass.
Then he groaned, and rolled over, and got up. Slowly. In stages. Knee, hand, other knee, up. His back cracked. He picked up his sword from where he had dropped it at the lip of the Trench.
"Saints preserve me from Thornes," he said. "Your father was the same. Never knew when to leave a man be."
But he set his own feet.
The others watched. They could not help it. There was nothing else to look at, at the edge of the Trench, in the dusk, with the Fold Hills black in front of them and the three frozen shapes behind, and the cold coming up out of the Glass. Arran sat down cross-legged on the black with his chin in his hands. Brin sat beside him. Vespera stood with her arms folded and her mouth thin, but she did not look away. Even Kestrel turned round from the hills.
Oren came at her. The same slow cut from high on the right.
Lyra slid. A hand's width. Turned from the hips. And her blade came up and took his, on the flat, and held.
One breath. Two.
Then Oren twisted, quick, the way he had in the morning, his blade going round hers and under. And Lyra—
Lyra did not let it.
Corin did not see exactly what she did. It was too quick. Something with the wrist. Something with the hips, a small sharp turn that took her whole body a little to the left, so that Oren's twist found nothing to twist against. And her blade was still there. Still up. Still on his.
Oren stepped back.
He lowered his sword. And looked at her. And his face, in the red last light, was doing something Corin had not seen it do before. Something between a grin and a frown, as if it could not decide.
"Where did that come from?" he said.
"I don't know." Lyra was breathing hard. Her face was white and set. "I don't know. I didn't think. It just—did it."
"That's not dancing."
"No."
"That's not anything I showed you."
"No."
Oren looked at her a long moment more. Then he lifted his sword again.
"Again," he said.
She held it three times out of five. Corin counted. He could count five. He watched her lose it twice, the sword going out of her hand and skidding off across the black with that long ringing scrape. And he watched her hold it three times, the blade coming up, the small sharp turn of the hips, Oren's twist finding nothing. And every time she held it, he saw her face change. Not a smile. Something fiercer than a smile, and quieter.
When they stopped, the sun was gone and the first stars were out above and below, and Lyra's hands were shaking on the hilt.
"That's enough," said Oren. "That's enough, girl. You'll wear it out." He sheathed his sword. He was breathing hard too. "Saints. Two days. Two days, and you're doing a thing I never taught you."
"Is that bad?"
"It's the best thing that's happened since the Edge." He shook his head. "You know what it is? It's your feet, finding something new to know. You took the dancing out and left a hole, and they've started putting something else in the hole. Something of their own." He clapped her on the shoulder, so hard she staggered. "That's how it goes, with soldiers who lose a hand. Or an eye. You think they're finished. And then a year on, they're doing things you never saw before, because they had to find a new way." He grinned. "Took them a year, mind. Took you two days. Showing off."
Lyra did not say anything.
She sheathed her sword. And turned round. And Corin, on the Glass, saw her face for a moment in the starlight before she saw him looking.
She was crying. Not much. Not so you would notice. But the tracks were there, on her cheeks, silver.
She wiped them with her sleeve, quickly. And came over and held out her hand to help him up.
He took it. She pulled. He came up, with the crutch, onto his good leg.
"One thing," he said.
"What?"
"For today. You've given me the heron. That's one." He got the crutch settled under his arm. "I'd like that one too. If I can have two."
She looked at him.
"What one?"
"That your feet found something new," said Corin. "On the Glass. Where nothing can be taken. Something came back."
Lyra did not answer for a long moment.
"It didn't come back," she said at last. "It's new. It's not the same."
"I know. I'll write that down too."
She let go of his hand.
But she walked beside him, after, when Kestrel turned them east along the lip toward the end of the Trench. On his bad side. Close. With her shoulder against his arm.
The Arrow
They did not rest long. Kestrel would not let them.
"They have stopped," she said. "Now we walk. All night. Into the hills. As far in as we can go before the sun." She looked at Oren and Lyra, standing breathing hard with their swords put away. "And no more fighting each other. Save it."
"Yes, General," said Oren.
They ate standing, in the last of the dusk, at the edge of the Trench. Bread and fat and dried goat. One mouthful of water each. Nobody talked. Corin leaned on his crutch and chewed and looked at the three dark shapes standing frozen on the swell to the south, very still against the purple sky, like trees in a field.
Then they walked. East along the north lip of the Trench, a little way, to where it ended.
It did not end all at once. It got shallower. The floor came up, and the walls came down, until it was only a long shallow dip in the Glass, waist-deep, then knee-deep, and then nothing. Just a last short stretch of wall, where the Trench had been dug into a low rise in the ground. A stub of wall, the height of a man's shoulder, standing up out of the dip at the very end. The last of it.
The last soldier stood there. A small man, by himself, at the very end of the Trench, with his back to the stub of wall and his face turned up to the north like all the rest.
Kestrel stopped beside him. She touched the gray line. Then she walked on.
And Corin, following on his crutch, came round the end of the last stub of wall. And saw it.
He stopped so suddenly that Lyra walked into his back.
"What—" she said.
Then she saw it too.
On the wall. On the end of the stub of wall, the side that faced north, toward the Fold Hills. The last smooth face of dark glass in the whole long Trench.
An arrow.
Drawn in chalk. White chalk, bright and thick, so fresh it almost glowed in the dusk. A big arrow, as long as Corin's arm. A straight line across the glass, with two short lines at one end to make the point. A little crooked. The lines did not quite meet at the tip. The point was a little too big for the shaft.
It pointed north. And east. Into the Fold Hills.
And under it, writing.
Capitals. Big ones, uneven, in the same white chalk. Some bigger than others. Leaning this way and that, as if they had been written by someone standing on tiptoe, or in a hurry, or both. The kind of capitals a child makes, who has learned her letters but not yet learned to make them all the same size.
THIS WAY IF YOURE NICE
Corin stared at it.
Nobody said anything.
Then everybody did, at once. Oren swearing by the Saints. Vespera saying "What in the—" and stopping. Arran laughing, short and startled, the bark, and then not laughing. Kestrel saying something sharp in the round speech to Oda and Sef, and the two of them going at once, spears up, one each way along the dip, looking.
Corin did not move.
He was looking at the letters.
THIS WAY IF YOURE NICE.
No mark between the R and the E. The little mark that should have been there. He knew where it should go. He could not have said how he knew, but he knew. YOU'RE. With a mark. There was no mark. Whoever had written it did not know about the mark. Or had forgotten. Or had not cared.
He put out his gloved hand, and touched the end of the arrow, very lightly, with one finger.
The chalk came off on the glove. White. Soft. Dry.
Fresh.
Not from the war. Not from years ago. Not a year. Not a month. Today, perhaps. Yesterday. This morning. Within a few days, at most. Before the wind had had time to touch it.
Somebody had stood here. Here, at the end of the Trench, at the edge of the Fold Hills, in the middle of the Glass, where nobody lived and nobody came. And taken out a stick of chalk. And drawn an arrow, standing on tiptoe, with their tongue between their teeth, probably, the way children do. And written under it, in big crooked capitals, for whoever came next.
IF YOURE NICE.
"Nobody lives out here," said Lyra. Her voice was strange. "Nobody. Kestrel said. Nobody comes out here but the Ashborn and the dead."
Kestrel did not answer.
She had come back. She was standing beside Corin, looking at the arrow. Her face, in the dusk, was quite still. But Corin, beside her, saw her hand go up. To the gray line on her face. And stop, halfway. And come down again, without touching it.
Oda and Sef came back. Kestrel asked them something. They shook their heads. Nothing. Nobody. Only the Glass and the dusk and the first stars and the Fold Hills going up black against the last of the light.
"A child," said Vespera. Very low. She was looking at the letters too. "That's a child's hand. A girl's. Seven, eight years old. Not more."
"Out here," said Oren.
"Out here."
Nobody said anything.
Corin looked from the letters to the arrow, and along the arrow to its crooked point, and along the point, out, north and east, into the Fold Hills. Into the dark between the great frozen waves, where the troughs were so black they looked like holes.
This way.
If you're nice.
He thought of the line of small footprints going south across the Glass. A child's. Twenty years and more.
He thought of the light that had come up under his hand in the night, and stayed, like a dog that hears a step it knows.
And he heard, very faint, somewhere far down inside himself, in the warm dim place where the old man had been, something that might have been a breath. Or might have been nothing at all.
He looked at Kestrel.
Kestrel looked at the arrow.
And then, slowly, she turned her face north and east. Toward the hills. Toward the way the crooked chalk point said.
"Well," she said, in the southern tongue, very quietly. "We are nice."
And she walked.
