Chapter 72: The Trench, Part 3 of 5: The Drummer
The Drummer
The boy was at a corner.
Not in the middle of a leg, like the others. Right in the angle where the Trench turned, tucked into it, the way a child tucks itself into the corner of a room when the grown-ups are talking. His back was against the place where the two walls met. His face was turned up to the north, like all of them. But he was so much smaller than all of them that his face only came up to where the others' chests would be.
Corin came round the corner and nearly walked into him.
He stopped. The crutch skidded on the ridged floor and he caught himself against the wall.
The others had stopped already. All of them. In a loose half-ring round the corner, a few steps back, the way people stand round something that has fallen in the street. Nobody was looking at anything else.
He was not yet a man, Corin thought. Older than a child, younger than a man. That age when the arms and legs have grown and the rest has not caught up. Thin. All knees and elbows, in the green glass. His coat was too big for him. Much too big. The sleeves came down past his hands, so that only the tips of his fingers showed, and the skirts came down nearly to his ankles, and the shoulders hung off him like a coat on a peg. Somebody else's coat. A father's. A big brother's.
And on his left hip, hanging from a strap that went over his right shoulder and across his chest, something round.
Corin looked at it.
It was a small round thing, a little wider than a dinner plate, and as deep as a hand is long. Hanging flat against the boy's hip, tilted, the way it would hang when he walked. The glass had kept its shape very clearly. The round top. The round sides. A pattern on the sides, a zigzag, like the zigzag of the Trench itself: cords, Corin thought, laced from the top rim to the bottom rim and back. And tucked through the strap across the boy's chest, two short thin sticks with round knobs on the ends.
The kind of thing a boy beats, on a march, to keep the men in step.
And the boy's head was turned up, and his mouth was a little open in the glass. As if he had been going to call something. Or sing.
At his feet, half sunk into the floor of the Trench, there was a pack.
Corin did not see it at first, because it was low down and in the corner's shadow. Then Lyra made a small sound, and he looked where she was looking.
It was a soldier's pack. A canvas bag, square, with a flap and two straps. Or the shape of one, in the glass. It had fallen from the boy's back, or been put down at his feet, and it lay on its side against the foot of the wall. And it had burst. The flap had come open and the side had split, the way a bag splits when it is dropped too hard or packed too full. And the things that had been in it had spilled out, a little way, onto the floor. And then the glass had come over everything. A thin skin of it. Clear. Clearer than the clouded green of the boy himself. So clear that it was like looking through a window. Into the burst pack. At what had spilled out.
A tin cup, on its side.
A spoon.
A square of something that might have been bread, gone dark.
A comb.
And half out of the split in the canvas, as if it had been pushed in last, on top of everything, so that it would be the first thing he found when he opened it at night: a doll.
A rag doll.
Corin went down on his good knee. Slowly. With the crutch. He did not know he was doing it until he was there, kneeling on the ridged floor in front of the burst pack, with his bad leg out stiff behind him and his face close to the glass.
He could see it very clearly.
It was small. Not much longer than his hand. Made of cloth that had been pale once, and was pale still, under the clear glass skin, because the glass had kept it as it was. A stuffed body, soft and a little flattened. Two arms. Two legs. A round head, stitched on.
A dress. Red. Faded, the way red fades, to a soft dull rose. With a little white collar.
A face. Stitched in thread. A small stitched mouth. Two eyes. One of them a black button, round and shining, sewn on tight. The other one gone. Only a few loose threads, where it had been.
And the stitches.
He bent closer.
The stitches round the collar, and down the seams of the arms, and round the edge of the face where it was sewn to the head. Very small. Very even. And every one of them leaning back. All the same way, a little. As if they were looking over their shoulder.
Corin knelt there and looked at them.
He had seen them before. He was sure of it. He did not have to reach for it. It was right there, in the Ledger, in his own square capitals: the doll in the basket with Robin, in the boat on the black water of the fens. Gray cloth. Faded blue dress. One button eye. Stitches that leaned back.
And the doll at Thistle Ford, in the long hall, by the fire, in a little girl's arms. Gray dress. Stitches that leaned back.
And now this one. Under the Glass. In a dead boy's pack. Sixty years old.
The same.
He did not know how long he knelt there. Long enough that his good knee began to ache on the hard ridges of the floor. Long enough that he heard, behind him, someone come up quietly and stand close. He knew by the breathing that it was Lyra.
And long enough that he heard, from far behind them, up on the open Glass, coming through the cool green wall, the ticking.
Tick.
Tick.
Tick.
For the Children Who March
"The valley mothers made them," said Kestrel.
She had come back. Corin had not heard her. She was standing at his shoulder, tall, with her spear grounded, looking down at the burst pack over his head. Her voice was very low. Lower even than the Trench made it.
He did not look up. He looked at the doll.
"When Sola called," said Kestrel, "the valleys came. Not only the men. The boys too. The ones who were old enough to carry, and some who were not. To beat the march. To carry water. To run with messages up and down the lines." She was quiet for a moment. "And the mothers did not want them to go. And the boys went anyway. Because everybody went."
She crouched down beside him. Slowly. Her white cape spread on the ridged floor.
"So the mothers sat up all night, the night before the march," she said. "In every house in every valley. And sewed. Little dolls, out of whatever they had. Old shirts. The ends of blankets. Each one for a child who was going. So they would have something to hold at night. Something from home, in the cold, in the dark, a long way off." She did not touch the glass. She looked at the doll through it. "Old Hask said his grandmother sewed three. For three boys from her valley. None of them came back."
"The stitches," said Corin.
His voice came out strange. Dry and cracked. He swallowed.
"They lean back," he said. "Every one."
"Yes."
"Why?"
"It is the old valley way. The old stitch. You sew it so." Kestrel moved her finger in the air, a little, over the glass. A small quick stitch, and a little twist of the wrist at the end of it. "So that every stitch looks back. Over its shoulder. Toward home." She lowered her hand. "So the child would always know the way back. That was what the mothers said. Old Hask said. Every stitch is looking home. Follow it."
Corin knelt there.
He thought of Granny Abbe. In the long hall at Thistle Ford, by the fire, with the little girl and her doll. He did not have to look it up. He had heard the old woman's voice say it, low, and written it down after. The old valley kind. Back-stitched. For the children who go over the glass.
"For the children who go over the glass," he said.
Kestrel looked at him.
"That's what they said at Thistle Ford," said Corin. "Granny Abbe. She said that's what the dolls were. For the children who go over the glass."
"Yes," said Kestrel.
She looked back at the boy. At his too-big coat, and his upturned face, and the round thing on his hip.
"Before it was glass," she said, "it was the march. The children who went over the hills with Sola. Who went to the war." She stood up. "And after it was glass, the valley people still said it. The same words. For the children who go over. But they did not know anymore what it meant. They only knew you sewed the old stitch, for a child who was going far." She looked down at Corin. "We think the valleys still sew them. A few old women. For a child who is going away. To the city, for work. Or over the Glass. Or anywhere far."
"Or for one who is coming back," said Corin.
He did not know why he said it.
Kestrel was silent.
Then she said, quite gently, "Get up, harvester. Your knee is shaking."
It was. He had not noticed. His good knee, the one he was kneeling on, was trembling against the ridges of the floor. He put his hand on the wall and pushed, and Kestrel took his other arm, and between them he got up. The crutch. The bad leg. Upright.
He looked down at the doll one more time. The faded red dress. The one button eye. The stitches, small and even and every one of them leaning back. Looking home.
Follow it, the mothers had said.
He thought about the line of small footprints, going south, across the Glass. Toward the valleys. Toward home.
And he did not say anything. He turned, and let Kestrel go, and stood leaning on his crutch in the corner of the Trench by the glass boy with his upturned face, and waited for his knee to stop shaking.
Robin's Doll
"Robin has one," said Lyra.
She said it to the glass. Not to Corin. Not to Kestrel. She was standing at the corner of the Trench with her arms folded tight across her chest, looking down at the burst pack, and she said it as if she were talking to herself.
"The same," she said. "Not red. Blue. Faded blue, like the sky before dark. But the same." She did not take her eyes off it. "Same size. Same button eye. One gone. Same stitches. Every one leaning back."
Corin looked at her.
He knew it already. He had seen it. But he had seen it the way he saw everything now: as a thing written down, a line in the Ledger, a fact without a feeling. Lyra had not seen Robin's doll. Not that he knew of. She had been gone ahead, with her father, on the Patient Grace, when he and Wat went through the fens in the punt with the boy asleep in his basket.
"How do you know?" he said.
"You told me." She still did not look at him. "At Thistle Ford. In the hall. After the little girl. You told me about the boat, and Wat, and the doll in Robin's basket. And I asked Granny Abbe about it. You were there." A pause. "You don't remember."
"No."
"It doesn't matter." She unfolded one arm and pushed her hair back off her forehead, under the eye hide. Her hand was not quite steady. "Wat said it was Old Pask's. The eel-man. At Sedge End. It had been up in his rafters for years, in the turf, with the eel-traps. And the day Robin wouldn't stop crying for his mother, Pask got it down and gave it to him. And he stopped."
She crouched down. The way Kestrel had. Close to the glass, with her arms round her knees.
"And Pask said a lady left it with him," said Lyra. "Years ago. A lady in a dark blue cloak. Asking the way to the coast. She said to give it to the one who comes after."
She said the last words very carefully. The one who comes after. As if each word were a stone she was setting down on a cairn.
Nobody said anything.
Corin looked at her face. The part of it he could see, under the edge of the hide. The line of her jaw. Her mouth, pressed tight at the corners. A small fold between her eyebrows, very deep, that had not been there that morning.
She was frowning. Not the way people frown when they are cross. The way people frown when they are trying to read something in a language they almost know.
"A lady in a dark blue cloak," she said again. To herself. "Asking the way to the coast."
"Lyra," said Corin.
"Eleven years ago," said Lyra. "In winter. In a hurry. Over the Narrow Sea." She was not talking to him. She was looking at the doll. "You'd go through the fens, wouldn't you? If you wanted to get to the coast and not be seen. Not on the high road. Through the fens, by the water, and out at the far side to the eastern ports."
She stopped.
Corin knew what she was not saying. He had heard her say it that very morning, walking on the Glass, in a low hard voice, after Vespera went ahead. Isolde left eleven years ago. Over the Narrow Sea. In winter. In a hurry. Nobody would tell us why.
"You think it was her," he said.
"I don't think anything." Lyra's voice was sharp. Too sharp. Then it went quiet again. "I don't know. A lot of ladies have dark blue cloaks. A lot of ladies go to the coast." She pressed her lips together. "But not a lot of them carry a doll that a valley mother sewed for a child who marched with Sola Vance. Not a lot of them have one of those to give away."
"How would she have one?"
"I don't know."
"Was she from the valleys? Your mother?"
"No." Lyra frowned harder. "Her people were from the east. Over the water. Fold it small. That's what my father said. That's all he ever said." She shook her head. "She didn't sew valley stitches. She sewed whitework. Flowers. White on white. Vespera said so."
She said Vespera's name and stopped. And Corin saw her eyes go, just for a moment, sideways. To where Vespera stood, a few steps back along the Trench, small and gray and very still, with her blue hands at her sides and her face turned away. Looking at the glass soldier by the north wall as if he were the most interesting thing she had ever seen.
Lyra looked at her for a long moment.
Then back at the doll.
"For the one who comes after," she said, under her breath.
She stayed crouched there, frowning at the glass, for a long time. Long enough that Oren shifted his feet behind them. Long enough that Kestrel looked up at the strip of sky over the Trench, at the light, which was beginning to go from white to gold. Long enough that the ticking came through the wall, twice, three times, louder each time, or seeming to be.
Corin did not hurry her.
He stood by her on his crutch and waited, the way you wait by someone who is reading a letter, until they look up.
She did not look up. In the end she only put out one finger and touched the glass over the doll. Just touched it. The way Kestrel had told them not to.
Nothing happened. The glass was cool and smooth, and gave nothing, and took nothing.
Lyra took her finger away. And stood up. And did not say anything else at all.
The Stitch
Vespera did not move away when Lyra stood up. She stayed where she was, by the north wall, with her face turned to the glass soldier there. But Corin, going past her on his crutch to follow the others round the corner, saw that she was not looking at the soldier at all.
She was looking at the doll. Sideways. Out of the corner of her eye. Through the gap between Lyra's shoulder and the boy's too-big coat.
He stopped beside her.
She did not look at him. "Go on, harvester. You'll be last again."
"I'm always last."
"Then go on and be last somewhere else."
He did not go. He leaned on the crutch and looked at her. Small and gray and bent, with her ragged hair and her sharp nose and her slit of a mouth, and her blue hands hanging at her sides. The rope belt with its needles and its bottles and its little bird scissors. Her eye hide pushed up on her forehead.
He knew her. He knew her the way he knew his own hands. Hook Lane. The back room. The needles laid out on the cloth, longest to finest. The crow laugh. The smell of ink and burnt honey. He knew all of it.
Except that there were holes. Wherever Lyra had been, in all of that, there were holes. And he could feel, standing beside Vespera now, that one of the holes was here. Close. Something to do with Lyra, and Vespera, and the stitching, and a thing Vespera was not saying.
He did not ask. He had learned, with Vespera, that asking was the surest way to be told nothing.
So he only said, "The stitch."
Vespera's mouth went thin.
"The one that leans back," said Corin. "Kestrel said it's the old valley stitch. You sew it so it looks home." He looked at her blue hands. "You'd know it. You know every stitch there is."
Vespera was quiet for a long time.
Then she lifted one of her blue hands, and looked at it. Turned it over. The way he had looked at his own gray one, before he put it on the soldier's chest.
"I was taught it," she said. "Once."
He waited.
"A long time ago," said Vespera. "Before Hook Lane. Before the city. Before I had a single needle of my own." She flexed the fingers. "By someone who sewed it better than anyone. The twist at the end. You do it with the wrist, not the fingers. Like this." Her hand moved in the air. Small, quick, and a little turn at the end, exactly as Kestrel's had. But neater. Much neater. Like a thing done ten thousand times. "Most people get it wrong. They lean it too far, and it looks drunk. Or not far enough, and it doesn't look like anything. You have to lean it just so. Just enough to look back. Not so much it falls over."
"Who taught you?"
Vespera lowered her hand.
"I've never used it," she said. As if he had not spoken. "Not once. Not in forty years. I've done every other stitch there is. Whitework and blackwork and tambour and the tiny ones for the inside of a wound. I've stitched names into skin. I've stitched a tree with a crown behind a baby's ear." She did not look at him when she said it. "But never that one. I never could bring myself to."
"Why not?"
"Because it's for the ones who go away," said Vespera. "And I never wanted to send anyone."
She pulled her eye hide down over her eyes, sharply. Hiding her face.
"Go on, harvester," she said. "You're blocking the Trench."
He went on.
But at the corner he looked back, once. And she was still standing there, by the north wall, small and gray with the hide over her eyes. And as he watched, she turned her head. Not to the soldier. Not to the doll. To Lyra's back, going away from her up the next leg of the Trench.
And she stood looking at it, with her blue hands folded tight together in front of her, until Lyra went round the next corner and was gone.
Oren and the Boy
Oren had not moved since they came round the corner.
He was standing in front of the boy. Close. Closer than anyone else had gone. His big boots on the ridged floor a step from the boy's sunken feet, and his head bent, and his eye hide pushed up on his forehead so that Corin could see his face. And his face was quite still.
He was looking at the round thing on the boy's hip.
Corin came and stood beside him. Lyra too, after a moment, on his other side. Nobody said anything for a long time.
Then Oren said, "He'd have been at the front."
His voice was low and rough. Steady.
"The boys with the—" He stopped.
Corin waited.
Oren's jaw moved under his beard. Corin could see him reaching for it. Reaching, the way Corin reached for numbers past nine. And not finding it. A word that should have been there, that had been there his whole life, that he had said ten thousand times on ten thousand roads. Gone. Paid away, a long time ago, in the city, for a wall of light.
"The thing you beat," said Oren at last. Slowly. "For the calls." He lifted one hand a little and made two small quick movements in the air. Down, down. Like a man knocking twice on a door. "That. The boys with that. They go at the front. Or in the middle, where everybody can hear. They keep the step. They beat the calls. Advance. Halt. Close up. Fall back." His hand came down again. "Everybody listens to them. A whole column of men, and they all listen to one boy. That's the job."
He was quiet for a moment.
"I marched with four of them, once," he said. "On the border. When I was young. Four boys, the youngest one not much older than this. Good lads. Cheeky. Used to steal the sergeant's tobacco and smoke it behind the wagons and be sick." Something that was almost a smile moved under the beard and went away. "One of them could beat the advance so fast you couldn't see his hands. Showed off with it. We used to make him do it for coins, round the fires."
Nobody asked what happened to them.
Oren told them anyway.
"They put them in front," he said. "That's where they go. So everybody can hear. And when the other lot opens up, the first thing they aim at is the front. Not because they want to kill boys. Because they want to stop the calls. You stop the calls, you stop the column." His voice did not change. "So the boys go first. Every time. That's the job too. Nobody tells them that part when they sign."
He looked at the glass boy's face. The open mouth. The lifted chin.
"Three of mine went in one morning," he said. "At a ford. The fourth one, the one who showed off, I carried out on my back. He had a ball in his hip. He lived. Never walked straight after." A pause. "He used to write to me. After. Every year. Till I couldn't read his letters anymore."
He put out his hand.
Kestrel had said not to touch them. Corin saw her see it. Saw her open her mouth, and then close it again, and say nothing.
Oren laid his big hand on the boy's shoulder.
On the glass. On the green clouded glass of the too-big coat, where it hung off the narrow shoulder like a coat on a peg. Gently. The way you would lay your hand on the shoulder of a boy who has done well, who has kept the step all day and not complained, at the end of a long march.
And left it there.
"You did all right, lad," said Oren.
His voice was steady.
His hand was not.
Corin saw it. They all saw it. The big square hand on the glass shoulder, with its scarred knuckles and its thick fingers, that had held a sword steady for thirty years in front of things Corin could not imagine. Shaking. A small fine fast shake, all through it, from the wrist to the fingertips. Like a leaf. Like the hand of a very old man.
Oren did not seem to know. He did not look at it. He kept his eyes on the boy's face, and his voice steady, and his hand where it was.
"You did all right," he said again. "You kept the step. You stood your ground. Nobody can say you didn't." A long breath. "You can put it down now. It's done. Somebody should have told you. I'm telling you. You can put it down."
The boy did not move. He could not. He was glass.
But Corin, watching, thought of what Kestrel had said. We think they are waiting for someone to come and tell them it is over. And we do not know the words.
Oren did not know the words either. He had lost so many. But he had found some.
He took his hand off the boy's shoulder. Slowly. And put it in his coat pocket, as if to hide it. And turned away.
"Right," he said. His voice was rough now. Very rough. "Right. That's enough. We've a long way. Let's go."
He went round the corner, into the next leg of the Trench, fast, without looking back.
After a moment, Kestrel went after him.
Corin stood by the boy a little longer. He looked at the shoulder where Oren's hand had been. There was no mark. The Glass did not take marks.
But he thought, for just a moment, in the green light, that the boy's lifted face was not quite so lifted. That the chin had come down, a hair. That he looked, very slightly, less like a boy waiting, and more like a boy who has been told he can stop.
It was the light. Corin knew it was the light. The sun had moved, up there, and the shadows in the Trench had moved with it.
He went on round the corner anyway, very quietly, the way you leave a room where someone has just gone to sleep.
