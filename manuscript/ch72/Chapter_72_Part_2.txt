Chapter 72: The Trench, Part 2 of 5: Valley Men
Valley Men
Oren walked slowly.
Corin had never seen him walk like that. Oren walked the way soldiers walk, all the time, even in a kitchen. A steady even pace, the same length every step, as if there were a drum somewhere that only he could hear. He had walked like that across the fens and up the drove road and over the Lip and down the thorn slope and all the way out onto the Glass, and he had never once changed it.
He changed it now.
He went along the Trench like a man going round a room full of sleeping people. Looking at each soldier as he came to him. Stopping, sometimes, for a breath. Then going on.
Corin went behind him, on the crutch, because he was slow anyway and Oren was slower. And he watched what Oren looked at.
The coats.
The glass had kept the shape of them. Long coats, past the knee, heavy, with wide skirts that flared out at the bottom. Most of them belted. Some with a short cape over the shoulders, like a carter's coat. They were not all the same. That was the first thing Corin saw. One was longer, one shorter. One had a hood down at the back, a soft bulge in the green. One had pockets, square ones, low on the hips. One had a patch on the elbow, a darker square in the glass where the cloth had been thicker.
Not uniforms. Not like the gray coats of the Enforcers, every one cut from the same bolt, every button in the same place.
Own coats. Coats from home.
And on every one, high on the left side of the chest, the badge.
Corin stopped by the next soldier and looked at it close. It was a small round shape, a little bigger than a coin, standing out from the front of the coat in the glass like a boss on a shield. The glass had kept it clearer than the face, because it had been hard. Metal, he thought. Brass, or tin. A disc, with something on it, raised.
Rays.
A circle, and lines going out from it all round, like the spokes of a wheel. Short. Even. More of them than he could feel.
A sun.
"Thistle Ford," said Lyra, behind him. Quietly. "The door of the long hall. The carved sun."
Corin remembered. The great oak door with the worn sun on it, that Granny Abbe had pushed open with her shoulder. And the torn banner over the hearth, with its faded yellow sun. And the cross at Haverstock, with the sun at the top.
"And the inn sign," said Lyra. "The sun and the pike."
Oren had stopped by a tall soldier at the next turn of the Trench. He was standing in front of him, close, with his hands at his sides. Looking at the coat.
Corin came up beside him.
This soldier's coat had a wide collar, turned up, as if it had been cold. And the skirts were tucked up, the front corners pulled back and fastened behind at the belt, the way men do when they have to march fast through wet ground, to keep the hems out of the mud.
"See that," said Oren. Very low. "The skirts. Hooked back."
"Yes."
"That's an old way. The old way. Valley way." He did not take his eyes off the coat. "My grandfather did that. With his work coat. Going out in the rain. Hooked the front back behind him like that and grumbled about it. I never knew why. I thought it was just how he was." He shook his head slowly. "He was a valley man. It was how valley men wore their coats. To march in."
He looked along the Trench. At the next soldier, and the next. Their own coats. Their own boots. Their badges.
"These aren't soldiers," said Oren.
Corin looked at him.
"Not real soldiers. Not like me. Not trained men, paid men." Oren's voice was very quiet. "These are farmers. Look at them. Look at the boots. That one's got boots three sizes too big, he's wound rags round his shins to keep them on. That one, there, the coat's been his father's, you can see where it's been let out. Valley men. Farmers with pikes." He looked at the red lace of the pike heads along the south wall. "Shepherds and smiths and men who mend walls. Came down off their hills with the pikes off their barn walls and stood in a ditch in the mud and waited for the gray coats."
He was quiet for a moment.
"The gray coats had muskets," he said. "Carbines. Cannon, likely. Proper drilled troops, in rows. I know how they'd have come. I've seen it. I've stood in a ditch and watched it come." His jaw moved under his beard. "And these lads stood here with pikes. Waiting for it. Knowing."
He said it with something in his voice that Corin had not heard there before. Not pity. Something much bigger than pity. The thing a man feels for someone who has done the thing he has spent his whole life being afraid he would not be brave enough to do.
"Farmers with pikes," said Oren again. Softly. As if it were a title. As if it were the highest name you could give a man.
He touched two fingers to the brim of his cap.
Then he walked on. Slowly. To the next one.
And Corin, behind him, found that he was counting them. The soldiers. He did not mean to. One. Two. Three. Every few yards, another. Four. Five. Each with his own coat, and his sun badge, and his face turned up to the north. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.
And then the gray place, the blur, where the numbers went.
He started again.
One.
Count, boy, the old man had said.
He thought, perhaps, he was beginning to understand what to count.
In the Shade
They walked east along the Trench through the noon and into the first of the afternoon, and it was the strangest walk of Corin's life. Or of the part of it that he had.
The Trench did not go straight. It went in its zigzag, the way it had looked from the top of the swell. A long leg one way, more steps than he could feel. Then a sharp corner, almost a right angle. Then a long leg the other way. Then another corner. On and on. You could never see more than one leg of it at a time. You came round a corner, and there was the next stretch, straight, green-lit, with its glass men standing along it. And at the end of it another corner, and a wall, and nothing beyond.
"Why does it bend like that?" said Vespera, at the third corner. Her voice was small in the dead air. "It's a ditch. Why not dig it straight?"
"So a cannonball can't go all the way down it," said Oren, from ahead, without turning round. "You dig it straight, one shot from the end kills every man in it. You dig it like this, one shot kills the men in one leg. The rest live." He came to the corner and stopped, and looked round it before he went on. Out of habit, Corin thought. "Somebody here knew their business."
The shade was good. That was the thing that mattered most, in the end, more than the glass men. The walls were high enough and the Trench narrow enough that only at the very middle of the day did the sun get down to the floor, and then only in a strip, down the center, a foot wide. If you walked close to the south wall you were in shadow. Cool green shadow. The air was still and dead, and it smelled of nothing. But it was not the white shaking furnace of the open Glass.
Corin could feel his body loosen in it. He had not known how tight he had been, up there, in the sun. Every muscle braced against the heat, the way you brace against wind. Down here he could breathe.
But he could not stop looking at them.
They stood along the walls in the green light, every few yards, and he went past each one on his crutch, slowly, close enough to touch, and each one was different. A man with a beard: you could see it, a soft blunt shape in the glass round the jaw. A man with no hat and a long plait down his back like Arran's. A man with something in his hand. Corin stopped for that one. A small square dark shape, held in the open fingers, at waist height. A book, perhaps. Or a tin box. Or a loaf of bread.
A boy, not much more than a boy, very thin, with his coat hanging off him.
Two men standing close together, shoulder to shoulder. Their arms were touching. One of them had his hand on the other's back, between the shoulder blades. The way you put your hand on someone's back to say, I'm here.
Corin went past those two very slowly.
Nobody talked. Kestrel had said not to speak loud, and nobody spoke at all. There was only the soft scuff of felt on the ridged floor, and the pat of his crutch, and breathing. And once, from somewhere ahead, Marrit's voice, very low, saying something in the round speech. A few words. Over and over. The same ward she had said that morning, after the light under his hand.
And behind them, under everything, the ticking.
He could hear it now without putting his hand to the Glass. Down here, in the dead quiet of the Trench, it came to them through the walls. Not loud. But clear. Tick. Tick. Tick. Steady and even and patient. Coming from behind them and above them, from the south, from the open Glass they had left. Getting no louder that he could hear. But never getting fainter.
Oren stopped at the next corner and listened.
Corin came up beside him. Oren had his head on one side, the way he did. His eyes were shut.
"Closer," said Oren.
"How close?"
Oren held up his hand. One finger, straight. And a second beside it, bent at the knuckle. Half a finger.
Corin looked at the hand. He could feel one. He could feel two. This was somewhere between, and the between was the part that frightened him.
"Less than two hours," said Oren quietly, seeing his face. "More than one. That's all you need to know." He opened his eyes. "They're going straight. Across the top. We're going down a ditch that bends like a dog's hind leg. Every corner costs us."
"Should we get out? Go across the top?"
"In that sun?" Oren shook his head. "We'd be dry as leaves by dusk. Kestrel knows. We stay down here till the sun's lower. Then we get out." He looked at Corin. "And then we run."
He went round the corner.
Corin stood a moment longer, listening to the ticking come through the green wall.
Then he followed. And the next leg of the Trench opened in front of him, long and straight and dim, with its glass men standing along it in the shade, and the strip of white sky overhead, and at the end of it another corner, and a wall, and nothing beyond.
Marrit
Marrit was waiting for him at the next corner.
Not for the others. For him. She was standing in the angle of the walls with her long pale pole planted on the floor and both her old hands folded on the top of it, and when he came round the corner on his crutch, she did not move aside. She stood in his way. And looked at him.
He stopped.
She was very old. He had known that, but he had not looked at her closely before, not in the day. In the green dimness of the Trench he could see her face better than he had ever seen it in the glare or the dark. It was brown and dry and folded, like a walnut, like Granny Abbe's. Her white hair was cut down to a short stubble over her skull. The gray line ran from her forehead down over her nose, and on, past the tip, over her upper lip, to the corner of her mouth. Further than Arran's. Not as far as Kestrel's. Her eyes were small and very dark, and they were looking at him without blinking.
"Marrit," he said.
She said something. In the round speech. Low and slow. Three or four words.
He did not understand them. He shook his head.
She said them again. Slower. And then she let go of the pole with one hand, and lifted the hand, and pointed. At his left hand. His gray one. On the crutch.
Then she turned the hand over, palm down, and held it flat in the air. And slowly lifted it. And as she lifted it, she moved her other fingers under it, wiggling them, rising. Something coming up. Following.
The light. In the night. Coming up under his palm.
She had seen. Lyra had said so, that morning. Marrit had been sitting on her heels with her pole across her knees, saying the ward over and over.
"Yes," said Corin. "I know. I don't know why."
Marrit looked at him.
Then she said a word, sharp, over her shoulder. And Arran came back round the corner from where the others had gone on. He looked at his great-aunt, or whatever she was to him. Then at Corin. Then at his feet.
"She wants me to tell you something," he said. He did not sound happy about it.
"Then tell me."
Arran listened while Marrit spoke. It was not long. A few sentences, in her low dry old voice, with her small dark eyes on Corin's face the whole time. When she stopped, Arran was quiet for a moment.
"She says," said Arran slowly, "that when she was a girl, her grandmother told her a thing about the lost ones. The lights. She says most people don't know it. The old ones don't tell it, because it frightens the children, and because it's never happened. Not in anybody's time."
"What thing?"
"That the lost ones come up to anyone," said Arran. "To a sleeping hand. Everybody knows that. They come up, and look, and go down again. But they only stay," he said, "for their own."
Corin did not say anything.
"That's what her grandmother said." Arran shifted on his feet. "That if one of the lost ones comes up to you, out of the deep, and stays, it is because you are its own. Its blood. Its child, or its grandchild, or its sister's child. Something of it is in you. And it knows. And it comes up to see." He swallowed. "She says that is what the story says. She says she doesn't know if it's true. She says nobody has seen it, to know."
He stopped.
Marrit said something else. Short.
"She says," said Arran, "until last night."
The Trench was very quiet. Far off, through the wall, the ticking. Tick. Tick.
Corin looked at the old woman.
"I've never been here," he said. His voice came out flat and hard, harder than he meant. "Tell her. I've never been here in my life. I'm from the city. I was a clerk. In the Harvesting Section. I don't have any family out here. I don't have any family anywhere. Tell her."
Arran told her.
Marrit listened. Then she did something Corin did not expect. She smiled. Not a big smile. A small one, at one corner of her mouth, where the gray line ended. The way an old woman smiles at a child who has just told her, very seriously, that he is not tired, while his eyes are closing.
She said something.
"She says," said Arran, "that's what the city does. It tells you you have nobody."
Then Marrit put her hand into the pouch at her belt. The one she had taken the river stone from, at the first cairn. And took out something else. Small. She held it out to him, on her flat old palm.
It was a scrap of thread. A short length of it, faded, the color of old blood, with a knot tied in the middle.
Corin looked at it.
"For the bed," said Arran, as Marrit spoke. "She says Kestrel told you. About the bed. The ones you carry, sitting on the edge." He was quiet a moment, listening. "She says, if the light comes again, and you want to know whose it is, you tie this round your finger. And sleep. And it may tell you. In the old way. Or it may not." A pause. "She says she does not think you will want to know. But it is yours to choose."
Corin did not take it.
Marrit did not take it back. She stood there, holding it out, with her small dark eyes on his face, as patient as the glass men.
In the end he took it. Because his hand moved. Because he could not stand there in the Trench with an old woman holding out her hand forever. He took the scrap of red thread off her palm with his gloved gray fingers and put it in his left coat pocket, with Wren's stone.
Marrit nodded, once. As if that were what she had expected.
Then she took up her pole in both hands again, and stepped aside, and let him pass.
He went round the corner. He did not look back at her. But he could feel the thread in his pocket, against the stone, all the rest of the way down the Trench. Weightless. Like nothing at all.
One Badge
It was in the fifth leg of the Trench, or the sixth. Corin had lost count of the corners. He was going slowly behind the others, because the crutch did not like the ridged floor; its felt foot kept catching on the edges of the old boot-holes and skidding off. And he had stopped to rest, for a breath, with his back to the cool south wall and his bad leg out straight in front of him.
And there was a soldier in front of him. On the other side of the Trench, by the north wall.
A tall one. Taller than the rest. Taller than Oren, Corin thought, though it was hard to tell, because the glass had slumped a little at the feet, so that he stood sunk to the ankles in the floor, as if he were standing in deep mud. He was thin. Long in the arm. His coat was a good one, better than most: longer, with a double row of round shapes down the front where the buttons had been, and a collar that came up high round the jaw. And no belt. A sash instead, it looked like. A soft wide band round the middle, tied at one side, with the ends hanging down.
His face was turned up to the north, like all of them.
But his hand was not at his side. His right hand was at his chest. Flat, over his heart. Over the place where the badge was.
Corin looked at the hand. Then, because the hand made him look, at the badge under it.
The long glass fingers covered most of it. But not all. Between the first finger and the second there was a gap, and through the gap he could see the edge of the badge, standing out from the coat in the glass. And it was not a sun.
He leaned forward a little, on the crutch, to see.
It was round, like the others. The same size. But there were no rays round the edge. Instead, inside the circle, there was a shape. He could see only part of it, through the gap in the fingers. A line going up, straight, from the bottom of the circle. And from the top of the line, lines going out. Branching. Curving. Like the lines of a tree drawn by a child: a trunk, and branches.
A tree.
And in the branches, near the top, a shape that was not a branch. He had to tilt his head to see it. A little shape with points on top. Three points, or four. Like a comb. Or the top of a fence. Or a—
A crown.
A tree, with a crown caught up in its branches.
Corin looked at it.
It was a pretty thing. That was what he thought. That was all he thought. It was a pretty little thing, very neatly made, the tree with its spreading branches and the tiny crown sitting up among them like a bird's nest. Somebody had taken trouble over it. Somebody had cut it into a disc of brass or tin, very small and fine, with a good tool and a steady hand, so that even through sixty years of glass, through a gap in a dead man's fingers, you could see the shape of every branch.
A family badge, he thought. Perhaps. The way the great houses in the city had their badges, painted on their carriage doors. A tree with a crown. Some valley family, very old, very proud, who had their own mark and wore it instead of the sun.
Or a guild. A trade sign. Woodcutters, perhaps. Or orchard men.
He did not know. It did not matter. It was a pretty badge.
He looked up at the tall soldier's face. Long, and narrow, with a high forehead under the glass. And the hand pressed flat over the tree with the crown, over his heart, as if he had put it there on purpose, at the last. As if, when he turned round and saw the light coming, the thing he had wanted to hold on to was that.
Corin did not know why that made his throat go tight. He did not know this man. Somewhere, he thought, he had seen something like the badge. Not long ago. On a carriage door in the city, perhaps, or a page in a book. He could not think where. It sat at the edge of his mind like a word on the tip of the tongue, and when he reached for it, it slid away, the way things did now.
It did not matter. It was only a badge.
"Corin." Lyra's voice, low, from the corner ahead. "Come on. Oren says we have to keep moving."
"Coming."
He pushed himself off the wall. Got the crutch under his arm. Took one more look at the tall soldier with his hand on his heart.
Then he went on, round the corner, after the others. And did not look back. And had forgotten the badge, almost, before he reached the next soldier.
Almost.
But not quite. All down the next long leg of the Trench, for no reason he could name, he found his gray hand going up, every little while, to the back of his head. Behind his left ear. To the place there where the hair grew thin. And rubbing it. The way you rub a spot that itches when you cannot remember what bit you.
