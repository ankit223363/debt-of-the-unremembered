Chapter 5: Juno on the Rooftops, Part 4 of 5: The Catch
The Catch
There was a moment when nothing happened at all.
He hung in the air. The gutter hung with him, a long gray tongue of lead sticking out from the side of the roof, tipping slowly outward. Everything was very quiet. He could see, with great clearness, a pigeon sitting on the ridge of the warehouse across the gap, looking at him with one orange eye. He could see a barge far below, with a dog asleep on its roof in the sun. He could see the brown water of the Cut, wrinkled by the wind, shining.
He thought: I will not remember this.
And then his hands closed.
They closed on the gutter. On the lip of it, the old soft lead edge, as it swung out past him. They did not ask him. They simply caught it, the way they caught everything, and held. And the gutter swung on out to the end of its swing, and stopped, with a jerk that nearly tore his arms out of their sockets, and hung there, groaning, with one end still bolted to the wall by a single rusted bracket and the other end sticking out over the Cut like a diving board.
And Corin hung under it.
His legs swung over nothing. His boots kicked at nothing. His whole weight hung from his two hands, his fingers hooked over the edge of a strip of rotten lead no wider than a belt, and the lead was bending. He could feel it bending. Slowly. Like soft cheese.
Above him, on the roof, the iron bracket gave a small, sharp tink, like a bell being touched.
"Mister V!"
The voice came from the left. From the wall. He turned his head and saw Juno.
She was on the drainpipe, a little way below the roof, where she had gone first. She was clinging to it with her arms and knees, the way a child clings to a tree, and her face had gone the color of paper. She was staring at him. At the gutter. At the gap between them, which was not very far. An arm's length, perhaps, and a bit. More than an arm's length. Too far.
"Hold on," she said. Her voice was high and thin. "Hold on."
"I'm holding."
"Hold harder."
The lead bent a little more. His fingers slipped, a fraction, and caught again.
Above them, on the ridge of the rope-walk, a shadow came. A big shadow, with a bandage round its head. The man with the shovel face leaned out over the edge of the ridge and looked down at them, breathing hard, and grinned, and his grin was all brown teeth.
"Well," he said. "Look at that."
He had the cosh in one gray-gloved hand, swinging from its leather loop. He looked at the gutter. He looked at the last iron bracket, holding it to the wall. He looked at Corin's hands, white on the gray lead.
He began to climb down the slope of the roof toward the bracket. Slowly. Carefully. Feeling with his boots. He did not need to hit Corin. He only needed to reach the bracket, and kick it.
Corin.
The voice came from his right side. From the long narrow pocket of his coat, pressed against his hip. Low and dry and sour, like an old man who has been woken from a nap and does not like it.
You've got yourself in a fine mess, haven't you.
He did not know the voice. And then, at the same time, he did. Not with his head. With his whole body, the way he had known the stairs at No. 14 and the chair at Vespera's. It belonged to the cold silver rod, and it had been in his ear for a long time, a very long time, and it was not his friend, exactly, but it was not his enemy either. It was something in between. Something like a bad-tempered uncle who lives in your house and will not leave.
Malakor, he thought. The name was there, in the dark, as if it had been carved on the inside of his skull. It had been carved on the rod. He had read it a hundred times.
At last. Listen to me. Your hands are going to fail in about ten heartbeats. That's not a guess. I've counted the fingers of a great many falling men. The voice was quick and flat. There's a sailor in the rod. Little one. Dock-rat. Bosun's boy. Drowned off the Long Quay in a winter storm nine years ago. Not much of him left, but what's left is hands. He hung in the rigging of a brig for three hours in a gale once, with the sea coming over him, and he didn't let go. Burn him.
Burn him?
Not all of him. Just the three hours. Just the holding. A pause. You don't burn your own. You know the rule. This is what the Ash is for. Do it, boy. Now. Eight heartbeats.
Corin did not know how.
And then he did.
It was like reaching into a dark cupboard for something you know is on the second shelf. He reached, not with his hands, but with something behind his eyes. Down. Into the cold. Into the rod, through the wool of his coat, through the silver, into a place that was full of whispering, like a crowded room heard through a wall. Voices. Hundreds of them. Fragments. A woman singing. A man counting coins. A child crying. A dog barking in a yard. And among them, small and wet and shivering, a boy. A thin boy in a wet jersey, with salt in his hair and his fingers frozen to a rope, and the black sea coming up under him, and the black sky coming down, and the wind screaming, and one thought in his head, one, over and over, louder than the wind: don't let go don't let go don't let go don't
Corin took it.
He took the three hours. He took the rope and the ice and the screaming wind. And he burned them.
It did not hurt. It felt like a match catching in a cold room. A small hard bright flare, behind his breastbone, and then heat running down his arms, through his elbows, through his wrists, into his fingers. And his fingers changed.
They did not grow. They did not look any different. But they closed. They closed on the soft lead the way a vise closes, slowly and without stopping, with a strength that was not his and never had been. The lead crumpled under them like paper. His fingertips went right through it, and curled under, and locked.
He could have hung there for three hours. He knew it. In a gale. With the sea coming over him.
Somewhere, on Juno's drainpipe, there was a sharp little gasp.
"Your eyes," she breathed. "Mister V, your eyes have gone all—"
"I know," said Corin. He did not know. But he could feel it. A cold at the edges of his sight, like frost creeping in from the corners of a window. "Juno. Listen. I can hold. I can hold a long time. But I can't climb. The gutter won't take it. I need you to—"
Tink, said the bracket above.
The big man had reached it. He was crouched on the slope of the roof just above the place where the gutter met the wall, with one boot braced against the slates and the other lifted over the bracket. He looked down at Corin and grinned again.
"Tull says hello," he said. "From his bed. He can't see out his left eye no more. He says to tell you—"
He never told him.
The roof under him gave way.
It was the rope-walk roof, old and long and tired, and he was a very big man, and he had come down its slope fast and heavy and stopped hard in one place, and the slates there had been sitting on rotten battens for fifty years waiting for exactly this. They went with a crack like a tree falling. A whole patch of roof, slates and battens and tar paper and all, the size of a door, simply dropped away under his boots. And the big man dropped with it, straight down, into the long dim building below, with a roar of surprise that turned into a howl.
There was a great twanging, whirring, crashing sound. The sound of a very heavy man falling into a machine full of very long ropes. Then shouting, from inside, many voices. Then the howling again, muffled now, and furious, and somehow tangled.
Corin hung from the gutter and listened.
The bracket held.
Well, said Malakor, in his ear, sounding almost pleased. That was luck. Don't get used to it.
"Mister V."
Juno had moved. She had come up the drainpipe while the big man was talking, and now she was at the very top of it, where it met the broken edge of the roof, close to the wall. She had wedged herself into the corner there, between the pipe and the brickwork, with her back against the bricks and both boots jammed against the pipe, and her knees bent. She looked like a cork wedged in the neck of a bottle. Her face was white and set and very serious.
She stretched out her arm toward him. Her right arm. As far as it would go.
It was not far enough. There was a gap between her fingers and his left hand. Not much. The length of a hand. But in that gap, there was nothing at all, all the way down to the brown water.
"Swing," said Juno.
"What?"
"Swing. Like a door. Toward me. Push off with your feet. The gutter'll go with you. When you come close, let go with one hand and grab mine. I'll hold."
"You can't hold me. You're half my size."
"I'm wedged," said Juno fiercely. "I'm wedged like a nail in a plank. I've hauled whole sacks of coal up this pipe for the bargemen. I can hold you. Swing."
He looked at her. Fourteen. Thin. White-faced. Wedged into the corner of a wall four stories above a canal, with her arm out, and her mouth set like her grandmother's.
He swung.
He pushed off with his boots against the air, which did not help, and then against the brick wall below the gutter, which did. The gutter groaned and swung toward Juno on its last bracket, like a gate. He swung with it. Closer. Her hand came toward him. Closer.
He let go with his left hand.
For one heartbeat he hung by his right hand alone, the sailor's hand, locked into the lead. The world tipped. The water below swung round like a wheel.
And Juno caught him.
Her hand closed round his wrist. Small and hard and hot and very strong. Her fingers did not meet round his wrist; they were not long enough. But they closed as far as they would go, and dug in, and held.
"Got you," she said through her teeth.
"Now what?"
"Now let go the gutter."
"Then I'll fall."
"Then you'll swing. Into the wall. Under me. And you'll grab the pipe with your other hand. Your magic hand. And you'll hold the pipe instead of the gutter. And then we go down." Her arm was shaking. He could feel it shaking, all the way down into his wrist. "Do it now, Mister V. I can hold. But I can't hold forever."
He let go of the gutter.
He fell. Not far. A short swinging drop, sideways, like a sack on a rope, and her arm jerked straight and she gave a cry, short and sharp, but she did not let go. And he swung in under her and hit the wall hard with his shoulder, and his right hand, the sailor's hand, shot out and found the drainpipe, cold and round and iron, and closed on it, and locked.
And he was holding the pipe. And she was holding him. And the gutter, empty, swung out over the Cut on its single bracket, and hung there, groaning, and then, slowly, with a long tired creak, tore free, and fell.
They watched it go. It turned over twice in the air, slowly, flashing gray in the sun. It hit the brown water between two barges with a smack like a hand slapping a table. The dog on the barge roof woke up and barked at it.
Juno did not let go of his wrist.
He was hanging just below her now, with his face close to hers, his right hand on the pipe and his left wrist in her fist. He could see the sweat on her lip. He could see a tiny white scar through one of her eyebrows. He could see the soot on her cheek, and the tears she was refusing to cry standing in her eyes, very bright.
And he could see the key.
It had swung out from her coat when she stretched, and it hung on its dirty string right in front of his face, turning slowly, an inch from his nose. A big old brass key, heavy, as long as his finger. The bit at the bottom had seven teeth, cut deep and square, like the battlements on a castle. The shaft was worn smooth and bright where fingers had rubbed it, for years. For a lifetime.
And the bow of the key, the round part at the top, was not round.
It was shaped. Cast in the brass, small and worn but clear. An open book, with its pages spread. And above the book, an eye.
Closed.
Corin knew that sign. He did not know how, but he did. He had seen it that morning on the brass buttons of his own coat. He had seen it carved over a pair of great iron doors, three times the height of a house. It was the sign of the Ministry of Records.
And under the book, on the flat of the bow, there were letters stamped into the brass. Tiny. Worn almost away. He had to look hard to read them, hanging there with his face an inch from the key and the brown water four stories down.
NIGHT DOOR. III.
"Don't," said Juno.
Her voice was very quiet. She was looking at him looking at the key.
"I'm not touching it."
"You're reading it." She swallowed. "Nobody reads it. Nobody's ever close enough." She took a long shaky breath. "Can you climb down now? On your own? With your magic hand?"
He could. He did not know how he knew. But the sailor's hands knew drainpipes, and ropes, and the sides of ships.
"Yes."
"Then I'm letting go. Don't fall off. If you fall off after all that I'll never speak to you again."
She let go of his wrist.
He did not fall. He went down the drainpipe hand under hand, the sailor's grip locking and unlocking, steady, slow, while the frost at the edges of his eyes began, slowly, to melt. Juno came down above him, fast and quiet. At the bottom, the pipe ran into the canal wall a few feet above the water, and a barge was tied up right beneath it, low and broad, with a black tarred deck and a heap of coal sacks in the middle and the dog on its roof.
Corin dropped onto the coal sacks. Juno dropped beside him. The dog looked at them both, decided they were not interesting, and went back to sleep.
They sat on the coal sacks in the sun, side by side, breathing.
Above them, through the hole in the rope-walk roof, a voice was still shouting, muffled and furious and tangled in rope. Further off, on the ridge, the man with no neck stood looking down at them with his hands on his hips. He did not try to come down. After a moment, he spat over the edge, carefully, and turned away.
Corin looked at his right hand. It was trembling now. All the strength had gone out of it, all at once, like water out of a cracked jug, and it felt hollow and light and strange. He opened and closed it. He tried to remember the boy in the wet jersey. The rigging. The gale.
There was nothing there. Only a cold place, very small, like the hole where a tooth has been.
Gone, said Malakor. That's how it works. You used him up. He held on for three hours once, and now he's held on for you, and now there's a little less of him in the world. A pause. The dry voice was almost gentle. Write him down, if you like. Bosun's boy. Long Quay. Nine winters back. It's more than anyone else will do.
Corin took out the Ledger. His hands were shaking so much he could hardly hold the pencil. He wrote it anyway, on one of his last pages, crooked, in letters that wandered up the paper: A bosun's boy, drowned off the Long Quay nine winters ago. He held the rigging for three hours in a gale. I burned it to hold a gutter over the Cut. Thank you.
Juno watched him write. She did not say anything for a long time. She was sitting with her knees pulled up and her arms wrapped round them, and her right arm, the one that had held him, was shaking too, and she had tucked her hand into her armpit as if it hurt.
"You burned something," she said at last. "Just then. Up there. I saw your eyes go."
"Not mine. Somebody else's. Somebody dead."
"Does that make it all right?"
Corin looked at the words he had written. Thank you.
"I don't know," he said honestly.
Juno nodded slowly, as if that was the right answer, or at least not the wrong one. Then she put her chin on her knees and looked out across the brown water of the Cut, at the tall black warehouse on the other side, and the great red chimney of the Dye-Works rising behind it into the bright sky.
"You owe me," she said.
"I know."
"More than two coppers."
"I know."
"Good," said Juno. Her voice was not quite steady. "Just so long as you know."
The Pigeon Loft
Nobody jumps the Cut. But anybody can walk it, if they know the barges.
Juno knew the barges. She went across the canal from one to the next as if they were stepping stones in a stream, without looking, hopping from the black tarred deck of the coal barge onto the low flat deck of a timber barge, and from there across a narrow strip of brown water onto the roof of a little painted cabin with geraniums in pots on top, and from there onto a barge loaded with sacks of grain. A woman came out of the painted cabin with a broom and shouted at her. Juno shouted something back that made the woman laugh. Corin followed more slowly, one barge at a time, and said sorry to everyone, and the woman with the broom looked at him and said to Juno, "Who's the long streak of misery?"
"He's mine," said Juno. "I'm training him."
"Good luck," said the woman, and went back inside.
On the far side of the Cut there was a stone quay, and a row of tall black warehouses standing on it with their feet in the water. Their walls were blackened brick, and their windows were small and barred, and great iron hooks hung out over the canal from their top floors, on beams, for hauling up sacks. The Dye-Works chimney stood up behind them, enormous, square, red. Up close it was so tall that Corin had to tip his head right back to see the top, and when he did, the whole chimney seemed to lean over him, slowly, as if it were about to fall.
Juno led him to the end of the row, to the last warehouse, which was the tallest and the blackest and the most broken-down. Half its windows had no glass. A sign over the great double doors said H. GRISWOLD & SONS. DYESTUFFS. ESTD. and then nothing, because the rest of it had fallen off. On the side of it, an iron stair went up the outside wall, zigzagging back and forth, rusty and steep, all the way to the roof.
"Nobody uses it," said Juno. "Griswold's been shut since before I was born. The sons all went to sea and never came back." She started up the stair. "Except me. I use it."
The stair shook and rang under their boots. It went up past five floors of broken windows. Through them, Corin saw long empty rooms with great stained vats standing in rows, crusted on the inside with old dye, blue and red and a sick yellow-green. The smell of it came out at him through the broken glass, sharp and sour and chemical, and under it the smell of pigeons. Thousands of pigeons. He could hear them, inside, cooing and rustling in the dark.
At the top of the stair, on the flat roof of the warehouse, there was a shed.
It was a pigeon loft. A long, low wooden shed, painted green once and now mostly gray, with a sloping tin roof and a row of little square doors along the front with landing boards under them. Pigeons sat on the boards and on the roof of the shed and on the parapet round the edge of the warehouse roof, gray and white and speckled and brown, dozens of them, puffing out their chests in the sun. They did not fly away when Juno came. They came toward her, walking, bobbing their heads, as if she were someone they knew.
"Mine," said Juno. She said it with great pride. "Well. Griswold's. But nobody's fed them in fourteen years except me, so they're mine now."
She opened the door at the end of the loft and went in, and after a moment Corin bent his head and followed her.
Inside, it was dim and warm and smelled of straw and feathers. Half of it was nesting boxes, rows of them, with pigeons murmuring in them. But the other half, at the far end, behind a partition of old planks, was something else. A nest of a different kind.
There was a heap of old sacks and blankets in the corner, for sleeping on. There was an upturned crate for a table, with a stub of candle on it in a saucer, and a tin cup, and half a loaf wrapped in a cloth. There was a biscuit tin with a picture of a ship on the lid. And the walls were covered.
Not with notes, like his. With maps.
They were drawn in chalk on the planks of the walls, in Juno's hand, big and careful and crooked. Maps of roofs. He could see that at once. Here was Tallow Street, a long line, with all the roofs along it drawn in as little peaks and boxes, and every chimney marked with a dot. Here was the rope-walk, a long thin rectangle. Here was the Cut, a double line with waves drawn in it. And all over them, small marks and arrows and symbols that he did not understand. A circle with a cross through it. A little drawing of a foot. A row of dots that went from one roof to another across a gap in the street. And in some places, words, written slowly in capitals with the letters the wrong way round sometimes.
SKILITE. DONT STEP.
BAD SLATES.
DOG.
PIKES ROOF. KEEP OFF OR ELSE.
THE CUT. NOBODY.
Corin stood in the middle of it all and turned slowly round.
"You made all these," he said.
"Course I did." Juno had sat down on her heap of sacks, cross-legged, and was rubbing her right arm, the one that had held him, up and down, wincing. "Took me years. Every roof from the river to the Salt Stairs. Where you can go and where you can't. Where it's slippy in rain. Where there's a dog that'll come up through a skylight at you." She shrugged, as if it were nothing. But she was watching his face. "That's how I know the quick ways. For the messages."
"It's a Ledger," said Corin.
"What?"
"Like mine." He touched his coat, over the book. "It's a map so you don't get lost."
Juno looked at the walls, as if she had never seen them before. Her mouth moved. She did not say anything. But after a moment she looked away, quickly, at the pigeons, and he saw that her ears had gone red.
He sat down on the upturned crate, carefully, because his legs had suddenly decided they did not want to hold him anymore. His right hand was still trembling. He held it in his left to keep it still.
There was a small window in the end wall of the loft. No glass in it, just a square hole with a wooden shutter propped open. Through it, very close, so close that it filled half the sky, there was the Dye-Works chimney. Red brick, square, enormous, going up and up. And near the top, the black iron band, with a narrow iron rail round it, like a little balcony, and an iron ladder going up one side of the chimney to reach it, rung after rung after rung.
"That's where they stood," said Juno, following his eyes. "Up on the rail. Looking back at me."
"How did they get up there?"
"Ladder. It's for the sweeps." She got up off her sacks and went to the biscuit tin and opened it. Inside, among a jumble of things he could not see, there was a small brass telescope, old and dented, the kind sailors carried. She pulled it out to its full length with a squeal of brass on brass and gave it to him. "Here. Look. Top of the ladder. On the rail."
He put it to his eye. It took him a moment to find the chimney. Everything was blurry and huge and jumping. Then he found the red bricks, and followed them up, and up, and up, rung after rung of the iron ladder, until he came to the black band and the rail.
Something was there.
Tied to the rail at the top of the ladder, with what looked like a piece of black thread, so that it hung down and turned slowly in the wind. Something white.
He twisted the end of the telescope, and the white thing jumped closer and came clear.
A flower.
A single white lily. Big and waxy and perfect, with its petals curling back and its long gold tongues sticking out from the middle. Tied by the stem to the iron rail of a factory chimney, a hundred feet above the ground, in the smoke and the soot, where nothing white should ever be.
It turned in the wind. Slowly. Round, and round. Like something on a string.
Corin lowered the telescope.
"That wasn't there before," said Juno. Her voice had gone small. She had taken the telescope from him and was looking through it herself. "This morning. When they stood up there and looked at me. There was nothing on that rail. I'd have seen. I see everything." She lowered it. Her face was white again. "They came back. While I was at your window. They came all the way back across the Cut and climbed all the way up there and tied that on. For me to see."
"Or for me," said Corin.
They looked at each other.
Outside, the pigeons cooed and shuffled. A long way below, a barge horn hooted on the Cut. The lily turned, far off and small now without the telescope, a white speck on the red chimney, like a star in the daytime.
Corin took out the Ledger. He wrote, on what he now saw was his second-to-last blank page: Dye-Works chimney. A white lily tied to the rail at the top. Not there at the seventh bell. There now. A message. He looked at it. Then he added: She knows Juno saw.
He did not show that line to Juno.
She had gone back to her sacks. She sat down hard, and pulled her knees up, and wrapped her arms round them, the way she had on the coal barge. She did not look at the window. She looked at her maps.
"Mister V," she said after a while.
"Yes."
"When you were hanging there. And you were reading my key." She did not look at him. "What did it say?"
Corin was quiet for a moment.
"You know what it says."
"I want to know what you read. Say it."
"An open book," said Corin. "And an eye. Closed. The Ministry's sign. And under it, Night Door. Three."
Juno nodded slowly. Her hand had gone up to the key, and closed round it, the way he had seen it close in his room. Her thumb was moving over the side of it. Slowly. Back and forth. Back and forth.
Corin watched her thumb.
And he saw, as clearly as if it were written in the Ledger, an old man in a too-big brown suit, sitting in a round patch of gray light on a dark landing, with his clean thin hands folded in his lap. And his right thumb moving. Back and forth. Back and forth. Across the side of his first finger. Over and over. As if it were holding something small that was not there.
And a pale line round his thin neck, where a string had been.
"Juno," he said softly. "It's Terrence's, isn't it."
The thumb stopped.
For a long time Juno did not say anything at all. She sat on her sacks with her knees pulled up and her fist closed round the key, and she looked at the chalk maps on the wall, and her mouth worked, the way her grandmother's had in the dark onion-smelling passage, as if there were something too heavy to lift.
"Grandpa," she said at last. Very quietly. "He's my grandpa."
The pigeons murmured.
"He was a night porter," said Juno. "At the Ministry. Forty years. Forty years, Gran says, every night, from the ninth bell to the sixth. He sat in a little room by a little door at the back of that big black building and he let people in and he let people out and he wrote their names in a book. Night Door Three. That was his door." Her fist tightened on the key. "He used to say it was the most boring job in the world. And the most important. Because nobody watches doors at night. Only him."
She stopped. Corin did not say anything. He waited, the way Vespera waited.
"When I was little," said Juno, "before. He used to bring me up on the roof. Our roof. On his nights off. He'd sit on the ridge with me in his lap and wrap his big coat round both of us, and he'd show me the stars. He knew all their names. Every one. He said the sailors gave them names so they wouldn't get lost, and if I knew the names, I'd never get lost either." Her voice wobbled. She pushed it flat again. "That's how I started on the roofs. Him. He taught me not to look down."
"What happened?"
"I was six." She said it flatly, like a date in a book. "He went to work. Same as always. Ninth bell. He kissed Gran and he kissed me and he said don't let the stew burn, love, same as always. And in the morning, a Ministry cab came to the door. A black one, with the eye on it. And two men in gray coats got out, like yours. And they brought him in. Holding his arms. And he was smiling."
She swallowed.
"He didn't know Gran. He didn't know me. He didn't know his own name. He just smiled, and said good morning, polite, to everyone, the way he does. And his hand was shut. His right hand. Shut so tight on this"—she lifted the key a little on its string—"that the knuckles were white. And they couldn't get it open. The Ministry men tried. Gran tried. The doctor tried. For a week he wouldn't let go of it. Not when he ate. Not when he slept. Like it was the last thing in the whole world he knew."
"And then?"
"And then one morning he just opened his hand, and it fell on the floor, and he didn't even look at it." Juno's voice cracked right down the middle, like a dropped plate. "And Gran picked it up and put it on a string round his neck, so he'd still have it. And he wore it two years. And then one day I asked if I could hold it. And Gran said yes. And I put it on." She stopped. "And I never took it off again. And he never asked for it back."
The thumb started moving again, slowly, on the brass.
"The Ministry sent a letter," she said. "Gran's got it in the tin with the rent book. An accident in the course of duty. That's all it says. And a pension. Two silver a month." She laughed. It was not a nice sound. "Two silver. For all of him."
Corin looked down at the Ledger on his knee. At the line he had written on the stairs this morning, about a thumb that moved and a pale line round an old man's neck. He had written it without knowing why.
He thought about the man in the gray coat, which was himself, and the gray coats that had brought Terrence home in a black cab. He thought about the brass buttons on his own coat, with their open book and their closed eye.
"Is that why you won't burn anything?" he asked. "Mrs. Marta told me. You won't burn a single memory. Not even a small one."
Juno's head came up fast.
"Gran doesn't know anything," she said. "Gran thinks I'm stupid. Gran thinks the lamps are wicked, and the shops on Rope Street that sell feelings in little bottles are wicked, and everybody who buys a happy afternoon for a copper is wicked. They're not. They're only memories. The people who sold them didn't need them. They got paid. It's fair." She glared at him. "That's not the same. That's buying and selling, like bread. It's different from—from what they did to Grandpa."
"How do you know?" said Corin.
He said it gently. He did not mean anything by it. The words simply came into his mouth from somewhere he could not find. A dark passage. A smell of onions. A small dry voice, very sure of itself. I tell her, how do you know? Did you ask them? He did not remember hearing it. But his mouth seemed to.
Juno stared at him.
For a moment he thought she was going to throw something at him. The tin cup. The biscuit tin. The telescope. Her face went red, and then white, and her mouth opened to say something fierce.
And then it closed again.
She looked away from him. Out of the window. At the white speck of the lily on the red chimney, turning, turning.
"You sound like Gran," she muttered.
"I think," said Corin slowly, "that might be where I got it."
Juno did not answer. But after a while, very quietly, she said, without looking at him: "I won't burn anything. Not ever. Not even a little thing. Not even if I'm dying." Her fist closed tighter on the key. "Because one day I'm going to find out what happened at Night Door Three. And when I do, I'm going to want to remember every single thing about him. Every star. All their names."
She was quiet for a moment more.
"He's still in there," she said. "Somewhere. His thumb still knows the key. You saw it. I know you saw it. You wrote it down." She turned and looked at him, and her eyes were very bright and very hard. "Didn't you."
"Yes," said Corin. "I wrote it down."
