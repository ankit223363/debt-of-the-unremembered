Chapter 5: Juno on the Rooftops, Part 3 of 5: The Girl on the Gutter
The Girl on the Gutter
There was a face at the window.
It was upside down.
Corin's hand went to the long pocket on the right side of his coat, and closed on the rod through the wool, and stopped there. The face looked at him. It was a small, thin, sharp face, brown from the sun and smudged with soot across one cheek, with a pointed chin and a wide mouth and two very bright dark eyes. It was hanging down past the top of the window frame, as if its owner were lying flat on the slates above with their head over the edge. A cap had been jammed down hard on its head so that it would not fall off, and a few strands of dark hair stuck out from under it, pointing at the ground.
The face grinned at him. Upside down, the grin looked like a frown, and that made it worse.
Then it vanished. There was a scrape and a thump on the roof, and a scuffle of boots on slate, and a pair of legs in patched gray trousers swung down past the window, and a pair of scuffed boots landed in the wide mossy gutter right outside, as lightly as a cat landing on a wall. And there was a girl crouching there, on the gutter, four stories above Tallow Street, with one hand holding the edge of the window frame and the other in her coat pocket, looking in at him through the cracked glass.
She was small and thin and fourteen, or near it. She was dressed like a boy, in a coat much too big for her, with the sleeves rolled up three times, and the gray trousers, and the boots, and the cap. Round her neck, on a piece of dirty string, there hung a key. A big old brass key, as long as his finger, with a round bow at the top and a heavy toothed bit at the bottom, swinging gently against her coat as she breathed.
She pointed through the glass at the hook of the latch, hanging loose. Then at herself. Then at the latch again. Her eyebrows went up.
Corin took his hand off the rod. He reached over and pushed the window open.
It swung outward on a squeal of rusty hinges. The girl leaned back to let it pass, without letting go of the frame, without even looking down, as if the gutter were a pavement and the four stories of air beneath her were a carpet.
"You let anyone in, Mister V?" she said.
Her voice was quick and husky and very much of the Lower Docks, all clipped ends and dropped letters. She did not wait for an answer. She swung one leg over the sill, then the other, and slid into the room and landed on his floor, and stood up, and dusted off her hands on her trousers. Close to, she smelled of soot and pigeon and the wet green smell of moss.
"Don't tell me," she said. "You don't know me."
Corin looked at her. At the cap. At the coat. At the brass key on its string.
At Vespera's, before he had even stood up out of the chair, he had read a line at the bottom of a page from two nights ago, written in the rain. He could see it now, in his head, the way you can see a word after you have looked away from it.
"Juno," he said. "Mrs. Marta's granddaughter. From the purple door. Two coppers a message. One if it's raining."
The girl stared at him.
For a long moment she did not say anything at all. Her quick mouth opened, and stayed open. Her bright eyes went very wide. Then her face did something complicated, a kind of scowl and a kind of grin fighting over the same space, and the grin won.
"Well, look at you," said Juno. "You remembered."
"I read it."
"Gran says that's cheating."
"Della says it's the same thing."
"Della's six." Juno sniffed. It was her grandmother's sniff exactly, small and sharp, like a cat sneezing. "Still. Nobody ever reads me. They read the important stuff. Rent and work and don't-forget-your-keys. They don't read Juno." She looked at him sideways, under the brim of the cap. "You wrote down my key, too."
"How do you know?"
"Because you looked at it. Just now. Same as you looked at it in the rain." Her hand went up and closed round the key, tight, the way a small child holds a toy that someone has tried to take. "Everybody looks at it. Nobody touches it."
"I won't."
"Good." She let go of it. It swung back against her coat. "Then we'll get on."
She turned away from him and walked round the little room, slowly, the way a cat walks round a room it has not been in before. Except that she had been in it before. He could see that. She did not look at the board of notes, or the bed, or the crate. She went straight to the window, and bent down, and looked at the cut in the brown paper. Then at the hook on the latch. Then at the gutter outside. Then back at him.
"Thought so," she said.
"You saw them."
It was not a question. She grinned again, that quick white flash he had seen from the street in the rain.
"Course I saw them. I see everything on these roofs. That's my job. Gran thinks I'm up here playing chase with the Pike boys. I'm not. I'm working." She hopped up onto the edge of his table, the short-legged one, and sat on it, swinging her boots, and the whole table tipped and rocked on its wad of folded paper. "I been watching your window two nights. Since the night you went out in the rain with your jar-stick at three in the morning and didn't come back. Gran was spitting. She said, that boy is going to get his throat cut one of these nights and nobody will know which one. So I watched." She shrugged. "Somebody had to."
Corin sat down slowly on the edge of his bed. He took out the Ledger. Juno watched him do it with great interest.
"Tell me," he said. "All of it. What you saw."
"It'll cost you."
"How much?"
She thought about it. She looked at his coat, and his boots, and the bare little room, and the empty jars on the shelf, and she seemed to do a sum in her head and not like the answer.
"Two coppers," she said at last. "Same as a message. Because it's sort of a message. From me to you."
He found the two coppers in his outside pocket, beside the nail and the white-beaded hatpin, and held them out. She took them very fast, and bit one, which he did not think anyone really did, and put them both away somewhere inside her enormous coat.
"Right," she said. "This morning. Seventh bell. I'm on Fisher's roof, across the way, the one with the bent chimney. Eating my bread. And there's a lady comes down Tallow Street in a white coat. Long. Right down to her boots. Clean as anything." Juno's nose wrinkled. "You don't see that down here. Nobody wears white down here. White is for the Hill, and for being buried in."
"Mrs. Marta told me. She knocked on her door."
"She did. Very polite. Gloves. Little black book." Juno swung her boots harder. "Only here's the thing, Mister V. Gran couldn't see what I saw, because Gran was looking at the white lady, like everybody always does. You can't help looking at white. That's the point of it, ain't it? You can't help looking." She leaned forward on the table. It rocked. "I wasn't looking at her. I was looking at the roofs. That's my job. And while the white lady was standing on our step being polite to Gran, somebody else came over the roofs from the riverside and went straight to your window."
Corin's pencil stopped.
"Somebody else."
"Not in white. Dark. A big dark cloak, the kind with a hood, the color of a wet roof, so's you'd hardly see it against the slates. Small. Smaller than you. Not so small as me." She held one hand up flat, at about the height of a short woman. "Moved good. Really good. Like they'd done roofs before. Didn't slip once. Not even on the green bit by Odding's skylight, and everybody slips on that." She sounded almost admiring. "Went down onto your gutter, cut your paper with a little knife, quick as that, lifted the hook, and went in."
"How long were they inside?"
"Long enough for the white lady to finish with Gran and walk all the way up to the top of the street." Juno thought. "Long enough for me to eat my whole bread and half an apple. Then they come out again, and pulled the window shut behind them, and went back the way they came. Over the ridge and off toward the river. And the white lady caught the tram on the Docks road. Going east."
East, Corin thought. Toward Hook Lane.
He wrote it all down, small, as fast as he could. Two of them. White coat at the front door, polite, while someone in a dark hooded cloak came over the roofs to my window. Small. Good on roofs. Went in, came out, back toward the river. White coat took the tram east afterward. He looked at what he had written. The white coat at the door, making everybody look. And the dark one going in at the back while they looked.
Together, then. Or not together at all, and only the same morning by bad luck. He did not know which.
"Did you follow the dark one?" he asked.
Juno's boots stopped swinging.
"I tried," she said. For the first time she sounded less sure of herself. Younger. "I went after them. Over Fisher's, over the chandler's, down the long roof of the rope-walk. They were fast. But I'm faster. I was nearly on them, nearly, by the time we got to the Cut." She scowled. "And then they jumped it."
"The Cut?"
"The canal. The old one, that goes from the river up behind the tanneries. It's got warehouses both sides, tall ones, and they lean in over the water, and at the top there's a gap between the roofs. About so wide." She held her arms out as far as they would go, and then a bit further, and then shook her head, because her arms were not long enough. "Nobody jumps the Cut. Not even the Pike boys. Not even me. Billy Pike tried it on a dare last summer and he broke both his legs on a barge and his mam still won't speak to Gran." She took a breath. "The dark one jumped it. Like it was nothing. Didn't even run up first. Just stepped off the edge and landed on the other side and kept going."
"And you lost them."
"I lost them." Juno's mouth went tight and angry. She did not like saying it. "But I saw where they were going. Before they went over the far ridge. They stopped for a breath on the top of the Dye-Works chimney, the big square one, and they looked back. At me." She was quiet for a moment. "I couldn't see a face. The hood was too deep. But they looked at me for a long time. And then they did a thing."
"What thing?"
Juno lifted one hand. She held it up, fingers together, palm toward him, the way you might hold your hand up to stop a cart. And then, slowly, she turned it over and closed it, finger by finger, into a fist. As if she were catching something out of the air and folding it away.
"Like that," she said. "Then they went over the ridge, and that was that."
Corin looked at the closed fist for a long moment.
"And there was a smell," said Juno.
She uncurled her hand and wiped it on her trousers, hard, as if something had got on it.
"When I got to your gutter. After. Right where they'd been crouching, at your window. It was still there." Her nose wrinkled up. "Flowers. Big white ones. Heavy and sweet and sort of sick, like when old Mrs. Coyle on Rope Street died and they had her laid out in the front room for three days in August."
The skin on the back of Corin's neck went cold.
You do not like lilies. He did not need the Ledger for that one. It was written on his arm.
"Funeral flowers," he said.
"That's it." Juno nodded. "Lilies. Gran won't have them in the house. She says they're for the dead and for liars." She looked at him. Her bright eyes had gone sharp and serious. "Mister V. Who'd you make cross?"
Corin did not answer. He looked down at the Ledger. At PARTNER, and Lyra Thorne, and Lilies. A woman. White. Was at Oakhaven. At Pell Dorran, who had smelled lilies in a dark lane and then been emptied into a silver cup.
He looked at the window, at the cut in the brown paper, at the loose hook.
She had been in here. Or someone who worked for her, who carried her smell on their cloak. In here, in his room, while he slept in Vespera's chair. Reading his jars. Untying his knot. Leaving him a note in his own hand, very nearly, so that he would wake up tomorrow and read it and believe it.
STOP LOOKING.
And she had looked back at a fourteen-year-old girl from the top of a chimney, and closed her hand into a fist, slowly, as if she were folding something away.
"Juno," he said.
"What?"
"Can you show me? The way they went. Over the roofs. As far as the Cut."
Juno looked at him. Then she looked at his long gray Ministry coat, and his town boots, and the long wooden case on the bed, and her wide mouth twitched.
"You?" she said. "On the roofs?"
"Me."
"You'll fall off and die and Gran'll kill me."
"Then I'll be careful."
"You're never careful. Mister Holt says so. Everybody says so." But she was already sliding off the table. She went to the window and put one boot on the sill, and looked back at him over her shoulder, and he saw that under the scowl she was pleased. Very pleased. Like someone who has been carrying a secret for two nights with nobody to show it to, and has finally found someone who wants to see.
"Leave the stick," she said.
"No."
"It'll get in the way."
"It comes with me." He looked at the long wooden case on the bed. He had carried it under his arm all the way from Hook Lane, and it was empty. The rod itself had been in the long narrow pocket on the right of his coat all night, silver and cold and as long as his forearm, with a word worn almost smooth along its side. He touched it through the wool. Then he left the empty case lying on the bed. "There. Lighter."
Juno looked at the rod in his pocket, and then quickly away, the way people always seemed to look away from the jars.
"Fine," she said. "But if you fall, I never knew you."
And she went out of the window and up onto the roof as if she were going up a flight of stairs. And after a moment, with his heart beating hard and his hands cold, Corin climbed out after her.
Over the Roofs
The first thing he learned was not to look down.
The second thing he learned was that it did not matter whether he looked down or not, because his stomach knew exactly how far away the street was, and it told him, over and over, in a cold sick voice.
He stood in the wide mossy gutter outside his own window with his back pressed against the slates and his hands flat on them on either side of him, and he breathed. The gutter was lead, old and soft and green, and it was wider than he had thought, as wide as his two boots side by side. It was solid. It did not move. But beyond its edge there was nothing at all, only air, and far below, four stories down, Tallow Street, very small and very far, with a cart going along it the size of a toy, and a man pushing a barrow, and the top of somebody's hat.
"Don't look down," said Juno, from somewhere above him.
"I'm not."
"You are. I can see the back of your neck. It's gone all white." Her head appeared over the edge of the slates, upside down again. "Turn round. Face the roof. Use your hands. The slates are rough, see, they grip. Go up on your toes and your fingers, like a cat going up a tree. Don't stand up straight till you're at the top. Standing up straight is how you go off."
He turned round. It was the hardest thing he had done that day, harder than reading the first page of his own life in a stranger's chair. He turned until his face was close to the slates, gray and rough and warm from the sun, with little yellow crusts of lichen on them like spilled mustard. He put his fingers on them. They did grip.
He went up.
And something strange happened.
His body knew how.
Not all of it. Not the way Juno knew, quick and easy as a squirrel. But his fingers found the edges of the slates by themselves, and his weight went forward over his toes without his telling it to, and his knees bent and stayed bent, and he went up the slope in a low crouch, steady, one hand and one foot at a time. It was as if someone else were moving him. Someone who had done this before, many times, on a different kind of slope. A slope that moved. A deck, perhaps, tipping in a high sea. A mast.
Ash, he thought. Someone's left something in me. A sailor. A roofer. He did not know who. He could not remember. But his hands remembered for him, and he let them.
He came over the ridge on his hands and knees and found Juno sitting astride it as if it were a horse, waiting for him, with her chin on her fist.
"Huh," she said. "You're not terrible."
"Thank you."
"I said not terrible. I didn't say good." But she looked pleased. She swung one leg over and stood up on the ridge, on the very top of it, where it was no wider than a plank, and stretched. "Right. Look."
He looked.
The roofs of the Lower Docks went away from him in every direction, like the sea. A gray and red and black sea, frozen in the middle of a storm. Peaks and valleys and ridges, all at different heights, all at different angles, tumbling down the hill toward the river in great broken waves. Gray slate. Red tile, faded pink by years of sun. Black tar paper, patched and bubbled. Chimney pots stood up everywhere, hundreds of them, thousands, in crooked rows and clumps, tall ones and short ones, round ones and square ones, some with little tin hats on, all of them smoking. The smoke went up in a hundred thin gray threads and leaned over in the wind off the river, all the same way, like grass bending.
And there was a whole life up here. He had never known. He could see it now. Washing lines strung from chimney to chimney, heavy with sheets and shirts and long gray stockings. Little wooden boxes nailed to the slates with pigeons in them, cooing. A flat bit of roof, between two peaks, where someone had put pots of earth and was growing beans up strings, and a little old woman in a black shawl was sitting on an upturned bucket among the beans, shelling peas into her lap. She looked up at them and nodded, as if people walked past on the ridge every day. Further off, two boys were flying a kite made of newspaper. A cat walked along a gutter with something in its mouth. A man in his shirtsleeves was lying flat on his back on a warm patch of tar paper with his hat over his face, asleep.
"Half the city lives on the roofs," said Corin, very quietly.
Juno looked at him sideways. "Who told you that?"
"A friend."
"Your friend's right." She pointed with her whole arm. "That's Fisher's, with the bent chimney. That's where I was. That's the chandler's after, the long one with the skylights, and you don't step on the skylights, ever, not even the ones that look strong, specially not the ones that look strong. Then Odding's, then the rope-walk. See it? The really long roof, all on its own, going right down to the river? That's the rope-walk. That's where they went. Along the top of it. And then the Cut's at the far end, past the tanneries. You can't see it from here. You can see the Dye-Works chimney, though. The big square one. That's where they stood."
He followed her arm. Far off, past the end of the long rope-walk roof, past a jumble of low black sheds that steamed and stank even from here, a great square chimney of red brick stood up above everything else, much taller, with a black iron band round the top and a thin brown smoke coming out of it. It stood against the bright sky like a finger pointing up.
"That's a long way," he said.
"That's why we're going now and not after you've had a nice sit down." Juno set off along the ridge.
She did not walk. She ran. Not fast, not yet, but easily, lightly, her arms out a little from her sides, her boots landing on the very crest of the ridge one after the other as if she were walking along a line painted on a road. At the end of his roof she did not stop. She dropped down onto the slope of the next, which was lower, and skidded on her heels down the slates to the gutter, and turned along it, and went up the other side, and he followed, slower, on his hands and feet, and his body kept on knowing things he did not.
They crossed Fisher's roof. There was a bent chimney there, as she had said, leaning over like a drunk man, and in its shadow, on a flat bit of lead, a heel of bread and an apple core, already black with ants.
"Breakfast," said Juno, without stopping.
They went along the chandler's roof, the long one with the skylights. Juno walked along the narrow strip of slate between the skylights without looking at her feet, and Corin walked along it looking at nothing else, and through the dirty glass on either side of him he saw straight down into a long dim room full of barrels and coils of rope and a man in an apron counting candles into a box.
At the end of the chandler's roof, Juno stopped and crouched by a chimney pot and sniffed, like a dog.
"Here," she said. "Smell."
He crouched beside her. He smelled smoke. Soot. Hot slate. Pigeons. And then, under all of it, very faint, so faint he could almost have imagined it: something sweet. Heavy and sweet and wrong, like a room where someone has been laid out on a table for three days with the windows shut.
He put his hand on the chimney pot. The smell was strongest there. On the rough clay, at about the height of a hand, as if someone had steadied themselves against it and left a little of themselves behind.
"Lilies," he said.
"Told you."
On a nail sticking out of the brickwork, a little lower down, there was a thread. A short dark thread of wool, the color of a wet roof. He did not touch it. He wrote it in the Ledger, on his last pages, crouching there behind the chimney pot with the whole city spread out below: Chandler's roof. Chimney at the end. Lilies on the pot. Dark wool thread on a nail.
He was putting the Ledger away when Juno went very still beside him.
Not the stillness of listening. A different stillness. The stillness of a bird on a branch when a shadow goes over.
"Mister V," she said, very quietly. "Don't stand up. Look down. Slow."
He looked down.
They were above a narrow street that ran down the side of the chandler's toward the river. Rope Street, the painted sign said on the corner wall. It was busy. Carts. People. A man selling eels from a bucket. And on the corner, under the sign, two men standing very still in the middle of all the moving.
They were big men. Both of them. One was tall and wide, with a face like a shovel and a dirty bandage wrapped round his head, over one ear, with a brown stain seeping through it. The other was shorter and broader, with no neck at all and a flat cap and arms like barrels. They both wore long brown coats. And they both wore gloves, though it was not cold.
Gray gloves.
The one with the bandage was holding a piece of paper. A broadsheet. He was showing it to the eel man, and the eel man was shaking his head, and the man with no neck was looking at the street, up and down, up and down, the way a dog looks at a rabbit hole.
Corin did not know them. He did not need to. The bandage told him. The gray gloves told him. And there was a page in his Ledger from last night, in his own hand, shaky and fast, that said Tull. Six men. Gray gloves. Black Bank. and, underneath, One cut on the head when the chain came down.
"Black Bank," whispered Juno. She knew too. Everybody in the Lower Docks knew the gray gloves. "What do they want?"
"Me, I think."
"You? What did you do?"
"I knocked their master down."
Juno turned her head and stared at him. For a long moment her face was perfectly blank. Then, very slowly, it split into the biggest grin he had seen on it yet.
"Tull?" she breathed. "You knocked down Tull?"
"I think so. Not on my own."
"Everybody's been saying. All morning. On the roofs. Somebody laid out Tull in the Inkwell like a sack of wet sand and nobody knows who." She looked at him with something like awe. "It was you. You. You can't even walk on a roof."
"I'm walking on one now."
"Crawling," said Juno. But she was still grinning.
Below them, the man with the bandage folded his broadsheet and turned away from the eel man. And as he turned, he lifted his head. Not for any reason. The way people do, when they are thinking, and their eyes wander up.
He looked straight at the chimney pot at the end of the chandler's roof.
Corin did not move. Juno did not move. They were crouched in the shadow of the chimney, low, still, gray against the gray slates. But Corin's hat had fallen back a little when he looked down, and there was the white of his face, and the white of his hand on the clay.
The man with the bandage stopped turning.
His eyes narrowed.
He put out one gray-gloved hand and hit the man with no neck on the arm, hard, without looking at him, and pointed straight up.
"There!" His voice came up over the noise of the street, hoarse and thick. "On the roof! That's him! That's the Ministry rat!"
"Oh, rot," said Juno.
She grabbed Corin's sleeve and pulled, and they went.
They went along the gutter, fast, bent double, and up over the next ridge, Odding's roof, and down the other side. Behind them and below, Corin heard shouting. Boots on cobbles. A woman screaming at somebody to mind her eels. And then, worse, a new sound. The scrape and clang of iron. Somebody climbing. There were iron ladders on the backs of some of the houses, for the sweeps and the slaters, and the Black Bank men had found one.
"They're coming up," said Corin.
"Let them," said Juno. "Up here's mine."
She went faster.
Odding's roof had the green bit by the skylight, slick with moss, the bit everybody slipped on. Juno went round it in a wide curve without slowing. Corin went round it in a wider one, and nearly slipped anyway, and his hand caught the edge of the skylight frame and held, and the glass rattled under his palm, and through it he saw a startled old man in a nightcap look up from his bed.
"Sorry," said Corin to the old man.
"Don't say sorry, run!" said Juno.
He ran.
Behind them, a head came up over the edge of the chandler's roof. A big head, with a dirty bandage round it. Then shoulders. Then the whole huge man, hauling himself up off the top of the iron ladder onto the slates, red in the face, breathing hard. He looked along the roofs. He saw them. He grinned.
And he came after them, not crawling, not careful, but running, heavy and fast, his boots smashing down on the slates so hard that bits of them cracked off and slid away and fell into the street. Behind him came the man with no neck, slower, swearing.
"He's big," panted Corin. "Roofs won't hold him."
"Some won't," said Juno. "Some will. Down here!"
She dropped off the end of Odding's roof without warning. Corin looked over the edge and saw that she had landed on a flat tar-paper roof, one story lower, with a pigeon loft on it and a line of washing. He did not think. He sat down on the edge and pushed off, and fell, and landed hard, and rolled, the way his body seemed to know how, and came up on his feet among the flapping sheets.
The rope-walk roof was ahead of them. Long and low and straight, longer than any roof he had ever seen, running all the way down the hill to the river like a great gray road. It was steep on both sides, but its ridge was broad and flat, made of old lead sheet, as wide as a garden path.
Juno was already on it, running.
He ran after her. Along the top of the rope-walk, high over everything, with the wind off the river in his face and the whole city tilting and turning on either side of him. Below, through the gaps in the slates, he could hear the slow creak and hum of the machines inside, twisting the long ropes. Behind him, he could hear the big man's boots hit the lead of the ridge, thump, thump, thump, gaining.
The tanneries came up on the left, low and black and stinking. The Dye-Works chimney stood up ahead, enormous now, square and red and close.
And the rope-walk ended.
It did not slope down. It did not drop to a lower roof. It simply stopped, at the edge of a great dark gap, like a road that ran off the edge of a cliff. And across the gap, a long way across, too far, there was another roof, a warehouse roof, tall and black, leaning in over the gap toward them.
And far below, in the bottom of the gap, there was water. Brown, slow, oily water, with barges on it, tied up nose to tail along both banks, and a long way down.
The Cut.
Juno stopped at the very edge of the ridge. Corin nearly ran into her. He grabbed her shoulder to stop himself and they both swayed there for a moment, on the end of the lead, with the gap at their feet.
"Nobody jumps the Cut," said Juno. She was breathing hard. Her face had gone pale under the soot. "I told you. Nobody."
Behind them, the big man came pounding down the ridge of the rope-walk, with the man with no neck far behind him. He was grinning. He had seen the gap. He knew they had nowhere to go. He slowed down, a little, and took something out of his coat as he came. Something short and heavy and black, on a leather loop round his wrist. A cosh.
"There's a way down," said Juno fast. "There's always a way down. There's a drainpipe, on the left side, by the gutter. Goes down to the barges. I'll go first. You come after. Quick. Quick, before he—"
She swung herself over the edge of the ridge and down onto the steep slope of the roof on the left side, and slid, and caught the gutter, and swung round onto a drainpipe as easily as a monkey, and was gone.
Corin went after her.
He swung his legs over the ridge. He let go. He slid down the steep slope on his back, fast, much too fast, the slates rough under his coat, and his heels hit the gutter, and he threw out his hands for the drainpipe where Juno had gone.
And the gutter broke.
It did not crack. It did not bend. It simply came away from the wall, a whole length of it, old rotten lead and rusted iron brackets, with a long tearing groan like a ship's timbers giving way. It tipped out over the Cut, slowly, like a door swinging open.
And Corin went with it, out over the edge, into the empty air.
