Chapter 5: Juno on the Rooftops, Part 1 of 5: Read This First
Debt of the Unremembered. Arc 1: The Siphon's Ledger.
Read This First
He woke because something was ticking.
Not a clock. Smaller than a clock. A soft, quick, uneven sound, tick, tick-tick, tick, like rain starting on a tin roof, or like a lot of very small things knocking gently against each other in a draft.
He opened his eyes.
Needles.
That was the first thing he saw. Hundreds of them, hanging from a low wooden ceiling on threads. Every size. Some as fine as a hair, some as long as a finger. They turned slowly in the air above his head, catching a thin gold light from somewhere, and when the draft moved them they touched, and that was the ticking. Tick. Tick-tick. Like a whole silver forest saying something very quietly to itself.
He lay still and watched them for a long time.
He did not know where he was.
He did not know who he was.
It did not frighten him. That was the strange part. He noticed that it did not frighten him, the way you might notice that you are not hungry. There was a kind of calm in it. A blankness, clean and wide, like a field of snow before anyone has walked on it. Somewhere at the bottom of the calm, very small, there was a feeling that this had happened before. Many times. That there was a way through it, and that his hands knew the way, even if he did not.
His hands moved.
They went to his chest. Not to his throat, or his face, or his pockets. To his chest, to the inside of a heavy wool coat, and they found a book there, in a pocket that fit it as if the pocket had been sewn for it. They took the book out. They held it up in front of his face.
It was a thick book, as thick as two fingers laid side by side, bound in dark brown leather that was almost black, cracked and worn pale along the spine and at the corners. There was a stub of pencil living in the spine. Low down on the cover, pressed into the leather, were small faded letters: GROUNDING LEDGER. B. HOLT, STATIONER. LOWER DOCKS.
His hands turned the book over, and opened it at the very beginning, at the inside of the front cover.
Big black letters, untidy, sloping hard to the right, pressed so deep into the cream-colored paper that some of the strokes had cut through.
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin.
He read it twice. Then he said it, out loud, very quietly, to the needles.
"Corin."
It fit. It fit the way a foot fits into an old boot. Something in him turned over and settled. Yes. That one.
You were born in Oakhaven.
The word meant nothing. And yet, this time, it did not feel like nothing. It felt like a word he had heard spoken aloud very recently. In a dark room. In a frightened voice. He could not think whose.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
A small ache under his ribs. As if someone had pressed a thumb there, gently, and then taken it away.
You used to fear the dark.
He looked up at the needles, and at the gold light on them, and at the shadows between them, and he did not feel afraid. Used to, he thought.
Turn the page. Then get up. Then eat something. In that order.
He turned the page.
THINGS THAT ARE TRUE TODAY.
He read the list slowly. He lived at No. 14 Tallow Street, in the Lower Docks, at the top of the house, in Room 9. He was a Senior Investigator at the Ministry of Records, Third Basement, Harvesting Section. He went to work at the seventh bell. He was to leave by the sixth bell and a half. His landlady was Mrs. Marta. His clerk was Toby. He was not to lend Toby money. He harvested Ash, which meant he breathed in what was left of dead mages so that he could use their memories instead of his own. It was illegal for everyone except him. He did not burn his own memories. Ever. If he had to burn something, he burned Ash. If there was no Ash, he ran. The rod in the long case was his. Master Hale had given it to him. He was not to lose it.
And at the bottom, underlined twice:
You wake up like this every morning. It is not an emergency. It is just Tuesday.
He almost laughed. He did not know why. Then he looked around him again, at the needles and the low ceiling and the gold light, and the laugh went away.
You live at No. 14 Tallow Street. Top floor. Room 9.
This was not Room 9.
He was not in a bed. He was sitting in a chair. A big old leather chair with a high back and wide arms, the kind of chair that a rich man might have had in his study fifty years ago, before it was sold and sold again and ended up somewhere poor. The leather was cracked and dark with age. On the left arm, the cracks were worse in one place, as if a hand had gripped it there, again and again, very hard. His own left hand was lying on that place now. His fingers fit the worn marks exactly.
He took his hand away and looked at it.
His sleeve had been rolled up. His forearm was covered in writing.
Not ink on the skin. Ink in the skin. Small neat lines of it, row after row, running from the wrist up toward the elbow like the rungs of a ladder, in a dozen different colors, some fresh and dark and some so faded they were almost gone. He tilted his arm toward the light and read the nearest one.
You are not Barnaby Fell.
The next.
You are not Annis Rook.
And the next, and the next. A long list of names, each one with You are not in front of it. He did not know any of them. He did not know why someone would write on his arm all the people he was not. And then, at the very bottom of the ladder, just above the wrist, a line that was newer than all the rest. Red and raised and a little sore, the skin around it puffy, the ink still shining.
You are not Jory Kestle. Kettle is gone. He fought his last fight, and he won it. Let him rest.
He touched it. It stung.
He did not know who Jory Kestle was. But when he read the name, his right hand closed into a fist all by itself, very hard, the knuckles going white. He had to look at it and tell it to open. It took a moment before it listened.
Ash, he thought. The word came from the list in the book. I breathe in what is left of dead men. He looked at the red line, and then at his own fist slowly uncurling, and he understood, a little. Not with his memory. With his skin.
He turned back to the Ledger. There was more after the rules. Many pages more. His hands went to the place where the writing stopped, and then back a little, to the last full pages, and he read.
The last pages were a mess. Arrows going every way. Words crossed out and written again. Names in boxes. Pell Dorran. Lilies. A woman. White. Was at Oakhaven. Lord Aurelian Thorne. FOLD. Clock sold. Pawnbroker? Find it. A small square of words set apart in the corner: U.W. Three. Sewn shut. And below it all, written larger than the rest, in capitals, with a line drawn hard underneath:
PARTNER.
And beside it, a name. Lyra Thorne.
And under that, in a different, quieter hand, as if he had been very tired when he wrote it: Lyra does not know Nurse's name. Find out who took it. Corin does not know his mother's name either.
And on the very last line of all, squeezed in at the bottom of the page in pencil, so faint and so slanting that it looked as if it had been written by someone already half asleep:
Vespera's shop. Hook Lane. You are safe here. Noon: Linden Walk, the gate with the stone pineapple. DON'T BE LATE.
He read that three times.
Then he lowered the book and looked properly at the room around him for the first time.
It was small and low and warm. The gold light came from a window, a bow window of thick greenish glass, and in the window there were bottles, dozens of tiny bottles on narrow shelves, each one with a different color of ink inside, so that the sunlight came through them red and blue and violet and green and lay in bright puddles on the floor like the light from a chapel window. There was a stove in the corner, ticking as it cooled. There was a long worktable scarred with knife marks and stained with a hundred colors. There were papers pinned all over the yellowed walls, covered in drawings of letters, the same names drawn again and again. And there was a narrow cot against the far wall.
On the edge of the cot, facing him, sat an old woman.
She was broad and square, with a brown face creased like old leather and gray hair standing out around her head like smoke. She was wearing a wool dress the color of wet earth. Her arms, where her sleeves had slipped back, were covered in writing too, much more than his, line after line, wrist to elbow, as far as he could see. She was sitting very straight, very still. In her lap, held loosely in both hands, there was a small knife with a bone handle. It was closed.
She was watching him. Not the way you watch a stranger. The way you watch a pot you have been waiting to boil.
"Morning, boy," she said.
Her voice was low and dry and tired, as if she had not slept. He looked at the knife in her hands. She saw him look. She did not put it away.
"It's not for you," she said. "Read your book. It'll tell you who I am."
He looked down at the last line. Vespera's shop. Hook Lane. You are safe here.
"Vespera," he said.
The old woman let out a breath, and something in her square shoulders came down a little.
"That's me," she said. "And you're Corin, and it's past the eighth bell, and you've slept through your work for the first time in all the years I've known you." Her mouth moved in something that was almost a smile and not quite. "Your Ministry will be in a rare state. Never mind. Some mornings matter more than desks."
You go to work at the seventh bell. Corin looked at the window, at the gold sunlight, high now, pouring in through the bottles. Not the first gray light of dawn. The full light of a morning well along. Somewhere outside, a bell had just finished ringing. He had not counted it.
"Eight," he said.
"And a bit."
"Toby," he said, reading it from the page. Your clerk is Toby. He is young and nervous. He is on your side. "He'll be worried."
"He'll live." Vespera got up off the cot, slowly, the way old people get up, one hand on her knee. Her joints cracked. She went to the stove and opened its little door and poked at what was inside, and a few red sparks woke up and glowed. "Eat first. Your book says so. Then we'll talk. There are things you need to know before you go out into that lane, and I'd rather you heard them with something in your belly."
She put the kettle on. She cut two thick slices from a heavy brown loaf on the worktable, and spread them with dripping from a jar, and sprinkled salt on them from her fingers, and put them on a cracked plate and gave it to him. Then she stood by the stove with her arms folded and watched while he ate, the way she had watched him wake.
The bread was dense and sour and the dripping was salty and good. He ate it all. He did not know if he had eaten anything yesterday. His body seemed to think not.
When he was done, she took the plate. She held it for a moment, looking down at him in the big chair.
"Do you know," she said, "how many times I've watched you wake up in that chair, Corin Vane?"
He shook his head.
"Neither do I. I stopped counting." She turned away to the stove. "But it's always the same. You open your eyes, and you look at my needles, and for a moment you look about six years old. And then your hands go for that book, before your head even knows there is a book. And then you read. And then you're you again." She poured the boiling water into a brown pot. "It's a terrible thing to watch, the first time. After a while it's almost beautiful."
Corin did not know what to say to that. So he did what his hands seemed to want to do. He took the stub of pencil from the spine of the Ledger, and turned to a clean page, and at the top of it he wrote the day. Then he looked up.
"What day is it?"
"Wednesday," said Vespera.
He wrote Wednesday. And under it, the first words of the new day, the way he must have done every morning of his life:
Woke in Vespera's chair. Hook Lane. Past the eighth bell. Late for work. She is holding a knife. She says it is not for me.
He looked at the last line, and then at her.
"Who is it for?" he said.
What the Old Woman Saw
Vespera did not answer at once.
She poured the tea. It was black and strong and smelled of smoke, and she put a great deal of sugar in his cup without asking, three spoons, the way you would for someone who had had a shock. She did not put any in her own. She brought the cups to the worktable and set his down by the arm of the big chair. Then she sat on the stool across from him, the one with the wobbly leg, and put the bone-handled knife down on the table between them, closed, where they could both see it.
"A little after the seventh bell," she said, "I was standing at that window with my tea. You were asleep. The lane was waking up. Bread and carts and the cobbler murdering his hymn. Ordinary. And across the way, between the bakery and the rag shop, there's an old cellar door with no door in it. Three steps down into the dark. Nobody uses it. Rats, mostly." She drank. "There was someone standing in it."
"Who?"
"I couldn't see a face. They stood too far back, where the light doesn't go." Her eyes were steady on his. "But I could see the coat. Long. To the ankle. And white, boy. White as a page before you write on it. Clean as if it had been washed that morning and nobody had let a speck of soot near it since."
Corin looked down at the Ledger in his lap. He turned back through the crowded pages. He did not know what he was looking for, but his hands seemed to, and they stopped at a page dated yesterday. Tuesday. A long page about a lane called Gutter Lane and a dead scribe called Pell, and then, near the bottom, a smaller entry:
Inquisitor Halloway. Young. Brown hair. Kross's. Took the jar. Asked what he hummed. When I hummed it, she knew it. I think. Something brown, pressed in the front of her notebook.
And above that, the word Inquisitors, and two words underlined: white coats.
He read it to her. She listened without moving.
"Halloway," she said when he had finished. She said it slowly, as if she were tasting it. "I don't know the name. But I know the coats. Everybody in the Ink Quarter knows the coats." She tapped one blunt finger on the table, next to the knife. "They don't stand in cellar doors watching old women's windows, as a rule. When the Inquisition wants you, they knock. Or they don't knock. They come in. Two of them, or four, with a writ and a wagon. They don't wait in the dark like a cat at a mousehole."
"Then why was this one waiting?"
"That's the question, isn't it." Vespera picked up her cup again. "Either they wanted to see who was in here and didn't want to be seen asking. Or they weren't sent at all. Somebody in a white coat came down Hook Lane at dawn on their own business. Not the Inquisition's." She looked at him over the rim. "I don't know which frightens me more."
Corin thought about that. Then he wrote it down. White coat. Cellar door across the lane. After the seventh bell. Watching. Not knocking. Sent? Or not sent?
"How long did they stay?"
"I looked away. Only for a breath. When I looked back, they were gone." Her mouth went tight. "I've been sitting here ever since, with Haddon's old knife in my lap, feeling like a fool. Seventy-one years old and jumping at a coat."
"You're not a fool."
"No," she agreed. "I'm not. That's why I didn't wake you." She nodded at his chest. "A tired man runs into the street to see what's there. A rested man sits still and thinks first. I wanted you rested."
His hand went to his chest without his asking it to. To the right side of his coat, the inside pocket, which was buttoned shut. He undid the button and reached in, and his fingers closed on something flat and square and warm, wrapped in cloth. He drew it out.
It was a small black square of heavy cloth, about the size of a little book, sewn shut all the way round with tiny black stitches, and sewn again through the middle in a cross, corner to corner. It was heavier than it looked. When he held it, he felt a strange weight in it, as if it were fuller than it should be. Like a cup brimming right to the edge.
He looked at Vespera.
"Don't open it," she said at once. "Your book will tell you. U.W. Three. Sewn shut. That's that. Those are the stones from the dead scribe's drawer. I sewed them up last night, and you promised, and the girl promised, that nobody opens that cloth until you know exactly what you're doing and exactly why. And you come to me first." She pointed at the pocket. "Put it back. Button it. Keep it away from the rod, and away from any ink."
He put it back. He buttoned the pocket. Then, because his hands seemed to want to, he went through all his other pockets one by one, the way the Ledger said, the way his body seemed to know already. Left outside pocket: keys, and a small glass jar with a brass lid, empty. He looked at the empty jar for a moment and did not know why it made him sad. Right side, the long narrow pocket: the rod. He touched it through the wool. It was cool, but not cold. Inside left: the Ledger's place. Outside right: a pen, two coppers, a bent nail.
He looked at the nail.
"You looked at that nail exactly the same way last night," said Vespera. "And then you put it back. Like you couldn't decide if it was yours."
He put it back.
"The girl," he said. He looked at the Ledger again. PARTNER. Lyra Thorne. "Lyra Thorne. She was here."
"She was. All night. She left at the sixth bell, to go home and sleep and tidy her face before anyone saw it. You're to meet her at noon. Linden Walk, over the stable." Vespera's eyebrows went up. "It's in your own writing, boy. Don't be late. In capitals."
"What is she like?"
Vespera thought about it. She took her time. She seemed to be a woman who liked to get words right, the way she would get a line of ink right on a man's arm, because once it was in, it was in.
"Proud," she said at last. "Poorer than she lets anyone see. Braver than is good for her. Quick with a sword and quicker with her tongue. Her father's gone to nothing and her brother's gone somewhere she won't talk about, and she's been holding the whole roof up on her own two hands for two years. And she can't thread a needle anymore." Her face did something strange and soft for a moment. "She asked to see the first page of your book. You showed her."
Corin looked down at the inside cover. You were born in Oakhaven.
"Did I?"
"You did. And you told her you were sorry for not telling her sooner. And she forgave you." Vespera set down her cup. "Hold on to that one, Corin. You don't get many."
He wrote: Lyra Thorne. Proud. Brave. Poorer than she shows. Showed her the first page. She forgave me. He looked at the last three words for a moment. Then he closed the Ledger.
When he did, he felt how thin the pages were at the back. He opened it again and turned to the end. There were only a handful of blank pages left. Four. Perhaps five, if he wrote small.
"You'll need Bram," said Vespera, watching him count. "He'll have a new one waiting for you. He always does. Go today, if you can. A man like you can't afford to run out of pages."
"Bram," he said, and wrote it at the top of the next blank page, with a box around it.
Then he stood up.
The room tipped a little, the way rooms do when you have slept sitting up. He put a hand on the chair arm, in the worn place, and waited, and the room settled. His legs were stiff. His neck ached. His left forearm, under the rolled sleeve, throbbed with a slow sore heat where the new red line was.
Vespera stood too. She picked up the bone-handled knife and held it out to him, handle first.
"Take it."
"I have the rod."
"The rod is for dead men. This is for live ones." She did not lower her hand. "I know what you're going to say. You'll say you don't know how to use it. Your hands know, boy. Your hands know a hundred things you don't. Take it."
He took it. It was small and heavy and old, the bone handle yellowed and smooth from years of a hand closing round it. He put it in his left outside pocket, beside the keys and the empty jar. It sat there like a stone.
"Don't go out the front," said Vespera.
She went to the back of the shop, past the green cupboard, to a low door he had not noticed, half hidden behind a curtain of hanging needles. She pushed the needles aside with her arm, and they chimed and ticked and swung, and she opened the door. Behind it was a dark, narrow passage that smelled of damp brick and machine oil.
"Through here," she said. "It goes behind Faber's, the clock-mender's next door. He's deaf in one ear and blind in one eye and he owes me for three tattoos, so he won't see you and he won't hear you. Out his back way, into Tanner's Steps. That'll take you down to the river, or up to the Docks road, whichever you like." She looked at him. "Where will you go?"
He looked at the Ledger. You live at No. 14 Tallow Street. You go to work at the seventh bell. Noon: Linden Walk.
"Home first," he said slowly. "I need a clean collar. And whoever was watching your window watched me come in with Lyra. If they know where you live, they'll know where I live. I want to see if anyone's been there."
Vespera nodded, as if that was what she had hoped he would say. "And then?"
"Then I don't know."
"That's honest, at least." She put one broad hand on his arm, above the new red line, very gently. "Go careful, Corin Vane. Look up as well as round. People forget to look up."
"Why up?"
"Because in the Lower Docks," said Vespera, "half the city lives on the roofs."
She let him go.
The passage behind the needles was so dark that he had to feel his way along the wall with one hand. Brick, cold and damp. Then wood. Then the passage turned, and there was a strip of gray light under a door, and a smell of oil and brass, and a sound he knew without knowing how. Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock. Dozens of clocks, a hundred, all out of step with each other, like rain on a hundred different roofs.
He came out into the back of a narrow shop crammed with clocks. Clocks on the walls, clocks on the shelves, clocks in pieces on a long bench under a lamp. An old man sat at the bench with a jeweler's glass screwed into one eye and a pair of tweezers in his fingers, bent over the open belly of a small gold watch. He did not look up. He did not seem to notice that anyone had come in at all. But as Corin went past him, he lifted one finger from the tweezers, just for a moment, and pointed it at a back door half hidden behind a tall dark case clock with a painted moon on its face.
"Thank you," said Corin.
The old man did not answer. He bent closer over the watch. On the bench beside his elbow, in a tin tray, lay a jumble of tiny brass wheels and springs, and among them, small and dull, a few old buttons. One of them caught Corin's eye as he passed. Brass. Stamped with a little leaf.
He did not stop. He would think about it later, and not know why it had stuck. He went out through the back door and pulled it shut behind him.
Tanner's Steps was a stone stair, steep and narrow and green with moss, that ran down between the backs of two rows of houses. Water dripped from broken gutters overhead. Washing hung on lines strung from window to window above him, shirts and sheets and long gray stockings, flapping slowly in the wind off the river. The steps were slippery. At the bottom, far below, he could see a slice of the river, brown and wide and shining in the morning sun, with a barge going past under a patched red sail.
At the top, the steps climbed back up to meet the lane.
He stood still for a moment and thought. Then he went up instead of down.
The steps came out at the far end of Hook Lane, around the bend, where it met a wider street. He stood in the shadow of a doorway and looked back along the lane, toward Vespera's green-glass window and the bakery and the rag shop and, between them, the old cellar door with no door in it.
It was empty. Of course it was. A broken barrel lay on its side at the top of the three worn steps. A ginger cat was sitting on the barrel washing its face.
He walked back along the lane, slowly, with the crowd. Nobody looked at him. A man with a tray of pies on his head went by, shouting. The cobbler was singing. He came level with the cellar door and stopped, as if he were looking at the rag shop window, and then he stepped sideways into the gap and went down the first of the three steps.
It was colder in the doorway. Darker. It smelled of rats and wet stone. The cat on the barrel stopped washing and looked at him with flat yellow eyes.
The dust on the steps had been disturbed. Even he could see that. On the second step, where someone standing in the dark could see across the lane without being seen, there were marks in the gray dust. The prints of two boots. Small and narrow. A woman's, or a slight man's. The heels were sharp and square and deep, the way new heels are, good leather, not cobbled and patched like everyone's on Hook Lane. The prints stood side by side, very neat, toes pointing straight at Vespera's window.
They had stood here a long time. You could tell. The dust around them was smoothed, as if the coat had brushed it, back and forth, back and forth, while its owner waited.
And caught on a splinter at the edge of the broken doorframe, at about the height of a shoulder, there was a single thread.
White.
Corin did not touch it. He looked at it for a long time. Then he took out the Ledger and wrote, as small as he could, on one of his last few pages: Cellar door, Hook Lane. Two bootprints. Small. New heels. Stood a long time. White thread, shoulder height. Left on the splinter.
He left it on the splinter.
Then he came up the three steps into the sunlight, past the ginger cat, and walked away along Hook Lane toward the Docks road, toward Tallow Street and home. He did not hurry. He did not look back.
But he remembered what Vespera had said. And at the corner, before he turned, he did look up.
The roofs of Hook Lane leaned together against the bright sky, gray slate and red tile and black tar paper, chimney pots and washing lines and the broken weathervane on the bakery. Pigeons sat in a row along a gutter. Smoke went up from a dozen chimneys.
And for just a moment, between two chimney pots, something moved. Something small and quick and dark, the size of a crouching child. A flick of a patched coat. A cap.
Then it was gone, and there was only the smoke, and the pigeons, and the sky.
