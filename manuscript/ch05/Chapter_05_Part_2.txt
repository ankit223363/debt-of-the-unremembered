Chapter 5: Juno on the Rooftops, Part 2 of 5: The Purple Door
The Purple Door
The Docks road ran along the top of the river wall, wide and loud and stinking, and Corin walked it with his collar up and his hat down and the long wooden case under his arm, like any other clerk going late to a desk.
It was a different city in daylight. He did not know that he had never seen it like this, at this hour, on a working day. But his body seemed to know. It kept slowing down to look at things, as if they surprised it.
The river was full of boats. Barges with red and brown sails, low in the water with coal and timber and bales of wool. Little rowing boats darting between them like water beetles. A big three-masted ship from somewhere far away, tied up at the Long Quay, with men swarming up and down its sides on rope ladders, and a smell coming off it of tar and spice and something sweet and rotten, like fruit left too long in the sun. Cranes swung over the quays on great creaking iron arms, lifting nets full of barrels into the air and setting them down again. Men shouted. Gulls screamed. A boy ran past with a basket of fish on his head, still flapping. A woman sat on an upturned crate selling hot pies out of a box lined with straw, and the smell of them made his stomach turn over, though he had eaten Vespera's bread not an hour ago.
And everywhere, on every corner, on the tall black posts with their necks curved like herons, the gemstone lamps. They were dark now, in the sunlight. Or not dark, quite. If you looked closely, there was still a faint blue glow deep inside each stone, the way there is still a little heat in a hearth long after the fire has gone out. He did not look closely. He found that he did not want to.
He turned off the Docks road at the Salt Stairs and went up into the Lower Docks proper.
The streets got narrower at once. Darker. The houses leaned in over him, as they had on Hook Lane, their top floors almost touching, so that the sky was a thin blue ribbon far above and the cobbles below were wet and green and slippery even on a dry morning. Washing hung from every window. Children played in the gutters, sailing chips of wood down the trickle of dirty water. An old man sat on a step mending a net, his fingers flying. A woman leaned out of an upstairs window and emptied a bucket into the street, and Corin stepped smartly sideways without thinking, and the water hit the cobbles where he had been a heartbeat before.
Your body knows a hundred things you don't, Vespera had said.
He was beginning to believe her.
Tallow Street came at him round a corner, steep and crooked, running down the hill toward the river. He knew it by the name painted on the corner wall in white letters gone gray. He did not know it any other way. But halfway down, on the left, there was a tall, thin, sooty house of five floors with a crooked number 14 nailed beside the door, and his feet slowed in front of it all by themselves, and he knew.
He stood on the bottom step and looked up at it. Five rows of small windows, one on top of another, all the way up to the roof. The top window, under the gutter, was small and square, with four panes, and one of them was patched with brown paper.
Room 9.
He went up the steps and pushed open the street door.
The smell hit him first. Onions. Cabbage. Coal smoke. Damp. Cats. He knew it the way he had known the chair at Vespera's, not with his head but somewhere deeper, in his chest and his stomach and the backs of his knees. He stood in the dark passage at the foot of the stairs and breathed it in, and something in him said, quietly, home.
Then a door opened at the end of the passage, and a voice like a rusty gate said, "There you are."
The door was purple. The woman standing in it was very small and very old and very angry.
She came up to the middle of his chest. She had a face like a dried apple, wrinkled all over, and two small black eyes like currants pushed into dough, and white hair pulled back so tight in a bun that it lifted her eyebrows. She wore a black dress and a purple shawl and a pair of round wire spectacles pushed up on her forehead, and she was holding a wooden spoon. She pointed it at him the way a soldier points a sword.
His hand was already reaching into his coat for the Ledger. He made it stop. He looked at the purple door. At the woman. He remembered, from the page he had read at Vespera's, a line in his own hand at the bottom of yesterday: Mrs. Marta. Purple door. She says be careful. She says she likes you.
"Good morning, Mrs. Marta," he said.
She stopped. Her spoon wavered.
"Did you remember that," she said suspiciously, "or read it?"
"I read it," said Corin. "Earlier. I wrote it down yesterday."
She snorted. A small, dry, sharp sound, like a cat sneezing.
"Honest. That's twice. I'll start to think you're sickening for something." She looked him up and down, slowly, from his dusty boots to his crumpled collar to his face, and her small black eyes missed nothing. "You didn't come home."
"No."
"Two bowls of stew. Two. Last night's, outside your door, stone-cold this morning with a skin on it you could skate on. And this morning's, gone the same way. I took them both back." She shook the spoon at him. "Do you know what onions cost? Do you know what mutton costs, even the bad end of it?"
"I'm sorry."
"You're sorry." She sniffed. "Where were you?"
He thought about what Vespera had said. Don't tell anyone where you've been. He thought about the white coat in the cellar door. He thought about the honest feeling of telling Mrs. Marta the truth about her name.
"With a friend," he said. "On the far side of the Docks. I fell asleep in a chair."
"A friend." Her eyebrows, already high, went higher. "You have friends now. Well. Well, well." Something in her face changed. The anger did not go away, exactly. It just got smaller, and something else came up under it, like a plant pushing up through a crack in a step. "A girl-friend, or a friend-friend?"
"A friend-friend."
"Hm," said Mrs. Marta, in a tone that meant she did not believe a word of it. Then she grabbed his left wrist with a hand like a bird's claw, small and dry and surprisingly strong, and turned it over, and pushed his sleeve up before he could stop her.
The red line was there. You are not Jory Kestle. Puffy and angry and shining.
Mrs. Marta looked at it for a long moment. She did not say anything. Her thumb moved, very lightly, along the edge of the swollen skin, not quite touching.
"Ah," she said at last, very quietly. "That sort of friend."
She pushed his sleeve back down. She patted it, twice, as if she were patting a dog.
"Goose grease," she said. "Tonight. I'll leave a pot out with your stew. Rub it in and don't scratch it, and don't you dare leave the stew for the rats a third time, or I'll put your things in the street and let a nice young couple have that room." She stepped back into her doorway. Then she stopped, the way she always seemed to stop, with one hand on the purple door.
"There was a woman," she said.
Corin went still.
"This morning. Just after the seventh bell. She knocked on my door. Very polite. Gloves on. She asked was this where Mister Vane lodged, and had he come home last night, and did I expect him." Mrs. Marta's mouth went thin. "She was dressed all in white. Long white coat, to her boots. Not a mark on it. Down here. In the Lower Docks." She shook her head slowly. "I've lived on Tallow Street fifty-two years, boy. I've never seen a white coat stay that clean past the end of the street."
"What did you tell her?"
"I told her you'd gone to Marrowmere to marry a fishwife." Mrs. Marta sniffed. "She didn't believe me. She wrote something in a little black book anyway. Then she thanked me and went away up the street. Didn't look back once." She paused. "She had a pretty voice. Cool, like well water. Young, too. Younger than you."
Corin took out the Ledger. His hands did it without asking. He wrote: White coat at No. 14. Seventh bell. Asked for me. Polite. Gloves. Black notebook. Young. Mrs. Marta told her Marrowmere. He looked at the words for a moment. Seventh bell. Vespera had seen the white coat on Hook Lane just after the seventh bell. Hook Lane was the far side of the Docks. Half an hour's walk. More.
Two of them, then. Or one of them, very fast, who had come here first, and then gone there. Or gone there first, and come here.
He wrote that down too, with a question mark.
Mrs. Marta watched him write. Her face had gone very still.
"You're in trouble," she said. It was not a question.
"I don't know yet."
"That means yes." She folded her thin arms over her purple shawl. "Well. You'll tell me when you know. Or you won't. You never tell me anything." She paused. "My Juno's been up and down the drainpipe all night like a squirrel with a fit. Says she's keeping an eye. On what, she won't say. If I find out she's been keeping an eye on you, Corin Vane, I'll—"
She stopped. She did not finish. Her mouth worked for a moment, as if the end of the sentence were something too heavy to lift.
"Juno," said Corin. His hand went to the Ledger again. He did not open it. He did not need to. He had read it at Vespera's, at the bottom of a page from two nights ago, in his own small writing: Juno. Mrs. Marta's granddaughter. On the roofs. Two coppers a message, one in the rain. Brass key on a string.
"She's fourteen," said Mrs. Marta. "Fourteen. And she thinks she can't fall." Her voice cracked, just a little, on the last word. Then she cleared her throat hard and pointed the spoon at him again. "Go and change that collar. You look like you slept in a ditch. And say good morning to Terrence on your way up. He likes it. Whatever they say, he likes it."
And she shut the purple door.
Corin stood in the onion-smelling passage for a moment. Then he started up the stairs.
They were narrow and steep and dark, and every step was a different height, and he found that his feet knew every one of them. The tall one at the bottom. The short one, third from the top of the first flight, that would catch your toe if you did not lift it. The one on the second turn that was cracked down the middle and moaned when you stood on it. His feet stepped over the cracked one without being told. He let them.
On the second-floor landing, the old man was in his chair.
He was sitting where the morning light came through the only window on the whole staircase, a small round dirty window like a porthole, and the light lay across his knees in a pale round patch. He was thin and very clean, in a brown suit much too big for him and a white shirt buttoned up to the collar. His white hair was combed flat with water. His hands lay folded in his lap. He was looking out of the round window at the sky with a small, calm, gentle smile.
THE MAN ON THE SECOND-FLOOR LANDING IS CALLED OLD TERRENCE. HE IS A SLATE. HE IS NOT DANGEROUS. SAY GOOD MORNING.
Corin had not read that note today. It was pinned to a board in his room, two floors above. But the words came to him anyway, the way the steps had. He stopped on the landing.
"Good morning, Terrence," he said.
The old man turned his head, very slowly, and looked at him. His eyes were pale blue and clear and perfectly empty, like two glasses of clean water.
"Good morning," said Old Terrence politely.
Then he turned back to the window.
Corin did not move on. He stood there, the way Mrs. Marta said he always did. Staring, she had said. Like Terrence was a ghost. And he understood why, now, a little. There was something about the old man that pulled at him. The stillness. The calm. The clean, combed, cared-for emptiness. Someone looked after him very well. Someone combed his hair with water every morning and buttoned his collar and sat him in the light where he could see the sky.
Whatever they say, he likes it.
Corin looked at the old man's hands.
They were folded in his lap, the right on top of the left. Long, thin, spotted hands, very clean, with the nails cut short. But the right hand was not quite flat. The fingers were curled, very slightly, the way fingers curl when they are holding something small. And the thumb was moving. Slowly. Over and over. Back and forth across the side of the first finger, in the same small place, the way a thumb moves over a coin in a pocket, or a stone, or a key.
There was nothing in the hand. It was empty. But the thumb kept moving, back and forth, back and forth, as if it had not been told.
And round the old man's thin neck, just above the white collar, where the skin was soft and loose and pale, there was a line. Very faint. A narrow band of skin a shade lighter than the rest, the way skin goes where something has rested against it for many, many years and then been taken away. As if he had worn a string there once. For a very long time.
Corin took out the Ledger. He did not know why. He wrote, very small, on one of his last pages: Old Terrence. His thumb moves like he's holding something. A pale line round his neck. A string, once?
He looked at the old man for a moment longer.
"Good morning, Terrence," he said again, softly.
This time the old man did not turn. But the smile, Corin thought, might have got a little wider. It was hard to tell, in that dim round light.
He went on up the stairs.
Room 9
On the third-floor landing, a little girl in a nightdress was sitting on the top step with a big ginger cat in her lap. The cat had a torn ear. The girl had a jam sandwich, and she was sharing it with the cat, one bite for her and one for him.
"Morning, Mister Vane," she said, with her mouth full. "You're late."
Corin did not know her. But there was a line in the Ledger, from yesterday, that his eyes had passed over at Vespera's: Della, No. 6, third floor. Cat called Admiral. You sewed his ear.
"Good morning, Della," he said. "Good morning, Admiral."
Della's eyes went very wide. The cat's did not. The cat did not care.
"You remembered," said Della.
"I wrote it down," said Corin.
"That's the same thing," said Della firmly, and went back to her sandwich.
He was not sure it was. But he did not argue with her. He stepped round her, sideways, past the bulge of the iron rail, and went on up.
The fourth flight was the darkest. There was a lamp on the landing, a little blue gemstone in a cage of rusty wire, and it was nearly burned out. Its light was so thin that the walls were only a brown blur. He went up slowly, one hand on the greasy wall, feeling for each step with his toe.
Near the top, his hand found something under the paint. Scratches. He stopped, and leaned close, and in the last faint blue light he could just make them out. Names, mostly, cut into the brown wall with a nail or a knife. DELLA. TOMMY B. KESTER WAS HERE. And smaller than all the rest, old and faint, as if it had been there before any of them:
WE REMEMBER.
He knew it was there. He did not know how he knew. His hand had gone to it as if it had touched it a thousand times.
But under it, this morning, there was something new.
It was very small. No bigger than his thumbnail. Freshly cut. The edges of the scratch were still pale, the paint around them flaking and bright, where all the rest had gone dark with years of greasy hands. It was not a word. It was a little drawing. A bird. A simple bird, the kind a child draws, a body like a seed and two lines for the wings. And above it, a single line, going up, as if the bird were hanging from a string.
Corin stood very still on the dark stair.
He took out the Ledger. He turned it over, to the front, to the inside of the cover. Down in the corner, in his own brown ink, there was a little tree. A child's tree. And hanging from its branches on strings, eight or nine small shapes, each one a body like a seed and two lines for the wings.
He looked from the book to the wall. From the wall to the book.
The same bird.
He wrote it down, there on the stairs, in the dark, pressing the Ledger against the wall to steady it. Fourth-floor landing. Under WE REMEMBER. New. A bird on a string. Same as the tree in my book. He did not know who had cut it, or when, or why. He did not know if it was a message, or a warning, or a joke. He only knew that his hands were cold.
He went on up.
The top landing was very small. There was only room for two doors, facing each other across a square of bare boards. One had a brass 8 nailed to it. The other had a 9. And all round the frame of the 9, pinned to the wood with long hatpins with little glass beads on their ends, there were notes. Strips of brown wrapping paper and the backs of old handbills, written on in purple ink in a small spiky hand that leaned hard to the right.
MARTA. LANDLADY. GROUND FLOOR, BACK. IF YOU FORGET ME AGAIN I WILL NOT BE PLEASED.
RENT: 4 SILVER A WEEK. YOU OWE 4 SILVER. DUE FRIDAY.
THIS IS YOUR DOOR. ROOM 9. THE ONE WITH THE PAPERS.
He almost smiled.
Then he counted the hatpins without meaning to, the way his eyes seemed to count everything, and stopped smiling. There were six notes. There were five hatpins. One note, the one about the stew, had been pinned with a bent nail instead. And there was a small fresh hole in the wood beside it, where a hatpin had been and was not.
He looked at the floor. Nothing. He looked at the door. It was shut. He put his hand in his left pocket, past the cold weight of Vespera's knife, and found the keys, and tried the door before he used them.
It was locked.
He unlocked it, and pushed it open slowly, and stood on the threshold, and looked.
The room was very small. He could see all of it from the door. A narrow iron bed with a thin mattress and one gray blanket. A shelf on the wall with a row of glass jars on it, all empty, all clean, their brass lids shining. A wooden crate beside the bed with a little brass oil lamp on it, turned down to nothing, out. A small table under the window with one short leg, propped level with a folded wad of paper. A tin cup. A water jug with a chipped spout. A chair. And on the wall above the bed, at the height where a man sitting up in the morning would see it first, a board covered in small square notes, pinned with tacks, in a big untidy black hand.
BOOTS FIRST, THEN COAT.
KEYS ARE IN THE LEFT COAT POCKET. ALWAYS LEFT.
THE MAN ON THE SECOND-FLOOR LANDING IS CALLED OLD TERRENCE.
DO NOT BUY MORE INK. YOU HAVE INK.
It all looked right. It all looked like a room that a man like him would live in. Bare and neat and poor, with everything where a man who forgot everything could find it again.
And it was wrong.
He did not know how he knew. He stood in the doorway with the long wooden case under his arm and let his eyes move, slowly, from one thing to the next, the way he had looked at the dust on the cellar step. The bed. The shelf. The crate. The table. The window.
The window.
It was small and square, with four panes. One pane had a crack across it, and the crack had been patched with a strip of brown paper pasted over the glass. He had seen that from the street. But from inside, he could see the latch. An iron hook and eye. The hook was not in the eye. It hung loose, swinging very slightly in the draft.
And the brown paper over the cracked pane had been cut. Neatly. A short straight slit, right beside the latch, just wide enough for two thin fingers to slip through and lift the hook.
Corin put the case down very gently on the bed. He did not take out the rod. He went to the window and looked at the slit in the paper without touching it. Then he looked out.
The window opened onto the roof. Not onto the street. The gutter ran right under the sill, wide and green with moss, and beyond it the slates went up at a steep slant to the ridge, and beyond the ridge there were more roofs, and more, a whole gray tumbling country of them, chimney pots and skylights and washing lines and pigeon lofts, all the way to the river. Anyone who could walk on that country could walk right up to his window. Anyone who knew how.
He turned back to the room.
Now that he knew, he could see it everywhere. Small things. Very small. The kind of small that only a man who wakes up every morning and has to learn his whole room from notes would ever notice.
The blanket on the bed was folded. He did not know if he folded his blanket. But it was folded with the hole on top, and his hands, when he reached for it, went to the other side, as if they were used to the hole being underneath.
The jars on the shelf had all been turned. He did not know how he knew that, either. But he looked at them and something said wrong, and he looked closer, and saw that every one of them had a small paper label pasted on, and every label was facing the wall. As if someone had picked them up one by one, and read them, and put them back without thinking which way they had been facing.
He looked under the bed.
There was a box there. A wooden box, long and flat, the kind that cutlery comes in, tied round with a piece of string. The jars on the shelf are empty. The jars in the box under the bed are NOT. Do not open the box under the bed unless you mean it. He did not need to read the rules to know that. His whole body knew it. The skin on the back of his neck went cold as he pulled the box out into the light.
The string was tied in a bow.
He sat back on his heels and looked at it. A bow. A neat, tidy, ordinary bow, like a ribbon on a parcel.
His hands knew, before he did. They reached for the string and their fingers tried, by themselves, to untie a knot that was not there. A different knot. A hard one, with a twist in it, the kind of knot you tie when you want to know if anyone else has been at your things. They fumbled at the bow, confused, and then stopped.
Someone had untied his knot. And they had not known how to tie it back. So they had tied a bow.
He untied it. He lifted the lid.
Inside, in rows, set in straw, there were jars. Eleven of them. Each one had a brass lid, and each one had a label in his handwriting, and in each one, curled at the bottom like smoke in a bottle, there was a thread of pale blue-white mist, turning slowly. Some brighter. Some fainter. One so bright it made a faint hum he could feel in his teeth.
He counted them twice. Eleven. He did not know if there should be eleven. He turned to the back of the Ledger, to the last pages, then further back, page after page, and found it at last, a list, written weeks ago: BOX UNDER BED. And below it, a column of names and dates. He ran his finger down it. Eleven names. Eleven jars.
Nothing was missing.
Whoever had been here had not wanted the jars. They had read the labels. They had turned the jars on the shelf and opened the box under the bed and looked at what he kept there, and put it all back, and gone.
They had wanted something else.
He put his hand flat on his coat, over his heart, over the Ledger.
She also wants your book, said a line he had read at Vespera's that morning, in his own hand, beside the name of a frightened boy called Dace. To see if he got any of it right.
He tied the box shut again, with the hard twisted knot his hands knew, and pushed it back under the bed. He stood up.
And then he saw the note.
It was on the board above the bed, among all the others. He had looked straight at it from the doorway and not seen it, because it belonged. It was a small square of paper, like the rest. It was written in big untidy black letters that sloped hard to the right, like the rest. It was pinned to the board like the rest.
But not with a tack.
With a hatpin. A long sharp hatpin, with a little glass bead on the end of it. And the bead was white.
He read the note.
STOP LOOKING. SOME THINGS ARE FORGOTTEN FOR A REASON.
He stood there for a long time.
It was his handwriting. It looked exactly like his handwriting. The same big letters. The same hard slope. The same way of pressing so deep that the pen nearly cut through. If he had found it there any other morning, he would have read it and believed it, the way he believed all the others. BOOTS FIRST. KEYS LEFT. STOP LOOKING. He would have thought he had written it to himself one night when he was tired and afraid. He would have obeyed it.
But it was not quite right.
He took out the Ledger and held it open beside the note and looked from one to the other. The S was wrong. His S leaned forward, like a man walking into the wind. This one stood up straight. And the G in THINGS had a little tail at the bottom that his did not. And the letters were too even. Too careful. His handwriting looked like someone writing fast because he was afraid of forgetting what he meant to say. This looked like someone writing slowly, to get his handwriting right.
Very nearly, he thought. And did not know why the words came to him, or why they made him feel sick.
He turned back through the Ledger. Back to yesterday. To a page about a seal, a red wax seal with a rose in thorns, on a forged note that had lured a scribe to his death. And there it was, in his own writing, underlined: The handwriting is Lord Thorne's. Very nearly. Not quite. Somebody who has studied it.
He pulled the hatpin out of the board. He held the forged note in one hand and the pin in the other and looked at the white glass bead, gleaming on the end of it like a drop of milk.
Mrs. Marta's beads were every color. Red and blue and green and yellow. He had seen them on the door frame. Five of them. And one missing.
So one pin had gone from his door, and a strange pin had come into his room. Two different things, perhaps. Or one thing that he did not understand yet. He did not like either answer.
He folded the note very small and put it inside the back cover of the Ledger. He put the hatpin in his outside pocket, beside the nail. Then he sat down on the edge of the narrow bed, in the little gray room that was his and was not quite his anymore, and wrote, on one of his last three blank pages:
Room 9. Someone came in through the window. Over the roofs. Cut the paper. Read the jars. Opened the box. Tied a bow. Took nothing. Left a note in my hand that is not my hand. STOP LOOKING. White-bead hatpin.
And under it, after a long moment:
They know I forget. They wanted me to wake up and believe it.
He was still sitting there, looking at those words, when something tapped on the window glass.
Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.
