Chapter 5: Juno on the Rooftops, Part 5 of 5: Terms
Terms
For a while neither of them said anything.
The sun had moved. It came in now through the gaps between the planks of the loft in long thin bars, full of floating dust and bits of feather, and lay across the straw floor in stripes. A pigeon walked through one of the bars and turned from gray to gold and back to gray again. Outside, the Dye-Works chimney poured its thin brown smoke into the sky, and the white speck at the top of it turned and turned.
Juno wiped her nose on her sleeve, hard, and sat up straight, and was herself again, all at once, the way a cat shakes off water.
"Right," she said. "You owe me."
"I know."
"You said that already. Saying it isn't paying it." She held out her hand, palm up. "Two coppers for telling you what I saw. Paid. Then there's the roofs. Then there's the catching. Catching's extra. Catching's a lot extra."
Corin felt in his outside pocket. A pen. A bent nail. A hatpin with a white bead. Nothing else. He felt in the other pocket. Keys. The empty jar. Vespera's knife.
He looked up.
"I gave you my last two coppers," he said.
Juno's hand stayed out. Her eyebrows went up and up and up, until they disappeared under the brim of her cap.
"You're a Senior Investigator," she said. "At the Ministry. Gran says you get paid every Friday in silver."
"And I owe your gran four silver on Friday." He took out the Ledger, because his hand wanted to, and opened it at the back, and looked. He found it at once, written in the corner of last week's page and underlined: Paid: 9 silver. Rent 4. Bram 2 (ink). Toby 1 (lent. DO NOT LEND TOBY MONEY.) Food. "I seem to buy a lot of ink."
"Everybody knows you buy a lot of ink," said Juno. "Mister Holt's got a whole board of it." She lowered her hand slowly. She looked at him with her head on one side, the way her grandmother looked at a bowl to see if he had really eaten what was in it. "You're poor."
"I think so."
"Poorer than us." She sounded amazed. Then, slowly, something in her face changed. The amazement went, and something sharper came instead. Something like a shopkeeper who has spotted a better deal behind the one on the counter. "Well, then. If you can't pay in money, you'll have to pay in something else."
"Like what?"
She did not answer at once. She looked at the chalk maps on her walls. At the window, and the chimney, and the white speck. And at last at her own closed fist, with the key in it.
"You work at the Ministry," she said slowly.
"Yes."
"In the big black building. With all the papers. Every paper in the whole city."
"Yes."
"Every door," said Juno. "Every porter. Every night. Every name of everybody that went in and out." Her eyes came up to his. They were very steady. "It's all written down somewhere in there. Isn't it. What happened at Night Door Three. The night Grandpa came home."
Corin was quiet for a moment. He did not know. He did not know anything about the Ministry except what was in his Ledger, and the Ledger said Third Basement, Harvesting Section, and Toby is on your side, and not much else.
But he looked down at the brass buttons on his own coat, at the open book and the closed eye, and he thought he knew what kind of place it was. A place that wrote everything down. Even the things it wanted nobody to read.
"If it was written down," he said, "it's in there."
"Then that's my price." Juno lifted her chin. "You find it. Whatever they wrote about Grandpa. The night book from Night Door Three. The real reason, not an accident in the course of duty. You find it, and you read it, and you bring it to me first. Before Gran. Before anybody." Her voice shook, just at the end. "That's what you owe me. Not coppers."
Corin looked at her for a long moment. At her thin dirty face, and her stubborn mouth, and her fist on the key.
He thought about Vespera's rules. He had read them that morning too, in his own hand, at the bottom of last night's mess of arrows and names. One: no lies between you. Two: nobody burns for the other one. Three: the stones stay sewn. Four: if one of you starts to forget the other, the other one has to say so.
They were rules for partners. He did not know if a fourteen-year-old message runner who lived in a pigeon loft could be a partner. But he did not think she would take anything less.
"I can't promise I'll find it," he said. "I can promise I'll look."
"That's not good enough."
"It's all I've got. If I promise more than that, it'll be a lie. And I don't want to start with a lie."
Juno scowled. She chewed the inside of her cheek. She looked at him the way she must look at a gap between two roofs, measuring.
"All right," she said at last, grudgingly. "Look, then. Look hard. And when you find anything, anything, even a scrap, you tell me. Same day."
"Same day."
"And I've got rules too. If I'm going to run for you. Proper. Not just a copper here and there." She held up one grimy finger. "One. I pick the way. Always. You don't tell me how to get somewhere. You tell me where, and I get there."
"All right."
A second finger. "Two. You never, ever, put any of that stuff in me. That Ash. From your stick. Not even if I'm dying. Not even if I beg."
Corin thought about the boy in the wet jersey, and the cold small hole where he had been. "All right."
A third finger. "Three. Nobody touches the key. Not you. Not anybody you send. If anybody ever tries, I'm gone and I'm not coming back."
"All right."
She hesitated. Then she held up a fourth finger, slowly, and when she spoke again her voice was different. Quieter. Less like a shopkeeper and more like a girl.
"Four," she said. "When you forget me. Because you will. Gran says you forget everybody, every morning, it's not your fault, it's the job." She did not look at him. "When you forget me, I'll tell you who I am. Once. And you write it down. Somewhere you'll see it. And you don't make me tell you twice."
Corin did not say all right to that one.
Instead he opened the Ledger. Not at the back, where his last few blank pages were. At the front. At the second page, the one headed THINGS THAT ARE TRUE TODAY, with its pencil lines ruled so straight and its list of everything he needed to be a person in the morning. Where he lived. Where he worked. Mrs. Marta. Toby. The rod. The box under the bed.
There was a little space at the bottom, above the line about Tuesday. Not much. A finger's width.
He took out the stub of pencil, and turned the book so that Juno could see, and wrote, very small and very carefully, in the space:
Juno. Mrs. Marta's granddaughter. Fourteen. Runner. She caught you over the Cut. She tells you once.
Juno leaned over to read it. She read it twice. Her lips moved on the second time.
Then she sat back on her sacks and looked at the wall for quite a long while, and did not say anything, and her ears went red again.
"That's the front," she said finally, in a strange voice. "That's the important page. With the rent."
"Yes."
"You don't put runners on the important page."
"I just did."
Juno sniffed. It was a very wet sniff this time, and nothing like a cat sneezing.
"Fine," she said. "Fine. Then you're hired. Or I'm hired. Whichever." She stood up, fast, and turned her back on him and went to the biscuit tin and started rummaging in it with a great deal of noise. "Have you got messages, or what? You've got the face of somebody with messages. Gran says you always have that face before you send me out in the rain."
He did have messages. He had been thinking about them for some time.
The Ledger had only two blank pages left, and he would not tear them. Juno saw him looking at them and pulled a handful of paper out of her biscuit tin and dropped it on the crate in front of him. Old handbills, printed on one side. GRAND SALE OF FINE SILKS, said one. THE AMAZING ZORRO AND HIS DANCING DOGS, said another. MISSING: GINGER TOM, ANSWERS TO ADMIRAL. They were blank on the back.
"I keep them," said Juno, "for people who haven't got paper. Which is everybody."
He wrote three messages on the backs of the handbills, small and quick, leaning on the crate.
The first was for Toby.
Toby. I'm alive and I'm well. Tell the Section Chief I'm sick. Anything. Something catching. I'll be in tomorrow. Was anyone asking for me this morning? A woman in a white coat, Halloway, or anyone? Write back on this. Give the girl a copper and DON'T lend her anything either. C.
The second was for Vespera.
Someone came into my room this morning over the roofs while a white coat kept Mrs. Marta busy at the front door. Smell of lilies. They read my jars and left a note in a hand very nearly mine. Then they tied a lily to the top of the Dye-Works chimney where Juno could see it. Bolt your door. Don't open it to white. C.
The third was for Bram, at the stationer's on Quill Row. He looked at the Ledger for that one, to get it right. Bram. Holt. Stationer. Lower Docks.
Mister Holt. My Ledger is nearly full. Two pages left. Please give the girl Volume Seven, on account. I'll come and copy the front pages tonight. I won't buy ink. C. Vane.
He folded them, one, two, three. Juno took them from him and looked at the outsides, where he had written the names. She read them slowly, her lips moving.
"Toby," she said. "Ministry. Third Basement. That's the side gate on Archive Lane, they won't let me in the front. Old Wilkin on the gate likes me. He'll fetch him up." She tucked that one into her cap. "Mister Holt, Quill Row. Easy. He gives me humbugs." Into her coat. "Vespera. Hook Lane. The ink lady. With the needles." She stopped, and looked at him. "You know Vespera?"
"So do you."
"Everybody on the roofs knows Vespera. I run for her. Half the Ink-Binders in the Quarter send things through me." Juno tucked the third note away carefully, deep inside her coat, in a different pocket from the others. "She's all right. She doesn't shout. And she never asks to see my key."
She went to the door of the loft and stood in it, with the bright sky behind her and the pigeons walking round her boots.
"Three runs," she said. "Ministry's the far one. Back here by a bit before noon, if Wilkin's quick and Mister Holt doesn't make me drink tea." She looked at him sternly. "You stay here. You don't go out on the roofs on your own. You'll fall off. And you don't touch my maps. And you don't feed the birds, they'll get fat."
"Juno."
"What?"
"I have to be on Linden Walk at noon."
She narrowed her eyes. "Linden Walk. That's posh. Nearly posh. What's on Linden Walk?"
"A stable. With rooms over it. And a girl called Lyra Thorne."
Juno's face changed. The narrowed eyes went wide.
"The Thorne girl?" she said. "Over the horse? With the sword?"
"You know her?"
"I took her a note!" Juno looked delighted and outraged at once. "Two nights back! From deaf Joss at the Inkwell! I came down off her stable roof and she nearly took my head off, and then she gave me two coppers instead of one because she said I'd earned it for the drop." She shook her head. "Lyra Thorne. And now you. It's all the same thing, ain't it. All of it. The Inkwell and Tull and the white coats and the lily."
"I think so."
"Rot," said Juno, with deep satisfaction, as if she had just found out the best secret on all the roofs of the Lower Docks and it was hers. "All right. I'll be back before noon. I'll take you to Linden Walk by the roofs. It's quicker. And if anybody in gray gloves is waiting on the street, they can wait."
She turned to go. Then she stopped in the doorway, and looked back at him over her shoulder, and for a moment she looked very young and very serious.
"Mister V," she said. "That lily. On the chimney."
"Yes."
"It's for both of us, isn't it."
Corin did not lie to her. He had promised not to start with a lie.
"I think so," he said.
Juno nodded slowly. Her fist went to the key, and held it, hard, for a moment. Then she let it go.
"Good," she said. "Then she's got two of us to deal with."
And she was gone. Out of the door and across the roof and over the parapet onto the iron stair, and he heard her boots ringing on it, fast and light, going down and down, and then not at all.
The pigeons settled.
Corin sat alone on the upturned crate in the warm dim loft, among the chalk maps and the murmuring birds, and looked at the front page of his Ledger, at the small new words at the bottom of THINGS THAT ARE TRUE TODAY.
She tells you once.
He touched them with one finger. Then he turned to the last pages, and wrote the time, as near as he could guess it, and the word Waiting, and sat with the book on his knee, and waited.
The Answer
Waiting was harder than the roofs.
On the roofs there had been something to do with his hands and his feet and his fear. In the loft there was nothing. Only the warm dim straw-smelling air, and the bars of sunlight moving slowly across the floor, and the pigeons murmuring to each other in their boxes, and the chalk maps on the walls, and his own thoughts going round and round like a dog chasing its tail.
He did not touch the maps. He did not feed the birds. He sat on the upturned crate with the Ledger on his knee and read it.
Not the last pages. The early ones. The ones he must have read a thousand times and never remembered reading. He turned back past yesterday and the day before, past last week, past the rent and the ink and the list of jars, back and back, until the pages were soft and gray at the edges from his thumbs. He read them the way you might read a letter from a stranger who knew a great deal about you. Small things. Tuesday. Rain. Mrs. Marta's stew had a bone in it. Toby fell asleep at his desk with his mouth open. A woman in the Low Quarter, burned out, sixty, harvested at the third bell. She remembered a dance. Blue dress. I gave her dying back in the jar. Her son came and took it home. And Thursday. Bram says my handwriting is getting worse. I said it was always bad. He said yes, but now it's bad in a different way. And, on its own, in the middle of an empty page, with no date at all: Today I could not remember what my own face looked like until I saw it in a shop window. It was all right. It was only a face.
He sat with that one for a long time.
You read too much, said the voice in his right side.
It was dry and sour, as before. But quieter now. As if the old man in the rod had settled back in a deep chair and was speaking with his eyes shut.
Malakor, Corin thought.
Who else. Unless you've collected another voice while I wasn't looking. You do collect them. A pause. How's the hand?
Corin looked at his right hand. It had stopped trembling. But it felt light. Hollow. As if the bones in it had been taken out and replaced with something thinner.
It's still there.
The hand is. The boy isn't. There was something in the voice he could not read. Not cruelty. Something more like tiredness. Every time you burn one of them, there's a bit less room in here. Did you know that? A bit less noise. A bit less crowd. One day you'll reach in for a dead sailor or a dead boxer, and there'll be nobody left in the rod at all. Only me. A dry little sound that might have been a laugh. And I'll be very bored.
Why do you stay?
The voice did not answer that.
Corin waited. Outside, a pigeon landed on the windowsill, looked at him with one orange eye, and flew away again.
The lily, he thought at last. On the chimney. You saw it.
I see what you see. I've been seeing what you see for a very long time, boy.
Do you know who left it?
A long silence. Longer than the one about staying.
I knew a woman once, said Malakor, very slowly, as if each word were being lifted out of deep water. A long time ago. Before the rod. Before Hale. She liked white. She liked lilies. She liked to give people little gifts, so they'd know she'd been thinking of them.
Who was she?
I don't remember.
Corin did not believe him. And he had the strangest feeling, sitting there in the warm loft with the pigeons, that Malakor knew he did not believe him, and did not mind, and was glad of it.
Ask me another night, said Malakor. And then, more quietly still, so quietly that Corin was not sure whether he had heard it or only felt it: The stones in your pocket are humming, boy. Can you hear them? Since the lily. Like bees in a wall.
Corin put his hand flat on the right side of his coat, over the buttoned pocket and the black sewn square.
He could not hear anything. But under his palm, through the cloth and the leather and the silk, there was a warmth that had not been there before. A very faint, very steady warmth. Like a hand held near a cup of tea.
He took his hand away.
He wrote: Malakor says he knew a woman who liked white and lilies and gifts. Before Hale. Says he doesn't remember who. He's lying. Says the stones are humming since the lily. I can't hear it. They're warm.
Then the voice was gone, and the loft was quiet, and he picked up Juno's brass telescope from the crate and looked out of the window at the chimney.
The lily was still there. Turning, slowly, on its black thread, a hundred feet up in the brown smoke.
He put the telescope down. He did not look again for a long time.
The bells were ringing the half hour before noon when he heard boots on the iron stair.
Fast and light, ringing up through five floors, and the pigeons on the parapet all lifted at once in a clatter of wings and came down again, and the loft door banged open, and Juno came in with her cap pushed back and her face red and shining and a parcel under her arm.
"Three runs," she panted. "Ministry. Mister Holt. Ink lady. And back. And Wilkin was slow and Mister Holt did make me drink tea, and I still beat the bell." She dropped onto her sacks and lay flat on her back with her arms out, breathing hard, and grinned at the tin roof. "I'm brilliant."
"You are."
"I know." She sat up and pulled the folded handbill out of her cap and held it out. "Toby first. He's funny. He nearly fainted when Wilkin said there was a girl at the gate with a message from you. He came running up all the stairs from the bottom of the building, and he's got spectacles that keep falling off, and he said oh thank the Saints, oh thank the Saints about nine times. And then he wrote on the back and gave me a copper and his lunch."
"His lunch?"
"Well. He didn't give me it. He put it down to write, and I was hungry." Juno looked not at all sorry. "It was cheese."
Corin unfolded the handbill. THE AMAZING ZORRO AND HIS DANCING DOGS. He turned it over. Under his own message there was another, in a small, round, careful, anxious hand that slanted backward as if it were trying to get away from the page.
C. Thank goodness. Told the Section Chief you have the spotted fever. He went pale and shut his door and won't come out. You can stay sick a week if you like.
Somebody DID ask. Not Halloway. The other one, the big one, Brack. He came down to the Third Basement at the eighth bell, by himself. Asked for you. Asked where you lodge. Asked if you keep your Ledger with you or leave it at home. I said I didn't know. Then he looked at me for a long time and asked if I knew anything about a red book. I said what red book. C, I am a TERRIBLE liar. I think he knew.
Come in tomorrow early. By the side gate on Archive Lane. Not the front.
T.
P.S. He said Halloway isn't in today. "Sick," he said. And laughed. It was not a nice laugh.
P.P.S. Your girl ate my lunch.
Corin read it twice. Then he copied it into the Ledger, onto his very last page but one, as small as he could, word for word. He did not copy the bit about the lunch.
Halloway isn't in today.
He thought about a white coat standing on Mrs. Marta's step at the seventh bell, polite, in gloves. A white coat catching a tram on the Docks road, going east. A white coat standing in a cellar doorway on Hook Lane, very still, watching Vespera's window. Two neat bootprints, small, with new square heels. A white thread on a splinter.
Not sent, then. Not by the Inquisition. Not today.
She had come on her own.
"Mister Holt's next," said Juno, and pushed the parcel across the straw at him.
It was wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, and it was heavy and square. He knew what it was before he opened it. His hands knew. They untied the string and folded back the paper, and there it was. A thick book, as thick as two fingers, bound in dark brown leather. But new. Smooth and shining and stiff, without a single crack. And low down on the cover, pressed into the leather, sharp and clean:
GROUNDING LEDGER. B. HOLT, STATIONER. LOWER DOCKS.
And under it: VII.
There was a folded note tucked inside the front cover. He opened it. The handwriting was big and square and heavy, every letter standing up on its own like a brick in a wall.
C.V. Seven, on account. Come tonight, I'll put the kettle on, you copy the front. DON'T BUY INK.
A woman came to the shop yesterday at closing. Not a lady. A lady's servant, I'd say. Small. Dark cloak. Very polite. Asked did I keep old books for a gentleman named Vane. Said her mistress was a friend of yours and wanted to return a favor. I said I never heard of you. Twice, the way you made me promise.
She smelled like a funeral.
Keep this one close. Closer than the last. B.H.
Corin sat very still with the new Ledger in his lap.
Small. Dark cloak. He did not need to read back to know where he had written those words before. An hour ago, on a page full of Juno's voice. Not in white. Dark. A big dark cloak, the kind with a hood, the color of a wet roof. Small. Smaller than you.
She had gone to Bram first. Yesterday, at closing. Asking for his old books. And Bram had said he had never heard of him. And so this morning she had come over the roofs to his room, to see if the books were there instead.
They were not. They were in a strongbox under the floor of a stationer's shop on Quill Row, six years of him, locked away. And the rest of him was here, in his coat, over his heart, with almost no room left to write. He held the new book in his hands. VII. Stiff. Empty. Waiting.
He put Bram's note inside the back cover of the old Ledger, beside the forged one, the note in the hand that was very nearly his. He wrapped the new Ledger in its brown paper again, carefully, and put it inside his coat, on the left side, against the old one. They sat there together, one worn soft and one stiff and new, like an old man and a boy.
"And the ink lady?" he said.
Juno had stopped grinning. She had been watching his face while he read.
"She read your note," she said. "Standing in her door. And she went sort of gray. And she said a word I'm not allowed to say. And then she bolted her door, right there, while I was still on the step, and opened her little window instead and talked to me through that." Juno scratched her nose. "She gave me a humbug. Then she said to tell you two things."
"What things?"
"First thing. She said, tell him I told him so. Up as well as round." Juno frowned. "I don't know what that means."
"I do," said Corin.
"Second thing." Juno hesitated. "She said, tell him white is a color that wants to be looked at. So look at what's standing behind it."
Corin wrote that down too. On his very last page. The last line on the last page of the old Ledger. He wrote it small, and it just fitted.
Look at what's standing behind it.
Then there was no more room.
He closed the old book and sat with his hand flat on its cover for a moment. It felt strange. Like closing a door on a room he had lived in for a long time.
Far off, across the roofs, a bell began to ring the noon.
Juno was on her feet at once. "Rot. Linden Walk. Come on, come on, it's right up the top of the hill, we'll have to go the quick way." She was already at the door, already half out of it, the key swinging on its string. "And you're not falling off. You've got my name on your important page now. If you fall off, I'll have to come and tell you who I am at the bottom of the Cut, and I'm not doing that."
He stood up. He followed her out.
The bright noon light hit him on the roof of the old warehouse like a wave. The pigeons went up all round them in a great gray cloud of wings and came down again on the parapet. The whole city lay spread out below in the sunshine, roofs and chimneys and the shining river and the boats, and, far off up the hill, the black towers of the Ministry, spiked like a crown against the sky, and above them, on the very top of the hill, the white houses of the High Quarter, small and clean and far away.
At the top of the iron stair, Corin stopped. He did not know why. He turned and looked back, one last time, at the Dye-Works chimney.
The iron rail at the top of the ladder was empty.
No lily. No white speck. No black thread. Only the red brick, and the black band, and the thin brown smoke going up into the blue.
Juno had stopped too. She was looking at the same place. Her hand had gone to her key.
"Wind took it," said Corin. "Maybe."
"Wind doesn't untie knots," said Juno.
They looked at the empty rail for a moment longer. Nobody was on the ladder. Nobody was on the roofs. Nobody was anywhere at all, as far as they could see. Only the pigeons, and the smoke, and the long gray sea of roofs.
Then Juno turned, and went down the iron stair two steps at a time, and he went after her.
She took him the quick way. Up out of the Lower Docks by roofs and gutters and the back stairs of tall houses, by a plank laid across an alley between two windows, by a church roof with a lead gutter so wide you could have driven a cart along it. The roofs got cleaner as they climbed. The slates got newer. The chimneys stopped leaning. The washing lines disappeared, and the bean pots, and the pigeon lofts, and the old women shelling peas. The roofs up here were empty and tidy and nobody lived on them.
At the top of the hill, on the edge of a long roof of new gray slate, Juno stopped and crouched and pointed down.
"Linden Walk," she said.
It was a quiet street. Wide and clean, with a row of young lime trees down the middle, their leaves yellow at the edges and starting to fall. The houses along it had been grand once. You could see that. Tall white fronts with columns by the doors and iron railings and big windows. But some of the windows were shuttered now, and some of the white fronts had gone gray and cracked, and at the far end, where the street turned, there was a stable yard behind a tall brick wall, with a gate in it.
On the gatepost, on top, there was a stone pineapple.
It was cracked down one side. Moss grew in the crack. But it was a pineapple, and it was stone, and it was there.
"That's her," said Juno. "Up the outside stair. Over the horse." She looked at him sideways. "You going down by the street, like a proper gentleman?"
"Like a proper gentleman."
"Shame." She stood up on the edge of the roof, easily, with the sky behind her. "I'll be up here. Somewhere. You won't see me." She paused. "But I'll see you."
He went down by a back stair and a side door and came out onto Linden Walk with his hat straight and his coat buttoned and his two Ledgers, old and new, against his heart, and walked along under the yellowing lime trees like any clerk on a message. Nobody looked at him. The noon bell was still ringing, slow and deep, across the whole city.
At the stable gate, under the stone pineapple, he stopped and looked up.
The stable was old red brick, with tall doors and a weathervane shaped like a running horse. An outside stair of worn stone went up its side to a green door on the floor above. And on the top step, in a plain gray dress with her dark hair pinned up and a long thin sword in a scabbard leaning against the wall beside her, there sat a young woman with her arms around her knees.
She was looking at him. She had been looking for some time, he thought. Waiting.
He did not know her face.
But he knew her. The way he had known the stairs, and the chair, and the shape of the slates under his hands. Something in him turned over and settled, like a key in a lock.
PARTNER.
"You're on time," said Lyra Thorne.
"I wrote it in capitals," said Corin.
And somewhere high above them, on a long gray roof against the noon sky, something small and dark sat down on a chimney stack to watch, and a brass key on a string caught the sun and flashed, once, like a signal.
