Chapter 15: Bram's Chalkboard, Part 2 of 5: The Room Under the Dome
The Room Under the Dome
The Lady's rooms were at the very top of the Bourse, under the dome, and the first thing Corin saw when Tam opened the doors was the sky.
The room was round. It was much smaller than the trading floor, far below, but it was still big, as big as the whole of Mrs. Marta's house laid flat. And its ceiling was the inside of the great dome itself. Not stone. Not copper. Glass. The whole curved roof over his head, from the walls all the way up to the very top, was made of panes of glass set in a web of thin black iron, like the veins in a leaf. And the glass was clear. Through it, he could see the evening sky, dark blue now, nearly black, with the last of the snow clouds drifting away across it in long gray rags. And beyond the clouds, very faint, the first stars.
Snow lay on the glass in places, where it had settled in the angles of the iron, in little white drifts. Between the drifts, the sky.
It made the room feel as if it had no roof at all. As if it stood on top of the world, open to the night.
The room itself was warm. Very warm. There was a fire in a great fireplace of black marble on the far side, roaring, and the air smelled of woodsmoke and of something sweet and dark and heavy, like burnt sugar and spice. The floor was covered in rugs. Thick soft rugs, laid one over another, so that his boots sank into them and made no sound at all. Deep red and gold and black and a blue like the night outside. There were low chairs, and couches, and little tables with lamps on them, and on the walls, all the way round the room, under the edge of the glass, there were cases.
Glass cases. Tall and narrow, with black wooden frames, and little silver locks. Like the cases in a jeweler's shop. And in every case, on shelves of black velvet, there were vials.
Not like the ones in the vault. These were bigger. As long as a man's hand, some of them. And they were not plain glass, with plain silver stoppers. They were cut, like crystal, so that they caught the firelight and threw it back in sparks, and their stoppers were gold, and some of them had little gold chains, and some had tiny jewels set in their tops. They stood on the black velvet in rows, glowing. Gold and rose and deep green and violet and the strange soft gray. And blue. Every blue there was.
And under each one, on a tiny card, in a beautiful sloping hand, there was a word. Just one.
Joy. Nerve. Mercy. Grace. Wit. Patience. Fear (removed). Shame (removed). Love (first).
"Mister Vane," said a voice.
She was sitting by the fire.
She had been there all along. He had not seen her, because of the sky, and the cases, and because she was sitting so still. She sat in a low, deep chair of black velvet, very close to the fire, with her feet on a little stool and a fan in her lap, closed. And she was looking at him.
She was not young. That was the first thing. She was not old either. She might have been fifty, or sixty, or forty and very tired. Her face was long and pale and smooth, with high cheekbones and a long straight nose, and it had been beautiful once, in a cold way, and it was still beautiful in a colder one. Her hair was black. Black as ink, without a single gray thread in it, piled up high on her head and held with long pins tipped with jet. She wore a gown of a deep, dark, shining violet, like the skin of a plum, very plain, with long sleeves, and a high collar.
And over the collar, round her throat, there hung a necklace.
It was vials. Like Cressida's. But Cressida's had been tiny, no bigger than grains of rice, hung close round her throat like a string of beads. These were not tiny. Each one was as long as Corin's little finger, cut like a jewel, with a gold stopper, and there were a dozen of them hanging from a heavy chain of gold in a long, deep loop that lay across her collar and down onto the violet silk. And each one was a different color. And each one was glowing. Brightly. Much more brightly than the ones in the cases. So brightly that they lit her face from below, the way a candle lights a face, and threw small colored shadows up under her chin and her cheekbones. Gold. Rose. Green. Violet. Blue.
And her eyes, above all that colored light, were heavy-lidded and dark and perfectly calm.
"Do sit down," said Lady Vivienne. "You look tired. Everybody who comes up in the vial-lift looks tired. I think it's the vault. It upsets people." She lifted one long pale hand, and turned it over, palm up, toward a low chair on the other side of the fire. "There. Where I can see you."
Corin sat down.
He sat carefully, on the very edge of the chair, with his gloved hands on his knees, not touching the arms. The chair was soft and deep and it wanted to swallow him. He did not let it.
Lady Vivienne watched him do it. Her mouth moved. Not a smile. The beginning of one.
"Tam told you about the gloves," she said.
"Yes."
"Good. He's a sensible boy. His mother was sensible too, before the Bank had her." She said it the way someone else might have said before the weather changed. "You can't be too careful in this house, Mister Vane. Everything in it has been touched by somebody, and some of it has been touched by a great many people. Glass remembers. And what glass remembers, it sometimes gives back." She turned her fan over in her lap. "Some of my guests take off their gloves on purpose, to see what they'll get. They think it's terribly amusing."
She looked past him.
"Remy, darling. Wine for Mister Vane. He won't drink it. But he should have the chance to refuse."
Corin had not seen the young man either.
He was standing by one of the glass cases, a little way off, in the shadow between two lamps. A slight, neat young man in a gray coat, with brown hair and a pale, serious face and very dark eyes. He had been looking into the case. He turned round now, without hurry, and went to a little table by the wall where there was a silver tray with a decanter on it, and poured a glass of wine, dark red, and brought it to Corin, and held it out.
By the stem.
Corin took it by the stem. He held it in his lap. He did not drink.
"Mister Castellan," he said.
"Mister Vane." Remy Castellan inclined his head, very slightly. "We met in the slag room. You were very kind about my interrupting you." His dark eyes rested on Corin's face for a moment. "And I watched you climb out of Niles's yesterday, over the roofs. With the Thorne girl and the little sweep. You were much better at it than I expected."
The bindery roof, thought Corin. The slight neat figure in the gray coat, in the snow.
"You were watching her men," he said. "Cressida's."
"I was watching everybody's men." Remy went back to his case, and leaned against the wall beside it, with his arms folded. "It's what I'm for."
"Remy is my eyes," said Lady Vivienne. "And my ears, sometimes, though they're not as good as Tam's. And my legs, always. I don't go out very much anymore. I find the city so tiring." She opened her fan, slowly, and looked at it. It was black lace. "So I have to rely on other people to tell me what's happening in it. And today, Mister Vane, a great deal has been happening."
She closed the fan.
"An old clockmaker who has been dead for eight years started a clock that nobody in this city has been able to start for thirty," she said. "Old Tide struck twelve at a quarter to one. Every pigeon in Veridia went up at once. Councillor Thorne's men chased a Ministry harvester, a Thorne girl, a chimney sweep and a deaf clock-mender across the Lantern Bridge in broad daylight, and the harvester lit up the middle of the bridge like a lightning strike and put a burner from the Ostmark docks on his knees with his eyes burned white." She tilted her head. "He'll see again. In a week or two. He's very upset about it. He's been telling everyone in the Cut that he was struck by a saint."
Corin said nothing.
"And last night," said Lady Vivienne, "Councillor Cressida Thorne sent a letter, on cream paper, with lily wax, to a room over a livery stable on Linden Walk. And this morning she sent her maid to every jeweler on the Hill, one after another, asking whether anyone had lately been offered a very fine round blue stone, about the size of a child's thumb." The heavy-lidded eyes did not blink. "She seems to want it very much. I can't think why. Can you?"
"No," said Corin.
It was the truth. He could not.
Lady Vivienne looked at him for a long moment.
"No," she agreed. "I don't suppose you can. Not yet."
The fire cracked. A log fell, and sparks went up the black marble chimney in a little whirl. Above them, through the glass of the dome, the last gray cloud drifted off the face of a white moon.
"I want to tell you about a man called Tull," said Lady Vivienne.
Corin's hand tightened on the stem of the glass.
Tull. He did not need the Ledger. A huge man in the Inkwell, with a wet pink mouth and a laugh like gravel in a bucket. Black Bank. Tull works for her. We work for Tull. The Lady at the Bourse.
"Tull was mine," said Lady Vivienne. "For eleven years. A stupid man, and a cruel one, but very useful. He collected debts for me in the Lower Docks. I am, as I'm sure your little book has told you, a lender. Among other things." She smoothed the black lace of her fan with one finger. "And then, last spring, Tull began to take orders from somebody else. Somebody in my own house. Somebody who came to my Thursday evenings, and drank my wine, and smiled at me, and went down the service stairs afterward to talk to my men." Her voice did not change at all. It was low and warm and slow. "I don't care to be used, Mister Vane. I don't care for it at all."
"She sent Tull to the Inkwell," said Corin slowly. "For Pell's drawer. For the red book. Not you."
"Not I." Lady Vivienne inclined her head. "Tull told his boys it was the Lady at the Bourse. Which was very clever of her. Because everyone knows that the Lady at the Bourse is me. And if a Ministry harvester ever came asking, he would come asking here. To me. And never think to look further." She almost smiled. "And here you are."
"Where's Tull now?"
"Nobody knows," said Lady Vivienne. "He went out one night last week to see a friend about a carriage. And he didn't come back." She opened her fan again. "People who work for a lady who smells of lilies often seem to go to the country."
She said it quite lightly. But for one moment, as she said lilies, the colored lights of the vials at her throat seemed to flicker. All of them at once. As if something cold had breathed on them.
"Now," said Lady Vivienne. "Business."
She sat up, a little, in the deep black chair. Not much. But the whole room seemed to change with it, the way a room changes when somebody closes a door.
"On Monday at noon," she said, "a carriage from the House of Rest at Fallowfield will come for Lord Aurelian Thorne. The papers are signed. Cressida had them signed by old Pembrook, in the Council chamber, last Tuesday, after dinner, when he had had a great deal of port and was in no state to read anything." Her dark eyes rested on him. "Pembrook owes me a very great deal of money, Mister Vane. More than he owes anyone. More than he could pay in three lifetimes. If I send him a note tonight, the Fallowfield order will be found to have a mistake on it. A wrong date. A missing seal. Something small. And it will have to go back to the Council. And the Council does not sit again until after the new year." She paused. "That would give the Thorne girl three months. Perhaps four. Long enough to come of age, in the spring. And once she is of age, Cressida is no longer the nearest family. Lyra is."
Corin sat very still.
He thought of Lyra at her plain wooden table that morning, under the dark beams, with her face as white as the snow. I'm not of age till spring. He thought of an old man in a white chair by a window, holding two blue stones in his still hand.
"And what do you want," he said, "for a note to Lord Pembrook?"
Lady Vivienne smiled.
It was a slow smile, and a warm one. It began at the corners of her red-brown mouth and spread, slowly, across her whole face, until it lit it, the way the colored lights at her throat lit it. It was the most beautiful smile Corin had ever seen.
It did not reach her eyes at all.
"Your Ledgers," she said.
Corin did not move.
"Not that one." She lifted her fan and pointed it, lightly, at the left side of his chest. At the stiff new book over his heart. "You need that one. I'm not cruel, whatever they say in the Cut. I mean the others. The old ones. One to six." Her voice was perfectly calm. "The ones in the strongbox under the floor of Bram Holt's shop on Quill Row."
The glass of wine in Corin's lap had begun, very slightly, to shake. He put his other hand round the stem to stop it.
"How do you know where they are?"
"Because I know where everything is, Mister Vane. That's what I'm for." She closed the fan. "And because somebody else has been asking for them. A small person in a dark cloak, with a hood, who went into Holt's shop at closing time a week ago and asked for Mister Vane's old books. And Holt said he'd never heard of you." Her mouth curved. "Which was very loyal of him. And very foolish. Because nobody who had never heard of you would bother to lie so badly."
"Why do you want them?"
Lady Vivienne looked at him for a long moment, with her heavy, calm, dark eyes.
"Because for six years," she said, "you have been writing down everything. Every death you were sent to. Every jar you filled. Every name you heard in a dead man's last minutes, and every face. Every lie the Ministry told you, and every truth you weren't supposed to find. You couldn't help it. You'd have forgotten otherwise." She tilted her head. "A man who forgets everything writes down everything. And so, without ever meaning to, you have made the only honest record in this whole city. The only one nobody has edited. The only one nobody has burned." She smiled again. "I would like to read it. I would like very much to see whether you got any of it right."
To see if he got any of it right. He had heard those words before. Or read them. In his own hand. In the Inkwell, in the dark, with a knife at his throat. The Lady wants his Ledger. To see if he got any of it right.
She had said it on purpose. He was sure of it. She was watching his face to see if he knew.
"And somebody else," he said slowly, "wants to see the same thing."
"Somebody else," said Lady Vivienne, "wants to burn them." She leaned back in her chair again. "I only want to read them. I think you'll find that's a very great difference."
She lifted one hand. Remy Castellan, by the wall, unfolded his arms and went to one of the tall glass cases, and took a tiny silver key from his waistcoat, and unlocked it. He took something out. Then he came back across the soft rugs, without a sound, and held it out to Corin, on the flat of his gray-gloved palm.
A vial.
Not one of the grand ones from the cases. A small plain one, like the ones in the vault. Plain glass, plain silver stopper. It glowed a soft, pale, wavering blue. And stuck to the bottom of it, there was a little paper label, in a small neat clerk's hand.
Voice of a sister, laughing. Poor (faded at edges).
Corin could not breathe.
"You stopped to read it," said Lady Vivienne gently. "In the vault. Between the racks. Dobbin saw you, from the wheel. He sees everything. He has nothing else to do." She watched his face. "It isn't hers, Mister Vane. Your sister's. I don't think so. It came in from the intake hall two winters ago, from a woman who sold everything she had in a single morning. But I don't know whose it was. Nobody does." She paused. "It might be. That's the thing about memories. You can never quite tell."
The vial glowed on Remy's palm, a hand's breadth from Corin's face. Soft and pale and blue.
If he took off his glove. If he touched it, with his bare finger, just once. He would hear it. A girl, laughing. Some girl. Somewhere. Long ago.
Cor, look.
"Bring me the six," said Lady Vivienne, "by Sunday midnight. To the kitchen door. Ask for Tam. And on Monday, no carriage will come to Pruett's yard. And this," she lifted her fan, very slightly, toward the vial, "is yours. As well. For nothing. A gift."
The fire cracked. The vials at her throat glowed. Above them, through the glass of the dome, the moon had come out, white and cold and very clear, and its light came down through the panes and lay across the rugs in long pale bars, between the shadows of the iron.
Corin looked at the vial for a long time.
Then he looked away from it. At the fire. At Lady Vivienne's calm dark eyes.
"I'll think about it," he said.
There was a silence.
Then Lady Vivienne laughed. Softly. Low in her throat. It was a real laugh, the only real thing he had seen in her face, and it surprised him.
"Vespera," she said. "Saints, I'd know it anywhere. She's been teaching that to half the Lower Docks for thirty years. Never say yes in that house. Never say no either." She shook her head slowly, still smiling. "She's quite right, of course. She always was." The smile went, as quickly as it had come. "Sunday midnight, Mister Vane. Think quickly. Remy, darling, give Mister Vane back to Tam. And put that away."
Remy closed his gray-gloved hand over the vial. The soft blue light went out between his fingers.
And Corin, who had not known until that moment that he had been leaning toward it, sat back in the deep black chair, and found that his face was wet.
The Ice House
Tam did not take him down in the vial-lift.
He took him down the servants' stair. A long, narrow, steep stone stair that wound down inside the wall of the great round building, round and round, with a rope for a rail and a little blue lamp at every turn. It was very busy. Footmen in plum came up it with trays of glasses, and maids in black came down it with armfuls of linen, and a boy in an apron went past them both at a run with a basket of bread on his head. Every one of them pressed back against the wall to let Tam and Corin by, and not one of them looked at Corin's face.
Tam did not speak. He went down ahead of Corin, quick and straight-backed in his plum coat, and he did not look round once. But Corin, behind him, saw that his left hand kept going to his right cuff. To the inside of the white cuff at his wrist. His thumb rubbed at it, over and over, a small hard rubbing movement, as if there were something under the cloth that he wanted to make sure was still there.
At the bottom of the stair they did not go back into the great hot kitchen. Tam turned the other way, down a short dark passage that smelled of damp stone and cabbage, and stopped at a heavy wooden door bound with iron straps. He took a key from his waistcoat, and opened it, and a breath of air came out round it so cold that it made Corin's teeth ache.
"In," said Tam. "Quick."
It was the ice house.
A small round room, sunk deep in the ground, with walls of thick stone and a floor of stone, and no window at all. It was full of ice. Great blocks of it, gray-white and cloudy, stacked on the floor in a tall square pile and covered over with straw and old sacking, and the cold came off it in a slow steady breath that you could see in the light of Tam's lamp, curling and drifting over the stone like mist on the river. There were hooks in the ceiling. On some of them hung things wrapped in white cloth. Joints of meat. A pair of pheasants, upside down, with their bright feathers dull with frost. A whole salmon, as long as Corin's arm, in a cloth like a shroud.
Tam shut the door behind them. He set the lamp down on top of the ice, in the straw. And then he leaned his back against the door, and shut his eyes, and let out a long, long breath, like a man putting down something very heavy that he has been carrying for a long way.
"Nobody comes in here," he said. "Not after the dinner's gone up. Cook keeps the only other key, and Cook's mine." He opened his eyes. "And the walls are three feet thick, and full of ice. Nothing gets through them. Not a sound. Not anything." He looked at Corin. "You can talk here, Mister Vane."
His breath went up between them in a white cloud. In the lamplight his thin face was gray, and very young, and very tired.
"You're one of them," said Corin. "Aren't you? Whoever draws that eye."
Tam did not answer at once. He looked at Corin for a long moment. Then he lifted his right hand, and with his left he turned back the white cuff at his wrist. Just a little. Just enough.
On the inside of the cuff, on the white linen, someone had sewn a tiny mark. In black thread. Very small and very neat. So small that you would never have seen it unless you knew it was there.
An open book. And above it, an eye. Open.
"My mother sewed it," said Tam. "She sewed one in every shirt I ever had. Since I was six." He turned the cuff back down, and his thumb went on rubbing at it, over the linen, the way it had on the stair. "She cleaned the Third Basement of the Ministry of Records for nine years. Nights. On her knees. With a bucket." His mouth twisted. "You know what the Third Basement is? It's where they bring the papers to be pulped. Forms with mistakes on them. Old files nobody wants. Letters. Reports. Records that somebody upstairs has decided never happened."
Corin thought of Toby. Of fifteen blank forms with the Archon's own seal on them, hidden in the back cover of REGULATIONS, BOOK ONE. They were meant to be pulped. All of them.
"She used to read them," said Tam. "On her knees. By the light of her lamp. Before she threw them in. She wasn't supposed to. She wore the gray gloves so the glass wouldn't take her hands, and she read every scrap of paper they gave her to burn. And some nights, if it was something that mattered, she copied it. On the inside of her apron. In pencil. Small." He swallowed. "And in the morning, on her way home, she'd stop at a shop in the Lower Docks. And leave her apron to be washed."
"Bram's," said Corin.
Tam looked at him.
"I didn't say that," he said. "I never said that."
"No," said Corin. "You didn't."
They stood in the cold. The ice ticked softly in its straw. One of the frozen pheasants on its hook turned, very slowly, in the draft from under the door, and turned back.
"They caught her," said Tam at last. "Three years ago. Not for the copying. They never knew about the copying. For a debt. Eight silver, on the rent, from a winter when I was sick. The Bank came for her on a Tuesday and took it out of her in the intake hall, and they took more than eight silver, because that's what they do, and when she came home she didn't know my name." His voice was quite flat. "She still lives with me. Over the mews behind this house. The Lady gave us the room. The Lady's very generous." He paused. "Mam's happy. She likes the window. She sews all day. Little marks. On everything. Open books and eyes. On the sheets, and the curtains, and my shirts. Hundreds of them. She doesn't know what they mean anymore. Her hands just keep making them."
He stopped rubbing his cuff. He looked down at his hand as if he had only just noticed what it was doing.
"That's why I took this job," he said. "Here. In the Lady's house. Because everything in this city that gets bought or sold comes through this building sooner or later. And somebody ought to be watching it." He lifted his chin. "Somebody ought to write it down."
He reached inside his plum-colored coat.
He took out an envelope.
It was small and square and plain. Gray paper. Not fine paper, like Cressida's cream. Cheap, rough, gray paper, the kind clerks used for the backs of things. It was sealed on the back with a blob of black wax, and pressed into the wax, very small and very clear, was the mark. The book. The eye.
On the front, in small square careful capitals, it said:
B. H. BY HAND. TONIGHT.
Tam held it out.
"It came for you," he said. "Not from me. I don't know what's in it. I don't know who wrote it. It came down the line this afternoon, the way things do, and it got to me because I was the one who'd see you." He held it steady. "I only carry. That's all any of us do. We carry things from one hand to the next, and nobody holds anything for long, and nobody knows more than they have to. That way, when they catch one of us, there's not much to take."
Corin took the envelope. It was very light. There was something in it, something flat and stiff, that shifted very slightly when he tilted it.
"Take it to Holt," said Tam. "Tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight. And don't open it in the street, and don't open it at your lodgings, and don't open it anywhere at all except in front of him. It isn't addressed to you. It's addressed to him. You're only carrying it too." He paused. "Like me."
Corin put it inside his coat. Not in the Ledger. In the inside pocket on the other side, the right side, where the rod lay along the lining, cold.
"Tam," he said. "What does it stand for? The eye. The book."
Tam shook his head.
"Ask Holt," he said. "He's the one who's allowed to tell. Not me." His mouth twitched. "I'm only a footman."
He picked up the lamp.
But he did not open the door at once. He stood with the lamp in his hand and his other hand on the iron latch, and looked at Corin. And his quick brown eyes were not quick anymore. They were very steady, and very serious.
"Mister Vane," he said. "Whatever she offered you up there. Don't."
Corin said nothing.
"I know what it was," said Tam. "I don't have to have been in the room. She wants your old books. She's wanted them since the spring, since the lily lady started asking. And she'll have offered you something you can't say no to. She always does. That's how she does it. She doesn't threaten. She never threatens. She finds out the one thing you want most in the whole world, and she holds it out to you, on a glove, and waits." His voice was low and fast. "And the worst of it is, she keeps her word. She always keeps her word. Whatever she promised you, she'll do it. Exactly. To the letter. You'll get it." He swallowed. "And then she'll have you. For good. Because you'll know she can do it again."
He looked down at the lamp in his hand.
"She gave me Mam's room," he said. "Over the mews. With the window Mam likes. And I've been carrying plates up her stairs for three years." He opened the door. The cold breath of the ice house went out past him into the dark passage in a long white curl. "Think about it, Mister Vane. Like Vespera says. Think hard."
He let Corin out by the red door into the lane.
It had stopped snowing. The sky over the narrow slot of the lane was clear and black and full of stars, more stars than Corin had ever seen, hard and white and cold, and the moon was up over the counting houses, and the snow in the lane was not blue anymore but silver. The cold was very great. It went into Corin's chest with his first breath and stayed there, like a stone.
The heap of broken vials in the horseless cart glittered in the moonlight.
Tam stood in the doorway in his plum coat, with the warm yellow light and the noise of the kitchen behind him. He did not say goodbye. He looked once up the lane and once down it, quick, the way he had when he first opened the door. Then he looked at Corin.
"Quill Row," he said. "Tonight."
And he shut the red door.
Corin heard the bolts go across. One. Two. And then there was nothing but the silver lane, and the stars, and the great dark curve of the Bourse wall going up beside him into the sky, with its tall arched windows full of blue light, all the way up to the green copper dome. At the very top of the dome, through the glass, he could see a faint warm glow. A fire, in a round room, under the stars.
He wondered if she was watching him.
He thought she probably was.
He turned his collar up, and put his gloved hands deep in his pockets, and walked down the lane toward Exchange Street, and the river, and the Lower Docks, with the gray envelope inside his coat, against the cold of the rod. He did not look back.
But all the way down the hill, through the silver streets, past the shuttered shops and the lamplit windows and the people hurrying home with their heads down in the cold, he could feel it. On the back of his neck. Like a fingertip.
Not the Lady.
The vial.
A little plain glass vial with a silver stopper, glowing a soft pale wavering blue, on the flat of a gray-gloved hand.
Voice of a sister, laughing.
He walked faster.
