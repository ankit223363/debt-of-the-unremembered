Chapter 15: Bram's Chalkboard, Part 5 of 5: A Mistake on a Paper
A Mistake on a Paper
The last coat was finished at a quarter to midnight.
Bram pressed the letters into its spine, HARBOUR TIDE TABLES. VOL. VI, and blew on them, and slid Ledger Six inside, and set it on the pile with the others. Six dull brown books. Six dull brown spines. Stacked on the end of the workbench under the lamp, as plain and as boring as a pile of bricks.
Then he picked them up, all six, against his great chest, and carried them out into the dark shop.
Corin followed him with the lamp.
The tide tables were on a high shelf near the back, behind the counter, under a thick gray blanket of dust. There were a great many of them. Forty, perhaps. Fifty. All the same dull brown cloth, all the same dull square letters on their spines. HARBOUR TIDE TABLES. Year after year after year, going back further than Corin could read in the dim light. Spiders had built between them. One of them had a dead moth pressed in the top of it, flat and gray, like the lily in Ledger Three.
Bram climbed up on a little wooden step, groaning, and slid the six new ones onto the end of the shelf, one after another, very carefully, so as not to disturb the dust on the others. Then he climbed down. Then he went back to his workroom and came back with a small soft brush, and climbed up again, and brushed a little dust from the shelf above onto the tops of the six new books. Lightly. Until they were gray. Until they looked as if they had sat there, untouched, for thirty years, like all the rest.
He climbed down and stood back and looked at them, with his hands on his hips.
"There," he said. "Find them now."
Corin looked at the shelf. At the long, dusty, dull brown row. He knew where they were. He had watched them go up. And he still could not tell, from where he stood, which six they were.
He thought of a white room behind a clock face. Of a man who had hidden inside a stopped clock for a year, because nobody ever looked at a stopped clock.
Nobody looks at tide tables.
"Thank you," he said.
"Don't thank me." Bram stepped down off the step. "Pay me." He went to the chalkboard at the end of the counter, and picked up a stub of chalk from the ledge, and under LEDGER VII. ON ACCOUNT, in his big square letters, he wrote: 6 COVERS, CLOTH. 1 NIGHT'S SEWING. 4 PRICKED FINGERS. ON ACCOUNT.
And then he reached down to the bottom corner of the board, to the tiny chalk drawing of the open book and the eye, and with the end of his thumb he rubbed it out.
Corin opened his mouth.
Bram did not look at him. He took the stub of chalk, and in the same place, very small, very neat, he drew it again. Fresh. The book. The eye. Open.
"Every morning," he said. "Thirty years." He put the chalk down. "And tonight, for once, at night as well."
They went back into the warm workroom and Bram made tea, black and strong, in a brown pot with a cracked lid, and they drank it at the end of the bench, out of two thick brown mugs, with the lamp turned low. Corin's fingers were sore. He looked at them. There were four tiny red dots on the ends of them where the needle had gone in, and a paper cut across the side of one thumb, and a crust of dried paste on the back of his hand. They looked like somebody else's hands. They looked, he thought, like somebody's hands who had done something.
He had not put his gloves back on.
"Bram," he said. "Monday."
Bram did not answer. He blew on his tea.
"If I don't take her the six," said Corin, "they take Lord Thorne. To Fallowfield. Two days' drive past the marshes, with a pass to visit, and a name on the pass, and the name is Cressida's." He looked down at his mug. "And Lyra will never see him again. Not properly. Not ever. And the shell box, with the cracked stone in it, by his bed. And the white chair by the window. And Mrs. Hobb." He stopped. "I don't know how to let that happen."
"And if you do take her the six?"
"Then she has them. The Lady. Six years of everything. Every name, every face. All the Keepers ever wanted kept." He turned the mug in his sore fingers. "And Tam says she keeps her word. And then she has me."
Bram drank his tea, slowly, and put the mug down on the bench.
"There's a thing they teach you," he said. "After you've been carrying a while. When you're old and grumpy and they think you can be trusted with it." He looked at Corin over the top of his spectacles. "Papers can be wrong."
Corin looked at him.
"That's all," said Bram. "That's the whole of it. Papers can be wrong. The Ministry thinks a paper is the truth, because they wrote it, and stamped it, and filed it. But a paper's only ink on a page, and ink can be smudged, and a stamp can be put on crooked, and a file can be put in the wrong drawer, and nobody ever finds it again." His big face was quite serious. "Your Lady on the dome knows that. That's what she's offering you. A mistake on a paper. A wrong date. A missing seal. It's not magic, lad. It's clerking." He snorted. "And she wants six years of your life for a bit of clerking."
"I don't have any clerks," said Corin. "I don't know anyone on the Council. I don't know Pembrook. I don't—"
He stopped.
He had stopped because something had come up into his head, out of nowhere, the way things sometimes did. A page in the Ledger. He did not need to look for it. He could see it. A thin pale young man with thick spectacles and ink on his fingers, in a cold basement, under a green-shaded lamp, holding up a thick dull book with a cover the color of porridge. REGULATIONS. BOOK ONE. And sliding a sheaf of papers out from inside the back cover, where they had been hidden. Fifteen sheets. Thin, cheap, printed forms. And at the bottom of every one, where there should have been a blank space for a supervisor to sign, there was a seal. Printed on. By mistake. In black.
Not a supervisor's seal.
The Archon's.
Form 88-C. Closure of File. A misprint, Inspector. Somebody at the press put the wrong plate in. The seal plate. They were meant to be pulped, all of them. I filled in the three-R and took it up to Supervisor Brightwater to sign. He was asleep. With his cat on his lap. And I didn't like to wake him.
Corin put his mug down on the bench, very slowly.
"Bram," he said. "When the Council makes an order for a ward. Somebody who can't look after himself. Like Lord Thorne. Does that order go through the Ministry of Records?"
Bram's caterpillar eyebrows went up.
"Everything goes through the Ministry of Records," he said. "Births. Deaths. Marriages. Debts. Orders. Every single paper anybody in this city ever signs ends up in a box in a basement with a number on it. That's what the Ministry is." He paused. "A ward order's a file like any other. Thorne, Aurelian. Ward of the Council. Removal to Fallowfield. It'll be sitting in a drawer on the second floor right now, waiting for Monday. With a clerk's number on it."
"A file," said Corin.
"A file."
"And a file can be closed."
Bram looked at him for a long, long moment.
Then he said, very quietly: "What've you got, lad?"
"Fifteen closure orders," said Corin. "Blank. With the Archon's own seal printed on the bottom. By mistake. Hidden in the back of a book of regulations in the Third Basement." He swallowed. "A clerk I know saved them from the pulp. He couldn't bear to throw them away."
Bram did not move.
He sat on his tall stool at the end of the workbench with his enormous red hands lying flat on the scarred wood, and his small gray eyes fixed on Corin's face over the top of his spectacles, and his big battered face had gone so still that it looked as though it had been carved out of the same old tree stump as the rest of him.
"Saints," he said at last. Very softly. "Saints and all their little fishes."
"Would it work?"
"Would it—" Bram let out a long breath through his broken nose. "Lad. A closure order with the Archon's seal on it closes anything. Anything at all. A debt. A trial. A case. A ward order. There's no clerk in that building who'd dare to open a file again once it's got that seal on the bottom saying closed. They'd sooner cut their own hands off." He shook his big head slowly. "It'd work. Saints help us. It'd work."
"Then—"
"But." Bram held up one huge finger. "But. Listen to me now. Listen hard." His voice had gone low and rough and urgent. "Those forms are the most dangerous pieces of paper in this city. More dangerous than your Ledgers. More dangerous than anything in that dome. Because the day one of them turns up with a name written on it, closing a file the Archon never closed, somebody upstairs is going to go looking for where it came from. And they will find the batch. And they will find the clerk who signed for the batch. And they will find the clerk who was supposed to send it back to be pulped, and didn't." He leaned forward. "Your friend in the Third Basement. What's his name?"
Corin did not answer.
Bram looked at him. And then he nodded, slowly, as if Corin had said something.
"Good," he said. "Don't tell me. Don't tell anyone." He sat back. "But you think about him, Corin. Before you do anything. You think about that boy, whoever he is, with his ink on his fingers and his book of regulations. Because if you use one of those papers, and it goes wrong, it won't be you they come for first. You're a harvester. You're useful. They'll want you whole." His face was grim. "It'll be him."
The lamp hissed. The stove ticked. In the frame on the wall, the half-sewn book turned slowly on its strings.
Corin thought about Toby. About a thin pale face, wet and pink and pleased with itself, under a sack in the rain on Mrs. Marta's step. About a half of a squashed bun. I saved you some. About a loud excited whisper in a dark lane. I did it, Inspector. I asked Faber.
I'm not brave. I'm not like you.
"I'll think about it," he said.
Bram looked at him for a moment longer. Then something that was almost a smile moved the corners of his wide mouth.
"You'll think about it," he said. "Vespera's got a lot to answer for."
He let Corin out by the front door at midnight.
On the step, with the cold coming in round them and the chain off and the bolts back, he stopped him, with one big hand on his arm.
"Your gloves," he said.
Corin looked down. His hands were bare. Sore and red and crusted with paste. The white gloves were on the stool in the back room, side by side, where he had laid them three hours ago.
Bram went and fetched them. He held them out.
"You'd leave your head here if it wasn't screwed on," he said. "Go home. Sleep. Write it all down first. All of it. The dome and the envelope and the old soldier and the lily in Three. Every word." He paused. "And don't buy any ink."
"I have ink," said Corin.
"You have ink," agreed Bram Holt.
And he shut the door, and Corin heard the bolts go across, one, two, and the key turn, and the chain go on. And then, through the thick wavy glass of the bow window, past the edge of the brown curtain, he saw the lamp in the back room go out.
The Note on the Door
He walked home through the sleeping city with his gloves on and his hands in his pockets and the cold of the night pressing on him like a weight.
It was very late. Later than he had ever been out, he thought, or at least later than he could remember, which was the same thing. The streets were empty. Not quiet, the way they had been quiet at seven, with the taverns full and the shutters glowing. Empty. The taverns had shut. The shutters were dark. The lamps burned blue on their posts at the corners, with their little caps of snow, and nothing moved under them at all. Not a cat. Not a cart. Not a man. The snow lay on the cobbles smooth and silver and hard, frozen now, with a crust on top that cracked under his boots like sugar on a pudding.
The sky was black and clear and blazing with stars. He had never seen so many. They went all the way from one side of the sky to the other, thick as frost, and in the middle of them, very high, the moon, small and white and hard as a coin.
He walked up from Quill Row to Pike Lane, and along Pike Lane to the bottom of Tanner's Steps, and up the Steps, and along the top of the hill toward Tallow Street. He did not need the Ledger. His feet knew the way. They always knew the way. He only had to let them.
On the way he thought.
He thought about a dome of glass with the stars showing through it, and a woman in violet by a fire with her necklace of colored lights. Bring me the six. He thought about a boy on a stool at the bottom of a stone shaft, turning a wheel, round and round, with nothing in his face. He thought about a cold round room full of ice, and a footman rubbing at his cuff. My mother sewed it. He thought about a gray envelope, and a pressed brown lily, and a scrap of torn black lace.
He thought about five shaking lines in a soft old book, and a blot in the middle of his sister's name.
HIS NAME IS CORIN. HE WONT REMEMBER ME. DONT TELL HIM.
An old soldier with a face like a walnut, holding him up by the collar on a doorstep in the rain. He's somebody they want to forget, Holt. So somebody had better remember.
He did not know who the old soldier was. He did not know why anybody would want to forget him. He was nobody. A harvester in a gray coat who could not remember what he had eaten for breakfast.
But he thought about it, all the way up the hill, with his breath going up in front of him white in the starlight. And he found that it did not frighten him the way he would have thought. It was more like the feeling of putting your hand into a coat you have not worn for a long time, and finding something in the pocket. Something you did not know was there. Something somebody put there for you, long ago, and never told you about.
Somebody had wanted him remembered. Enough to hammer on a door at two in the morning in the rain.
That was something.
Tallow Street was white and silent under the moon.
His own footprints were still there, from that morning. A single line of them, going down the middle of the street from the door of Number 14 toward the river. They had frozen hard in the snow, every one, sharp-edged and clear, like a line of little dark pools. Nobody else had walked on them. Other people had walked on Tallow Street since. There were other prints now, a great many, going up and down and across, the marks of a whole day of people going about their business. But none of them had stepped in his. They had all gone round.
He stood at the bottom of the street and looked at them for a moment. The first footprints of his life in the first snow of his life.
Then he walked up beside them, not in them, to the door.
The house was dark. The front door was on the latch, the way Mrs. Marta always left it for him, and he let himself in, quietly, and shut it behind him, and stood in the dark of the hall with the cold falling off his coat. The hall smelled of onions. Of course it did. Further down, at the end of the short dark passage, the purple door was shut, and there was no light under it.
He went up the stairs.
They creaked on every step. He went up slowly, in the dark, with one hand on the wall, counting. On the third landing his hand found the place on the plaster, low down, where the letters were scratched. WE REMEMBER. He did not need to see them. He touched them with his gloved fingertips as he passed, the way you touch the head of a dog in the dark to let it know you are there.
At the top, outside his own door, something was on the floor.
He nearly kicked it. He stopped just in time, with his boot an inch from it, and crouched, and felt for it in the dark.
A bowl. A heavy china bowl, on the bare boards, with a plate turned upside down over the top of it to keep it covered. And on top of the plate, something soft. A folded cloth.
He took out his little brass lamp and turned it up, and the small blue light bloomed in his hand.
The cloth was a dishcloth, folded square. On it there was a note, on a strip of brown paper, in purple ink, in a big firm hand.
Juno says you burned my stew. Here's some more. Don't burn it. — M.
And underneath, smaller, as if she had added it after, with the pen already going back into the pot:
It's cold. Eat it anyway.
Corin knelt on the bare boards at the top of the stairs, in the blue light of his lamp, and looked at it for a long time.
Then he took the plate off the bowl.
It was the stew. Brown and thick, with lumps of potato, and bits of onion, gone stiff and cold with a skin of fat on the top. There was a spoon in it, standing up. And a heel of bread, tucked in at the side.
He pulled off his right glove with his teeth. He took the spoon. And there on the floor at the top of the stairs, outside his own door, kneeling, in his coat, with the lamp beside him on the boards, he ate it.
It was cold. It was very cold. The fat was stiff, and the potato was solid, and the onion had gone soft and sweet and slippery.
It was the best thing he had ever tasted.
He knew it was the best, because he had nothing to compare it with. Because whatever he had tasted that morning was gone, burned to a blaze of white light in the middle of the Lantern Bridge, and this was the first time. The very first time, for him, in all the world. Salt, and onion, and the soft dull earthy taste of potato, and the dark brown taste of the gravy, which he could not have named, but which was the taste, he thought, of a small fierce old woman standing over a pot in a kitchen that smelled of onions for forty years.
Then you will taste it again, Malakor had said. For the first time.
He ate every bit of it. He wiped the bowl with the bread. He sat back on his heels when it was empty and found that his eyes were stinging, and he did not know why, and he did not mind.
"Thank you, Mrs. Marta," he said, to the dark stairs.
Nobody answered. Two floors below, behind her purple door, Mrs. Marta was asleep.
He stood up, with the empty bowl in one hand and the lamp in the other, and turned to his door.
The notes were there, round the frame, the way they always were. Strips of brown paper and the backs of old handbills, pinned to the wood with long hatpins with little glass beads on the ends. Purple ink. Mrs. Marta's big firm hand. Rent due Friday. Eat the stew. Your name is Corin. Top room. This one. He held the lamp up and looked at them, the way he had looked at them that morning, one by one, in the blue light.
And then he stopped.
There was a new one.
It was pinned at the very top of the frame, in the middle, over the door. Where you would see it the moment you came up the stairs, if you looked up. Where you could not possibly miss it.
It was not on brown paper. It was on cream. A small square of thick, fine, cream-colored paper, with a soft rough edge.
It was not written in purple ink. It was written in black.
And it was not pinned with one of Mrs. Marta's hatpins, with their little beads of blue and green and red glass. It was pinned with a long thin hatpin with a single round bead on the end. A white one. Milky white, like a pearl. Like a drop of cream.
Corin held the lamp up to it.
The writing was big and black and untidy, sloping hard to the right, pressed so deep into the paper that some of the strokes had cut through.
It was his own hand.
Or very nearly.
SELL THEM.
YOU NEVER READ THEM ANYWAY.
He stood quite still on the landing, in the cold, with the empty bowl in his hand.
He did not take it down at first. He stood and looked at it in the blue light of the lamp, the way you look at a spider on the wall when you are not sure yet whether it is alive. The black letters. The hard slope. The white bead, glinting.
Very nearly.
It was good. It was very good. If he had come up the stairs another night, a night when he was tired, a night when he had not spent three hours at a workbench learning the shape of his own hand from six books on a rag rug, he might have read it and believed it. The way he believed all the others. The way he believed every note on this door frame every single morning of his life, without asking, because he had no choice.
But tonight he had held Ledger One in his hands. He had seen what his own writing looked like when it was frightened. He had seen what it looked like when it was cold, and when it was crying, and when it was learning. He had watched it get steadier, page after page, year after year, until it became the hand he had now.
And this was not it. This was a copy. A careful copy, by somebody who had studied it. Somebody who had seen a great deal of it. Somebody who had perhaps held one of his old books, once, for a little while. Or found some of his writing somewhere. On a note. On a door frame.
The letters were all exactly the same size.
His never were.
He reached up, and pulled out the white-beaded hatpin, and took the cream paper down. It smelled, very faintly, of something sweet and white and heavy.
He opened his door.
The room was cold and dark and exactly as he had left it. He turned the lamp up and looked at everything. The narrow iron bed, with its thin blanket gone, because the blanket was round his neck. The crate beside it. The little square window, with the frost on the inside of the glass in long white feathers. The cracked mirror. The washstand, with the jug and its broken skin of ice.
The jar.
It was on the crate, where he had left it. BERTRAM ASHCOMBE. STEWARD. FORGIVE ME. The thin gray layer in the bottom, faintly shining.
But it had been moved.
Not much. An inch. Less. It had been on the left side of the crate this morning, close to the bed. It was in the middle now. As if somebody had picked it up, and looked at it, and read the label, and put it down again, carefully, nearly in the same place.
Nearly.
And on the windowsill, inside the room, under the little square window, there was a small heap of snow. A little drift, no bigger than a handful, melting slowly into a wet dark patch on the boards. As if the window had been opened, from outside, and shut again, and some of the snow on the sill had fallen in.
The latch was down. Somebody had closed it from inside.
And then gone out by the door.
And pinned a note to the frame, at the top, in the middle, where he could not miss it.
Corin stood in the middle of his small cold room for a long time with the lamp in his hand.
Then he put the cream-colored note down on the crate, next to Bertram's jar, and the white-beaded hatpin on top of it. He sat down on the edge of the narrow iron bed. He took the Ledger out of his coat. And he began to write.
He wrote it all. The Bourse, and the blue light on the snow. The kitchen door and the chalk mark over it. Tam, and the vault, and the racks of vials, and the labels on them. Voice of a sister, laughing. Dobbin on his stool. The trading floor and the chalkboards. FIRST SNOW. 6 gold. The room under the dome, and the Lady, and the cases, and Remy. Tull. The offer. Monday. The vial on the glove.
The ice house. Tam's cuff. The envelope.
Quill Row, and Bram, and the mark in the corner of the board that he had drawn fresh every morning for thirty years. V. K. U. Veritas Keeps Unbroken. The Keepers. The lily and the lace. They are not only his.
Six Ledgers on a rag rug. Five shaking lines. An old soldier with a white mustache. DONT TELL HIM.
A brown ghost of a lily in Ledger Three. She asked me my name. I told her.
Tide tables. Forty gold the set.
Fifteen forms in the back of REGULATIONS, BOOK ONE. Toby. Think about him, Corin. It'll be him.
Cold stew on the landing. The best thing he had ever tasted.
And last, at the bottom of the page, he wrote:
Somebody was in this room tonight. They came in by the window. They moved Bertram's jar. They pinned a note on my door, on cream paper, with a white hatpin, in my hand. NEARLY my hand. It said: SELL THEM. YOU NEVER READ THEM ANYWAY.
The letters were all the same size. Mine never are.
He stopped.
He looked at what he had written. At his own letters, big and black and untidy, going up and down across the page like a fence on a hill. No two of them the same.
Then he wrote one more line. Underneath. Pressing so hard that the pencil went through the paper, the way it always did.
I read them tonight.
I'm not selling them.
He closed the book and held it against his chest.
Outside the little square window, across the river, a long way off, a great bell struck once. Deep and slow. At no hour at all. Three quarters of an hour behind the rest of the world.
BONG.
And Corin Vane, who did not remember a single day of the last six years, lay down on his narrow iron bed in his coat, with his Ledger in his arms and his gloves still on, and for the first time in a long time, slept without dreaming.
