Chapter 15: Bram's Chalkboard, Part 4 of 5: Six Years of You
Six Years of You
Bram rolled back the rug.
He did it slowly, on his knees, with a grunt for every foot of it, because he was old and big and his knees, he said, were made of chalk and string. Under the rug the floor was stone. Big square flagstones, gray and worn smooth, with lines of black dirt in the cracks between them. And one of them, near the stove, under the corner of the workbench, had an iron ring set into it. A small one. Lying flat in a little hollow cut to fit it, so that it did not stick up, and so that you would never have seen it under the rug, or even with the rug rolled back, unless you knew to look.
Bram got his thick fingers under the ring and lifted it. Then he braced his feet, and pulled.
The stone came up. Slowly. With a long gritty scrape. And under it there was a hole in the floor, square and dark and not very deep, about as deep as Corin's arm was long. And in the hole there was a box.
It was iron. Black, and plain, and a little rusty at the corners, with a heavy padlock through a hasp on the front. Bram reached down into the hole and lifted it out with both hands, breathing hard, and set it on the floor between them, and took a key from round his neck, on a string, under his shirt, where the spectacles hung outside. He unlocked the padlock. He lifted the lid.
Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, there were six books.
They lay in two rows of three, side by side, very neat, each in its own wrapping of brown oilcloth tied with string. Bram lifted them out one at a time and laid them on the rag rug in a row, and untied the strings, and folded back the oilcloth, and there they were.
Six Ledgers.
They were all the same, and all different. The same size. The same shape. The same dark brown leather. The same small rectangle pressed into each cover, low down. GROUNDING LEDGER. B. HOLT, STATIONER. LOWER DOCKS. And under each one, a number. I. II. III. IV. V. VI.
But they were all different, the way six brothers are different. The sixth one was the newest, and it still had some of its shine, though it was swollen and soft at the corners, and the spine was cracked in a long white line, and there were dark stains on the cover from rain, or from something worse. The fifth was older. The fourth was older still. And the first one, at the end of the row, was so old and so worn that it hardly looked like a book anymore. Its leather had gone soft and pale and furry, like the skin of a very old man's hand. Its corners were rounded right off. Its spine had split, and been mended with a strip of new leather, and the new leather had split too, and been mended again. It had swollen, the way a book swells when it has been wet many times and dried again, so that it would not lie flat, and its pages had gone gray and soft at the edges, and some of them had come loose and been tucked back in, so that they stuck out at the top and the bottom, like feathers.
Corin knelt on the rag rug and looked at them.
Six years. That was what Bram had said, once, in the shop, a long time ago, on the first page of this Ledger. Six years of you. Six books, as thick as two fingers, packed so full of black untidy writing that it went through the paper.
He did not remember writing one word of it.
"Go on," said Bram quietly. "They're yours."
Corin picked up the first one.
It was lighter than he expected. That was the first thing. Lighter than the one in his coat. As if the years had worn the weight out of it, the way they wear the weight out of a bone. It was soft in his hands. Warm, almost. The leather gave under his fingers like cloth.
He opened it.
The first page said:
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin.
You were born in Oakhaven.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
You used to fear the dark.
It was the same. Word for word. The same five lines that were on the first page of the Ledger in his coat, and the one before that, and the one before that. He had copied them out six times, at the end of Bram's counter, with a pot of tea, grumbling about his handwriting.
But the hand was different.
It was his. He knew it at once. The same big black letters. The same hard slope to the right. The same way of pressing so deep that the pen cut the paper. But it was not the hand in his coat. This hand shook. Every letter of it shook. The lines went up and down like a fence on a hill, and the letters were not all the same size, and some of them were made wrong, and had been crossed out, and made again. The C of Corin had been written three times, one on top of another. The O of Oakhaven was not closed at the top. And in the middle of Elara, the pen had stopped, and made a blot, a big black spreading blot, as if the hand holding it had stopped dead in the middle of the word and could not go on for a long time.
It was the hand of a man who was very ill. Or very cold. Or very frightened.
Or all three.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
Then he looked at the line under it. Because there was a line under it, on the first page. A line that was not on the first page of the Ledger in his coat. He had not copied it. He had never copied it, in six books. He had left it out every time.
It was not in his hand at all.
It was written in pencil. Small and stiff and old-fashioned, all capitals, the way a man writes who learned his letters late and never quite trusted them. The pencil had been pressed so hard that it had shone.
HIS NAME IS CORIN. HE WONT REMEMBER ME. DONT TELL HIM. KEEP EVERYTHING HE WRITES. — D.
Corin's hands had gone cold on the soft leather.
"Who's D?" he said.
Bram was sitting back on his heels on the rag rug, with his big hands on his knees, watching him. He did not answer at once.
"I don't know," he said at last. "That's the honest truth, lad. I never knew his name. I asked. He wouldn't give it." He rubbed his broken nose. "An old soldier. Very old. Older than me, and I'm older than the river. A face like a walnut, all folds. And a white mustache, long, hanging down past his chin on both sides, like a walrus." He was quiet a moment. "He brought you in here. Six years ago. In the autumn. In the rain. At two in the morning. He hammered on my door till I came down in my nightshirt with the poker, and when I opened it there he was on my step, holding you up by the collar like a sack of wet washing."
Corin did not move.
"You were soaked through," said Bram. "Blue with cold. You'd been sleeping under the Lantern Bridge, he said, on the steps, for he didn't know how long. Weeks. And you'd got into something. A dead woman. A burner, in a doorway on Gutter Street, who'd died that night with everything still in her, and you'd gone and put your hands on her. Without knowing what you were. Without anyone to tell you." His voice was low and rough. "You had her Ash in you. All of it. Rattling round inside you like stones in a tin. You didn't know your own name. You didn't know hers. You kept talking in her voice. Asking for somebody called Mattie. Over and over. Mattie, where's Mattie, has anyone seen my Mattie."
The lamp hissed. The glue pot went blup.
"He sat you on that stool," said Bram. He pointed with one thick finger at the tall stool at the end of the workbench. "And he said, Make him a book. Just like that. Make him a book, Holt. A good one. Strong. And give him a pen. And when he's filled it, keep it, and make him another. And keep everything he writes. And I said, Who is he? And he looked at me with those old eyes, and he said, He's somebody they want to forget, Holt. So somebody had better remember." Bram breathed out. "And he took a stub of pencil out of his coat, and he wrote that. On the first page. Before you'd written anything. And then he went out into the rain. And I never saw him again."
"And then?"
"And then I made you a book. That night. Out of the best leather I had. And I sat you by the stove with a blanket round you and a pot of tea, and I put a pen in your hand." Bram nodded at the shaking letters on the page. "And that's what you wrote. The first thing. Before I'd even asked you to. You just took the pen and you wrote it, very slow, with your hand going all over the place, and your teeth chattering. Your name is Corin. You were born in Oakhaven. And then you started crying. And you didn't stop." He looked away, at the stove. "And before it was light I walked you down to Hook Lane, to Vespera. Because she was the only one I knew who could get a dead woman out of a living man."
Corin looked down at the page. At the five shaking lines, and the blot in the middle of his sister's name, and the stiff pencil capitals under them.
DONT TELL HIM.
"You didn't tell me," he said.
"No."
"For six years."
"For six years." Bram's face did not change. "He said not to. And you never asked. And I never knew if it would hurt you." He paused. "Tonight, the envelope said they are not only his. And I thought, well. If they're not only his, then it's not only my secret either. Is it."
Corin turned the page.
The second page was in his own hand. Still shaking. But less. The letters were a little steadier, a little more the same size. It was a list.
THINGS THAT ARE TRUE TODAY.
I am in a shop. It smells of paper. The man is called Holt. He is cross. He is kind.
A woman is in my head. Her name was Agnes. She wants Mattie. I don't know who Mattie is. I'm sorry, Agnes.
It is raining.
I am very cold.
He turned the page again. And again. And then he was turning them faster, the soft gray pages whispering under his gloved fingers, and the writing went past him in a long black stream. Getting steadier. Getting bigger. Getting more like his own. Names went past. Places. Dates. Vespera. Hook Lane. She has writing all over her hands. She got Agnes out. It took all night. I don't remember what Agnes was like anymore, only that she was there. Toby. Ministry. Third Basement. He is very nervous. He says I'm the best harvester he's ever seen. A jar. My first jar. A man on the docks. I took his Ash with the rod and it didn't come into me. It went into the jar. Malakor says I did it well. Mrs. Marta. Purple door. She says I have to eat.
Six years. Going past under his fingers. A whole life. His life. And he did not remember one moment of it.
He stopped turning.
He had come to a page near the middle of the book, and something on it had caught his eye. Not the writing. A mark. Low down, in the corner. Drawn in ink, very small, by a hand that was not his.
The book. The eye.
And next to it, in his own big untidy writing, a single line.
Bram drew this here. He says if I ever see it on a door, or a wall, or a scrap of paper, it means a friend has been there. He says not to tell anyone. I won't. I'll forget. That's the best way to keep a secret.
Corin sat on the rag rug and read it three times.
Then he looked up at Bram.
The old man had not moved. He was still sitting back on his heels, with his hands on his knees and his half-moon spectacles hanging on their string, and his big red battered face was very still. But his small gray eyes were wet.
"You told me," said Corin. "Six years ago. You told me what it meant."
"I did," said Bram. "And you forgot. Like you said you would." He wiped his nose on the back of his huge hand. "And every morning since, I've drawn it fresh on that board. In the corner. Where you'd see it, if you looked." He gave a short, rough laugh, like a cough. "You never looked. Six years. Not once. Not until the morning you started Seven."
Corin closed the first Ledger, gently, and held it on his knee.
The lamp hissed. The stove ticked. On the workbench under the lamp, between the iron dish of ash and the bone folder, the pressed lily lay flat and brown and faintly sweet, and the scrap of black lace lay beside it, small and torn.
He looked at the row of books on the rug. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.
And he thought about a woman in a dark cloak, small and polite, looking at the chalkboard and smiling. Of course not, Mister Holt. My mistake. And about a woman in violet by a fire under a dome of glass, holding out a vial on a gray glove. Bring me the six.
One of them wanted to burn them.
One of them wanted to read them.
And neither of them, he thought, had once wondered what he wanted.
"Bram," he said. "The note said not the usual place."
"It did."
"Then where?"
Bram Holt looked at the six old books lying on his rug. Then he looked round the little crowded back room. At the press, with its stack of new books squeezed between its boards, drying. At the frame on the wall, with the half-sewn book hanging in it by its spine. At the shelves over the bench, crammed from end to end with paper and leather and boards and thread.
And slowly, his big red face began to change. The caterpillar eyebrows went up. The broken nose wrinkled. And the corners of his wide mouth, under it, began to curl.
"Where do you hide a leaf?" he said.
Corin looked at him.
"In a forest," said Bram Holt. He got up off the floor, with a groan and a crack of both knees, and stood over the row of Ledgers with his hands on his hips. "And where do you hide a book, lad? Six of them? With every lily and every dome in the city looking for them?" He jerked his chin at the shelves, the press, the frame, the piles of paper going up to the ceiling. "In a bookbinder's shop. On the shelf. In the window. With a price on them so high that nobody in their right mind would ever buy them." He rubbed his enormous hands together. "Put the kettle on, Corin. And take your gloves off. You're going to learn to sew."
Tide Tables
They worked until nearly midnight.
Bram did not take the Ledgers apart. Corin had been afraid he would. He had sat on the rag rug with the first one held against his chest and watched the old man go to his shelves and come back with a knife, and his heart had gone cold, and he had said, "Don't cut them," before he knew he was going to say anything at all.
Bram had stopped, with the knife in his hand, and looked at him over his spectacles.
"Cut them?" he said. "Lad. I made them. I'd sooner cut my own hand off." He put the knife down on the bench. "No. We're not going to change what's inside. We're going to change what's outside. That's all a cover is. A coat for a book. And a book can wear more than one coat, same as a man."
So they made coats.
Bram cut boards, first. Thin, stiff boards of gray pasteboard, each one a little bigger than a Ledger, twelve of them, a front and a back for each book. He measured them with a brass rule and a sharp pencil and cut them with a long knife against a steel edge, one stroke each, clean and straight, and the gray board fell away under the blade with a soft dry sound like a sigh. Then he cut spines. Then he took down a roll of cloth from a high shelf. Cheap cloth, dull, the color of mud, the kind that clerks' account books were bound in, that nobody ever looked at twice. And he cut it into pieces, and laid them out on the bench in a row, and began to paste.
"Gloves off," he said to Corin. "I told you."
Corin looked at his hands.
"It's paper," said Bram, more gently. "Just paper, and paste, and cloth. It's not glass. It won't take anything from you. It'll only give you paper cuts." He held out a brush, fat and round, dripping with glue. "Paste goes on the board, not the cloth. Thin. Like butter on bread when you're poor. Go on."
Corin took off his white gloves, one finger at a time, and laid them on the stool, carefully, side by side. His hands felt very bare in the warm air. Very thin. He took the brush.
He was terrible at it.
He put on too much paste, and it oozed out of the edges when Bram pressed the cloth down, and ran onto the bench. He put on too little, and the cloth would not stick, and came up in a bubble. He got paste on his fingers, and then on the board, and then on his sleeve, and then, somehow, on the end of his nose. Bram did not laugh. He did not say anything at all, except thinner, and there, and now press, press, from the middle out, like smoothing a sheet, over and over, very patiently, in his deep rough voice. And slowly, Corin got better.
By the third board he could do it. By the fifth, he could do it without thinking. His hands seemed to know how. As if they had done something like it once, a long time ago, and were remembering it now, a little at a time, in the warmth.
Bram watched him paste the sixth board, and nodded.
"You've done that before," he said.
"I don't think so."
"Your hands have." Bram took the board from him and turned it over and looked at it, and set it on the pile. "Hands remember things heads don't. Ask any binder. Ask any harvester." He picked up a needle. "Now. Sewing."
The sewing was harder.
The new covers were not glued to the Ledgers. Bram would not do that. They were made like little coats, with the boards sewn into the cloth, and the cloth folded over at the edges into flaps, like the cuffs of a sleeve, so that each Ledger could slide inside its new cover and be held there, snug, and slide out again whenever anyone wanted it. It took a great deal of sewing. Small stitches, in strong brown thread, all along the folded edges of the cloth, through the cloth and round and back, through and round and back, with a curved needle as fine as a fishhook.
Corin pricked himself four times. The fourth time he bled on the cloth, a small dark spot, and stared at it in horror, and Bram looked at it and grunted and said, "Good. Every book should have a bit of the binder in it. Keeps it honest." And went on sewing.
They sewed side by side at the long bench, under the lamp, with the stove ticking in the corner and the glue pot going blup and the half-made book in its frame turning slowly on the wall. The old man's huge red scarred hands moved fast and sure, the curved needle flashing in and out, in and out, so quick that Corin could hardly see it. His own moved slowly, and badly, and stopped and started, and got tangled in the thread.
It was the most peaceful he had felt in a very long time.
He did not know why. He thought about it, sitting there with the needle in his fingers and the warm lamp on his face. Perhaps it was the smell, of paper and paste and leather. Perhaps it was the old man beside him, breathing heavily through his broken nose, saying nothing. Perhaps it was only that for the first time all day, all week, perhaps for six years, he was doing something with his hands that did not need him to remember anything at all.
You put the needle in. You pulled it through. You went round. You did it again.
Fold it small, he thought, for no reason. And keep it close.
When the first coat was finished, Bram took it from him, and looked at it under the lamp, and pulled at the stitches with his thumbnail. Two of them came loose. He sewed them up again without a word. Then he picked up a tiny brass tool from the bench, like a little hammer with letters on its face, and heated it on the stove, and pressed it into the mud-colored cloth of the spine. Once. Twice. Three times. Changing the letters each time.
When he lifted it away, there were words on the spine. Pressed into the cloth, in small dull square capitals, the way words are pressed on the spines of a thousand dull books that nobody ever reads.
HARBOUR TIDE TABLES. VOL. I.
Corin stared at it.
"Tide tables," he said.
"Tide tables." Bram looked very pleased with himself. "The dullest books ever printed. Columns of numbers. High water, low water, every day of the year, every year since the beginning of the world. The Harbour Office used to print a set every spring, for the ship captains. Nobody's bought one for thirty years. Not since Old Tide stopped and the captains all started using the bells at Saltmarsh instead." He slid the first Ledger, very gently, into its new mud-colored coat, and closed it, and held it up. It looked like nothing at all. A dull brown book with a dull brown spine. "I've got a whole shelf of real ones out in the shop. Dusty as a tomb. Nobody's touched them in years. I'll put these on the end."
"Won't someone notice? That there are six more?"
"Who? Nobody looks at tide tables." Bram set the book down. "And if they do, they'll open one, and see a lot of writing, and think it's numbers, and shut it again. Because that's what people do with tide tables." He snorted. "Mind you, after today, with Old Tide going again, perhaps a few captains will want them back." He picked up the second Ledger. "I'll put the price up. Forty gold the set. Nobody'll pay forty gold for tide tables. Not even a fool."
While Bram pressed the letters into the second spine and the third, Corin sat with Ledger Three open on his knee.
He had not meant to read it. He had picked it up to hand it to Bram, and it had fallen open in his hands, the way old books do, at a place where it had been opened many times before. And he had looked down. And he had kept looking.
It was not the writing that stopped him. The writing was ordinary. His own big untidy hand, steadier now, three years steadier, going across the page in a long black stream. A day, like all the other days. A death on the docks. A jar. Rent. Toby. Ink.
It was what was between the pages.
The book had fallen open there because there was something in it. Something flat and thin, pressed between two pages, near the middle, so long ago and so hard that it had left a mark on both of them. A ghost. A shape, brown and faint, stained into the gray paper on either side, like the shadow a leaf leaves on a stone when it has lain there all winter.
Six long pointed petals, opened out in a star. A long thin stem. One narrow leaf.
The flower itself was gone. Crumbled away to dust, long ago. There were only a few brown flakes of it left, caught in the fold of the spine. But the shape was there, stained into the paper. And the smell. Very faint. So faint that he would never have known it, if he had not smelled the other one, on Bram's bench, an hour ago.
Sweet. White. Heavy.
And under the brown ghost of the lily, in his own hand, three years ago, there was a line.
A lady on the Lantern Bridge tonight. In white. Very kind. She asked me my name. I told her. She said it was a good name and I should keep it safe. She gave me a flower from her hat. A lily. I've put it in here so I don't forget her.
I wonder who she was.
Corin sat on the stool with the book open on his knee, and looked at it, and did not breathe.
She asked me my name.
Three years ago. On the Lantern Bridge. At night. A lady in white.
I told her.
"Bram," he said.
It came out very strange. Bram looked up from the press, with the hot brass letters in his hand, and saw his face, and put the letters down on the stove at once, and came.
He looked at the page for a long time, over Corin's shoulder. At the brown ghost of the lily. At the line underneath. He did not say anything. He breathed through his broken nose, slowly, in and out, and Corin could feel the warmth of his big body standing close behind him, solid as a wall.
"Three years," said Bram at last, very low. "Three years she's known your name."
"She asked me," said Corin. "And I told her. And then I forgot." He closed his eyes. "And she didn't."
Bram put one enormous hand on his shoulder. Just for a moment. Heavy and warm. Then he took it away, and reached down past him, and very gently took Ledger Three out of his hands, and closed it, with the brown ghost of the lily still inside it, and slid it into its new mud-colored coat.
HARBOUR TIDE TABLES. VOL. III.
"That's why," he said. "That's why she wants them, lad. Not to read them. She knows what she did. She doesn't need to read about it." He set the book down on the pile, on top of the other two, very carefully, the way you set down something that might break. "She wants them so that nobody else ever can."
