Chapter 15: Bram's Chalkboard, Part 3 of 5: Quill Row
Quill Row
The Lower Docks at night, after the first snow, were not like the Lower Docks in his Ledger.
The Ledger said they were loud. It said they smelled of fish and tar and smoke and the river, and that the lanes were so full of carts and barrows and dockers and sailors and girls selling hot chestnuts that you could not walk ten steps without someone treading on your feet. It said the lamps were blue and the cobbles were black and slippery and the gutters ran with things you did not want to look at.
Tonight they were quiet.
The snow had done it. It lay on everything, deep and soft and silver under the moon, and it had swallowed the noise the way a blanket swallows a cough. The carts had gone home. The barrows were covered with sacking and left in doorways, humped and white, like sleeping animals. The dockers were in the taverns, and the taverns had their doors shut against the cold, and the light and the noise came out of them only in little warm yellow cracks round the edges of the shutters. Here and there, someone had shoveled a path from a door to the middle of the lane, and the paths lay black and narrow in the white, like lines drawn with a ruler. Everywhere else, the snow was smooth.
The lamps were still blue. They stood at every corner on their iron posts, with snow on their tops, and their light lay on the silver snow in round blue pools. Corin walked from one pool to the next, and between them, in the dark, his boots crunched in the snow, and his breath went up white, and nobody else was there.
Once, a cat crossed the lane in front of him. A thin gray cat, picking its way through the snow very carefully, lifting each paw high and shaking it, the way you would shake water off your fingers. It stopped in a pool of blue light and looked at him with flat green eyes. Then it went on.
Once, somewhere down by the river, a ship's bell rang. Twice. Slow. And then, much further off, across the water, as if it were answering, a deep slow BONG, that went on and on, and died away.
Old Tide. Striking something. Some hour that was not the hour.
Corin stood still in the snow and listened to it until it had gone. Then he walked on.
Quill Row was a short, narrow, crooked street that ran down from Pike Lane toward the river, and it was all shops. Small ones. Old ones. Leaning together on both sides of the cobbles like old men on a bench, with their upper floors bulging out over the street on wooden beams, so that at the top they nearly touched. A pen-maker. A seal-cutter. A printer, with a press in the window, black and iron and still. A shop that sold maps. A shop that sold nothing but string. A pie shop, shut, with its shutters up, but with the smell of baked pastry and hot gravy still hanging in the cold air outside it, so strong and so good that Corin's empty stomach turned over again.
And next to the pie shop, with a bow window of thick, old, wavy glass that bulged out over the cobbles like the belly of a fat man, there was the stationer's.
He knew it. Not from the Ledger. From somewhere else. From his feet. His feet had walked him here a hundred times, the Ledger said, without his meaning to, and they knew the way. They stopped by themselves, in front of the bow window, and stood there in the snow, and waited for him to catch up.
The window was dark. Behind the thick greenish glass he could just see the shapes of things. Stacks of paper. Rows of pens in a tray. Bottles of ink, standing on a shelf, with the moonlight in them. A big leather-bound book, open on a stand, with its pages turned to the street, blank.
Over the window, on a wooden sign, there were tall gold letters, with snow along their tops.
B. HOLT. STATIONER & BOOKBINDER. EST.
And then a space, where the date should have been. Painted over. Very neatly. In black.
The shop was shut. The door had its blind down, and a card hanging in the window of it, on a string, with CLOSED written on it in big square chalk letters. There was no light in the front.
But at the back, through the wavy glass of the window, past the dark shop, Corin could see a door standing a little open. And through the door, very faint, a glow. Yellow. Warm. A lamp, in a back room. And on the snow in front of the shop, coming out from under the door, there was a narrow line of the same yellow light, lying across the silver like a thread.
He knocked.
Nothing happened.
He knocked again. Harder. The blind on the door shivered.
From somewhere inside, at the back, a voice. Deep and rough and cross.
"Shut! Can't you read? It says shut! On the card! In letters as big as your head! Come back in the morning!"
"Bram," said Corin. "It's me."
There was a silence.
Then footsteps. Heavy ones. Slow. Coming through the dark shop toward the door. A bolt went back. Another. A key turned. The door opened, the width of a hand, and the shop bell over it gave a single small cracked clink, like a spoon dropped in a saucer, and a face looked out round the edge of it.
It was a big face. Broad and red and old, with a big red nose that had been broken at least twice and pushed back into place by someone who was not a doctor. Over the nose, two bushy gray eyebrows grew together in the middle, like two caterpillars meeting, and under them two small gray eyes looked out at Corin over the top of a pair of half-moon spectacles, very sharp. The top of the head was bald and shiny, with a fringe of white hair round the sides sticking out in tufts. There was a pencil behind each ear.
The Ledger said trust him. Corin had read it that morning. In pencil, very small, on the back of the second page.
"You," said Bram Holt. "It's half past seven at night. It's snowing. It's colder than a dead man's feet. And you're standing on my step in your shirtsleeves."
"I'm not in my shirtsleeves."
"You will be. You always leave your coat somewhere." The small sharp eyes went over him, top to bottom. The clean collar. The white gloves. The snow on his boots up to the ankle. The way he was standing, a little bent, as if he were holding something inside his coat against the cold. "What've you done?"
"Nothing."
"You've done something. You've got a face on you like a boy who's broken a window." Bram's eyes narrowed. "And you've been on the Hill. That's a Hill collar. Somebody's put a Hill collar on you. Nobody on the Lower Docks owns a collar like that." He sniffed. "And you smell of wine. And woodsmoke. And…" He sniffed again, and his big face changed. "And the Bourse."
"Yes," said Corin.
He did not explain. He took his right hand out of his pocket, and reached inside his coat, on the right side, and took out the small square gray envelope with the black wax seal. He held it up, in the line of yellow light from the back of the shop.
So that Bram could see the front of it. B. H. BY HAND. TONIGHT.
And then turned it over, so that he could see the back. The black wax. The tiny mark, pressed into it. The book. The eye.
Bram Holt did not move.
He stood in the narrow gap of his door, with the snow beginning to blow in round his feet, and looked at the envelope. He looked at it for a long, long time. And while he looked, the color went out of his big red face, slowly, the way the color goes out of the sky after the sun has set, until his broken nose stood out on it, dark, like a bruise.
Then his eyes came up from the envelope to Corin's face.
"Who gave you that?" he said. Very quietly. He had not said anything quietly before.
"A footman. At the Bourse. He said it came down the line."
Bram's caterpillar eyebrows went up, and then down.
"Did he," he said.
He looked past Corin. Up Quill Row, to the left, toward Pike Lane. Down Quill Row, to the right, toward the river. At the snow, smooth and silver and empty under the blue lamps, with one line of footprints in it, coming down the middle of the street from Pike Lane to his door.
Then he opened the door all the way.
"Get in," he said. "Get in, get in, get in. Quick. And stamp your feet on the mat, I'm not having snow on my floor. And don't touch the paper."
The shop was dark and warm and crowded, and it smelled the way the Ledger said it smelled, and the way his body seemed to remember, all at once, the moment he stepped through the door. Paper. That was the main smell. Thousands of sheets of it. Then leather, rich and dark and oily. Then glue, sharp and sweet and a little rotten, from a black iron pot on the stove in the back room. Then ink. Then wood shavings, and beeswax, and dust, and, very faint, pipe smoke.
He stood just inside the door, on the mat, stamping his feet, and breathed it in. And something in his chest that had been held tight for the whole of that long, long day, since the window, since the snow, since the stew he could no longer taste, let go. Just a little. Like a knot coming loose.
Bram was locking the door behind him. Two bolts. A key. A chain, which Corin had not known was there. Then he pulled the blind down further, all the way, so that its bottom edge touched the sill. Then he went to the bow window and drew a thick brown curtain across it, on a brass rail, that Corin had not known was there either, and the moonlight from the street went out, and the shop was dark except for the yellow glow from the back room, coming through the half-open door.
In that glow, on the wall at the end of the counter, beside the door to the back room, Corin could see the chalkboard.
It was as big as a door, in a heavy wooden frame. It was covered in chalk writing, white and yellow, in big square heavy letters. Names, and marks, and numbers. And one name, near the top, with a long, long column of marks under it, going down almost to the bottom.
VANE.
Ink, black. 1 bottle. Ink, black. 1 bottle. Ink, black. 1 bottle—
Each one crossed through with a small neat chalk circle and a line. And at the bottom, in letters twice as big, underlined three times:
YOU HAVE INK. — B.
And under that, newer, in the same square hand: LEDGER VII. ON ACCOUNT.
Corin walked down the length of the dark counter toward it, slowly, and stopped in front of it.
He was not looking at his own name.
He was looking at the bottom corner of the board. Below all the names and the marks and the numbers. Low down, where you would never look unless you were looking for something. Where a child might have drawn, or a man leaning on the counter, idly, waiting for his change.
A tiny chalk drawing. No bigger than his thumbnail. Very neat.
An open book. And above it, an eye. Open.
Customer drew it, Bram had said. The Ledger said so. Months ago. Keep meaning to rub it out.
Corin turned round.
Bram was standing behind him, in the dark of the shop, in his old stained leather apron, with his enormous scarred red hands hanging at his sides. He was not looking at Corin. He was looking past him. At the little chalk drawing in the corner of the board.
"You didn't rub it out," said Corin.
"No," said Bram.
"That was months ago. The Ledger says."
"It was six years ago," said Bram Holt. "The day you first walked in here." He took the half-moon spectacles off his nose, slowly, and let them hang on their string on his chest. Without them, his small gray eyes looked older, and more tired, and much less cross. "I drew it fresh that morning. I draw it fresh every morning. Have done for thirty years." He held out one huge hand, palm up. "Give me the envelope, Corin. And come into the back. I think it's time I told you what your old friend Bram has been keeping under his floor."
The Envelope
The back room was where Bram made things.
It was small and square and warm, and it was very full, the way the Tide Hall bench had been full, every tool and every thing in its own place. The stove was in the corner, black and squat, with the iron pot of glue on top of it, bubbling very slowly, blup, blup, like a sleepy toad. Along one wall there was a long workbench, scarred and stained with a hundred years of ink and glue and knife cuts, and on it lay the tools of a bookbinder. Bone folders, smooth and yellow as old teeth. Awls. Knives with short curved blades. Fat round brushes for the glue. Spools of linen thread, gray and white and brown. Squares of leather, brown and red and black and green, stacked in a pile like the pages of a book. A wooden frame, upright, with strings stretched across it, and half a book hanging in it by its spine, its pages sewn and its cover not yet on, like a bird with no feathers.
In the middle of the room there was a press. A great iron press, as tall as Corin's chest, with a big iron wheel on top like the wheel of a ship, and two flat heavy boards underneath, and between the boards, squeezed tight, a stack of new books, drying. The press smelled of iron and oil. The books smelled of glue.
And on the floor, under everything, there was a rug. An old rag rug, made of strips of cloth woven together, faded to no color at all, and worn through in a path from the door to the stove.
Bram sat down at the end of the workbench, on a tall stool, and put the gray envelope down on the wood in front of him, under the lamp.
He did not open it at once. He sat and looked at it, with his enormous hands flat on the bench on either side of it. Then he put his half-moon spectacles back on his nose. Then he took them off again. Then he reached for a bone folder, a long smooth yellow one, and turned the envelope over, and slid the thin end of the folder under the edge of the flap, beside the black wax. Very carefully. Not breaking the seal. Lifting the paper round it, so that the wax came away whole, still stuck to the flap, with the tiny book and the tiny eye pressed into it, unbroken.
"You never break the mark," he said, without looking up. "Never. Not if you can help it. That's the first thing they teach you."
"Who?"
Bram did not answer. He opened the flap. He tipped the envelope up, gently, over the bench.
Two things fell out.
The first was a flower.
It had been pressed. Flattened, very carefully, between two sheets of paper, under something heavy, for a long time, until there was no thickness left in it at all. It lay on the scarred wood of the workbench under the lamp like a picture of a flower drawn on brown paper. Six long pointed petals, opened out flat in a star. A long thin stem, with one narrow leaf. And in the middle, very faint, the little stalks that stood up from the heart of it, pressed flat too, with tiny brown dots on their tips.
It had been white once. Corin could see that. Here and there, at the very edges of the petals, there was still a trace of it, a thin line of creamy white, like the edge of a fingernail. But the rest had gone the soft, pale, papery brown of an old letter, or a dead leaf in the spring.
And it still smelled.
Very faintly. So faintly that he had to lean close to the bench to be sure. But it was there. Sweet and white and heavy.
He did not need the Ledger for that one either. Not anymore.
"A lily," he said.
"A lily," said Bram. He was looking at it with his heavy gray brows drawn right down, so that they made one line across his face.
The second thing was a scrap of cloth.
It was very small. No bigger than the end of Corin's little finger. Black. And it was not ordinary cloth. It was lace. Fine black lace, so fine that the threads of it were like the threads of a spider's web, in a pattern of tiny flowers and leaves, and one edge of it was straight and neat, and the other three edges were ragged, as if it had been torn, very quickly, from something bigger.
Corin looked at it for a long moment.
A black lace fan. Opening and closing, slowly, in a long white hand, by a fire, under a dome of glass.
"Oh," he said.
Bram looked up at him over the bench. "You know it?"
"I've just come from her."
Bram's caterpillar eyebrows went up, very slowly, all the way up his forehead, until they nearly met the shiny bald top of his head. Then they came down again.
"Well," he said. "Well, well, well."
He tipped the envelope up again, and shook it, gently. And a third thing slid out, that had been caught inside, against the back.
A slip of paper. Gray, like the envelope. Cheap and rough. Folded once.
Bram unfolded it with the end of his bone folder, not with his fingers, and laid it flat on the bench under the lamp, between the lily and the lace. And they both bent over it.
The writing on it was small and square and careful. The same capitals as the front of the envelope.
V. K. U.
The lily has asked for the six. So has the dome. Neither shall have them.
Move them before Sunday. Not to the usual place. The usual place is known.
They are not only his.
And under that, very small, instead of a name, the mark. Drawn in ink. The open book. The eye.
For a long time, neither of them said anything.
The glue pot went blup on the stove. The lamp hissed very softly. In the frame against the wall, the half-sewn book hung by its spine and turned, a little, in the warm air from the stove, and turned back.
"The six," said Corin. "That's my Ledgers. One to six."
"Yes."
"In your strongbox. Under the floor."
Bram did not answer. But his eyes went, once, very quickly, to the faded rag rug on the floor of the little back room. To a place near the stove, under the corner of the workbench, where the rug lay a little flatter than anywhere else. Then they came back to the slip of gray paper.
"The usual place is known," he read aloud, very quietly. "Well. She would know, wouldn't she. She knows where everything is." His big red face twisted. "Her girl came in here last week. At closing. In a dark cloak, with the hood up, small, very polite. Said she'd been sent to fetch Mister Vane's old books. And I said I'd never heard of any Mister Vane." He snorted. "And she looked at the chalkboard. Right at it. Right at your name, VANE, a foot high, and all those bottles of ink. And she smiled. And she said, Of course not, Mister Holt. My mistake. And she went away."
"That was Cressida's girl," said Corin. "The lily one."
"I know whose she was. I could smell her from the back room." Bram picked up the pressed lily on the end of his bone folder, very carefully, and looked at it, and put it down again. "And I thought, there, Bram, you've done it now. You've told her as plain as if you'd drawn her a map." He shook his head. "And now the dome wants them too."
"The dome is Lady Vivienne," said Corin. "Isn't it."
"The dome is Lady Vivienne. The lily is your Councillor Thorne. Everybody who matters in this city is a flower or a piece of a building or a color, to us. It's safer. You can't say a name by accident if you never say it at all." Bram tapped the black lace with the end of the folder. "And somebody tore this off her fan. Tonight. Or this afternoon. To tell me she'd asked. Somebody close enough to her to get their fingers on her fan."
Remy, thought Corin. Or Tam. And then, after a moment, coldly: Or she tore it off herself, and handed it to Tam, and smiled.
He did not know which. He did not think Bram knew either.
"She offered me something," he said. "For them. The six."
Bram looked up.
Corin told him. Not all of it. Not the vial. He did not know how to tell anybody about the vial. But the rest. Lord Thorne and the white chair by the window. Cressida's letter. The House of Rest at Fallowfield. Monday at noon. Lord Pembrook and his debts and his port. A mistake on a paper, a missing seal, three months, four. Lyra, in the spring, of age.
Bring me the six by Sunday midnight.
Bram listened without saying a word. He sat on his tall stool with his hands flat on the bench and his eyes on Corin's face, and when Corin had finished he did not say anything for a long time.
Then he took his spectacles off again, and rubbed his eyes with the heel of one enormous hand.
"And you came here," he said. "Instead of going straight to the strongbox, and lifting the floor, and taking them, and walking back up the hill. Which you could have done. Tonight. I wouldn't have stopped you. They're yours."
"Tam said bring the envelope."
"Tam said." Bram put his spectacles back on. "Tam's a good boy. His mother was the best of us. She copied more in nine years on her knees than I've bound in thirty." He looked at the slip of gray paper. "They are not only his."
"What does that mean?"
"It means what it says." Bram looked up at him. And there was something in his face that Corin had never seen there. Something careful. Something that was trying to decide how much to say, and how fast, and whether it would break anything. "Sit down, Corin. On that stool. Over there. Don't touch the glue."
Corin sat down.
"V. K. U.," said Bram Holt. He said the three letters slowly, one at a time, like a man laying three stones on a wall. "Veritas Keeps Unbroken. It's old words. Older than the Ministry. They used to be cut in the stone over a door, in the old Archive, on the floor that isn't there anymore. Some clerk put them up, hundreds of years ago, when they still let clerks put things up. The truth keeps. The truth stays whole. Whatever they do to it." He breathed out through his broken nose. "And then they closed that floor. And they chiseled the words off the stone, the way they chisel everything off. But somebody had written them down first. Somebody always does."
"The Keepers," said Corin. He did not know where the word came from. It was simply there, in his mouth, as if it had been waiting.
Bram looked at him very sharply.
"The Keepers of the Unbroken," he said, after a moment. "Yes. That's what we call ourselves. When we call ourselves anything, which isn't often." He lifted one big hand off the bench and let it fall again. "We're nobody. That's the point. Clerks. Binders. Printers. Porters. Cleaners, like Tam's mother. Night men on the Ministry doors. Women who wash the aprons. Anybody whose hands touch paper that somebody upstairs wants gone. When the Ministry sends a file to be pulped, or a book to be burned, or a ledger to be corrected—" he said the word as if it tasted bad "—one of us reads it first. And if it's true, and it matters, and they want it gone, we copy it. Small. On whatever's to hand. An apron. A shirt cuff. The inside of a boot. And we pass it on. Hand to hand. And somewhere, at the end of all the hands, somebody keeps it."
"Where?"
Bram smiled. It was the first time Corin had ever seen him smile. It made his big red battered face look like a cliff with the sun on it.
"Nobody knows that," he said. "Not me. Not Tam. Not anybody who carries. That's the second thing they teach you."
He picked up the slip of gray paper with the end of the bone folder, and held it to the edge of the lamp, and watched it catch. The flame went up the gray paper, quick and yellow, and curled it, and blackened it, and he held it until the fire nearly reached the folder, and then he dropped it into an iron dish on the bench and watched it burn to nothing.
The lily and the lace he left where they were, on the scarred wood, under the lamp.
"And for six years," he said, watching the last of the ash curl in the dish, "you've been writing it down too. Without knowing. Everything they burn, you've been putting back. Out of dead men's heads. Out of the Ash. Every name. Every face. Every lie. In a big black untidy hand that goes through the paper." He looked up. "That's why they're not only yours, Corin. You've been one of us since the day you walked in here. You just never knew."
