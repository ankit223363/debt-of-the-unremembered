Chapter 15: Bram's Chalkboard, Part 1 of 5: The Kitchen Door
The Kitchen Door
The Memory Bourse stood at the top of Exchange Street, where the hill flattened out, and it was the biggest building Corin had ever seen that was not a church.
He came up Exchange Street in the dusk with the last of the snow drifting down round him, and he saw it at the top long before he got there. A great round building, made of pale stone, as round as a drum and as wide across as a whole street of houses. All the way round it, at the front, stood a ring of tall stone pillars, white with snow on their tops, like a row of giants wearing hats. And above the pillars, sitting on top of the whole building like a lid on a pot, there was a dome. A huge, smooth, round dome, sheathed in copper that had gone green with age, the soft dull green of old pennies left out in the rain. Snow lay on it in a thin white crust, thicker at the bottom where the curve flattened out, so that it looked like a great green egg that somebody had dusted with sugar.
The windows were lit.
They ran all the way round the building between the pillars, tall and arched, three rows of them, one above the other. And every one of them was full of blue light. Not yellow, like the oil lamps in Hook Lane. Not white, like candles. Blue. A cold, clear, shining blue, the color of the little lanterns on the bridge, but stronger. Much stronger. So strong that it came out through the glass and lay on the snow outside in long blue shapes, one under every window, and turned the falling flakes blue as they went past.
Corin stopped at the bottom of the great front steps and looked up at it.
Amnesiac lamps, said Malakor, very quietly, from inside his coat. Every one of them. Burning somebody's afternoon, somebody's song, somebody's first day at school. A pause. They light the whole building that way. Every night. It is how they show that they can.
People were coming down the steps. A great many people. Men in tall hats and long dark coats with fur collars, and ladies in cloaks, and clerks with ledgers under their arms, hurrying, with their heads down against the snow. The trading floor was closing. Corin could hear it, through the great open doors at the top of the steps. A roar of voices, like the sea, going slowly quiet. A bell ringing. A man shouting numbers, very fast, in a high cracked voice, and then stopping.
Nobody looked at him. A thin man in a gray Ministry coat, with snow on his shoulders and a clean collar and white cotton gloves, standing at the bottom of the steps. He might have been anybody. He might have been a clerk.
He did not go up the steps.
The kitchen door, the note had said. Not the front.
The kitchen door was round the back, down a lane so narrow and so steep that it was nearly a gutter.
It ran down the side of the great round building between the curved wall of the Bourse on one side and the high blank backs of a row of counting houses on the other, and it was dark, because the blue light did not reach down here. Only a little of it, spilling over the top of the wall from the windows high above, so that the snow in the lane was a dim, ghostly, bluish white. The lane was full of things. Barrels. Crates. Empty sacks, frozen stiff. A pile of cabbage leaves gone black with frost. A cart with no horse, its shafts resting on the ground, and in it a great heap of broken glass, sparkling faintly in the blue dark. Small glass. Thin glass. Corin looked at it as he went past, and saw that it was vials. Hundreds of them. Tiny glass vials, the size of a finger, broken and thrown away in a heap, like eggshells.
He did not look at them again.
Halfway down the lane there was a door in the curved wall. A plain wooden door, painted dark red, with an iron grating set into it at the height of a man's face. Light came out through the grating. Yellow light, this time. Warm, ordinary, kitchen-yellow light, and steam, and noise. The clatter of pans. A woman's voice, shouting at someone to mind the gravy. A smell of roasting meat so rich and so strong that Corin's stomach turned over inside him, all at once, and he remembered that he had eaten nothing since a bowl of stew he could no longer taste.
Over the door, on a bracket, hung a lantern. And under the lantern, on the wall, someone had chalked a small mark.
He had to go close to see it. It was very small, no bigger than his thumbnail, and it was half hidden by a patch of frost on the stone. But it was there.
An open book. And above it, an eye. Open.
He stood and looked at it for a long moment.
Then he took the note out of the back of his Ledger and looked at that, too, in the yellow light from the grating. The same mark. Drawn small, in ink, at the bottom of the paper. Ask for Tam at the kitchen door.
And somewhere in his Ledger, far back, on a page he had read that morning without stopping, there was a line about a chalkboard. A tiny drawing in the corner. An open book, and an eye. Bram says a customer drew it.
He knocked on the red door.
The grating slid open with a bang. A face looked out. A big, red, wet, angry face, under a white cap, with flour on its nose.
"What?" said the face.
"I've come to see Tam," said Corin. "Tam Hollin."
The face looked at him. At his gray coat, and his clean collar, and his white gloves. Its eyes narrowed.
"Ministry," it said.
"Yes."
"We don't want the Ministry in our kitchen. We've got a dinner for forty going up at seven and the sauce has split and the girl's dropped a whole tray of tarts on the floor." The face was going redder. "Go round the front like everybody else."
"I was told the kitchen door," said Corin. "Not the front."
The face stopped. Something changed in it. The angry red did not go away, but the eyes in the middle of it went very still, and then flicked, once, quickly, up and to the left. To the wall over the door. To the place where the small chalk mark was.
Then back to Corin.
"Wait," said the face. And the grating slid shut.
Corin waited.
He stood in the dim blue lane with the snow falling on him, very lightly now, a flake at a time, and the steam from the grating curling round him, warm and wet and smelling of meat. Somewhere far above him, high up on the great round wall, a window opened, and somebody laughed, a woman, a high bright tinkling laugh like glass breaking. And the window shut again.
He heard footsteps on the other side of the door. Quick ones. Then a bolt, and another bolt, and the red door opened.
A young man stood in the doorway.
He was not tall. He was slim and quick and narrow-shouldered, with a thin clever face and brown hair cut short and neat, and quick brown eyes that went over Corin from top to bottom in one glance, and then up and down the dark lane behind him, both ways, and then back to Corin's face. He wore a footman's livery. A long coat of deep plum-colored cloth, with silver buttons, very fine, and white stockings, and buckled shoes, and a white stock at his throat tied very neatly. But his sleeves were pushed up to the elbow, as if he had been helping in the kitchen, and there was a smear of something red on his cuff that might have been jam.
And his gloves were white cotton. Like Corin's. With the fingers worn thin.
"Mister Vane," said Tam Hollin.
He did not say hello. He did not say come in. He just stood in the doorway and looked at Corin, and Corin saw that his face was very pale under the kitchen heat, and that there were dark marks under his eyes, the way there had been under Lyra's that morning.
"You came," said Tam. "I didn't think you would. I thought you'd forget."
"I wrote it down."
Tam's mouth twitched. Very slightly. It might almost have been a smile.
"Course you did," he said. "Show me your hands."
Corin held them out. Tam looked at the white gloves, front and back. He took Corin's right hand in his own, very briefly, and turned it over and pressed his thumb on the palm, the way you might press on a loaf to see if it was baked, and let it go.
"They'll do," he said. "Just. They're thin at the ends. Don't touch anything with your fingertips. Not the glass. Not the walls. Not the tables. Not the banisters. Not the vials, specially not the vials. If someone offers you a glass of wine, hold it by the stem and don't drink it. If someone offers you their hand, bow." He stepped back from the doorway. "Come in. Quick. Mind the step, it's greasy. And don't look at Cook."
The kitchen of the Memory Bourse was as big as a church.
Corin had not expected that. He had expected a kitchen like Mrs. Marta's, a dark little room with a stove and a table and a smell of onions. This was something else. It was a huge, long, low, stone-vaulted room, deep under the round building, with a ceiling of curved brick arches held up on thick stone pillars, and it was full of fire and steam and noise and people. Along one whole wall, end to end, there were ovens and fires and ranges, a dozen of them, roaring and glowing red behind their iron doors, with copper pans on every one of them, bubbling and spitting and steaming. The copper shone. Everywhere. Pans and pots and kettles and molds and ladles, hundreds of them, hanging from racks over the long scrubbed tables, glowing red-gold in the firelight like a dragon's hoard.
And people. Cooks in white caps and aprons, shouting. Kitchen girls in brown, running. A boy turning a spit with a whole pig on it, his face red and running with sweat. A man in shirtsleeves beating eggs in a copper bowl so hard and so fast that his arm was a blur. Two footmen in plum, like Tam, carrying a great silver dish between them with a domed silver lid, very carefully, toward a flight of stone stairs at the far end. A girl on her knees by the door, crying, picking up broken tarts off the stone floor one by one and putting them in her apron.
Nobody looked at Corin. Not one of them. They looked at their pans, and their spoons, and their trays. They looked at the floor. They looked at the fires. They looked, it seemed to Corin, at everything in that great hot roaring room except the thin man in the gray coat who had just come in at the kitchen door with Tam Hollin.
It was not the way people do not look at you when they have not noticed you. It was the way people do not look at you when they have.
"They know," said Corin, very low.
"Course they know." Tam did not slow down. He went straight across the kitchen, between the long tables, quick and neat, and Corin followed close behind him. "Everybody down here knows everything. That's what below stairs is. Upstairs they don't know anything, because they never ask anybody who carries the plates." He ducked under a rack of hanging pans without looking. "Cook knows you're Ministry. Cook knows you're the harvester from the broadsheet, the one at the Ink Quarter. Cook knows I went out on Monday and came back with a note gone from my pocket. And Cook's not going to say one word about any of it to anybody upstairs, because twenty years ago the Bank took her husband's hands for a debt of six silver, and he's still standing at her table every day, because she won't let them turn him out."
The man in shirtsleeves beating the eggs did not look up. But the whisk in his hand, Corin saw, was strapped to his wrist with a leather band. And the hand at the end of the wrist was not moving at all. Only the arm.
"Here," said Tam.
He had stopped at the far end of the kitchen, beside the flight of stone stairs the silver dish had gone up. But he did not go up them. He turned, instead, to a small low door in the wall beside them, so low that a tall man would have had to bend double to go through it, and he took a key from his waistcoat pocket and unlocked it.
Behind it there were more stairs. Narrow ones. Going down.
"The Lady's rooms are up," said Tam. "Top of the house. Under the dome. But we're not going up the front way, and we're not going up the servants' way either. Too many people. We're going up the way the vials go." He looked at Corin. "Through the floor."
"Through what floor?"
Tam did not answer. He held the little door open, and a breath of air came up out of the dark below it, cool and dry and strange, smelling of dust and cold stone, and of something else that Corin did not know at first. Something sharp, and clean, and very faint, like the smell of the air just before a thunderstorm.
Then he did know it. He had smelled it in a hundred dead men's rooms. He had smelled it on his own hands, after.
Ash.
"The trading floor," said Tam Hollin quietly. "Where they keep what they sell."
Under the Trading Floor
The stairs went down a long way. Further than the kitchen. Further, Corin thought, than any cellar had a right to go. They were narrow and steep and made of smooth gray stone, and they turned as they went, the way the stair in the Tide Hall had turned, and every few steps there was a little iron lamp set into the wall with a blue stone burning in it, so that the light on the stairs was cold and blue and steady and cast no warm shadows at all.
Tam went first. He did not talk. He went down quickly and quietly in his buckled shoes, with one hand held a little out from the wall, not touching it, and Corin did the same.
At the bottom there was a door. An iron one, with no handle on the outside, only a keyhole, and a little brass plate at the height of a man's eyes with words cut into it.
STOCK. NO NAKED HANDS.
Tam unlocked it.
It was a vault.
That was the only word. A great round vault, as wide as the whole building above it, with a low curved stone ceiling held up on pillars, row after row of them, going away into the distance like the trunks of trees in a winter wood. And between the pillars there were racks.
Iron racks, tall and narrow, standing in long straight rows, like the shelves in a library. And on every shelf of every rack, side by side, in their hundreds and their thousands, there were vials.
They glowed.
All of them. Each one by itself, very softly. Small glass vials no longer than a finger, with silver stoppers, standing upright in little holes cut into the shelves, so close together that their light ran into one long line. Blue, most of them. Every shade of blue, from the pale clear blue of a winter sky to a blue so deep it was nearly black. But not only blue. Here and there, scattered among them, there were other colors. A warm gold. A rose. A green like moss. A soft gray that hardly glowed at all, like a coal going out. Row after row after row of them, rack after rack, going away between the pillars into the dimness, all glowing, all quite still, all quite silent, like a field of tiny lamps.
The air was cold and dry and smelled of stone and that sharp clean thunder-smell, much stronger now. And it was very quiet. The roar of the kitchen had gone. The roar of the trading floor had gone. There was only, very faint, coming from everywhere and nowhere, a sort of humming. A soft, high, thin humming, like the sound a wet finger makes going round the rim of a glass. It came from the vials. Corin was sure of it. It was the sound of all of them at once, each one too quiet to hear, all of them together like bees in a hive a long way off.
Do not listen too closely, said Malakor. If you listen too closely, you will start to hear words.
Corin stopped listening.
There were people in the vault. Not many. Clerks. A dozen of them, perhaps, in long gray coats and gray gloves, working among the racks with little lamps on poles. One was standing on a ladder, taking vials down from a high shelf one by one and laying them in a padded wooden tray. One was sitting at a high desk at the end of a row, with a great open ledger in front of him and a pen in his gloved hand, writing, slowly, very carefully. One was pasting little paper labels onto the bottoms of a tray of new vials, with a tiny brush, and holding each one up to the lamp to read it before she pasted it.
None of them looked up. They were too far away, or too busy, or too used to people going past.
Tam led him down the end of the nearest row, quickly, between two racks, with the glow of the vials on both sides of them and his own shadow and Corin's thrown long and blue across the stone floor. Corin did not mean to read the labels. He read them anyway. They were stuck to the shelves under each little row of vials, written in a small neat clerk's hand.
Summer evening, garden, with father. Clear. 5 years old.
Recipe, plum cake. Grandmother's. Complete.
Swimming. Full. River-taught.
First day, Low Dock School. Fair.
Voice of a sister, laughing. Poor (faded at edges).
He stopped reading.
He stopped, and stood quite still between the racks, with the vials glowing softly on both sides of him, and he found that he could not breathe properly. As if there were a band round his chest, pulling tighter.
Voice of a sister, laughing.
Tam had stopped too. He had turned round, a few steps ahead, and was looking at Corin's face. And whatever he saw in it made his own face change. Just for a moment. The quick, careful, watchful look went out of it, and something else came in. Something younger. Something tired and sorry.
"I know," he said, very quietly. "First time I came down here, I was sick in the corner. Over there." He tipped his head at a pillar. "They made me scrub it. With my hands. Without my gloves." He swallowed. "I couldn't feel anything in my fingers for a week. Just cold. Just… glass."
Corin made himself breathe.
"Where do they come from?" he said. "All of them?"
"Everywhere." Tam turned and went on, and Corin followed. "The intake hall at the Bank, mostly. The poor come in every morning and stand in the line and sell, a copper here and a silver there, and the Bank sends the best of it up here, to be sold on. That's what the Bourse is. A market. A market for the market." His voice was flat. "And the debt rooms. When people can't pay, and the Ministry takes it instead. That comes here too, the good stuff. And the dead." He paused. "The ones whose families sell what's left."
"And the Black Bank?"
Tam did not answer that one.
At the far side of the vault, set into the curved wall, there was a cage.
It was an iron cage, square, about as big as a wardrobe, with a floor of iron plates and walls of iron bars, and it hung from a thick chain that went straight up through a square hole in the stone ceiling and disappeared into the dark. Beside it, in the wall, there was a great iron wheel with a handle, as tall as a boy. And beside the wheel, sitting on a stool, there was a boy.
He was about ten. He had a thin pale face and enormous dark eyes, and he wore a gray coat much too big for him, and gray gloves, and he was sitting on the stool with his hands in his lap, perfectly still, looking at nothing. When Tam came up, he did not move. He did not look at them. He did not do anything at all.
"Up, Dobbin," said Tam gently. "The top. The Lady's floor. Two of us."
The boy got up off his stool, and put his gray-gloved hands on the handle of the great wheel, and began to turn it.
He turned it steadily, round and round, without stopping, without hurrying, without any look on his face at all. The chain began to move. Slowly, with a deep clank and rattle, it began to wind up out of the hole in the ceiling, and the cage rose an inch off the floor, and swung a little, and steadied.
Tam pulled open the cage door and stepped in, and Corin stepped in after him, and Tam pulled the door shut again, and the boy went on turning the wheel. The cage went up.
The vault fell away beneath them. Corin looked down through the iron floor and saw the rows of glowing racks, small and neat, getting smaller, and the clerks moving among them with their lamps on poles, and the boy at the wheel, his thin arms going round and round, looking up at nothing. Then the stone ceiling came down past them, close, so close that Corin could have touched it, and they were in the dark, going up through a square stone shaft with the chain rattling and clanking above their heads.
"That boy," said Corin.
"Dobbin." Tam did not look round. "The Bank had his mother. Last year. She owed for the rent, and they took it out of her in pieces, and then there weren't any pieces left." He watched the stone go past through the bars. "Dobbin was in the room. He was eight. He saw." A pause. "So he sold it. All of it. Everything about her. Every single thing, from the first time she held him to the last time she looked at him. To the Bank. For eleven silver. So he wouldn't have to remember."
The chain clanked over their heads.
"He's been very calm ever since," said Tam. "The Lady gave him a job, on the wheel. She says he's the best wheel-boy she's ever had. Never complains. Never gets tired. Never cries." His voice did not change at all. "Why would he? He hasn't got anything to cry about."
Corin did not say anything.
He thought of a little brass mouse on a bench, washing its face, over and over, for eleven years. A kind of wanting. He thought of a boy who had sold the one thing he had to make the hurt stop, and who would turn a wheel for the rest of his life, round and round, not knowing why there was a hole in him where something should have been.
He thought of his own Ledger. Of the first page. You had a younger sister named Elara.
Voice of a sister, laughing. Poor (faded at edges).
The shaft opened out.
It happened all at once. The stone walls fell away on every side, and there was light, a flood of blue light, and noise, and space. A huge space. The cage was rising up through a square hole in a great floor, and the floor was made of glass.
Corin looked down. He could not help it. Thick panes of greenish glass, each as big as a door, set in a web of iron, and through them, very dim, the glow of the racks in the vault below, like the lights of a town seen from a hill at night.
Then he looked up, and round, and he forgot about the glass.
They were coming up into the trading floor of the Memory Bourse. Into the middle of it. And it was round, and it was enormous, and it went up and up over his head into the great hollow of the copper dome, so high that the top of it was lost in a blue haze. All round the edge, between the tall arched windows, there were galleries, one above another, three of them, with iron railings, like the balconies in a theater. And on the walls between the galleries, from the floor nearly to the dome, there were boards.
Chalkboards.
Great black chalkboards, as tall as houses, a dozen of them, set all round the curved wall of the hall. And they were covered in writing. White chalk, and yellow, and here and there red. Columns of it. Rows of it. Names and numbers, written in big square letters that you could read from the middle of the floor. On narrow wooden platforms in front of each board, on long ladders, men in shirtsleeves were climbing up and down with sticks of chalk and wet sponges, rubbing out numbers and writing in new ones, very fast, while down below on the glass floor a few last traders in tall hats were standing about in little groups, looking up at the boards and talking in low voices.
Corin read the nearest board as the cage rose past it.
TODAY'S CLOSING.
CHILDHOOD. FIRST SNOW. Fair. — 6 gold. And beside it, in red, an arrow pointing up, and (was 3).
MOTHER'S VOICE, SINGING. Good. — 11 gold.
SWIMMING. Full. — 14 gold.
COURAGE. One night. Strong. — 9 gold.
OSTMARK. Fluent, reading & speaking. — 40 gold.
SWORDCRAFT. Master. 20 years. — 310 gold.
GRIEF. Removal. — Buyer paid 2 silver.
The cage went up past the board, and past the first gallery, and he lost sight of it.
"First snow's doubled," said Tam, beside him. Not looking at the board. He had not looked at it once. "Because of today. Every lady on the Hill woke up this morning and saw the snow and wanted to feel the way she felt the first time she saw it. When she was little. Before she got bored of it." He shrugged one plum-colored shoulder. "So they sent their maids down here with their purses. And somewhere down by the river there's a hundred children who don't know what snow is anymore. And tomorrow the price will drop again, because everyone's got one."
Corin thought of a window that morning, in a cold gray room at the top of a house on Tallow Street. Of a city gone white. Of a tiny six-armed star of ice touching the glass and melting.
He had not had a first snow to sell. He had only had today's.
He was glad, all at once, so fiercely that it surprised him, that nobody could buy that.
The cage went up past the second gallery, and the third, and into another shaft, dark and close, and the noise of the trading floor fell away below them. And as it went, Tam spoke again. Very low. Very fast. Without turning his head.
"Listen. We haven't got long. When this stops, there'll be a door, and she'll be on the other side of it, and after that you'll be on your own." His quick brown eyes flicked sideways at Corin, once, and away. "You don't know me. You've never met me. I'm a footman. I showed you up because I was told to. That's all."
"And the note?" said Corin. "In the gloves. With the eye."
Tam's jaw tightened.
"Not here," he said. "These walls have ears, and some of the ears are for sale." He paused. "After. When I take you down. If you come out."
"If?"
Tam did not answer. The cage was slowing. The chain clanked, and slowed, and clanked again. Above them, a square of warm yellow light was opening in the dark.
"She'll want something," said Tam, very quickly now. "She always does. Whatever she asks for, don't say yes. Don't say no either. Say you'll think about it."
Corin almost laughed. It came up in his chest, sudden and strange.
"That's what Vespera said."
"Then Vespera's got sense." The cage came up into the light, and stopped, with a jolt. Tam reached past him and pulled open the iron door, and stepped out, and was a footman again, all at once, straight-backed and blank-faced, with his hands at his sides. "This way, sir," he said, quite loudly, in a smooth flat voice like a polished plate. "The Lady is expecting you."
