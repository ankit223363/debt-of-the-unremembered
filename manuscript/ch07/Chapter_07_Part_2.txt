Chapter 7: The Ministry of Records, Part 2 of 5: Toby in the Dark
Toby in the Dark
The paper stairs went down a long way.
They were wide and shallow and worn into dips in the middle, made for men carrying heavy bundles on their backs, and they were lit by nothing at all except a gray glow from the yard behind him, which faded quickly, and then a red glow from somewhere ahead, which grew. The walls were rough stone, sweating with damp. The air got warmer as he went down instead of colder, and thicker, and it began to smell of coal and hot iron and oil. And the growl grew louder. The low, heavy, endless growl of something enormous breathing in its sleep.
At the bottom, the stairs opened into a long, low, brick-vaulted room, and the boiler was there.
It filled half the room. A great black iron belly, as big as a house, lying on its side on a bed of brick, with pipes coming out of it in every direction like the legs of a spider, going up into the ceiling, going off into the walls. It was riveted all over, thousands of iron rivets in long rows, and in the middle of its front there was a round iron door, half open, and through the gap Corin could see fire. Not flames. A deep, steady, roaring orange, like the inside of the sun. The heat came off it in waves that he could feel on his face from across the room.
A man in a leather apron, black from head to foot with coal dust, was shoveling coal through the half-open door. He did not look up. He's deaf, Wilkin had said. He shoveled and shoveled, slow and steady, and the fire roared and the boiler growled, and Corin walked past behind him, close against the far wall, and the man never turned his head.
At the far end of the room there was an iron door, and beside it a short flight of stone steps going up, with a gap of light at the top. And on the bottom step, in the warm red glow from the boiler, there was a grating in the wall at the height of his knee. Iron bars, black and rusted. Cool air came through it from outside, from Archive Lane, and met the hot air of the boiler room, and made a little whispering draft.
The boiler-room step. He knew it. He did not remember it, but he knew it. His hand went down by itself and touched the stone of the bottom step, and the crack between it and the step above. Dust. Grit. Nothing else now.
He went up the steps.
At the top there was a corridor, long and dim and cold after the heat, lit by small gemstone lamps in wire cages. On one side, a heavy wooden door with a brass plate: COPYING HALL. FIRST BASEMENT. No sound came from behind it. Too early. At the end of the corridor, a narrow stone spiral, going down.
He went down. One turn. Two. The air got colder and damper. At the bottom of the second turn there was a black iron door with no handle and a small square window of thick cloudy glass, and a brass plate.
HARVESTING SECTION. DANGER. DO NOT ENTER WITHOUT A HARVESTER PRESENT.
There was light behind the cloudy glass. A little. Yellow. The light of a candle that had been burning a long time.
Corin knocked.
The bolt slid back before his hand had even left the iron. Then another. And the door opened three inches, and an eye looked out at him through the gap. A pale brown eye, enormous behind a thick round lens.
"Oh," said the eye. "Oh, thank the saints. Oh, thank the saints. It's you." The door flew open. "It is you. Isn't it? Is it you? Do you know me?"
Corin looked at the young man in the doorway. Thin and narrow-shouldered, with a long neck and big ears and sandy hair sticking up at the back in a tuft. Gray coat and gray cap, both too big. Thick round spectacles. Ink on his fingers, ink on his chin, ink on the end of his pointed nose.
Your clerk is Toby. He is young and nervous. He is on your side.
"Toby," said Corin.
Toby's face lit up like a lamp. Then, almost at once, it crumpled.
"You read it," he said.
"I read it."
"You always read it. You never just know." He did not sound hurt. He sounded too tired to be hurt. He stood aside, and Corin went in, and Toby slammed the iron door behind him, and shot the bolt, and the second bolt, and then, after a moment's thought, a third bolt at the very bottom of the door that Corin had not noticed was there, and which squealed as if it had not been used in years.
The Harvesting Section was long and low and cold, cut out of the rock under the hill. The curved ceiling was so close that Corin had to duck under the iron lamp-cages. The walls were bare damp stone, with white crusts of salt where the water came through. And along both walls, from floor to ceiling, there were shelves, and on the shelves there were jars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Glass jars with brass lids, most of them empty, some with a faint blue-white mist turning slowly inside, each with a small paper label.
At the far end, under a single gemstone lamp that had not been lit, there were two desks. One huge and old and scarred, buried under a landslide of papers and folders and books and empty jars and dirty plates. The other small and neat, with every pen in a line and every paper squared, and a little potted plant on its corner, very clearly dying. On the small neat desk there was a candle in a saucer. It had burned down almost to nothing. There were three other candle stubs in the saucer beside it, burned right down, their wax run together into a little white puddle.
On the floor beside the big desk, in a small sad heap, lay the pieces of a broken teacup.
"You've been here all night," said Corin.
"Since the fifth bell." Toby sat down at his neat desk. Or rather, his legs gave way, and the chair happened to be under him. "I couldn't sleep. I came in. I thought, if anyone comes for you, I want to be here first. I want to… I don't know what I want. I just didn't want to be at home, lying in bed, wondering." He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "I got your note. Yesterday. The girl brought it. The one with the key and no manners. She ate my lunch."
"I know. You wrote it on the back."
Toby put his spectacles back on and stared at him. "You remember that?"
"No. I copied it into my book last night. At Bram's. Your note's in the old book, in the strongbox. Your words are in the new one." Corin touched his coat. "P.P.S. Your girl ate my lunch."
Toby looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed. It was a small, cracked, exhausted laugh, and it went on a little too long, and when it stopped there were tears behind the thick lenses.
"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm just very tired. And very frightened. And I've been sitting here in the dark for two hours listening to the jars hum and thinking about the big Inquisitor standing exactly where you're standing now, with his little silver-capped stick, looking at me." He swallowed. "Asking about the red book."
"Tell me."
So Toby told him. Properly, this time, not on the back of a handbill. Corin sat down in the big creaking chair at the big desk, which fit him the way an old shoe fits, and listened, and wrote.
The eighth bell, yesterday. A knock on the iron door. Not a polite knock. Three heavy blows, as if with the end of a stick. Toby had opened it three inches, as he always did, and there had been a white coat in the gap, and above it a flat square bored face like a slab of cold meat, and a shaved head.
Inquisitor Brack. Alone.
He had not waited to be asked in. He had pushed the door open with one hand, quite gently, as if Toby were not there, and walked in, and looked round the long low room. At the jars. At the desks. At the broken teacup on the floor. He had walked slowly all the way down to the big desk at the end, and stood there, and looked at the landslide of papers, and not touched any of them.
"Then he asked where you were," said Toby. "And I said you were sick. And he said how sick. And I said very. Spots. And he looked at me, and he said, Harvester Vane was seen on Hook Lane in the Ink Quarter at the seventh bell this morning, Junior Clerk, walking quite well."
Corin's pencil stopped.
"Then he asked where you lodged," said Toby. "I said I didn't know. Which was true. I didn't, then. I've never been to your rooms. And he looked at me some more. And then he said, Does Harvester Vane keep his Ledger with him, or at home? And I said I didn't know that either. Which wasn't true. Everybody knows you never put it down." He twisted his inky fingers together. "And then he said it."
"The red book."
"A red book, Junior Clerk. Bound in calfskin. With a seal on it. Taken from a printer's drawer on Needle Street in the small hours of Wednesday morning. Have you seen such a book?" Toby's voice had gone flat, copying the way the big man spoke. Then it went back to his own. "And I said, What red book? And my voice went up at the end. Like a girl's. Like a squeak." He put his face in his hands. "I'm such a terrible liar. I've never lied in my life. Not properly. Not to an Inquisitor."
"What did he do?"
"Nothing." Toby took his hands away. "That's the worst part. He didn't do anything. He just looked at me. For a long time. And then he nodded, as if I'd told him something. And he said, Thank you, Junior Clerk. That will be all. And he went out." Toby stared at the iron door. "He didn't even close it behind him."
Corin wrote: Brack. 8th bell, Wednesday. Alone. Knew I was on Hook Lane at the 7th bell. Asked where I lodge. Asked if I keep my Ledger with me. Asked about the red book. Knew it was taken from a printer's drawer on Needle Street in the small hours. How?
He looked at the last word for a while.
"Somebody in this building," he said quietly.
Toby went still.
"You said that," he whispered. "On Tuesday. About Pell. You said someone in this building knew he'd found the book. Within a day." He swallowed. "I didn't want to believe it. I still don't want to. But…"
"But?"
Toby looked at him. Then he opened the top drawer of his neat desk and took out a small square of paper and laid it on the corner of Corin's desk, among the landslide, very carefully, the way you might lay down something that could explode.
It was a request slip. Corin knew that without knowing how he knew. A small printed form, gray paper, with boxes. RECORDS OFFICE. REQUEST FOR STAFF FILE. In the box marked Name, in Toby's small neat hand: DORRAN, PELL. Scribe, Grade 3. First Basement. In the box marked Purpose: Next of kin. Address. To return personal effects.
And across the bottom of the slip, stamped in red ink, slightly crooked, as if by someone in a hurry:
FILE WITHDRAWN. OFFICE OF THE ARCHON.
And a date. Wednesday. The ninth bell.
"I sent that up yesterday morning," said Toby. "After Brack left. I thought, I have to get Nan's money to her. I promised. I've got the sock right here." He touched his coat pocket. It clinked. "So I sent for Pell's staff file. Every clerk's got one. It has your next of kin, your village, your parish, everything. And it came back down the pipe an hour later. Like that." He looked at the red stamp. "Withdrawn. The day after he died. By the Archon's own office."
"Has that happened before?"
"Never." Toby's voice was very small. "Not in five years. Not to anybody. Staff files don't get withdrawn. They get closed. When somebody dies or leaves, the file gets a black stripe and goes to the Second Basement and sits in a box till the mice eat it. Nobody withdraws them." He swallowed. "Somebody upstairs didn't want anybody to find out where Pell came from."
Corin looked at the stamp for a long time.
Oakhaven, said a word on the inside cover of the new Ledger. You were born in Oakhaven.
He did not say it aloud.
Instead, he put the request slip inside the back cover of Volume Seven, beside a cream envelope that smelled faintly of flowers. Then he looked at Toby, sitting at his neat desk in the cold, with his inky fingers knotted together, and his tuft of hair sticking up, and his eyes red behind the thick lenses from no sleep and too much fear.
He thought about the line in the Ledger. He is on your side.
He thought about a line under it, in the copied pages, that he had read that morning by the gray window. You will be asked to stop. Don't.
"Toby," he said. "I need to find some things. In the files. Today. Before anybody upstairs knows I'm here."
Toby looked at him.
"What things?"
Corin opened the Ledger at the page with the list.
"The Council Benevolent Fund," he read. "Payments to the family of Lord Aurelian Thorne. Why they stopped, last autumn. A man called Bertram, who was the Thorne steward. A doctor called Aske. A Ministry carriage that went to the old Thorne house two years ago, on the morning after Lord Thorne burned. Who sent it. Who was in it." He paused. "And the night book from Night Door Three. From a night eight years ago."
Toby's face had gone slowly whiter and whiter as the list went on. By the end of it, it was the color of the salt crusts on the wall.
"Inspector," he said. "Do you know what you're asking?"
"No," said Corin honestly. "That's why I'm asking you."
Toby stared at him. Then at the jars. Then at the iron door with its three bolts. Then down at his own hands, and the little gray square of the request slip that was no longer there.
He took a long breath.
"Then I'd better tell you how this place works," he said. "Because if we're going to do this, we're going to do it properly. In triplicate." His mouth twitched, very slightly, at the corner. "And without getting caught."
Four Numbers
Toby lit a new candle from the stub of the old one. He set it in the saucer, in the little white puddle of wax, and pressed it down until it stood up straight. Then he pulled a clean sheet of paper toward him, and squared its corners, and dipped his pen. It was what he did, Corin thought, when he was frightened. He made something neat.
"Everything in this building," said Toby, "has a number."
He drew a box at the top of the sheet. Then three lines under it.
"Every file. Every ledger. Every book, every box, every scrap of paper anybody ever wrote on and sent here. There are… I don't know. Millions. Nobody knows. Supervisor Brightwater says nobody's ever counted, and if anybody tried they'd die of old age before they got to the end. But every single one of them has a number. Four numbers. Like this."
He wrote, in his small neat hand: 2 — 14 — 6 — 31.
"That's a real one," he said. "It's the box with last year's crockery allowance in it. I had to fetch it in the spring when they cut the cups." He touched the numbers one by one with the end of his pen. "The first is the level. Which floor. Two is the Second Basement. Storage. The second is the row. Fourteen. The third is the stack. That's the shelves, going up, in a row, like a bookcase. Six. And the fourth is the box. Thirty-one. Level, row, stack, box." He looked up. "Every clerk learns it the first day. You can find anything in the whole Ministry if you have the four numbers. And you can't find anything at all if you don't."
Corin was looking at the sheet of paper. At the four numbers in a row, with the little dashes between them.
He took out the Ledger. He turned to the back. And he laid it open on the desk beside Toby's sheet, at the page where he had written, yesterday, in a small white room full of gold light:
4 — 2 — 9 — 3. Always the same. Long pause after 3. Then again.
Toby looked at it.
He looked at it for a long time. Corin watched his eyes go from one page to the other. From his own 2 — 14 — 6 — 31 to Corin's 4 — 2 — 9 — 3. Back and forth. Back and forth. Behind the thick round lenses, the pale brown eyes had gone very wide.
"Where did you get that?" he whispered.
"A man taps it on a wall. Every day. For two years. A man who doesn't know his own name."
Toby swallowed.
"It's a call mark," he said. "It looks exactly like a call mark. Level four. Row two. Stack nine. Box three." He touched the first number on Corin's page with the very tip of his pen, not quite touching the paper, the way you might point at a spider. "Except…"
"Except?"
"Except there is no level four."
The candle flame wavered in a draft from the door.
"There's the Great Hall," said Toby slowly. "And the floors above it, the offices, all the way up to the Archon's rooms at the top of the tower. Those have letters, not numbers. A, B, C, up to K. And then there's down. The First Basement, the Copying Hall. The Second, Storage. The Third, us." He gestured round at the long low room, the jars, the dripping walls. "And that's all. That's the bottom. The stairs stop here. Outside that door, the spiral goes down one more turn and then there's just a wall. Solid rock. I've been down. I was curious, my first week. I took a candle." He shook his head. "Nothing. Just rock. And a lot of very old dust."
Corin looked at the four numbers in his own hand. 4 — 2 — 9 — 3.
"Could it be something else?" he said. "Not a call mark. Something that only looks like one."
"It could be anything," said Toby. "It could be a date. A page. A lock. A street number. The number of a room in an inn." He pushed his spectacles up his nose with one inky finger. "But if I saw it written on a slip, I'd think it was a call mark. Anybody in this building would. And then I'd think somebody had made a mistake, and sent it back up."
He was quiet for a moment, looking at the page.
"Funny," he said. Almost to himself.
"What is?"
"Old Brightwater." Toby frowned a little. "Supervisor Brightwater. He's the head of our Section. He doesn't come down much. He has an office on C, with a window, and a fire, and a cat, and he does the reviews and signs the forms and complains about his knees. He's been here forty years. He's the oldest man in the building, except for Wilkin on the gate, I think." He hesitated. "I was helping him carry boxes, my first winter. Up from Storage, for an audit. And he stopped on the stairs to get his breath. Right outside that door. And he looked down, down the spiral, toward the rock, and he said…"
Toby stopped.
"What did he say?"
"He said, When I started, lad, there were four." Toby's voice had gone very quiet. "I said, four what, sir? And he said, Four levels. Four floors down. The fourth was the Deep Archive. The oldest papers. Older than the Ministry. And I said, what happened to it? And he said, They closed it up. After the war."
The jars hummed on their shelves. Somewhere in the walls, water dripped.
"What war?" said Corin.
"That's what I said." Toby looked at him. "What war, sir? And he looked at me. Really looked. As if he'd just woken up and didn't know how he'd got onto the stairs. And then he laughed. And he said, Did I say war? I meant the plague year, lad. The fever. Before you were born. They closed it up for the damp. And he picked up his box and went on up the stairs." Toby swallowed. "He's never said anything about it since. I asked him once, a year later, about the Deep Archive. He said he'd never heard of it. He said I must have dreamed it."
Corin wrote it all down. Slowly. Under the four numbers, at the back of the new Ledger.
Ministry call marks: Level, Row, Stack, Box. 4-2-9-3 looks like one. But there is no Level 4. The stairs stop at 3. Rock. Old Supervisor Brightwater, once, on the stairs: "When I started, there were four. The fourth was the Deep Archive. Oldest papers. They closed it up after the war." Then: "Did I say war? I meant the plague year." Later: says he never heard of it.
And under it, after a long moment, two letters. Small. Not a word. He did not know why he wrote them. His hand did it.
U.W.
In the pocket on the right side of his coat, against his ribs, the black sewn square was warm. A little warmer than it had been in Archive Lane. Like a cup of tea that has just been topped up.
He did not touch it.
Toby had not seen the two letters. He was looking down at his own sheet of paper, at the neat box he had drawn at the top, and the four numbers. He picked up his pen again. When he spoke, his voice had steadied. It had gone back to being a clerk's voice. Careful. Precise. As if he had decided that if the world was going to be frightening, it could at least be frightening in the correct order.
"Right," he said. "Your list. Let's take it one thing at a time. And I'll tell you where every one of them lives. And then I'll tell you which ones we can get to without anybody upstairs knowing, and which ones we can't."
He wrote as he talked. A neat column, down the left side of the page.
"The Council Benevolent Fund. That's money. Money is the Treasury Office, on D, but the old books, the ones that are finished, come down to Storage when they're full. Every quarter. If the Thorne payments stopped last autumn, the book for last year will be closed by now. It'll be downstairs. Second Basement. Row thirty-something, I think, with the Council accounts." He wrote 2 — 3? — ? — ? beside it. "We can get that. Probably."
"How?"
"I'll show you." He went on. "The carriage. If it was a Ministry carriage, with the eye on the door, it's in the Carriage Book. Every carriage that goes out of the yard gets written down. Which one. When it went. Where. Who was in it. Who sent it. When it came back. What it carried." A small proud tilt of the chin. "We write everything down here. The Carriage Books for two years ago will be in Storage too." He wrote 2 — ? beside it.
"And Doctor Aske?"
Toby frowned. "Is he Ministry?"
"I don't think so. A private doctor. Lord Thorne's own."
"Then we won't have a staff file on him. But…" Toby's pen tapped the page. "Every death in Veridia gets written down. Here. In the Death Registers. The Watch sends the reports, like they sent us Pell's. And then a clerk in the Copying Hall copies it into the great register, and the register comes down to Storage when it's full." He looked up. "If anything ever happened to your Doctor Aske, he'll be in a register. If he's alive, he won't be anywhere."
"And Bertram?"
"A steward? A private servant?" Toby shook his head. "Nothing. Not unless he died too. Or did something to get written down. We don't keep servants."
Corin nodded slowly.
"And Night Door Three," he said.
Toby put his pen down.
He looked at the column of neat lines on his sheet of paper. Then at the black iron door with its three bolts. Then at Corin.
"The Night Books," he said quietly. "Every night door has a book. The porter writes in it. Every name that comes in, every name that goes out, every hour, all night. When it's full, it comes down to Storage. Row one, I think. Right by the stairs. The oldest row there is." He hesitated. "Inspector. What's on Night Door Three?"
"A fire bucket," said Corin. "Now."
Toby stared at him.
"It was bricked up," said Corin. "Eight years ago. The week after its porter was brought home in a Ministry cab, smiling, not knowing his own name. Two clerks asked about it afterward. They stopped coming to work."
Toby's face had gone very still.
"Who told you that?"
"A man on a gate."
Toby did not ask which man, or which gate. He looked down at his neat sheet of paper for a long time. At the four lines. At the four sets of numbers, some of them question marks.
Then he picked up his pen again, and at the bottom of the column, in small neat letters, he wrote: Night Book. Door 3. Eight years ago. 2 — 1 — ? — ?
And beside it, smaller still, he drew a tiny square. And inside the square, a tiny cross.
"What's that?" said Corin.
"It's what we put," said Toby, "on a form, when a thing is dangerous to handle." He did not look up. "Like on the jars. Do not open."
He blotted the page. He folded it in half, and in half again, and put it in the inside pocket of his too-big gray coat, next to the red sock that clinked.
Then he stood up and took a lantern from a hook on the wall. A real lantern, with a candle in it, not a gemstone. He lit it from the candle on his desk. His hands were steady now.
"Storage opens at the seventh bell," he said. "That's when old Fennick comes on, with his sign-in book. Everybody who goes into Storage has to sign the book. Name, Section, time in, time out, call mark of what you took." He looked at the little clock on the wall above the shelves of jars. It said a quarter to the seventh bell. "Fennick comes in at the seventh bell. Not before. Never before. He's very particular."
"So?"
"So we have a quarter of an hour," said Toby, "before there's a book to sign."
He unbolted the door. All three bolts. And picked up the lantern, and looked back at Corin over his shoulder, and his thin pale face in the yellow light was frightened and determined at once, like a rabbit that has decided, against all sense, to stand its ground.
"A harvester has to be present," he said. "In the Third Basement. It says so on the door. But it doesn't say anything at all about the Second." His mouth twitched. "And nobody ever reads the doors anyway."
