Chapter 7: The Ministry of Records, Part 3 of 5: The Second Basement
The Second Basement
Storage smelled of mice.
That was the first thing. Before the dark, before the cold, before the size of it. The smell came up the last turn of the spiral to meet them, dry and sour and dusty, mouse droppings and mildew and old paper going soft and brown at the edges, and under it something else, something sweetish and sad, like a library where nobody has opened a window for a hundred years.
The door at the bottom of the spiral was wood, not iron. It was not locked. Beside it, on a high wooden desk like a pulpit, there was a great leather book lying open, with a pen in an inkwell beside it, and a brass plate on the front of the desk: STORAGE. ALL VISITORS TO SIGN. BY ORDER. H. FENNICK, KEEPER. The pen was dry. The page was blank. The chair behind the desk was empty.
Toby looked at the empty chair. He looked at the book. Then he looked away from both of them, very firmly, the way you look away from a cake you are not allowed to eat, and lifted his lantern, and pushed the wooden door open.
And there was the dark.
It went on forever. That was what it felt like. The lantern light went out from Toby's hand in a small yellow circle, and it touched the edge of something, and the something went away into the black in both directions further than the light could follow. Shelves. Wooden shelves, from the floor to far above their heads, higher than the light could reach. Stacked with boxes. Gray cardboard boxes, brown wooden boxes, bundles of paper tied with tape, great leather-bound ledgers lying on their sides, all of it gray with dust. Row after row after row. Long narrow aisles going off between them into the dark like the streets of a city where nobody lived.
At the end of each row, on the tall upright post of the shelf, a number had been painted in white. Very old paint, flaking. 1. 2. 3. Going away into the dark. Somewhere far off, something small scuttled across the floor and was gone.
"Carriage Books first," whispered Toby. He did not know why he was whispering. Neither did Corin. But it seemed like the only way to speak in that place. "Row nine. I think. Come on. And don't touch anything you don't have to. Some of these shelves haven't been dusted since before the Archon's grandfather."
They went down the long dark street of shelves with the lantern swinging, and their own shadows swinging huge across the boxes, and the numbers on the posts going by. Seven. Eight. At Nine, Toby turned in.
The Carriage Books were big and flat and bound in black, a hundred of them, two hundred, standing up on the shelves like a row of tombstones with years painted on their spines in white. Toby ran his finger along them, mouthing the years. He stopped. He pulled one out. It came away from its neighbors with a dry tearing sound, as if they had grown together, and a little cloud of dust came off it and made them both cough.
"Two years ago," he whispered. "Autumn. Do you know the day?"
"No. Look for the Thorne house."
Toby laid the book on the edge of the shelf, in the lantern light, and opened it toward the back. The pages were ruled in columns. CARRIAGE. OUT. TO. PASSENGERS. SENT BY. IN. CARRIED. Every line filled in by a different hand, some neat and some scrawled, in brown ink and black and once, for a whole page, in purple. Hundreds of lines. Toby's finger went down them, fast, the way only a clerk's finger can go down a column, not reading every word, only looking for one shape.
It stopped.
He did not say anything. He turned the book a little toward Corin, and held the lantern closer, and pointed.
Carriage 9 (closed).
Out: 5th bell and a half.
To: Thorne House, the Hill.
Passengers: Harvester L. Hale. One guest (see sealed note 7).
Sent by: Office of the Archon.
In: 8th bell.
Carried: One case, sealed. To Lower Levels.
Corin read it once. And then he read the second line of Passengers again, and his hand went out by itself and touched the page, just beside the words, the way you touch a wall in the dark to find out where you are.
Harvester L. Hale.
He did not know the name. And then he did. Not as a face. Not as a voice. As a line in a list, written in his own big untidy hand on the second page of every Ledger he had ever owned, on the page headed THINGS THAT ARE TRUE TODAY.
The rod in the long case is yours. Master Hale gave it to you. Do not lose it.
In the narrow pocket on the right side of his coat, the rod went cold.
Not the ordinary cold. The other cold. The cold of a cellar in deep winter, of a hand that has been dead a long time. It came through the wool and through his shirt and into the skin of his side like a blade of ice, and he gasped, and pressed his hand flat against it without thinking.
And behind his left ear, very close, very quiet, so quiet he almost did not hear it, a voice said one word.
Linus.
Not sour. Not clever. Not bored. The voice of an old man saying the name of someone he had known for a very long time, and had not expected to hear again.
Malakor, Corin thought. You know him.
No answer.
Malakor.
Nothing. The cold stayed in the rod. But the voice had gone back down into the dark, the way it did when it did not want to be asked things.
"Inspector?" Toby was staring at him. "Inspector, are you all right? You've gone… your eyes have gone…"
"I'm all right."
"You're not. You look like you've been hit." Toby's voice was a frightened whisper. "Who's Harvester Hale?"
"I don't know," said Corin. It was true. It was also not true at all. "He gave me the rod."
Toby looked at the line in the book. Then at Corin's hand, pressed against his side. Then at the line again.
"Oh," he said, very small.
Corin took out the Ledger. His fingers were stiff with the cold from the rod. He wrote it word for word, the whole entry, every column, slowly, so as not to get anything wrong. Carriage 9. Thorne House. Harvester L. Hale. One guest, see sealed note 7. Office of the Archon. One case, sealed. To Lower Levels.
He looked at the last two words for a long time.
Lower Levels. There were three Lower Levels. The Copying Hall. Storage. The Harvesting Section. If the case had come down to the Third Basement, he would have been there. Toby would have been there. It would have gone on a shelf, with a label.
"Toby," he said. "If a sealed case came down to us. Two years ago. From the Thorne house. Would it be on our shelves?"
"Everything that comes to us goes on our shelves," said Toby. "With a number. I'd know. I've catalogued every jar in the Section twice."
"And is it there?"
Toby did not answer at once. Then he shook his head, slowly.
"No," he said. "Nothing from the Thorne house. Nothing from that autumn at all that I don't know. I'd remember a sealed case. We don't get sealed cases. We get jars." He swallowed. "It went somewhere else."
Lower Levels, said the page.
4 — 2 — 9 — 3, said the page before it.
Corin closed the Ledger.
They did not have much time. Toby put the Carriage Book back, carefully, in its exact place, and pushed its neighbors against it so that the gap in the dust would not show. Then they went on, down the long dark street, deeper, with the lantern swinging. Fourteen. Twenty. Twenty-six. At Thirty-one, the Council accounts. Long rows of fat red ledgers with gold numbers on their spines. Toby found last year's in less than a minute. He opened it on his knee.
COUNCIL BENEVOLENT FUND. QUARTERLY DISBURSEMENTS.
The names were in alphabetical order. Councillors who had gone mad, or blind, or burned themselves to nothing, and the families who looked after them, and how much they were paid each quarter to do it. SARRAN, Lady E. TALLOW, Ld. M. THORNE, Ld. A.
The Thorne line ran across the page in neat columns. Spring: paid. Summer: paid. And in the autumn column, instead of a sum, two words in brown ink, in a small upright hand that was different from all the rest of the page:
Stopped. By request of the family.
And under it, smaller still:
Private arrangement made. C.
Corin looked at that letter for a long time.
By request of the family. The family was Lyra, and her father who could not remember his own name, and a brother who was somewhere she would not talk about. And Lyra had not asked for anything to stop. Lyra had not known it had stopped.
C.
He copied it down. Every word. And the shape of the letter, as near as he could. A round open C, with a little hook at the top, like a fishhook.
The Death Registers were further still. Row forty. Huge, heavy books, so big that each one took both of Toby's arms to lift, bound in black with a white stripe down the spine. Toby found the year. He found the autumn. He went down the columns with his finger, his lips moving. A. A. A. The names were in order of the day they were written, not the alphabet, and there were hundreds of them, every death in the whole great city, every drowning and fever and fall and fire, in small cramped clerk's writing, and it took a long time.
Corin held the lantern. The light shook a little. He made it stop.
And somewhere above them, very faint, through all the stone, a bell began to ring.
One. Two. Three.
Toby's finger went faster.
Four. Five.
"There," he breathed.
ASKE, Matthias. Physician. Of Ferrier's Row. Drowned. Found by a bargeman below the Lantern Bridge steps. No marks. Taken from the river.
And a date.
Toby looked at the date. Then he turned back three pages, very fast, to a page he had passed. He looked at that date. Then forward again.
"Nine days," he whispered. "Inspector. This is nine days before. Nine days before the carriage went to the Thorne house."
Corin did not answer.
He was thinking of a big scarred man sitting at a kitchen table over a stable, with his fists closed on the wood. Bertram said I must ride to the other side of the city and fetch Doctor Aske, my lord's own doctor, and nobody else would do. And the doctor wasn't there. His girl said he'd gone to the country a week since. To his sister.
Nine days.
Bertram had sent Oren across the city, in the rain, two hours there and back, to fetch a man who had been lying in the river for more than a week.
Six. Seven.
The seventh bell. Somewhere above, at the top of the spiral, a door banged.
Toby went white. "Fennick."
There were footsteps on the stone stair. Slow ones. Heavy and particular, one at a time, the steps of an old man who has walked down the same stairs every morning for thirty years and will not be hurried by anybody. And a voice, querulous, muttering, coming down ahead of them. …and who's left the door open, I'd like to know, and who's been… is that lamp smoke? Is that… somebody's been…
"The Night Book," said Corin. "Row one. Quickly."
"There isn't time—"
"Row one is by the stairs. You said so. It's on the way out."
Toby stared at him for one wild moment. Then he snapped the Death Register shut, and shoved it back on its shelf, and ran.
They ran back up the long dark street with the lantern swinging wildly and their shadows leaping over the boxes like mad things. Thirty. Twenty. Ten. Toby was faster than he looked. He ran like a hare, with his coat flapping and his spectacles bouncing on his nose, and at One he swung in round the post so sharply that the lantern banged against the shelf.
The Night Books were small. Smaller than any of the others. Thin, gray, plain, each with a door number and a year written on a paper label on its spine. A long row of them, hundreds, all the same. Toby went along them with the lantern held high, muttering under his breath.
Footsteps on the stairs. Closer. The old man's voice, nearer. …left the book open, the pen dry, I said to Brightwater, I said…
"Here," whispered Toby. "Door Three. That year."
He pointed.
There was a gap on the shelf. A thin gap, the width of one small gray book, between Door 3 of the year before and Door 3 of the year after. And in the gap, propped up against the next book so that it would be seen, there was a slip of gray paper. A request slip, like the one on Toby's desk. Across it, in red ink, a little crooked, as if stamped in a hurry, two lines.
WITHDRAWN.
OFFICE OF THE ARCHON.
Corin did not need to read it twice.
He looked along the shelf. To the left of the gap. Then to the right. And there, three books along, with the same year on its label, was a different number.
Door 2.
He pulled it out.
"Inspector—"
"One minute."
The footsteps had reached the bottom of the stair. There was a grunt, and a creak, as if someone heavy had sat down in a wooden chair behind a high desk. And then a long, slow, suspicious sniff.
Corin opened the Night Book for Door Two at the autumn. The handwriting was small and square and very careful, the hand of a man who did not write much and took a long time to do it. A one-armed man, perhaps, holding the book still with his elbow. He went down the dates. Every night. Every hour. Midnight. Knocked twice. Answered. Fourth bell. Knocked once. Answered. Over and over. Night after night. He turned the pages. He found the month. He found the night.
The lines were the same as all the others. Until the fourth bell.
Fourth bell. Knocked once. NO ANSWER.
The words NO ANSWER had been written in capitals, and pressed so hard the pen had gone through the paper.
Fourth bell and a quarter. Left my door, against orders. Door 3 locked. Light under it. Blue. Very bright. Smell of flowers. Knocked. Called. No answer.
Fourth bell and a half. Two Inq. came. Sent back to my door.
And then a line in a different hand. Not small and square. Tall and flowing and careless, with long loops, written in a hurry, the way a man writes when he is bored and does not care who reads it.
Porter Wilkin returned to post. Door 3 closed for repair. S.K.
And under that, one more line. In the small square careful hand again. Written very small. Squeezed in at the very bottom of the page, as if the writer had waited until the tall man with the long loops had gone away, and then picked up his pen again, and pressed down, and written it anyway.
Fifth bell. A lady went out past my door. Came from the inside passage, toward Three. In white. Would not give a name. I asked. She smiled. She said, "A friend of the Ministry." Smell of flowers.
Corin did not breathe.
Behind them, at the far end of row one, past the wooden door, a chair scraped. The slow heavy footsteps began again. Coming toward the door. Coming in.
"Who's in there?" said an old man's voice, sharp and cracked. "Who's in my Storage without signing my book?"
Corin closed the Night Book. He put it back in its place on the shelf. He did not copy a single line. There was no time. He did not need to. He had read it once, and he would read it again, every morning, for as long as he lived, because he would write it down the moment he was out of this room, in letters so big they filled the page.
A friend of the Ministry.
From a friend.
Toby had blown out the lantern.
In the sudden black, Corin felt a thin cold hand close round his wrist. Toby's hand. Shaking. Pulling.
"There's a way out at the back of row one," breathed Toby's voice, right beside his ear. "Behind the shelves. For the mice men. I found it my first week. Come on. Don't step on anything."
They went, in the dark, hand in hand, like two children in a wood, while behind them an old man stood in the doorway of his kingdom with his own lantern raised, sniffing the air, and saying to nobody, over and over, in a voice of deep offense: "Somebody's been in my Storage. Somebody's been in my Storage."
Lucian
The mice men's way came out behind a loose panel at the bottom of the spiral, just above the place where the stair ran into solid rock. Toby pushed the panel back into place with both hands, very gently, so that it would not click. Then they stood in the dark on the cold stone steps and listened.
Far above them, faint, through the stone, an old man's voice was still saying somebody's been in my Storage to nobody at all.
Toby let out a breath that shook all the way down.
They went down one turn, to the black iron door, and Toby unbolted it with fingers that would not keep still, and they went in, and he bolted it again behind them, all three bolts, and leaned his back against it, and slid slowly down it until he was sitting on the cold stone floor with his knees up and his lantern beside him, and his spectacles crooked, and his eyes shut.
"I've never," he said, "never, in my life—"
"I know."
"Not signed the book. Not once. In five years. Fennick says I'm the only clerk in the building who signs in his best hand." He opened his eyes. "And now I've crawled out of Storage behind a panel for the rat-catchers, in the dark, holding a Harvester's hand, with a book in my head that says a lady in white walked out past Night Door Two at the fifth bell eight years ago and called herself a friend of the Ministry." His voice had gone up and up until it squeaked. He stopped. He took a breath. "I need to sit here for a minute."
"Sit here for a minute," said Corin.
He went to the big desk and sat down in the creaking chair and opened the Ledger and wrote. He wrote for a long time. Everything. The Carriage Book, every column. The Benevolent Fund, and the little letter with its fishhook top. Matthias Aske, physician, drowned, nine days before. The gap on the shelf. The red stamp. And Door Two. Every line. He did not have it in front of him. But he found that he did not need it. The words were still there, sharp and clear, the way the smell of something stays in a room after it has been taken away. He wrote them before they could go.
And at the end, across the whole width of the page, as big as the letters on the first page that said READ THIS FIRST:
A FRIEND OF THE MINISTRY.
FROM A FRIEND.
THE SAME.
Then he sat and looked at it.
Toby had got up off the floor. He was standing behind Corin's chair, looking over his shoulder, reading. He did not say anything. After a while he went to his own neat desk and sat down and squared the corners of a blank sheet of paper, three times, and then put his hands flat on it and left them there.
The little clock on the wall said a quarter past the seventh bell. Then half past. The jars hummed. The water dripped. Somewhere above them, very faint, the Great Hall had woken up, and the scratching of ten thousand pens came down through the rock like the sound of rain a long way off.
At the eighth bell, the pipe above Toby's desk went whoosh and thump, and a brass cylinder dropped out of the hatch onto his blotter.
Toby jumped as if it had bitten him.
He opened it. He took out the roll of paper. He read it. And his face did something Corin had not seen it do before. It did not go white. It went a sort of gray-green, like the water in a horse trough.
"It's from upstairs," he said. "From K."
"What's K?"
"The top. The very top. The tower." Toby swallowed. "The Archon's floor." He held the paper out with a hand that was shaking so badly that it rattled. "Harvester Vane and Junior Clerk Toby to remain in the Section. A representative of the Office of the Archon will attend at the ninth bell."
Corin took the paper and read it. It said exactly that. Nothing more. The handwriting was small and very even and very careful, each letter exactly like the last one of its kind. At the bottom there was no signature. Only a small round stamp in black ink. An open book. And above it, an eye. Closed.
"Have they ever done that before?" he said. "Sent someone down?"
"Never," said Toby faintly. "Not in five years. Nobody comes down here. Nobody wants to come down here. That's the whole point of down here."
They waited.
It was the longest hour Corin could remember. Which, he thought, was not saying much. But it was long. Toby got up and swept up the broken teacup at last, piece by piece, into a dustpan, and then did not know what to do with the pieces, and put them in a drawer. He straightened every pen on his desk. He straightened them again. He watered the dying plant, and then looked at it in despair, because it was already too late for water. Corin sat at the big desk with the Ledger open in front of him and his hand flat on the page and the rod cold in his side and the black sewn square warm in his pocket, and did not move.
At the ninth bell, exactly, there was a knock on the iron door.
It was not like Brack's knock. It was small and quick and polite. Three little taps. Then a pause. Then three more, exactly the same, as if whoever was knocking was not sure the first three had been done properly.
Toby unbolted the door. All three bolts. He opened it.
A young man came in.
He was perhaps twenty-five. Thin, like Toby, but in a different way. Toby was thin like a young tree that has grown too fast. This young man was thin like a pen. Narrow and straight and very neat, from the top of his head to the soles of his shoes. His hair was black and cut very short and combed flat with something that made it shine. His face was pale and clean-shaven and narrow, with a high forehead and large dark anxious eyes and a mouth that kept pressing itself together, as if it were trying to stop something from getting out. He wore a long coat, not gray, but black. A deep, perfect black, without a speck on it. On the left breast, embroidered small in silver thread, the open book and the closed eye. And above the eye, a tiny silver crown.
Under one arm he carried a leather folder, very thick, tied with black ribbon. In his other hand he held a book. A slim tall book bound in black, with a silver clasp. He held it against his chest, flat, with his fingers spread over it, the way some people hold a prayer book in church.
He stopped just inside the door. His eyes went round the room once. Fast. The shelves. The jars. The landslide on the big desk. Toby. Corin. The jars again. His mouth pressed itself together a little harder when he looked at the jars, and he took one tiny step to his left, away from the nearest shelf, as if the mist inside it might reach out through the glass.
Then he turned back to the door, and looked at it. At the three bolts. He counted them. Corin saw his lips move. One. Two. Three. Then he looked at Toby.
"Bolt it, please," he said. "All three. Thank you."
His voice was soft and quick and precise, every word set down separately, like coins counted onto a table.
Toby bolted the door. All three.
The young man watched him do it. Then he counted the bolts again. Then he turned to Corin.
"Harvester Vane," he said. "Good morning. My name is Lucian. I am secretary to his Excellency the Archon." He did not hold out his hand. He did not seem to know what to do with his hands at all, except hold the folder and the book. "I am sorry to come down without more notice. The notice was short. I wrote it myself. I checked it. It was correct." He paused. "It was correct, wasn't it?"
"It was correct," said Corin.
Something in the young man's narrow shoulders came down a fraction. "Good," he said. "Good. Thank you." He looked at the chair beside Toby's desk. "May I sit? I would prefer to sit. I have been up and down a great many stairs this morning."
"Please."
Lucian sat. He did it carefully, on the very front edge of the chair, with his knees together and his back very straight, and put the leather folder on his knees, and the slim black book on top of the folder, and both hands flat on top of the book. He looked at Corin across the room.
Then he said, in exactly the same soft precise voice:
"Storage Keeper Fennick has reported an unsigned visit to the Second Basement this morning. Between the half hour before the seventh bell and the seventh bell itself. He says that a lamp was used. Not a gemstone lamp. A candle in a lantern. Tallow." His dark anxious eyes went, just for a moment, to the saucer on Toby's desk, with its little white puddle of run wax and its four burned-down stubs. Then back. "He says he can smell tallow on the Night Books. And on the Death Registers for two years ago. And on the Carriage Books. And the Council accounts." A pause. "He has a very good nose, Storage Keeper Fennick. He has been in Storage for thirty years. He says it is the only thing he has left that works."
Toby made a small sound, like a kettle just before it boils.
Corin did not move.
"There are only two lower sections in this building that still use tallow," said Lucian. "Because of the allowance. Storage itself. And this one." He looked at the four burned-down stubs again. "Storage Keeper Fennick does not take the Carriage Books down from the shelf. He has no reason to."
The room was very quiet.
"I am not here about that," said Lucian.
Corin looked at him.
"I want you to understand," said Lucian. He said it quickly. The words came out faster now, tumbling over each other a little, as if they had been waiting a long time behind his pressed-together mouth. "I am not here about that. I have not been sent about that. Storage Keeper Fennick wrote his report on the proper form and sent it up the pipe to the Office of the Archon, as he should. And the Office of the Archon is me. I open the pipe. I read every form that comes up. Every one. Three times. Before anything is placed before his Excellency." He swallowed. "I read Storage Keeper Fennick's report at a quarter past the seventh bell. I have not yet placed it before his Excellency."
"Why not?"
Lucian's mouth pressed itself together very hard. For a moment Corin thought he was not going to answer.
Then he said, very low: "Because it was not filled in correctly. Storage Keeper Fennick forgot to write the time of departure. Only the time of arrival. A report without a time of departure is incomplete. An incomplete report must be returned to the sender for correction before it can be placed before his Excellency. That is the rule." He looked down at his hands on the black book. "I have returned it. By the slow pipe. It will reach Storage Keeper Fennick by the eleventh bell. He will correct it and send it back by the noon pipe. I will read it at the first bell after noon. Three times." A pause. "And then I will place it before his Excellency."
Nobody said anything.
"I am telling you this," said Lucian, "so that you will know that I know. And so that you will know that if I know, his Excellency will know, by the second bell after noon." He looked up. His dark eyes were very large, and very frightened, and very steady. "I am not telling you anything else. I want that to be quite clear, Harvester. I have not told you anything at all. I have only told you about a form that was not filled in correctly."
Corin looked at the young man on the edge of the chair for a long moment.
Then he said, quietly: "Thank you."
Lucian shook his head. Very small. Very fast.
"Don't," he said. "Please. I check my work three times. I always check it three times. Because if I make one mistake, one, on one form, his Excellency will notice. He notices everything. And he is very kind about it. He is always very kind. He says, Lucian, we all make mistakes. Let me help you not to make that one again." Lucian's hands had begun to tremble on the black book. He pressed them flatter. "And then I don't. I never make that mistake again. I can't. Because I don't remember how."
The jars hummed on their shelves.
Toby had put his hand over his mouth.
Lucian took a breath, and let it out, and when he spoke again his voice was soft and precise and even once more, every word set down separately like a coin on a table.
"I have come about something else," he said. "I have come on his Excellency's business. Which is the matter of Scribe Pell Dorran."
He untied the black ribbon on the leather folder. Carefully. One loop, and then the other. He opened it. And he took out a single sheet of thick white paper, printed in black, with boxes, and a great deal of very small print, and at the bottom, a seal. A real one. Round. In black wax. An open book. A closed eye. A tiny crown.
He held it out across the room toward Corin. He did not get up. His arm was quite straight, and quite still, now, and the paper did not shake at all.
"Form eighty-eight C," said Lucian. "Closure of File. By order of his Excellency the Archon."
