Chapter 7: The Ministry of Records, Part 1 of 5: Volume Seven
Debt of the Unremembered. Arc 1: The Siphon's Ledger.
Volume Seven
Corin Vane woke up with a book on his chest, and the book was stiff.
That was the first thing he knew. Not the weight of it, though it was heavy, a square weight on his breastbone that rose and fell when he breathed. Not the smell of it, though it smelled of new leather and glue and something sharp and clean, like a stationer's shop. The stiffness. His hand was lying flat on the cover, and the cover did not give under his fingers the way old leather gives. It was hard and smooth and slightly slippery, like a boot that has never been walked in.
He lay very still on his back and let his eyes move.
A low gray ceiling with a long brown water stain across it shaped a little like a fish. A crack running from the fish's tail to the corner. A small square window with four panes, one of them patched with a strip of brown paper. Except that the brown paper had a neat straight cut in it beside the latch, and someone had pasted a second strip over the first, crossways, so that the cut was covered, and on the new strip, in big black untidy letters, someone had written: LOCKED. CHECK IT.
He did not know what that meant. Yet.
Gray light. Not morning light, exactly. The light that comes before morning, thin and cold, the color of dishwater.
His hand moved. It did not ask him. It lifted the stiff book off his chest and held it up in front of his face, and he looked at it.
Dark brown leather, almost black. New. Not a single crack. Low on the cover, pressed into the leather, small sharp letters: GROUNDING LEDGER. B. HOLT, STATIONER. LOWER DOCKS. And under it, a number. VII.
His hand opened it at the very beginning.
The writing on the inside of the front cover was big and black and untidy and sloped hard to the right. It had been pressed so deep that some of the strokes had cut into the paper. He knew before he finished the first line that it was his own. But it was fresh. The ink was still glossy in places, as if it had been written only a few hours ago.
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin.
You were born in Oakhaven.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
You used to fear the dark.
And below, squeezed in small: Turn the page. Then get up. Then eat something. In that order.
And then, below that, in a different hand altogether, a hand he did not know, big and square and heavy, every letter standing up on its own like a brick in a wall:
Copied out at Holt's, Wednesday night, the tenth bell. B.H. watched. He grumbled about his handwriting the whole time. Don't buy ink.
Corin read it twice. He found that he was smiling, a little, and did not know why.
He turned the page.
THINGS THAT ARE TRUE TODAY. The same list he did not remember reading but somehow knew the shape of, like the shape of a room in the dark. No. 14 Tallow Street. Top floor. Room 9. Senior Investigator. Ministry of Records. Third Basement. Harvesting Section. Mrs. Marta. Toby. The rod. The box under the bed.
And at the bottom, squeezed in, a line that seemed to have been copied very carefully, with the pen pressed harder than the rest:
Juno. Mrs. Marta's granddaughter. Fourteen. Runner. She caught you over the Cut. She tells you once.
He did not know who Juno was. Or what the Cut was. But the words she caught you made something warm turn over in his chest.
He turned the page again.
The third page was not in his writing. It was in a neat, clear, slanting hand, a hand that had been taught to write by someone patient, in a room with good light. A careful, well-brought-up hand, with long loops and small even letters, all leaning the same way like grass in a steady wind.
Lyra Thorne. Linden Walk, the gate with the stone pineapple, up the outside stair, over the stable. She is your partner. You chose it. She will tell you the rest.
He read it many times.
Partner.
He did not know her. He did not know her face, or her voice, or what she was partner to. But his hand, holding the book, had gone very still, the way a hand goes still on something it does not want to drop.
After that page there were more. Many more. He turned them slowly. They were full. Crowded with his own writing, small and fast, and they seemed to have been copied in a great hurry from somewhere else, because the lines ran crooked and some words had been crossed out and written again. Pell Dorran. Lilies. Lord Aurelian Thorne. FOLD. The clock, sold, find it. U.W., three, sewn shut. A white coat in a cellar door. Room 9: someone came in over the roofs. STOP LOOKING. Not my hand. He did not understand most of it. He read it anyway, all of it, the way his body seemed to know he must, while the gray light in the window grew a little less gray.
Near the end of the copied pages, there was a page that was not copied. He could tell. The writing was slower and quieter, as if it had been written at the very end of the night by a very tired man.
Tomorrow, Thursday:
1. Ministry. Early. By the SIDE GATE on Archive Lane. Not the front. Toby will be there.
2. Find: Council Benevolent Fund (Thorne). Bertram, the Thorne steward. Doctor Aske. The black carriage, the morning after Lord Thorne burned. Night Door Three, eight years ago (for Juno. You promised).
3. 4-2-9-3. What is it?
4. Rule five: tell Lyra the same day. Whatever you find.
5. You will be asked to stop. Don't.
He looked at the last line for a long time.
Then he looked at the board on the wall above his bed. At all the small square notes pinned there in his own hand. BOOTS FIRST, THEN COAT. KEYS ARE IN THE LEFT COAT POCKET. ALWAYS LEFT. There was a new one, at the top, pinned with a tack, newer than the rest:
VOLUME SEVEN IS IN YOUR HAND. THE OLD ONE IS IN THE STRONGBOX AT BRAM'S. YOU TOOK IT THERE LAST NIGHT. YOU ARE NOT MISSING ANYTHING.
He let out a breath he had not known he was holding.
Then he got up, and put on his boots first, and then his coat.
On the bare boards outside his door there was a bowl with a plate on top of it, and steam coming out under the plate, and beside it a small stone pot with a cork in it and a strip of brown paper tied round its neck. On the strip, in small spiky purple writing that leaned hard to the right like thorns in a wind, it said: GOOSE GREASE. FOR YOUR ARM. RUB IT IN. DO NOT EAT IT.
He ate the stew crouching in the doorway. It was hot and salty and slightly burned on the bottom, and it was the best thing he had ever tasted. Then he pushed up his left sleeve, because the note seemed to think he should, and found a ladder of small neat lines written into the skin of his forearm, in a dozen colors of ink, You are not this, You are not that, and at the bottom a new red line, still sore, still puffy. You are not Jory Kestle. Kettle is gone.
He rubbed the goose grease into it. It smelled terrible. It helped.
The bells across the city were ringing the half hour before the sixth when he went down the narrow crooked stairs, past a scratched bird under two scratched words on the fourth-floor landing, past a little girl's door with a cat's saucer outside it, past an old man in a chair by a round dirty window who said good morning very politely, past a purple door that smelled of onions, and out into Tallow Street.
The lamps were still burning, cold and blue on their tall black heron-necks. The street was almost empty. A milk cart went by. A man swept a step.
And a voice above him said, "You're early."
He looked up.
There was a girl sitting on the edge of a gutter four stories above the street, with her boots dangling over nothing, eating an apple. She was small and thin and dressed like a boy, in a patched coat too big for her and a cap pulled down hard over her ears. Round her neck, on a string, a big brass key caught the blue lamplight.
He did not know her.
The girl looked down at him. She stopped chewing. And something in her small sharp face went very still and very careful, the way a cat goes still before a jump.
"Juno," she said. "Mrs. Marta's granddaughter. I'm your runner. I caught you over the Cut yesterday." A pause. "That's once."
Corin opened the Ledger. He did not need to. But he did it anyway, so that she could see him do it. He turned to the second page, and found the line at the bottom, and read it, and looked up at her.
"She tells you once," he said.
Juno's face did something complicated. A scowl and a grin, fighting over the same small space. The grin won.
"Right," she said, and threw the apple core over her shoulder, and stood up on the edge of the gutter as if it were a kitchen floor. "Archive Lane. Side gate. I'll take you the quick way. Try not to fall off anything."
Archive Lane
The quick way was mostly not roofs, which Corin was glad of, although he did not know why he was glad.
It was alleys. Narrow ones, black ones, ones so tight that he had to turn sideways to get through, with water dripping off broken gutters onto his hat. It was the backs of houses, and yards full of washing, and a tannery's drying ground where the hides hung on frames like great brown ghosts and the smell made his eyes water. It was a wall with a gap in it that Juno slid through like a cat and he had to climb over. It was a short flight of iron steps up the side of a warehouse and a plank across to the next one, ten feet above a yard full of geese, which he crossed very slowly while the geese shouted at him, and Juno, on the other side, shouted at the geese.
And it was always up. Up and up and up. The Lower Docks fell away behind them, and the river with it, a long gray shine under the gray sky. The streets got wider and cleaner. The houses got taller and plainer. And at last they came out of the mouth of an alley into a wide quiet cobbled lane, and Corin stopped, and looked up, and up, and up.
A wall.
It was black. Built black, out of dark gray stone that had gone darker still with a century of coal smoke, until now it was the color of a wet slate roof at night. It went straight up from the cobbles, smooth and blank and sheer, without a window, without a ledge, without a single crack, for six stories. Then, far above, the windows began. Tall narrow pointed ones, in long rows, with stone frames carved like leaves. And above the windows, towers. Thin and black and spiked, going up into the low gray sky like the spines on the back of some enormous sleeping animal.
He had seen it before. He did not remember. But his whole body had gone small and quiet and careful, the way a body does in the presence of something much bigger than itself.
"Ministry," said Juno, unnecessarily. "The back of it. Nobody comes round the back. Everybody goes up the big stairs at the front, under the big stone eye, so the big stone eye can see them." She sniffed. "This is Archive Lane. Where the carts come in with the paper. And where people like Wilkin let people like me in, sometimes, if we're nice."
The lane ran along the foot of the great black wall for what seemed like half a mile, curving gently, following the curve of the hill. On the other side of the lane there were low brick sheds and stables and a coal yard. At this hour there was nobody about. A single cart stood empty by the coal yard gate, with its horse asleep between the shafts, and somewhere in the sheds a dog barked once, and stopped.
And in the black wall, a little way along, there was a gate.
It was small. After the size of the wall, it looked almost like a toy. An iron gate, black like everything else, set into a deep arch of stone, with spikes along its top and a smaller door cut into one side of it for people on foot. Beside it, built right into the thickness of the wall, there was a tiny gatehouse, no bigger than a coal shed, with one window glowing yellow, and a tin chimney sticking out of the stone with a thread of smoke coming out of it.
Juno walked straight up to the yellow window and rapped on it with her knuckles.
"Wilkin," she said. "Wake up. It's me. I've brought him."
There was a grunt from inside. A creak. The scrape of a chair. And then the little door in the gate opened, with a great deal of rattling of chains and grinding of bolts, and an old man looked out.
He was very old. Older than Mrs. Marta, Corin thought, and she was very old. He was small and bent, in a long gray Ministry coat, very worn, with the brass buttons rubbed almost smooth, and a gray cap pulled down over thin white hair. He had a face like a walnut, brown and creased all over, and a short white beard, and a clay pipe clamped in the corner of his mouth, unlit. His left sleeve was empty. It had been folded up and pinned neatly to his shoulder with a big brass safety pin.
He looked at Juno. Then up at Corin. Then down at Juno again.
"Mouse," he said. Around the pipe. His voice was cracked and dry, like old leather. "You again."
"Me again," said Juno cheerfully. "This is Mister Vane. Harvester. Third Basement. Toby said to bring him by the side, not the front. You remember Toby. I came yesterday with a message for him and you fetched him up."
"I remember Toby." Wilkin looked at Corin. His eyes were small and pale and very sharp under the brim of his cap. "I remember you, too, Harvester. You come in the front. Every day for six years. You never once come round the back." He chewed the stem of his pipe. "Why now?"
Corin did not know what to say. So he said what was true.
"Because somebody's watching the front," he said. "I think."
Wilkin looked at him for a long time. Then he took the pipe out of his mouth and spat neatly into the gutter, and put the pipe back.
"Ah," he said. "Like that, is it."
He stood aside.
Corin did not go in at once. He stood on the cobbles of Archive Lane, in front of the little door in the gate, and felt in his pockets, one by one, the way the Ledger said he did every morning, the way his hands seemed to know. Left outside: keys, a small empty glass jar with a brass lid, and a small heavy knife with a yellowed bone handle that he did not remember owning. Right inside, buttoned: a flat square of black cloth, sewn shut all the way round, heavier than it should be. It was warm. Very slightly warm, through the cloth, like a cup of tea that has been left to stand. He took his hand away from it quickly. Right side, the long narrow pocket: the rod. Cold. Left inside, over his heart: the new Ledger.
Everything was where it should be.
Juno had been watching him check. She did not say anything about it. She just waited till he was done, and then she pointed along the black wall, past the gate, to a place about thirty paces further on, where the cobbles of the lane met the foot of the wall.
There was a grating there. Low down, almost at the level of the cobbles. Iron bars, thick and black and rusted, across a square opening in the stone about the size of a small window. Warm air was breathing out of it in a slow steady stream. Corin could see it shimmering. And under the warm air, coming up from somewhere deep below, he could hear a sound. A low, heavy, endless growl, like a dragon asleep.
"Boiler room," said Juno. "That's the grate. It goes down right next to the boiler room steps, inside. Toby told me. He says he eats his lunch down there, where it's warm." She looked up at Corin. "So if you want me, that's where you send. Shove a note out through the bars and whistle. I'll be on the coal shed roof, over there, all morning. I'll see."
"What if I don't need you?"
"Then I'll sit on the coal shed roof all morning and eat Wilkin's biscuits." She said it as if it were the most reasonable thing in the world. "And at noon I'll come by the grate anyway. In case. Rule…" She frowned. "What number are we up to?"
"Five."
"Rule five, then. Same day." She grinned. "I listen at doors. I heard the posh lady say it."
She was turning to go when Wilkin spoke again. From the doorway. Around the pipe.
"Mouse."
Juno stopped.
"That key," said the old man. He was looking at her chest. At the big brass key hanging on its dirty string against her patched coat. He had looked at it before, Corin thought. Many times. But not like this. "You still wearing that."
Juno's hand went up and closed round the key.
"Always," she said.
Wilkin did not say anything for a moment. His walnut face did not move. But his one hand, his right hand, had come up from his side, slowly, and the thumb was moving. Slowly. Back and forth. Across the side of his first finger. As if it were remembering something it used to hold.
Then he saw Corin looking. And the hand went down.
"Go on, then," he said gruffly to Juno. "Get off with you. There's biscuits in the tin on the shelf. Don't take more than two."
Juno went. Across the lane, fast and quiet, and up the side of the coal shed by the drainpipe, and over the edge onto its sloping tin roof, and there she sat, with her knees pulled up, small and dark against the gray morning, watching.
Corin stepped through the little door in the gate.
Inside, there was a yard. A long narrow cobbled yard, running along the inside of the great black wall, with heaps of coal and stacks of empty crates and a row of handcarts, all piled with bundles of paper tied up with string. Paper everywhere. Damp and gray and waiting. At the far end, a pair of wide wooden doors stood half open into the building, and beyond them there was darkness, and the smell of ink and dust and old stone.
Wilkin was bolting the little door behind him. He did it slowly, one-handed, with great care, every bolt in turn. Then he turned round.
"Through there," he said, jerking his chin at the wooden doors. "Down the paper stairs. Past the boiler. You'll come out by your Third Basement in a hundred steps. You won't see a soul at this hour, only rats and the boiler man, and he's deaf."
"Thank you."
"Don't thank me." Wilkin put his pipe back between his teeth. He looked up at Corin from under his cap, with his small pale sharp eyes. "Harvester."
"Yes?"
"That girl. Mouse. She'll have told you, I expect. About her grandpa. About his door."
Corin did not answer. He did not have to.
Wilkin nodded slowly.
"Thought so," he said. "She tells everybody, in the end. Everybody she trusts." He chewed the pipe stem. "Well. I'll tell you something too, then. Since she trusts you." He took the pipe out of his mouth. "I sat the night door next to his. Night Door Two. Thirty-one years. Terrence Marta on Three, and me on Two, and a wall between us you could knock on. We knocked on it. Every night. Twice at midnight, once at the fourth bell, just to say still awake, still here." His voice did not change. It stayed dry and cracked and flat. But his hand had started moving again at his side, the thumb going back and forth. "Eight years ago, at the fourth bell, I knocked. And nobody knocked back."
The yard was very quiet.
"I went round," said Wilkin. "I wasn't supposed to leave my door. I went anyway. And Three was locked. From the inside. And there was a light under it. A blue light. Bright. So bright it came out under the door and lay on the stone like water." He stopped. "And there was a smell."
Corin did not breathe.
"Like a chapel," said Wilkin. "On a funeral day. Flowers." He put the pipe back in his mouth. "I banged on the door. Nobody came. Then two Inquisitors came down the corridor and took me by the arm, my good arm, and walked me back to Two, very polite, and sat me down, and one of them stood outside my door till the sixth bell. And in the morning they told me Terrence had been taken ill in the night."
"And Night Door Three?"
"Bricked up," said Wilkin. "The very next week. Brick and plaster. Painted over. You can walk past it now and never know there was a door at all. There's a fire bucket hanging there." He looked at Corin. "And I'll tell you one more thing, Harvester, and then I'm going back to my chair and my fire, and you never heard any of it from me. Two men asked about Night Door Three. After. Clerks. Nosy young clerks, the way they are. One in the spring after, and one the year after that."
"What happened to them?"
"They stopped coming to work," said Wilkin.
He turned his back. He went into his little gatehouse and shut the door, and through the yellow window Corin saw him lower himself into his chair by the tiny stove, slowly, with his one hand on the arm of it, and sit there, looking at the fire, with his thumb moving back and forth, back and forth, across the side of his first finger.
Corin stood alone in the long damp yard among the bundles of paper.
Then he took out the Ledger and wrote, with his back against the cold black wall:
Wilkin. Gatekeeper, Archive Lane. One arm. Sat Night Door Two, thirty-one years, next to Terrence on Three. They knocked on the wall: twice at midnight, once at the fourth bell. Eight years ago, at the fourth bell, nobody knocked back. Three was locked from inside. Bright blue light under the door. Smell of funeral flowers. Two Inquisitors walked him away. Three was bricked up the next week. There's a fire bucket there now. Two clerks asked about it later. They stopped coming to work.
And under it:
Wilkin's thumb moves the same way as Terrence's.
He put the Ledger away, and went in through the half-open wooden doors, into the dark, and down.
