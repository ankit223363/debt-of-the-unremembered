Chapter 7: The Ministry of Records, Part 4 of 5: Form 88-C
Form 88-C
Corin got up from the big desk and crossed the room and took the paper.
It was heavy. Heavier than paper should be. Thick and smooth and very white, the kind of paper they used on the floors with windows, not the floors with jars. He carried it back to the desk and laid it down on the landslide, on top of an old folder and a cold plate, under the light of the one gemstone lamp, and read it.
MINISTRY OF RECORDS. OFFICE OF THE ARCHON.
FORM 88-C. CLOSURE OF FILE.
In the matter of: DORRAN, PELL. Scribe, Grade Three. First Basement.
Finding: Death by misadventure. A fall. The deceased, being in drink, fell from the loading platform behind the Inkwell printing house, Gutter Lane, and struck his head.
Corin stopped.
He read that line again. Then he turned back through the Ledger, to the pages he had copied out at Bram's counter last night from the old book in his own crooked fast hand. He found it. A Watch report, word for word. SKULL BROKEN. BUT THAT'S NOT IT. HIS HEAD IS EMPTY. NOTHING IN IT. CLEAN AS A SLATE. NEVER SEEN THE LIKE.
He turned back to the form.
The file is closed by order of his Excellency. No further inquiry is to be made by any Section.
All residue, field notes, objects, papers and materials of any kind associated with the deceased are to be surrendered to the Office of the Archon this day.
The Harvester assigned to the matter (VANE, C., Senior Investigator, Harvesting Section) is relieved of it, with the thanks of the Office, and is reassigned from tomorrow until further notice to the Intake Hall of the Oblivion Bank, Lantern Quay, under Warden Briggs.
An Auditor of the Oblivion Bank will attend the Harvesting Section tomorrow at the ninth bell to close the Dorran account.
The Harvester will sign below to show that he has read and understood this order.
And under that, a line for a signature. And a line for a date. And the seal. Black wax, round, smooth, pressed deep. The open book. The closed eye. The tiny crown.
Corin read the whole thing twice more. Then he looked up.
Lucian had not moved from the edge of his chair. He was watching Corin read. He had watched every line, Corin thought, the way you watch someone walk across ice that you have walked across yourself, knowing exactly where it cracks.
"Being in drink," said Corin.
Lucian's mouth pressed itself together.
"Pell Dorran ate bread and dripping on the boiler-room steps every day," said Corin, "so he could save his money in a sock in his boot for a sister who was twelve years old. Toby says he drank cider once a year, at the harvest feast."
"I did not write the finding," said Lucian, very low. "I only copied it. Three times."
"Who wrote it?"
Lucian did not answer.
Toby had come up behind Corin's chair. He was reading over Corin's shoulder again. Corin heard him breathing. Short, quick breaths, through his nose. When he got to being in drink, the breathing stopped altogether for a moment. When he got to the end, it started again, faster.
"Field notes," said Toby.
His voice was not loud. But it was not a squeak, either. It was a strange, tight, careful voice, the voice of a man who has just seen something on a form that he does not like, and is going to say so, even though every bone in his body is telling him not to.
Lucian looked at him.
"All residue, field notes, objects, papers," said Toby. "Field notes." He pointed at the line with one inky finger, not quite touching the paper. "Does the Office of the Archon mean, by field notes, the Harvester's Grounding Ledger?"
Lucian said nothing.
"Because," said Toby, and his voice shook, just slightly, and then steadied, "if it does, Scribe Lucian, then I must respectfully draw the attention of the Office to the Regulations of the Harvesting Section. Book One. Regulation Fourteen. Paragraph Three."
He did not go to a shelf. He did not open a book. He stood behind Corin's chair with his hands at his sides, and his spectacles crooked, and his tuft of hair sticking up at the back, and he said it from memory, word for word, in his clear small careful clerk's voice, the way a child says a lesson it has learned by heart.
"The Grounding Ledger of a Harvester is the personal property of the Harvester. It may not be taken, read, copied, held, or destroyed by any Office, Section, or person, for any cause, as its loss would put in danger the mind of the Harvester and the safety of the Section, and of all persons near him."
He stopped. He took a breath.
"Signed by the Archon," he said. "Not this Archon. The one before. But nobody ever took it out. I know. I checked. I check all the Regulations every spring, when they send down the new ones, to see which have been taken out." His voice wobbled. "That one is still in."
The room was very quiet.
Lucian was looking at Toby. Not the way an important man looks at a junior clerk. Corin could not quite read the way he was looking. It was something like surprise. And something like relief. And something else underneath both, small and quick and hidden, like a fish under a dark pond.
"Yes," said Lucian at last. Softly. "Yes. That is correct." He looked down at his hands on the black book. "I checked it. On the stairs. Three times. Before I came in." A pause. "I hoped somebody down here would know it."
Toby blinked.
"The form does not mean the Ledger," said Lucian. "It cannot mean the Ledger. Regulation Fourteen. Paragraph Three." He said it the way you might repeat a password. "I will write that, in the margin of the copy for the file. Grounding Ledger excluded, Reg. 14.3. In my own hand. So that nobody upstairs can say it was forgotten."
He looked at Corin.
"But the rest of it stands, Harvester," he said. "Residue. Objects. Papers. Anything belonging to Scribe Dorran, or found with him, or taken from where he left it. Today."
Corin did not move.
He was thinking of a red book bound in calfskin, with a rose and thorns on the cover, lying somewhere in two small rooms over a stable on Linden Walk. He was thinking of a black square of cloth, sewn shut, sewn again in a cross, warm in the pocket on the right side of his coat. Three stones, in black silk, with two letters pressed into the wax with a pin. From Pell's drawer, said a line in the Ledger. Don't open it. Not near ink.
The square was warm. Warmer, now, than it had been all morning.
His hand had moved. He had not told it to. It had gone to his coat, to the right side, and was resting there, flat, over the buttoned pocket. As if it were keeping something in. Or as if something were keeping it there.
Lucian's eyes went to his hand.
They stayed there for a moment. One breath. Two.
Then they came back to Corin's face, and nothing in Lucian's narrow pale face had changed at all.
"Do you have anything to surrender, Harvester?" he said. "Anything at all of Scribe Dorran's?"
Rule one, said the Ledger, in Vespera's words, in his own hand. No lies between you.
But Lucian was not his partner.
"The residue went to the Inquisition on Tuesday," said Corin. "Inquisitor Halloway took it from my hand on Needle Street. It's on the form. Form twelve-B. Toby sent it up the pipe."
"I have read Form twelve-B," said Lucian. "Three times."
"Then you know what I had."
"I know what is on the form."
They looked at each other across the long cold room.
It was not an answer. Corin knew it was not an answer. And he knew that Lucian knew. He could see it in the large dark anxious eyes, in the pressed-together mouth, in the hands lying very flat on the black book. Lucian had heard what Corin had not said. He had heard it the way he heard a form that had not been filled in correctly.
And he did not say anything about it.
"Then there is nothing to surrender," said Lucian slowly. "According to the form." He took a small pen from an inside pocket of his black coat, and a tiny bottle of ink, and opened it, and held them out across the room. "Please sign, Harvester. To show that you have read the order, and understood it."
Corin looked at the pen.
5. You will be asked to stop. Don't.
He got up, and crossed the room, and took the pen and the little bottle from Lucian's hand. Lucian's fingers were very cold. Colder than the room. Like a man who has been standing outside in the snow for a long time.
He went back to the desk. He dipped the pen. He looked at the line for his signature, under all the small black print, under the Harvester will sign below to show that he has read and understood this order.
Read and understood.
Not obeyed. Not agreed. Not will do as he is told.
Understood.
He looked up at Lucian. Lucian was watching him. And Corin saw, or thought he saw, the smallest thing happen at the corner of that pressed-together mouth. Not a smile. Lucian did not look like a man who smiled often, or easily. Just a tiny loosening. Like a knot that has been tied too tight for a long time, letting go by the width of one thread.
Corin signed.
C. Vane. In his big untidy hand that sloped hard to the right, pressing so deep that the pen nearly cut through the thick white paper.
He dated it. Thursday. He did not know the number of the day. He wrote Thursday anyway, and nobody told him it was wrong.
He blotted it, and carried it back across the room, and gave it to Lucian with the pen and the little bottle of ink.
Lucian took them. He looked at the signature. He read the whole form through again, from the top. Then a second time. Then a third. His lips moved very slightly as he read. When he had finished the third time, he took his pen and wrote something very small in the margin, beside field notes, and blotted it, and showed it to Corin without being asked.
Grounding Ledger excluded. Reg. 14.3. L.
Then he put the form back in the leather folder, and tied the black ribbon, one loop and then the other, and stood up.
"Thank you, Harvester," he said. "Thank you, Junior Clerk. The Office is obliged."
He went to the iron door. Toby went with him, to unbolt it. One. Two. Three. Lucian watched the bolts go back, and his lips moved, counting.
Then, with his hand on the door, he stopped.
He did not turn round. He stood with his back to the room, very straight and very thin in his perfect black coat, with the leather folder under his arm and the slim black book held flat against his chest, and he said, in a voice so soft and so careful that Corin almost did not hear it:
"His Excellency asked me to give you a message, Harvester. Not on the form. In words."
"What message?"
Lucian was quiet for a moment.
"He said, Tell Harvester Vane that Master Hale sends his regards."
The rod went cold.
Not the ordinary cold. The other cold. The cellar cold, the dead-hand cold. It came through the wool of Corin's coat and through his shirt and into the skin of his side, so sudden and so deep that it took his breath away, and he stood there by the big desk with one hand pressed flat against it and could not speak.
Lucian did not turn round to see.
"I do not know what it means," he said, to the door. "I did not ask. I do not ask his Excellency what things mean." A pause. "But I have been his secretary for four years, Harvester. And he has never once before asked me to carry a message in words. Only on forms."
He opened the door.
"Check your work," he said. "Three times."
And he went out, and up the cold stone spiral, and they heard his quick precise footsteps going up, and up, and up, one turn, two, until they were gone.
Toby bolted the door. All three bolts. Then he stood with his back against it, the way he had after Storage, and looked at Corin.
Corin had not moved. His hand was still pressed against his side. The cold was still there in the rod. Deep. Steady. Like a block of ice packed against his ribs.
Malakor, he thought.
Nothing.
Malakor. You said his name. In Storage. You said Linus.
For a long time there was nothing at all.
And then, very far down, so faint that it was hardly a voice at all, only a feeling, like the cold breath that comes up out of a deep well:
He's alive, then.
That was all. Nothing else would come.
Corin took his hand away from his side. He sat down slowly in the big creaking chair. He opened the Ledger. And he wrote, under A FRIEND OF THE MINISTRY, under THE SAME, in letters that shook:
Form 88-C. Pell's file closed. "Death by misadventure. Being in drink, fell and struck his head." A lie. Everything of Pell's to be surrendered today. I said there was nothing. Lucian heard what I didn't say. He didn't say anything. I signed "read and understood." Not "obeyed."
Ledger safe: Reg. 14.3. Toby knew it by heart.
From tomorrow: reassigned to the Intake Hall, Oblivion Bank, Lantern Quay. Warden Briggs.
Tomorrow, ninth bell: an Auditor of the Bank comes here to close the Dorran account.
The Archon says: "Tell Harvester Vane that Master Hale sends his regards."
Master Hale gave me the rod. Master Hale was in the black carriage. Malakor knows him. Malakor says: "He's alive, then."
He stopped. He looked at what he had written. Then he wrote one more line, and underlined it twice, the way he had underlined Tuesday on the second page.
The Archon knows I've been looking. He wants me to know he knows.
The Misprint
For a while neither of them said anything.
Toby stayed with his back against the iron door. Corin stayed in the big chair. The lamp hummed. The jars hummed. Far above, through all the rock, the pens of the Great Hall went on scratching, like rain that would never stop.
Then Toby pushed himself off the door, and walked very slowly to his own neat desk, and sat down at it, and put his face in his hands.
"Being in drink," he said, through his fingers. "Being in drink. He'd have laughed. He'd have laughed so hard. That big stupid donkey laugh. Me? In drink? On a Monday? With what money?" His voice cracked. "And now it's written down. On the best paper. With the Archon's seal on it. And in a hundred years, when somebody opens the box, that's all there'll be. Pell Dorran. Scribe. Grade Three. Fell down drunk."
Corin did not say anything. There was nothing to say that would help.
After a while Toby took his hands away from his face. His spectacles were fogged. He took them off and wiped them on his sleeve, slowly, one lens and then the other, and put them back on. And then he did something Corin did not expect.
He bent down and opened the bottom drawer of his neat desk. The deep one. It stuck. He pulled harder, and it came out with a squeal, and he reached right into the back of it, behind a box of pen nibs and a ball of string and a tin that rattled, and brought out a flat bundle of paper, tied round with a piece of faded pink tape.
He untied the tape. He laid the bundle on his desk, under the candle. And he looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at Corin.
"Come and see," he said. "Please."
Corin got up and went.
It was a stack of forms. Twelve or fifteen of them. Thick white paper, the same heavy smooth paper that Lucian had brought down. Printed in black, with boxes, and lines, and a great deal of very small print.
MINISTRY OF RECORDS. OFFICE OF THE ARCHON.
FORM 88-C. CLOSURE OF FILE.
Blank. Every box empty. Every line waiting. In the matter of. Finding. The file is closed by order of his Excellency. Nothing filled in at all.
Except at the bottom.
At the bottom of every single one, in the place where the seal should go, there was a seal. Not wax. Ink. Printed right onto the paper by the same press that had printed the boxes and the lines. Round and black and perfect. The open book. The closed eye. The tiny crown.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
"They were a mistake," said Toby quietly. "A misprint. Two springs ago. The Ministry sends its forms out to be printed, at the big presses in the Ink Quarter. Somebody at the press put the wrong plate in. The seal plate. It's meant for the Archon's notices, the ones they paste up on walls. It was never meant to go on a form." He touched the edge of the top sheet, very lightly, with one inky finger. "A whole batch came back like this. Five hundred. With the Archon's seal already printed on every one."
"What happened to them?"
"They were meant to be pulped. All of them. A form with a mistake on it has to be sent back to the Stationery Office on F with a Form three-R, Return of Spoiled Stationery, signed by the Supervisor of the Section, and then the Stationery Office pulps them." Toby's ears had gone pink. "Somebody upstairs sent the whole batch round all the Sections by mistake, before anybody noticed. We got fifteen. I filled in the Form three-R. I took it up to Supervisor Brightwater to sign." He swallowed. "He was asleep. With his cat on his lap. And I didn't like to wake him."
"So you kept them."
"I meant to take them back. The next day. And then the next day there was a jar that broke, and the day after that the paper allowance was cut, and…" Toby trailed off. "And they were in the back of my drawer. And I never quite…" He stopped. "I know. I know. Five years and I never lied on a form, and I've had fifteen spoiled forms in my drawer for two years without a three-R." He looked at Corin with a kind of miserable defiance. "It's the only rule I've ever broken. Before today."
Corin looked down at the top form. At the empty boxes. At the black printed seal at the bottom, perfect, official, waiting.
The file is closed by order of his Excellency.
No further inquiry is to be made by any Section.
"Toby," he said slowly. "If someone filled one of these in. Any file. Any name. And sent it up the pipe. What would happen?"
Toby went very still.
"It would be closed," he whispered. "Whatever it was. It would look exactly like a real one. The seal's real. It's the real plate. Nobody upstairs could tell the difference. Not without holding it up to the light and feeling for the wax, and nobody ever does that. Nobody ever questions an eighty-eight C. That's the whole point of an eighty-eight C." He stared at the stack. "You could close anything. An Inquiry. An Inquisition file. A… a warrant. Anything."
They both looked at the forms.
The candle flame bent in the draft from the door, and straightened.
"I'm not saying we should," said Corin.
"No."
"I'm not saying we will."
"No."
"I'm saying," said Corin, "don't take them back."
Toby looked up at him. His eyes behind the thick lenses were very wide and very frightened. But under the fear there was something else. Something Corin had seen in them once already that morning, in the dark of Storage, when a thin cold hand had closed round his wrist. Something like a rabbit that had decided, against all sense, to stand.
"No," said Toby slowly. "No. I don't suppose I will."
He tied the pink tape round the stack again. Carefully. A neat bow. Then he did not put it back in the drawer. He sat looking at it for a moment, thinking. Then he got up and went to the shelves on the far wall, behind his desk, where there were no jars. Only books. Old ones. Heavy and fat and bound in brown leather gone gray with damp, with their titles stamped on their spines in gold that had mostly flaked away. He ran his finger along them until he found the one he wanted. The fattest and the oldest and the dullest-looking of them all.
REGULATIONS OF THE HARVESTING SECTION. BOOK ONE.
He took it down. It was so heavy that he had to hold it against his chest with both arms. He carried it to his desk and opened it at the back. The back cover was thick, made of two layers of board glued together with leather over them, and at the bottom corner, where the leather had come away a little from the board, there was a gap. A thin dark gap between the two layers, like a mouth that has been shut for a very long time.
Toby slid the stack of forms into the gap. All of them. It took a little pushing. When they were in, he pressed the leather down over the corner with his thumb, and smoothed it, and it closed.
You could not tell. You could not tell at all.
"Nobody ever opens Book One," said Toby. "Nobody. Except me. Every spring. To check which Regulations they've taken out." He put it back on the shelf, in its exact place, between Book Two and an old ledger with no name on its spine. "And now Scribe Lucian knows I know it by heart. So he'll never need to look at it either."
He came back and sat down at his desk and looked at the empty place where the forms had been. Then at his own hands.
"Inspector," he said.
"Yes."
"What Lucian said. About the Archon. About…" He stopped. He did not seem to know how to say it. "He's very kind about it. Let me help you not to make that one again. And then he doesn't remember how."
"I heard."
"He's been his secretary for four years." Toby's voice was very quiet. "Four years of checking every form three times. Four years of being helped." He swallowed. "How many things do you think he doesn't remember how to do anymore?"
Corin did not answer. He thought of a young man as thin as a pen, sitting on the very front edge of a chair, with his hands spread flat over a black book as if it might fly away. He thought of a margin note in a small careful hand. Grounding Ledger excluded. Reg. 14.3. L. He thought of a report from Storage, sent back down the slow pipe because one box had been left empty.
"Enough to be frightened," he said at last. "And not so many that he's stopped being brave."
Toby looked at him. Then he nodded, slowly, as if that were something he would want to write down later.
Corin took out the Ledger. He wrote, under all the rest:
Toby's drawer: 15 blank Form 88-C, from a misprinted batch two springs ago. The Archon's real seal printed on every one by mistake. Never returned (no 3-R signed). If filled in and sent up, would close ANY file. Nobody questions an 88-C. Now hidden in the back cover of REGULATIONS, BOOK ONE, on the shelf behind Toby's desk.
He looked at it. Then he added, very small:
Don't use them. Not yet.
And the clock on the wall said a quarter past the tenth bell, and the pipe above Toby's desk said nothing at all, and somewhere far above them, Corin thought, a report from Storage was going slowly down a slow pipe toward an old man with a very good nose.
