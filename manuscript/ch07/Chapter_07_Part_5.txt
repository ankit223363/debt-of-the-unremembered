Chapter 7: The Ministry of Records, Part 5 of 5: Wren
Wren
The second knock came at the eleventh bell.
It was not three heavy blows, like Brack's. It was not three small taps and then three more, like Lucian's. It was one knock. Just one. Firm and clear and quite loud, the way you knock on a door when you expect it to be opened and do not intend to knock twice.
Toby looked at Corin. Corin looked at the door.
"Ask who it is," said Corin.
Toby went to the door. He did not unbolt it. He put his mouth close to the small square of thick cloudy glass and said, in a voice that was trying very hard not to shake: "Who is it, please?"
A pause. Then a voice through the iron, clear and cool and polite, like well water.
"Inquisitor Halloway. For Harvester Vane. Alone, if he will. Or with his clerk, if he won't." Another pause. "I'm not here to take anything. You have my word."
Toby turned his head and looked at Corin with enormous eyes.
Corin opened the Ledger. He did not need to. He knew what it said. Inquisitor Halloway. Young. Brown hair. Kross's. Took the jar. Asked what he hummed. When I hummed it, she knew it. I think. Something brown, pressed in the front of her notebook. And below it, from yesterday, in a pigeon loft: Halloway isn't in today. "Sick." She had come on her own.
He closed it.
"Let her in," he said. "And stay."
Toby unbolted the door.
She came in out of the dark of the stairs like a lamp being carried into a cellar. That was what it was like. The white coat caught the little light there was, the yellow of Toby's candle and the cold blue of the one gemstone, and gave it back, so that for a moment she seemed to glow in the long gray room among the jars. She was not tall. She was slim and straight-backed, and very neat, from the top of her brown hair, pulled back so tight from her face that it looked painted on, to the shining toes of her boots. Small boots, Corin noticed. Narrow. With new square heels.
Her face was pale and sharp and careful, with a long nose and a narrow chin and dark straight eyebrows drawn across it like two strokes of a pen. She had a small black notebook in one gloved hand. No pencil. And at her throat, where the high white collar of her coat closed, on the left side, the Ministry's sign was embroidered in silver thread. The open book. And above it, an eye. Not closed.
Open. Wide open. Staring.
She stopped just inside the door, and looked at Corin, and Corin looked at her.
He did not know her. He had read her name a minute ago. He knew she had taken a jar from his hand on Tuesday, on a street called Needle Street, and asked him a question that had not come from a form. He knew that yesterday she had stood on Mrs. Marta's step at the seventh bell, and caught a tram going east, and stood in a cellar doorway on Hook Lane, very still, watching an old woman's window.
That was all he knew.
But when he looked at her face, the three notes came into his throat. All by themselves. Up. And up. And up. He swallowed them down.
She saw him swallow. Something moved behind her eyes, very fast, like a bird flying past a window.
"Harvester," she said.
"Inquisitor."
"You won't remember me."
"No. But I wrote you down."
She looked at the book in his hand. Then at her own. The small black notebook in her glove. And for a moment her sharp careful face did something that was almost, not quite, a smile.
"So do I," she said.
She did not sit. She came a few steps into the room, not far, and stood in the space between Toby's neat desk and Corin's landslide, where she could see them both, and the door, and the jars. She did not look at the jars the way Lucian had, as if they might reach for her. She looked at them the way you look at a crowd of people you do not know, politely, without fear.
"I'll be quick," she said. "I'm not supposed to be here. I'm not supposed to be anywhere today. I'm sick." The not-quite-smile again, thinner. "Inquisitor Brack told your clerk so yesterday, I think. And laughed."
Toby went pink.
"Three things," said Wren Halloway. "Then I'll go."
She held up one gloved finger.
"One. The jar you gave me on Needle Street. Dorran, P. Last minutes. Stable. Do not open. He hummed." She said the label word for word, in the same cool voice, without looking at her notebook. "I carried it myself to the Inquisition's vault under the east tower. I put it on the shelf myself. Seal unbroken. Brass lid tight. And the mist was there, inside, turning. I saw it." She paused. "On Wednesday morning, I went to look at it again. I don't know why. I wanted to." Another pause. "The seal was still unbroken. The lid was still tight. The label was still in your hand."
"And?"
"And the jar was empty," said Wren.
The lamp hummed.
"Not a thread," she said. "Not a wisp. Clean glass. As if it had never held anything at all." She looked at him very steadily. "I've been an Inquisitor for four years, Harvester. I've handled a thousand jars. I've never seen one emptied with its seal still on. I asked an old man in the vault how it could be done. He said there's only one way he's ever heard of. Through the glass. Without opening it. With a rod. An old kind of rod, and an old kind of harvester, who knows how to draw Ash through a wall the way a magnet draws iron through a table."
Corin's hand had gone to his side. To the long narrow pocket. He made it stop.
She saw it anyway.
"I didn't say it was you," she said quietly. "It wasn't you. You were in a printer's shop on Needle Street with Lord Thorne's daughter on Tuesday night. And on Wednesday morning you were asleep in an old woman's chair on Hook Lane." A beat. "I know. I was watching."
Toby made a small sound.
Corin did not.
"Two," said Wren. She held up a second finger. "While I was standing on your landlady's step yesterday morning, someone went into your room. Over the roofs. I didn't see them go in. I saw them come out, from the street, when I'd finished with your landlady and walked up to the corner and looked back." Her mouth tightened. "A dark shape on a white sky. Very quick. Very good. Going toward the river."
"You didn't follow."
"I can't run on roofs, Harvester. I can barely run on stairs." A flicker of something like irritation, at herself. "I knew I'd never catch them. So I did the only other thing I could think of. I took the tram east, to the place I thought they might be going next. Or the place I thought you were." She paused. "Hook Lane."
"You stood in a cellar door."
Her eyebrows went up. Very slightly.
"An old woman with a knife saw you," said Corin. "And a white thread on a splinter."
Wren looked down at the left sleeve of her white coat. At the shoulder. There was a tiny pull in the weave there, a small loop of thread standing up out of the cloth, where something sharp had caught it.
She looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked up at Corin, and this time the almost-smile was real.
"You are good," she said. "Even when you don't remember." Then it went away. "I stood there an hour. Watching that old woman's window, waiting for the dark shape to come over the roofs. It never came. I don't know where it went. I only know it went to your room first, and it took nothing, and it left something."
"How do you know it left something?"
"Because that's what she does," said Wren. "She leaves things."
The room went very still.
"Who?" said Corin.
Wren did not answer. She looked at him for a long moment with her careful pale face. Then she looked down at the small black notebook in her gloved hand. And she opened it, at the front, at the inside of the cover, and held it out toward him, turned so that he could see.
There was something pressed there. Flat and thin and brown and very old, tucked into a small paper pocket that had been glued inside the cover. He could not see what it was. Only the edge of it, dry and curled, like a leaf. Or a petal. Or a feather.
And above it, written on the inside of the cover in faded pencil, in a round, careful, childish hand, the hand of someone just learning letters:
Keep this. Don't let them take it.
She closed the notebook again before he could see more.
"I don't know who," she said. "That's the honest answer. I don't know her name. I've never seen her face. But I've been an Inquisitor for four years, and Inquisitor Kross's student for longer, and in all that time, whenever something has gone missing from a place where it should not go missing, and something else has been left behind in its place, there has always been the same smell in the room afterward." She paused. "You know which smell."
Corin did not need to say it. Toby said it for him, in a whisper.
"Lilies."
Wren did not look at Toby. She kept looking at Corin.
"Three," she said. And now her voice had changed. It had gone lower, and quicker, and less cool. "Tomorrow, at the ninth bell, the Bank is sending an Auditor here. To close Scribe Dorran's account. You know that. It's on the eighty-eight C." She did not ask how he knew she knew about the eighty-eight C. "Harvester. Listen to me. The Bank's Auditors read people. By touch. Skin on skin. They put their hand on yours, and they see your balance. Every memory you've got, and every memory you've lost, and every one you've borrowed, like a column of numbers. What you're owed. What you owe." Her eyes were very steady. "And you are carrying a great many things that aren't yours."
Corin's hand was on his side again. On the cold rod. He did not take it away this time.
"Don't let her touch you," said Wren. "Not your hand. Not your face. Nothing. Wear your gloves. Keep the desk between you. If she asks to shake hands, don't." A pause. "This one's new. I don't know her. They say she came up from the Bank's own school. They say she's the best they've had in fifty years. They say she can read a man's whole life in the time it takes to say good morning." She took a breath. "And they say she has never once in her life forgotten anything she read."
The room was very quiet.
"Why are you telling me this?" said Corin.
Wren looked at him for a long time.
Then, without answering, she did something he did not expect.
She hummed.
Very softly. Through her nose. With her mouth closed and her eyes on his face. Three notes. Up. And up. And up. Exactly the same as his. The same three notes that had come out of a dead man's last minutes in a dark lane, and had lived in his throat ever since, asking their question.
And then a fourth.
It came down. Down from the third note, softly, gently, the way a bird comes down onto a branch. The answer to the question. The end of the line.
Corin's chest hurt.
It was not a small hurt. It was a sudden, huge, terrible ache, right in the middle of him, behind his breastbone, the place where the Ledger sat. As if someone had put their hand flat on his chest and pressed, hard, and kept pressing. His eyes stung. He did not know why. He did not know anything. He only knew that the fourth note was right. That he had been waiting for it, without knowing he was waiting, for as long as he could remember. Which was not saying much. And which was saying everything.
Wren had stopped humming.
She was looking at him. And her pale careful face had changed too. The coolness had gone out of it. What was left was young, and lost, and very frightened, and very, very far away. Like a girl standing at the end of a long road, looking back at something she cannot see.
"Where did you learn it?" said Corin. His voice was hardly there.
"I don't know," said Wren Halloway. "I've always known it. As long as I can remember." She swallowed. "I was raised in the orphan school on the Hill. Inquisitor Kross found me there when I was nine and took me for his student. I don't know where I was before the school. Nobody does. There's no file." A pause. "I asked. Once. There's no file."
She put the small black notebook back inside her white coat, over her heart, the way he kept the Ledger.
"I've never heard anyone else hum it," she said. "Not once. In my whole life. Until Tuesday. On Needle Street. A dead scribe's last minutes, in a jar, and a harvester in a gray coat, and three notes." Her voice cracked, just a little, on the last word, and she stopped, and pressed her lips together. "That's why I'm telling you. That's the only reason. I don't know what it means. I don't know if it means anything."
She turned to the door.
"Wren," said Corin.
She stopped. She did not turn round.
He did not know he knew her first name until he said it. It was not in the Ledger. It had come from somewhere else. From somewhere very deep, and very old, and dark. He did not know where.
She stood very still, with her back to him, in her white coat, with her gloved hand on the cold iron of the door.
"Nobody calls me that," she said, very quietly. "Nobody's called me that since the school." A pause. "How did you know?"
"I don't know."
She was quiet for a long moment.
Then she said, without turning, "Don't let the Auditor touch you, Harvester." And she unbolted the door herself, one, two, three, and went out, and up the stairs, and was gone, and the white of her coat went up into the dark like a candle being carried away.
Toby bolted the door behind her. He did not lean against it this time. He just stood there with his hand on the bottom bolt, very still.
"Inspector," he said, without turning round. "You've gone white."
Corin did not answer. He opened the Ledger. His hand was shaking so much that the pencil skidded on the page, and he had to stop, and hold his wrist with his other hand, and start again.
Wren Halloway. Inquisitor. The Dorran jar was emptied in the Inquisition's vault, seal unbroken. Only an old kind of harvester, with an old kind of rod, can draw Ash through glass. She was on Mrs. Marta's step while someone went into my room. She saw them leave over the roofs. She went to Hook Lane. She stood in the cellar door an hour. Her sleeve has a pulled thread at the shoulder.
"That's what she does. She leaves things." The lily woman.
Something brown, pressed in a paper pocket in her notebook. Above it, in a child's hand: "Keep this. Don't let them take it."
Tomorrow's Auditor reads people by touch. DO NOT LET HER TOUCH YOU. Gloves. Desk between.
Wren hummed the three notes. And a fourth. Coming down. The end of it. It hurt.
She doesn't know where she learned it. Orphan school on the Hill. Kross found her at nine. "There's no file."
I called her Wren. It isn't written anywhere. I don't know how I knew.
He stopped. He looked at the last line for a long time.
Then, under it, very slowly, he wrote the four notes. Not as words. As a little line on the page, going up, up, up, and then, at the end, coming down. Like a hill. Like the roof of a house. Like the shape of a bird's wing.
It looked like the wings of the little bird on a string, cut into the wall under WE REMEMBER, on the stairs at No. 14 Tallow Street.
The Boiler-Room Step
At noon, Toby said, "We have to eat."
He said it in the voice of a man reading out a Regulation. Then he opened the bottom drawer of his desk again, the deep one, and took out a small parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, and held it up.
"Bread and dripping," he said. "And an onion. I bring two, now. Since the girl." He looked at it. "I used to bring two before. For Pell. Then I didn't need to. Then the girl ate mine, so I brought two again." He swallowed. "It's a habit. I don't know how to stop."
"Don't stop," said Corin.
They went up the cold stone spiral, one turn, past the closed wooden door of the Copying Hall, where the pens scratched behind the wood like hens in a yard, and along the dim corridor, and down the short flight of stone steps to the boiler room. The heat came up to meet them like a hand. The great black iron belly growled on its bed of brick. The deaf man in the leather apron was sitting on an upturned bucket in the far corner, eating something out of a tin with his fingers, with his eyes shut. He did not open them.
Toby sat down on the bottom step. Not in the middle of it. On the left side, close to the wall, where the stone had been worn into a shallow dip by a great many years of somebody sitting in exactly that place. He left the right side empty.
Corin looked at the empty right side for a moment. Then he sat down on the step above it, instead, so that it stayed empty.
Toby did not say anything. But he unwrapped the brown paper parcel very slowly, and when he handed Corin his half of the bread, his hand was not quite steady.
They ate.
The bread was dense and sour and the dripping was salty and the onion made Corin's eyes water, which was a relief, because his eyes had been wanting to water all morning and now they had an excuse. The boiler growled. The heat soaked into his back through his coat. Low down in the wall beside the bottom step, the iron grating breathed cool air in from Archive Lane, and the cool air met the warm air of the boiler room and made a little whispering draft that stirred the dust on the stone.
Halfway through the bread, the draft whistled.
Not the draft. Something on the other side of it. A thin, sharp, two-note whistle, through somebody's teeth. Up and down. Like a bird that has learned to be rude.
Toby jumped.
Corin bent down to the grating. Through the rusted bars, at the level of his face, he could see a slice of gray daylight, and a slice of cobbles, and a pair of scuffed boots. Then the boots folded up, and a face came down to the bars from the other side. Small and sharp and smudged with soot, under a cap. Bright dark eyes. A brass key swinging on a string.
"Noon," said Juno. "Rule five. Anything?"
Corin did not answer at once. He took out the Ledger, and tore nothing out of it, and instead took one of the old handbills he had found folded in the back of it that morning, a blank-backed one that said GRAND SALE OF FINE SILKS, and spread it on his knee, and wrote on the back of it. Fast. Small. Everything that Lyra needed to know, the way Lyra's rule said, the same day, even if it was bad.
Lyra. Rule five.
Ministry Storage, this morning, with Toby:
1. Carriage Book. The morning after your father burned: Carriage 9 went to your old house. Passengers: Harvester L. Hale, and "one guest, see sealed note 7." Sent by the Office of the Archon. Brought back "one case, sealed" to the Lower Levels. It's not in our Section. Toby would know.
2. Hale is the man who gave me my rod. I don't remember him. The Archon sent me a message: "Master Hale sends his regards."
3. Council Benevolent Fund: your father's payments stopped last autumn "by request of the family." Then: "Private arrangement made. C." A round C with a hook on top. Do you know anyone in your family whose name begins with C?
4. Doctor Aske drowned. Found below the Lantern Bridge steps. NINE DAYS BEFORE the carriage. Bertram sent Oren to fetch a man who'd been dead a week.
5. The Night Door book for Juno's grandfather is gone. But Door Two's isn't. At the fifth bell that night, a lady in white went out past Door Two and said she was "a friend of the Ministry." Your nurse's envelopes say "from a friend." The same.
6. Pell's file is closed. "Fell, in drink." A lie. I signed that I read it. Not that I'll obey it.
7. From tomorrow I'm sent to the Bank's Intake Hall. And tomorrow at the ninth bell, an Auditor from the Bank comes to read me. By touch. I've been told not to let her.
I'm all right. Toby is all right. Tell Oren. Bolt the door. C.
He folded it small and pushed it through the bars. Juno's grubby fingers took it.
"Linden Walk," she said. "The posh lady." She tucked it into her cap. Then she paused, with her face close to the bars, and looked at him through them. "She's been at it all morning, you know. Your posh lady. I went by at the ninth bell, on the way here. Sat on the stable roof a bit." A pause. "Tapping. On the wall in the old man's room. I could see her through the window. Four. Then she waits. A long time. Then four again."
"Does he answer?"
Juno shook her head.
"No," she said. "But she doesn't stop."
Then her face went away from the bars, and the boots unfolded, and there was a scuff of cobbles, and she was gone.
Corin sat back on the step.
Toby had been watching. He had not said anything all the time Corin was writing. He had read over Corin's shoulder, the way he did, and his face had gone paler and paler with every numbered line. When Corin got to Bertram sent Oren to fetch a man who'd been dead a week, he had put his bread down on the step and not picked it up again.
Now he said, very quietly: "Who's Oren?"
"A man who loses words," said Corin. "To keep other people safe."
Toby looked at him. Then he looked down at his own hands, and at the bread, and at the empty right side of the step beside him, worn smooth by somebody who would not be sitting there again.
He put his hand into the inside pocket of his too-big gray coat, and took out the red sock.
It was faded to the color of dried roses, and darned at the heel with blue thread, and it clinked when he held it. The top of it was knotted shut with string. He held it in both hands, in his lap, the way you might hold a small animal that was cold.
"I've still got it," he said. "Nan's money. And her letter." He looked at it. "I promised I'd find her. I said, I'll take it to her myself. You were there. You heard me."
"I wrote it down."
"And now his file's gone. Withdrawn. By the Archon. And there's no address on the letter, and the village isn't on any map, and I don't know where she is, Inspector. She's twelve. She's waiting for letters. She's practicing her handwriting so she can be a scribe like him. And the letters have stopped, and she doesn't know why, and I've got her brother's money in a sock in my pocket and I don't know where to take it." His voice broke. He pressed his lips together hard. "Every day I carry it, I think, today. Today I'll think of something. And I don't."
Corin looked at the red sock for a long time.
Then he said, "There's a button."
Toby looked up.
"On his cuff," said Corin. He did not remember it. But it was in the Ledger, copied out last night at Bram's counter, in his own crooked hand, from a page in the old book about a body on a rubbish heap. Right cuff. Old brass button. Oak leaf. "From home." Village not on the maps. "An old brass button with an oak leaf on it. His gran told him to keep it safe and not show it at the Ministry. Nan said so in the letter."
"Yes," said Toby slowly. "I remember. You found it. On his sleeve."
"I saw another one," said Corin. "Yesterday morning. In a tray on a clock-mender's bench on Hook Lane. Among the springs and the wheels. A brass button with a little leaf." He did not know why he had written it down at the time. He had written it anyway. An oak-leaf button in Faber the clock-mender's tray. "Somebody else came from the same place, Toby. Or knew somebody who did. Somebody who sold a button to a clock-mender for a copper, or traded it, or lost it." He looked at the sock in Toby's hands. "Buttons don't walk to Hook Lane on their own."
Toby stared at him.
Then, very slowly, his thin pale face changed. It did not light up, the way it had at the door that morning, like a shutter being opened. It did something quieter than that. It set. The way a face sets when somebody has decided something, and is not going to change their mind.
"Faber," he said. "Hook Lane."
"He's deaf in one ear and blind in one eye. And he owes a woman called Vespera for three tattoos."
"I'll go," said Toby. "On Saturday. When the Section's shut." He put the red sock back into his pocket, and buttoned the pocket over it, and patted it, once. "I'll go and ask him where he got his button."
He picked up his bread again. He ate it. All of it. And then the onion, too, in three big bites, with his eyes streaming behind his thick spectacles, and he did not wipe them, and Corin did not say anything about it.
The boiler growled. The deaf man in the corner had fallen asleep on his bucket with his head against the warm brick and his tin in his lap.
They went back down to the Third Basement at the half hour after noon.
And at the first bell after noon, nothing happened.
And at the second bell after noon, nothing happened either. Corin sat at the big desk with the Ledger open and the rod cold in his side and his eyes on the brass hatch in the pipe above Toby's desk, and the hatch stayed shut, and the pipe stayed quiet. Somewhere far above them, on K, at the very top of the tower, a young man as thin as a pen would have read Storage Keeper Fennick's corrected report by now. Three times. And placed it before his Excellency.
Nobody came down the stairs.
That was worse, Corin thought, than if they had.
At the fourth bell after noon, when the light in the little cloudy window of the iron door had begun to go from gray to a darker gray, and Toby had straightened every pen on his desk eleven times, the pipe above Toby's desk went whoosh.
And thump.
And a brass cylinder dropped out of the hatch onto the blotter.
Toby did not jump this time. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he picked it up, slowly, and turned it in his hands, and looked at the seal on the cap.
It was not the Archon's black wax with the open book and the closed eye. It was gold. Pale gold wax, pressed with a little picture Corin had never seen before. A pair of scales, like the ones a merchant uses to weigh coins. And on one side of the scales, a coin. And on the other, a tiny eye. Open.
"The Bank," whispered Toby. "It's from the Bank."
He broke the seal. He took out the paper. It was thick and cream-colored and smelled faintly of something dry and clean, like the inside of a new purse. He unrolled it, and read it, and then, without a word, he held it out to Corin.
The handwriting was beautiful. That was the first thing Corin noticed. Small and very upright and perfectly even, every letter exactly the height of every other, the lines as straight as if they had been ruled. A hand that had never in its life made a mistake. Or never let one show.
THE OBLIVION BANK. FOURTH DESK. AUDIT AND CLOSURE.
To the Harvesting Section, Ministry of Records.
In the matter of the account of DORRAN, PELL, now closed by order (88-C):
An Auditor of this Bank will attend your Section tomorrow at the ninth bell precisely, to read, settle, and close the account, and to examine the Harvester last in contact with the residue.
Kindly have the Harvester present. Kindly have him ungloved.
And under it, signed in the same small, perfect, upright hand:
Elara.
Auditor, Fourth Desk.
Corin looked at the name.
He did not know her. He did not know anything about her. He did not know her face, or her voice, or how old she was, or whether she was tall or short or cruel or kind. He did not know why a Bank Auditor should sign herself with only one name, like a child, or a queen.
He only knew that his chest hurt.
The same hurt as before. Right behind his breastbone. Where the new stiff Ledger sat. As if someone had pressed a thumb there, gently, and then pressed harder, and harder, and would not take it away.
He opened the Ledger. Not at the back. At the front. At the inside of the cover. At the big black untidy letters, pressed so deep they had cut into the paper, that he had copied out at Bram's counter only last night.
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin.
You were born in Oakhaven.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
He looked from the line in the Ledger to the name on the cream-colored paper. From one Elara to the other. His own hand, big and untidy and sloping, pressing too hard. And hers, small and upright and perfect.
The same five letters.
Toby was watching his face.
"Inspector?" he said, very quietly. "What is it? Do you know her?"
Corin did not answer for a long time.
The lamp hummed. The jars hummed. Far above, through the rock, ten thousand pens went on scratching, writing everything down, so that it would be true.
"No," he said at last. "I don't know her."
He closed the Ledger. He held it shut with both hands, flat, against the desk, the way Lucian had held his black book, as if it might fly away.
"It's a common name," he said. "Isn't it?"
Toby looked at the cream-colored paper. Then at the closed Ledger. Then at Corin.
"I don't know," said Toby. "I've never met anyone called it."
And neither of them said anything else, for a long time, while the little cloudy window in the iron door went slowly dark.
