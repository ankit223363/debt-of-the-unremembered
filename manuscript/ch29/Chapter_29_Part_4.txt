Chapter 29: The Eraser Knocks, Part 4 of 5: The Oldest Words
The Oldest Words
The fourth cellar was full of coal.
He knew it before he saw it. He smelled it. The thick black dry smell of the coal wharf, of chimneys, of stoves that have not been cleaned in a year. And then he saw it. Because there was light ahead. Not much. A dim gray glow, coming down from somewhere high up in the dark, in a long slanting shaft full of floating black dust.
The coal chute.
It came down through the roof of the cellar from the street above, a square tin slide, like the one behind the chandler's that Juno had sent him down on Monday morning. The rain was coming down it. He could hear it, rattling on the tin, and see it, a thin gray trickle running down the slide and dripping off the end. And under the end of the chute, a great black hill of coal. Higher than his head. Spilling out across the floor in every direction, in drifts and heaps and loose black slides.
And at the back of the cellar, past the coal, in the floor, a square.
Iron. Crosshatched. A grating.
He went toward it. Round the side of the coal hill, slipping, stumbling, with the lumps sliding away under his boots and the black dust going up into his face. He was nearly there. Ten steps. Five. He could see the grating clearly now in the gray light from the chute. Old iron, rusty, set into a frame of stone. A padlock on one side, big and black and old. And under the bars, darkness. And the sound of water. Running. Going down.
He got the brass key out of his pocket. His hand was shaking so badly that he nearly dropped it.
"Stop."
The voice came from behind him. Close. Much too close.
He turned.
Rowan Thorne was standing at the edge of the coal, ten paces away. The lantern was in his left hand, held up high, so that its yellow light fell on Corin's face. And in his right hand there was a sword.
It was not a big sword. A cadet's sword. Short and straight and plain, with a simple cross guard and a grip wrapped in black leather. But it was very sharp. Corin could see that from where he stood. The edge of it caught the lantern light in a thin bright line, like a hair of fire. And the boy held it the way a master holds a sword. Easily. Lightly. The point low and steady, not moving at all. As if his arm had been holding swords for twenty years.
Bought, Wren had said. In little stones. From the Bank. His hands know what to do. But he never learned it, so it doesn't feel like his.
The boy's hands did not shake. But his face did.
Corin could see it now. Close. In the lantern light. Young, and pale, and thin. Lyra's face. Lyra's eyes, dark and straight-browed. Lyra's mouth, pressed hard. And under it, under all the hardness, something that was not hard at all. Something that was sixteen, and frightened, and very, very tired.
"Harvester Vane," said Rowan Thorne. His voice cracked on the name and he cleared his throat and said it again, lower. "Harvester Vane. In the name of the Ministry of Records and the Office of the Inquisition, I—" He stopped. He did not seem to know how the rest of it went.
"Rowan," said Corin.
The point of the sword came up. An inch.
"Don't," said the boy. "Don't say my name. You don't know me."
"No." Corin did not move. He stood by the grating, with the brass key in his fist and the coal under his boots. "But I know your sister."
Something went across the boy's face. Fast. Like the shadow of a bird across a window.
"I know," said Rowan Thorne. "Everybody knows. You've been helping her. Running round the city with her. Breaking into houses." His voice went up, high and hard. "She ruined us. Did she tell you that? She ruined us. Our father. Our name. Our house. Everything. And now she's ruined you too. And I'm going to—"
"She writes to you," said Corin quietly. "Every month."
The sword point stopped.
"I know she does," said the boy. "I burn them."
"Not all of them."
Rowan Thorne went white.
He stood at the edge of the coal with the lantern in one hand and the sword in the other, and he went white to the lips, and his left hand, the lantern hand, moved. Without his meaning it to. Toward his chest. Toward the flat square shape under his coat. And stopped, halfway, in the air.
"Who told you that?" he whispered.
And from behind him, back across the black length of the old cellars, came a sound.
A long, deep, splitting crash. The sound of old boards giving way all at once. Mrs. Marta's scullery floor. And then a heavy, slow, sliding thump, like something enormous falling a long way down onto earth. And then nothing.
And then, very slowly, very patiently:
Thud.
It was through.
Rowan heard it too. Corin saw him hear it. He saw the boy's head jerk round, toward the sound, and the lantern swing, and the sword point waver for the first time. And in the boy's face, just for a moment, before he could hide it, Corin saw the same thing he had seen in every face that ever heard that sound coming toward it in the dark.
Fear.
Thud.
And a light, pleasant voice, far back in the dark, echoing off the low brick roof.
"Rowan? Have you found him?"
The boy turned back to Corin. His face had gone hard again. Hard and white and set. He lifted the sword. Not low anymore. High. The point at Corin's throat. Close. Very close. Corin could feel the cold of it.
"Here, sir!" called Rowan Thorne. His voice cracked. "He's here! By the grating!"
Thud.
Corin looked at the sword point an inch from his throat.
He looked at the boy's face behind it.
And he looked inside himself.
He did it the way he always did. The way you reach into a bag in the dark for something you know is there. Feeling along the edges of what he had left. Looking for something big enough. Not a smell. Not a taste. Not one evening, or one face, or one afternoon. Something that would burn like a bonfire.
He went through the rooms.
Empty. Empty. Empty. Oakhaven on the doors, in chalk. He went past them. Past the new rooms, the full ones, the ones he had made in the last six years. Juno on the roof. Mrs. Marta's pie. Vespera's laugh like a crow. He could not burn those. He would not. He went past Tam's bed under the slate roof, with the rain on it, and did not stop.
He went back. Further. To the oldest things. The things that had come through the empty rooms with him, the way some things did, without his ever knowing they were there. How to tie a knot. How to read. Which side of the road to walk on.
How to speak.
And there it was.
Not the words he spoke now. Not the words of Veridia, the city words, the Ministry words, the words he had learned again in the long white room with forty beds, from a lady in gray with a bell. Under them. Older. Much older. Lying at the very bottom of him like a stone at the bottom of a well, so deep that he had never once in six years known it was there.
Another language.
He could feel it. A whole room of it, warm and full. Words he had never said, that he did not know he knew. Soft words. Round words. Words with the shape of hills in them, and the sound of a stream, and the smell of an oak tree in summer. The words his mother had said to him. The words she had sung, bending over a narrow bed. The words he had shouted on a rope swing, swinging out so high he could see over the roof of the schoolhouse and down the whole valley.
The words a little girl with a gap in her teeth had used to say his name.
The first words he ever had.
Boy, said Malakor. Very quietly. Very fast. Boy, no. Not that. Not that one. You'll never—
Thud.
Corin took hold of it.
The whole room. All of it. Every soft round word with a hill in it. He took hold of it the way you take hold of a rope, with both hands, and he pulled.
And he burned it.
It went up like a haystack.
He felt it go. All at once. A great rushing roar, inside him, like a fire going up a chimney. Every word, every song, every name his mother had ever called him, every shout from the rope swing, going up together in one blaze. And it came out of him.
Not as light. Not as fire. As a sound.
A word.
One word. In a language nobody in Veridia had heard for eighteen years. It came out of his mouth and out of his chest and out of every bone in him, all at once, and it was not loud, not exactly, but it was enormous. It filled the old cellar the way water fills a bowl. It went up into the low brick roof and down into the coal and out along the dark between the pillars, and everything it touched rang. The bricks rang. The iron of the grating rang. The coal hill shivered and slid. The rain on the tin chute stopped, just for a moment, as if the rain itself had heard and was listening.
The lantern in Rowan's hand burst.
The glass went outward in a shower of bright pieces, and the flame went up once, tall and gold, and went out. The sword flew out of the boy's hand. Corin saw it go, spinning, flashing, over the coal, into the dark. And the boy himself went backward. Not thrown. Pushed. Gently. The way the sea pushes you back up the beach. He went back three steps, four, and his heels caught on the coal, and he sat down hard on the black slope with his arms out, and stayed there.
The word ended.
The ringing went on for a moment, in the bricks and the iron. Then it faded. And there was only the gray light from the chute, and the rain starting again on the tin, and the dust settling, very slowly, black and soft, through the gray air.
And far back in the dark, the thudding had stopped.
Corin stood by the grating.
He was shaking. All over. His knees. His hands. His jaw. He opened his mouth, and he did not know what he had been going to say. He shut it again.
Something was gone.
He could feel the shape of where it had been. A room. At the very bottom of him. Under all the others. A big room, that had been warm, and full, and was now empty and cold and swept to the boards. He could not remember what had been in it. He only knew that something had. Something very old. Something that had been his before anything else was.
He tried to think of a word. Any word. The word for mother.
Mother. That came. The Veridian word. Flat and plain. Mother.
But there had been another one. Under it. Older. Softer. He knew there had. He could feel the hollow where it had lain.
He reached for it.
Nothing.
On the coal, Rowan Thorne was sitting with his arms out and his face white, staring at Corin. Not angry. Not hard. Just staring. With his mouth a little open. Like a boy who has heard something he does not understand, and knows he will never forget.
And as Corin looked at him, he saw that something had fallen out of the boy's coat.
It was lying on the coal beside his hand. Flat. Square-cornered. Crumpled. Cheap cream paper, gone soft and gray with handling. With a dark brown stain across one corner. And on the front, in small fierce writing that slanted hard to the right:
Rowan.
The Grating
The boy saw where Corin was looking.
He looked down at the letter on the coal beside his hand. And his face, which had been white, went red. Dark red. All at once, from his collar to his hair. He grabbed at the letter. His fingers were black with coal dust, and he left four black marks on the cream paper as he snatched it up and pushed it back inside his coat, flat against his chest, and held it there with his whole hand spread over it.
"It's nothing," he said. His voice shook. "It's not—I was going to burn it."
"All right," said Corin.
His own voice sounded strange to him. Thin. Far off. As if it came from the other side of a wall. He did not know if that was because of the word he had just burned, or because his ears were still ringing, or because he was so tired that his whole body felt like a coat hung on a nail. He put his hand on the edge of the grating to steady himself. The iron was cold and wet and rough with rust.
"You haven't opened it," he said.
The boy said nothing.
"She wrote it the night before the Hollow," said Corin. "Didn't she. Before she went into the Black Tower. Before she knew if she'd come out."
Rowan Thorne stared at him.
"The stain," said Corin. "On the corner. That's hers. She had a cut on her hand. The day after." He did not know how he knew that. Then he did. Wren had told him. In the kitchen, yesterday, with the beach stone on the table between them. He keeps one. I've seen it. There's blood on it. He doesn't know I've seen. "You kept it because it might have been the last one."
"Shut up," whispered the boy.
"It wasn't the last one," said Corin. "She's alive. She's well. She's on a barge on the river right now with your father. She got him out. Did they tell you that? She got him out of the Hill."
"Shut up."
"Read it," said Corin. "That's all. You don't have to do anything else. Just read it."
Thud.
Close now. Very close. Two cellars back. Maybe less. And the sound of brick falling, as if something was too big for the old arches and was simply walking through them.
"Rowan!" The light voice again. Not so pleasant now. Sharper. "Answer me, boy!"
Rowan Thorne did not answer.
He sat on the coal, with his hand flat over his chest, and looked at Corin. And Corin looked back. And for one long moment neither of them moved. Then the boy's eyes went, very slowly, to the side. To the dark where his sword had gone spinning. And then back. And he did not get up to fetch it.
Corin turned to the grating.
His hands were shaking so hard that it took him three tries to get the brass key into the padlock. The first time he missed the hole altogether, and the key scraped across the iron with a sound like a nail on a slate. The second time it went in halfway and stuck. He pulled it out. He looked at it in the gray light from the chute. The little brass tag. The number 4, stamped deep. And there was a curl of rust on the end of the key, where it had been sitting in Mrs. Marta's dresser drawer for thirty years. He rubbed it off on his sleeve.
The third time, it went in.
He turned it.
Nothing happened.
He turned it harder. The key bent. He could feel it bending in his fingers, soft old brass, and he thought: if it breaks, that's the end. If it breaks, I'm standing in a coal cellar with an Eraser two rooms away and nowhere to go.
Thud.
And the padlock clicked open.
It was such a small sound. A dry, tired little click, like an old man clearing his throat. Corin pulled the lock off and threw it on the coal. He got his fingers through the bars of the grating and pulled. It was heavy. Much heavier than it looked. He pulled, and his shoulders cracked, and the grating came up an inch, two inches, with a long scraping groan of iron on stone, and then stuck.
He pulled again. It did not move.
And then there was another pair of hands on the bars beside his. Black with coal dust. Thin. Young.
Corin did not look round. Neither did the boy. They pulled together. And the grating came up. Slowly. Groaning. Up and up, until it stood on its edge, and then it fell back against the wall with a crash that shook the coal hill and sent a black slide of lumps rolling down across the floor.
Under it was a hole.
Square. The width of a man's shoulders. Lined with old brick, black and wet. And iron rungs going down the side of it, the way they went down the side of the scullery trap. And at the bottom, not far, eight feet maybe, nine, the gleam of moving water. He could hear it. Rushing. Hurrying. A loud, cold, busy sound, full of echoes. And the smell coming up out of it. Wet stone. Old leaves. Rot. And under the rot, very faint, the river.
Corin looked at the boy.
Rowan Thorne was kneeling by the hole with his black hands on his knees. He was breathing hard. He would not meet Corin's eyes.
"Why?" said Corin.
The boy shook his head. He did not know. Or he knew and would not say. Or he could not say, because there were no words for it in him yet, the same way there was now no word for mother in Corin. A hollow where it should be.
"Go," said Rowan Thorne. "Just go. Before I—just go."
Corin swung his legs over the edge of the hole.
His boots found the first rung. Then the second. The iron was slick and cold and furry with something green. He went down three rungs, until his head was level with the floor of the cellar, and he stopped. He looked up. The gray light from the chute. The black coal. The boy's white face, looking down at him.
"Tell her," said Corin. "When you read it. Write back. Tell her you read it."
The boy's mouth opened.
And from the far side of the coal hill there came a light.
Not lantern light. Not candle. A soft, steady, silvery glow. The kind of glow that comes from the tip of a rod, held up high in a dark place by somebody who is used to dark places and does not care for them. It came round the edge of the coal, growing. Throwing long black shadows. And with it, footsteps. Two kinds. A light, quick, careful pair, like somebody walking on ice. And another.
Heavy. Slow. Patient.
Thud.
Corin let go of the rung.
He dropped.
It was further than he thought. The water was deeper than he thought. He went into it feet first, and it closed over his knees, over his waist, cold, so cold that it stopped his breath, and his feet hit a sloping floor slimy with mud and went out from under him. He fell backward. The water went over his head. Black. Loud. Pulling. He came up choking, with his hands out, and hit a wall, and grabbed at it, and found a crack between two bricks, and held on.
The water was pulling at his legs. Hard. Wanting to take him down the slope, away into the dark. He held on to the crack. His fingers were slipping. He found another crack with his other hand and held on to that too. And he pressed his face against the wet brick, and kept still, and listened.
Above him, through the square of the grating hole, he could see the gray glow change to silver.
And he heard voices.
"Where is he?" Kross. Out of breath. Not pleasant at all now. "Rowan. Where is he?"
Nothing. The boy did not answer.
"Sir." Another voice. A girl's voice. Flat and quick and level. "Sir, look. The grating's up. He's gone down."
Wren.
Corin pressed himself flat against the wall. He did not breathe.
"Down," said Kross. He was standing at the edge of the hole now. Corin could see the silver glow brighten. He could see the edge of a fog-gray coat, and the toe of a polished boot. "Then down we go. Eraser. In."
"Sir, it won't fit." Wren again. Quick. "Look at the hole, sir. It barely fit through the trap. It'll stick in that, and then we'll have to dig it out, and that's an hour gone. And he'll be at the river."
A silence.
"Then where does the drain go?" said Kross. His voice was very soft. "You read the plans, Halloway. You read everything. Where does it go?"
Another silence. Shorter.
"It joins the big sewer under Wharf Street, sir," said Wren. "And the big sewer goes two ways. Left, down to the river mouth by the Customs House. And right, back up under the Hill. Under the old Ministry cellars. There's a door down there, sir. From the sewer into the Ministry's own basement. It was bricked up a hundred years ago, but the records say it was opened again during the war."
"So?"
"So he's a Ministry man, sir," said Wren. "Eleven years. He'll know about that door. He'll go right. He'll go up under the Hill, into the Ministry, into the Archive. Into the drawers. That's where he took the rod from, sir, six years ago. He knows those cellars better than anyone alive. If I were him, sir, and I wanted to vanish, that's where I'd go. Right under your feet. The last place you'd look."
Corin, holding on to the wet brick in the black rushing water, felt his heart stop.
She was lying.
There was no door. Or if there was, he had never heard of it. He did not know the Ministry cellars at all. He had been down into the Archive drawers once in six years, and that was the night he stole the rod, and he had nearly not found his way out. And she knew that. She had his file. She had read everything. She knew he would go left. She had told him to go left. She had drawn it on the map in her own neat hand, with an arrow, and the word LEFT underlined twice.
And she was standing at the top of the hole, with the silver light on her sharp pale face, sending them right.
"Right," said Kross slowly. "Toward the Hill."
"Yes, sir."
"Into the Ministry."
"Yes, sir. I can show you the way down, sir. There's a stair from the Archive. We'll go in at the top and meet him coming up."
A long silence.
Corin hung on the brick. The water roared past his legs. Cold. So cold that he could not feel his feet anymore. He held his breath and waited and thought: he knows. He'll know. He reads people the way I read Ash. He'll hear it in her voice and he'll know.
"Rowan," said Kross. "Is that where he said he was going?"
Corin shut his eyes.
The boy had seen. The boy had been at the grating, with his hands on the bars beside Corin's. The boy had heard Corin say nothing at all about where he was going. And the boy had a letter in his coat, unopened, and a sister on a barge, and a whole life that had been bought and sold for him in little stones.
"Rowan."
"I didn't hear, sir," said Rowan Thorne. His voice was flat. Dead flat. Like a stone dropped on a floor. "I was on the coal. He did something. A word. I couldn't hear anything after. My ears are still ringing."
A very long silence.
Then the silver glow moved away from the edge of the hole.
"The Hill," said Kross. "Halloway, you're with me. Rowan, take the Eraser back up the way we came. Slowly. Don't let it break anything else. Mrs. Marta's floor is going to cost the Ministry quite enough." A pause. And then, lighter, almost pleasantly again: "And Rowan. Fetch your sword. A cadet doesn't leave his sword lying in the coal. It's untidy."
"Yes, sir."
Footsteps. Going away. The light quick pair, and the polished boots. And the silver glow going with them, back round the coal hill, fading, until the square of the grating hole above Corin was only gray again.
And then, slowly, heavily, going the other way:
Thud.
Thud.
Thud.
Fading.
Corin did not move for a long time.
When at last he looked up, there was a face in the square of gray. Small. Pale. Black with coal. Looking down.
It did not say anything. It looked down at him for a moment, in the dark water, holding on to the wall. And then it went away. And a moment later something came down through the hole, turning over and over, and landed with a small wet slap on the water beside him and began to float away.
He grabbed it.
It was a barley-sugar wrapper. Folded small. Very small. Into a hard tight square, the size of a fingernail. Folded exactly the way Kross folded his.
He held it in his wet fist, and looked up at the empty square of gray, and the grating came down over it with a long iron groan and a crash, and the gray went dark.
