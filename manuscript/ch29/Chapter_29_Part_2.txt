Chapter 29: The Eraser Knocks, Part 2 of 5: Under the Potatoes
Under the Potatoes
The trap was heavier than it looked.
He knelt on the wet flagstones of the scullery beside the copper, and pushed back the rag rug, and got his fingers under the iron ring, and pulled. Nothing. He pulled harder. The square of old dark wood came up an inch, with a long dry groan from the hinges, and a breath of cold air came up through the gap. Cold and damp and earthy, smelling of soil and coal and old sacking and, very faintly, of ale.
He heaved. The trap came up and over and stood open on its hinges, leaning against the wall.
Under it, a square of black.
Not gray. Not dim. Black. The kind of black that has no bottom. He could see the top of a ladder, two rungs of it, gray wood, going down. And then nothing. As if the ladder went down into the night itself.
He knelt on the edge and looked down into it, and his hands went wet.
He had known it would be dark. He had known it since Wren put the point of her pen on the square in the scullery floor and said Here. Under the potatoes. With the trap shut over your head. He had known it all night, and all the long walk home in the rain, and all through Mrs. Marta's porridge. He had known it and not let himself think about it.
He thought about it now.
You used to fear the dark.
Not used to. He still did. Every night. Every single night of his life that he could remember. On the bad nights it came up out of the corners of his little room at the top of the house and pressed down on his chest like a hand, and his heart went like a drum, and he could not breathe, and he lay there with the sweat running cold down his sides and his eyes wide open, staring into nothing, until the gray came up in the window. He did not know why. Nobody had ever told him why. It was the oldest thing in him. Older than his name.
And now he was going to climb down into it, and shut the lid over his head, and sit in it, alone, for most of an hour. Without a light. Because a light would show through the cracks round the edge of the trap.
He knelt on the flagstones and looked down into the black.
Steady, boy, said Malakor, behind his ear. Very quietly. Steady. It's only a cellar. Potatoes and coal and old Terrence's ale. That's all that's down there. I'd tell you if there was anything else.
Would you?
I'd tell you.
Corin took a breath. He put the canvas bag over his shoulder, and Juno's bundle under his arm. He put his foot on the first rung.
And went down.
It was like going down into cold water. The dark closed over his feet first, then his knees, then his waist. The ladder was old and the rungs were rough and some of them bent under his weight with a soft creak. He counted them. One. Two. Three. The square of gray light above him got smaller. Seven. Eight. The smell got stronger. Soil and coal and sacking. Eleven. Twelve.
His boot touched the floor. Hard-packed earth, dry, gritty, with something lumpy and round under his heel that rolled away. A potato.
He stood at the bottom of the ladder and looked up.
The trap was a square of dull gray, very high above him. Higher than he had thought. Twelve rungs did not sound very many, but they were long rungs, far apart, and the cellar was deep. He could see the edge of the copper up there, and the corner of the scullery window, and the gray falling rain beyond it. The last of the day.
He climbed back up four rungs. He reached up. He got his hand on the edge of the trap door where it leaned against the wall, and he pulled it toward him, slowly, so that it would not bang. It came over. It got heavier as it came. He held it, with his arm shaking, as it came down over his head, and the square of gray got narrower and narrower, a slot, a crack, a line.
And went out.
The lid settled into its frame with a soft dull thud, and the dark came down on him like a blanket.
He stood on the ladder in the black and did not breathe.
It was total. He had known it would be, and it was worse. It was not the dark of his room at night, where there was always a little gray from the window, or the blue glow from the landing lamp under the door. It was not the dark of the rope-walk, where there had been Vespera's breath beside him and the rope under his hand. It was nothing. Nothing at all. He held his hand up in front of his face and could not see it. He could not see the ladder. He could not see the rung his feet were on. He could not tell, if he shut his eyes and opened them again, which was which.
His heart started to go.
He felt it start. Down low, under his ribs. A thump. And then another, faster. And then faster. And his breath went short and quick and shallow, and the sweat came out on his palms where they gripped the rough wood of the ladder, and the black pressed in on him from every side, close, closer, like the lid of a box, and there was something in it, there was something in the dark with him, he could feel it, right behind him, breathing—
Count the things you can hear.
His own voice. In his own hand. On the first page of the list at the back of his Ledger. FOR THE DARK.
He counted.
The rain. On the scullery roof, above him, through the trap. A soft steady roar. That was one.
The drip of water somewhere in the cellar, off to his left, slow and regular. Plink. Plink. That was two.
His own breath. Ragged and quick. That was three.
That's three. You're still here.
He was still here.
He made himself come down the four rungs. Slowly. Feeling for each one with his toe. One. Two. Three. Four. The earth floor under his boot. He let go of the ladder with one hand. Then the other. He stood on the floor of the cellar in the total dark with his hands empty at his sides and his heart going like a hammer, and he made himself breathe. In. Out. Slowly.
The dark is only the room with the light gone out. Everything that was in it is still there.
The potatoes were still there. The coal. The sacking. Old Terrence's barrel of ale. The ladder. The walls. The floor under his feet. All of it, exactly where it had been when the light was on. The dark had not taken any of it away. The dark had only taken his eyes.
Mrs. Marta snores.
She did not. Not now. She was gone, with Juno, through the rain, to the Watch house on Rope Street. The house above him was empty. There was nobody in it at all. Not in the kitchen, not in the passage, not on the ninety-one steps, not in his little room under the roof with the bed made and the candle cold.
Nobody. Except whatever was in the dark with him.
There's nothing in the dark with you, boy, said Malakor. Only me. And I'm in your pocket.
Corin's heart went on hammering.
And then, without meaning to, without deciding to, he found that he was saying the line. Not the ones in his own ordinary hand. The other one. The one on the very last page, glued to the back cover, in the hand that shook.
He said it under his breath. Hardly a whisper. Into the black.
"The dark is the day with its eyes shut."
And something happened.
It was the same thing that had happened in Wren's attic, at two in the morning, by the candle. Only stronger. A knock. Very soft. From the other side of a door, deep down in him, at the far end of all the empty rooms. One knock. As gentle as a hand on a sleeping child's shoulder.
And the hammering in his chest slowed.
Not all at once. Not all the way. But it slowed. And his breath came a little longer. And the black, pressing in on him from every side, eased back. A little. The way a crowd eases back when somebody says Give him room.
He said it again.
"The dark is the day with its eyes shut."
When it opens its eyes again, said a voice, very far away, very young, in his memory or in his dream or somewhere he could not reach, it'll be morning.
Corin stood in the cellar under Mrs. Marta's scullery, in the total dark, with the rain roaring on the roof above him and the water dripping somewhere to his left, and he was afraid. He was still afraid. He would always be afraid. But he could breathe.
He felt his way to the back wall.
It took a long time. He went with his hands out in front of him like a blind man, shuffling, feeling the floor with his feet before he put his weight on it. He tripped over a sack of potatoes and went down on one knee and got up again. His hand found the cold curved side of a barrel, and the smell of old ale came up off it, sour and thick. Terrence's barrel. He went round it. His hand found the wall.
Brick. Cold and damp and gritty under his fingers. He felt along it. Up, and across, and down. Rough brick, and rough mortar, crumbling a little where his fingers pressed. And then, low down, at about the height of his hip, the brick changed.
It was newer. He could feel it. Smoother, and squarer, and the mortar between the bricks was different, softer, sandier, as if it had been put in by somebody in a hurry who did not much care whether it held. It went across in a rough arch, about as wide as his shoulders, and as high as his chest.
A doorway. Bricked up.
The bricks are loose, Wren had said. You can push them.
He put his hand flat on the middle of the arch and pushed. Gently.
Something moved. A brick. Just a little. He felt it shift under his palm, with a soft gritty sound, like a tooth moving in a gum.
He did not push it any further. Not yet. He took his hand away, and stood with his back to the old bricked-up doorway, facing the dark where the ladder was, and the trap above it, and the house above that.
And he waited.
He did not know how long. There was no way to tell, in the dark. He counted, for a while, and lost count, and started again. He thought about Juno, running over the wet roofs toward the river with the rain in her face. He thought about Wren, putting on her white coat in her attic, and buttoning it to the throat, and looking at herself in a piece of mirror, and making her face very still. He thought about Rowan Thorne, sixteen, in a cadet's gray coat, with a bloodied letter under it against his skin.
He thought about the red book. Not the red reader, in his Ledger. The other one. The book in his memory, the one he had copied pages out of on the second day, with a rose in thorns on its cover. Witness. K. For my children, if I forget.
K.
He had not thought of that in a long time.
And then, above him, a long way off, through the trap and the scullery and the walls of the empty house, very faint through the roar of the rain, the bell of Saint Bride's began to ring the quarter.
One stroke. Two. Three.
A quarter to eleven.
Corin stood very still in the dark under the potatoes, with his back to a bricked-up door and his hand on the silver rod in his pocket, and listened.
And under the rain, at the top of Tallow Street, very far away, he heard something else.
Thud.
And a long pause.
Thud.
Ten Minutes to Eleven
Thud.
It was not loud. Through the rain and the trap and the scullery and the walls, it was hardly a sound at all. More a feeling. A small dull shiver that came up through the earth floor of the cellar into the soles of his boots, and was gone.
And then a long pause.
And then another.
Thud.
Corin had heard that sound once before. In the dark, crouched behind a water butt, with his heart in his mouth. Two tall gray shapes coming in under the arch of a gate, bending their heads at exactly the same moment, by exactly the same amount, like two trees bending in the same wind. And walking across the cobbles. Not like men walk. Slower. Heavier. Each step set down as if the ground had to be tested before it would be trusted. Thud. And a long pause. Thud.
He had written it down, afterwards. 7 ft. Fog-gray coats. White egg faces. No eyes. They do not hear. Rook saw to that.
They do not hear, he told himself, standing in the dark with his back to the bricked-up doorway. Rook saw to that. They do not hear.
But the man walking in front of them did.
Thud.
Closer now. He could feel it more clearly. A small shiver in the floor, and a tiny rattle somewhere, off to his right, where something on a shelf in the dark was trembling against something else. A jar. A bottle. Mrs. Marta's pickles.
Thud.
And voices.
He could not hear the words. Not through the rain. Not at first. Only the sound of them, coming down Tallow Street from the top, from the chandler's corner, the way Wren had said they would. A man's voice. Light and easy and pleasant, going on and on, the way a man talks when he is walking with a friend and pointing things out. And another voice. Younger. Higher. Answering, now and then. A word. Two.
And under the voices, steady, the thuds. Two sets of them. Not quite together. Thud. Thud. And a pause. Thud. Thud.
Corin closed his eyes. It made no difference to what he could see. But it made it easier to listen.
The voices came closer. Closer. They were passing the cobbler's now, he thought. The cookshop. The pawnbroker with the three brass balls. They were outside No. 12. They were—
They stopped.
The thuds stopped. The voices stopped. There was only the rain.
Corin did not breathe.
And then, very clearly, through the rain and the walls and the empty house, he heard the light, pleasant voice say:
"Here we are."
They were outside No. 14.
"It's not much to look at, is it," said Silas Kross. Conversationally. As if he were showing the young man beside him a house he had once thought of buying. "Five floors. One staircase. I'm told the staircase was built by someone who hated people." A small pause. The rustle of paper. A barley sugar being unwrapped, Corin thought. Even now. Even in the rain. "I always think you can tell a great deal about a man from where he chooses to live. Don't you, Rowan?"
"Yes, sir."
The young voice. Clipped and careful and very correct. The voice of a cadet answering a master on a drill ground. But there was something in it. A tightness. A thinness at the edges, like a wire pulled almost to snapping.
"And what does this one tell you?"
A pause.
"That he doesn't have much money, sir."
Kross laughed. Softly. A warm, kind, friendly laugh.
"No," he said. "No, he doesn't. Harvesters aren't paid very much, I'm afraid. The Ministry has always been mean with its Harvesters." Another pause. "What else?"
"I don't—"
"Look at the door, Rowan. The front door. What do you see?"
A longer pause.
"It's open, sir."
"It's open," agreed Kross. "In the rain. On a Wednesday morning. In the Lower Docks." Corin could hear the smile in his voice. "Now. In your experience, Cadet, which sort of man leaves his front door open in the rain?"
"A man who isn't afraid of anybody coming in, sir."
"Or?"
Rowan did not answer.
"Or a man who wants you to think he isn't," said Kross gently. "Inquisitor Halloway. Would you agree?"
And a third voice.
Cool and clear and polite. The voice of someone reading out the minutes of a meeting. Corin had heard it last night, very quietly, reading the names of the dead out of a black book by candlelight. He heard it now, through the rain, and it was exactly the same, and it was not the same at all.
"The landlady always leaves it open in the daytime, sir," said Wren Halloway. "I noted it on Saturday. And Sunday. And Monday. It's in my report."
"So it is," said Kross. "So it is. Thank you, Inquisitor. You're always so thorough." The rustle of paper again. Folding. Corin could almost see it. In half. And in half again. And again. "Shall we go in?"
And the thuds began again.
Not along the street now. Up. Up the front step of No. 14. Thud. The whole house shivered. Corin felt it in the walls of the cellar around him, a small deep shudder, like a horse twitching its skin to get rid of a fly. Somewhere over his head, a cup fell off a hook in Mrs. Marta's kitchen and broke on the flagstones.
Thud.
In at the front door. He heard the lintel creak. They would be bending their heads now, both of them, at exactly the same moment, by exactly the same amount.
Thud.
Into the hall.
The voices were inside now. Close. Much closer. He could hear them through the floor of the scullery and the floor of the kitchen and the boards of the passage, muffled, but clear enough. Footsteps that were not thuds. Ordinary footsteps. A man's light quick step, on the bare boards of the hall. A heavier step, a boy's, in new boots that creaked. And a third, very even, very quiet. Hers.
"Smells of onions," said Kross.
"The landlady cooks, sir," said Wren.
"Evidently." A pause. The footsteps stopped. Corin thought they must be standing at the foot of the stairs. In the dim gray light from the open door, under the first blue lamp in its cage of rusty wire. Looking up. At the narrow staircase going up and up into the dark. "And the landlady herself? Mrs. M.? And the girl?"
A very small pause.
"I don't know, sir," said Wren. "I haven't seen them this morning."
"No," said Kross thoughtfully. "No, nor have I." Another pause. Longer. "That door. At the end of the passage. The purple one."
Corin's heart stopped.
"The landlady's rooms, sir."
"Is it locked?"
Footsteps. Hers. Quick and light, going down the passage. Toward the purple door. Toward the kitchen. Toward the scullery, and the trap in the floor, and Corin standing under it in the dark with his hand on the silver rod.
The latch of the purple door lifted. He heard it. A small iron click.
"Unlocked, sir," said Wren's voice. Very close now. Just through the kitchen. "The rooms are empty. The stove's still warm. There's porridge in a pot." A pause. "I think they've gone out, sir. To market, perhaps. It's Wednesday."
For a long moment, nobody said anything.
Then Kross laughed again. That soft, warm, kind laugh.
"To market," he said. "Of course. Of course they have." The rustle of paper. "Well. It doesn't matter. It's not the landlady we've come for, is it, Rowan? Not today."
"No, sir."
"No. Today it's the man at the top of the house." The light quick footsteps moved again. Toward the stairs. "Inquisitor Halloway, you'll come up behind. Cadet Thorne, you'll walk with me. And the constructs will go first." A pause. "They always go first. That's what they're for."
The latch of the purple door clicked again. Wren's footsteps came back up the passage. Quick and light. Even. Not hurrying.
And then the thuds started up the stairs.
Thud.
The first step. The whole house groaned. Corin heard the old staircase that had been built by someone who hated people take the weight of something seven feet tall and very, very heavy, and creak, a long, deep, aching creak, like a ship's timbers in a storm.
Thud.
The second step.
Corin opened his eyes. In the dark, it made no difference. He stood with his back to the bricked-up doorway and his face turned up toward the trap he could not see, and he counted.
Three.
Four.
Ninety-one steps.
They had a long way to go.
