Chapter 29: The Eraser Knocks, Part 3 of 5: Three Knocks
Three Knocks
He counted every one.
It was the longest climb of his life, and he was not even on the stairs. He stood in the dark under the potatoes with his back against the cold bricked-up doorway and his face turned up toward a trap he could not see, and he felt the constructs climb the ninety-one steps of No. 14 Tallow Street as if they were climbing up the inside of his own ribs.
Thud.
Eleven.
The house shook with every step. He could feel it in the walls of the cellar. A deep, slow shudder, coming down through the old bricks and the earth floor, as if the whole tall thin building were a living thing that flinched each time it was trodden on. The jars on the shelf in the dark rattled against each other. Dust sifted down from somewhere above him and fell on his face, soft and dry, like the first flakes of snow.
Thud.
Twenty-two.
The first floor. He knew it by the sound. The stairs on the first landing were wider, and the boards were looser, and when the weight came onto them they did not creak, they boomed, like a drum. He heard the boom. And then the pause. And then the thud on the first step of the second flight.
And the voices. Smaller now. Further away. Going up.
"—quite remarkable, really. Rook built them to climb any stair ever made. Even this one. He was very proud of that." Kross. Light and easy, a little out of breath. "Mind the third step, Rowan. It's short."
"Yes, sir."
Thud.
Thirty.
Thud.
Forty.
The third floor. The broken banister. Corin's hand, in the dark, reached out by itself for the rail that was not there. Forty-one. He felt his fingers close on nothing. And far above him, through the walls, he heard a small sharp sound. A boy's voice, startled, cut off.
"Steady, Cadet," said Kross. Kindly. "There's a gap in the rail. I should have warned you. Give me your hand."
And then the climbing again.
Thud.
Fifty.
Thud.
Sixty-six.
The fourth floor. The young couple who never spoke to anybody. Corin wondered, standing in the black, whether they were in there. Behind their shut door. Very still. Listening to the white faces go past on the stairs, a hand's width from their door, and holding each other's hands, and not breathing.
He hoped they were not there. He hoped they had gone to market.
Thud.
Eighty.
Thud.
Ninety.
And the last.
Thud.
Ninety-one.
The top of the house. The tiny landing under the slope of the roof, hardly bigger than a doormat, where the ceiling came down so low that a man had to bend his head. Corin could not imagine how the constructs fitted there. Seven feet tall. Their white egg faces would be pressed against the underside of the slates. They would have to stoop almost double, both of them, at exactly the same moment, by exactly the same amount.
And in front of them, a plain door of gray boards. With a latch. And a keyhole under the latch for a key that had been lost years ago.
His door.
The house went quiet.
Not silent. The rain was still roaring on the roof, on the scullery, on the yard, on all the roofs of the Lower Docks. Water was still dripping somewhere off to his left in the dark. Plink. Plink. But the thudding had stopped. The shaking had stopped. The voices had stopped.
Five floors above him, at the very top of the stairs, something was standing in front of his door.
Waiting.
Corin took his hand off the silver rod in his pocket. He turned, slowly, in the dark, and put both his hands flat on the middle of the bricked-up doorway behind him. On the newer bricks, the smoother, squarer ones, with the soft sandy mortar between them. He felt for the loose one. There. It shifted under his palm, the way it had before, like a tooth moving in a gum.
And he waited too.
The knock came.
He heard it. Even down here. Even through five floors of old boards and plaster and the roar of the rain. Not loud. Not a hammering. A knock. The way you would knock on the door of a sickroom, softly, so as not to startle the person inside.
One.
And a long pause.
The longest pause in the world. He felt it go through him. Rook's moment. So that whoever is on the other side has a moment. One moment. To hide something. Or to run. Or to hold somebody's hand.
Two.
And another pause.
He thought of the room behind the door. The narrow iron bed with its gray blanket pulled straight and tucked in at the sides, the way he never did. The hole in the corner, where you could put your toe through. The table, with the cold candle stub in its saucer. The shelf, with eleven empty jars in a row. The small square window, with the rain running down it in long gray streams.
Empty. All of it. Waiting for nobody.
Three.
And then the pause after the third knock. The one Rook had built in. The length of a long breath.
Corin counted it.
One.
He pushed.
The loose brick went back, out of the doorway, into whatever was on the other side. He heard it fall. A soft gritty thud on earth. Not loud. Lost in the rain.
Two.
He got his fingers into the hole where it had been and pulled at the next one. It came out into his hand. Rough and cold and heavy. He laid it down on the floor at his feet, quietly, the way you lay down a sleeping baby. The next. The next. The hole was as big as his head now. Cold air came through it, from the other side. Older air. Damper. Smelling of stone and water and something else, something sour and green and very old.
Three.
The breath ended.
Five floors above him, at the top of the house, there was a sound.
It was not a crash. It was not a bang. It was a long, slow, splitting groan, like a tree going over in a storm. The sound of old gray boards giving way. Not kicked. Not broken. Pushed. Steadily. By something very heavy and very patient, that had waited exactly as long as it had been made to wait, and not one moment longer.
His door.
Corin stood in the dark with a brick in each hand and his heart going like a hammer, and listened.
Silence.
And then, very faintly, through all the floors and all the walls and all the rain, a voice. The light, pleasant voice. Coming down the stairwell from the top of the house. Not shouting. Not angry. Just a little surprised, the way a man is surprised when he opens a box he was told was full and finds it empty.
"Well," said Silas Kross.
And then, after a long moment, softer:
"Well, well."
The Folded Paper
They came down faster than they had gone up.
Not the constructs. Corin could still feel them, up there at the top of the house, the slow heavy shudder of them turning round on the tiny landing. Very slowly. With great care. Like two great ships turning in a narrow harbor. They would take a long time to come down. Rook had built them to climb any stair ever made. He had not, it seemed, thought very much about how they would turn round at the top.
But the footsteps came fast.
Light and quick, on the stairs. Kross. And behind him, heavier, clumsier, in new boots that creaked, Rowan. And behind them both, very even, very quiet, Wren. Coming down. Fifty steps. Forty. Thirty. Corin heard the boom of the first-floor landing. Then the bare boards of the hall. Then the passage.
The purple door opened.
He heard it. The iron click of the latch. The faint creak of the hinges. And footsteps in Mrs. Marta's kitchen, directly over his head now, through the floor. Not in the scullery. Not yet. In the kitchen. On the flagstones by the stove.
And a voice. Very close.
"He was here," said Silas Kross.
He said it quietly. Thoughtfully. The way a man says something to himself, not to anybody else.
"The bed was made," he said. "Have you ever known a harvester to make his bed, Rowan? I haven't. Not in twenty years. They come home at three in the morning with their heads full of other people's deaths and they lie down in their boots." A small pause. "He made his bed. This morning. And he opened his window. The sill was wet. And three of his jars were empty that weren't empty last week. Fisk counted them."
"Sir."
"He was here. He's been here all morning. He's let his landlady go to market." The light voice was almost admiring. "And he's somewhere in this house. Now. Listening to us."
Corin did not breathe.
There was light. He had not noticed it before. A very thin line of gray light, above his head, a little to the right. A crack. A gap between two of the old boards of the trap, where the wood had shrunk with age, no wider than a knife blade. He had been standing in the black for an hour and his eyes had opened as wide as eyes can open, and now, with the kitchen lamp lit above, or the light from the scullery window, he could see that crack. A thin gray line in the dark. Like a hair on a slate.
He moved toward it. Very slowly. Stepping on the earth floor of the cellar as if it were ice. He came to the ladder. He put one hand on it. He did not climb. He only tilted his head back and put his eye, as close as he dared, to the line of light.
And he could see.
Not much. A strip. A narrow slice of the scullery, the size of a ribbon. The edge of the copper, gray and dull. A bit of the wet flagstone floor. The bottom of the scullery door into the kitchen, standing open. And through it, the kitchen. The leg of the table. The rag rug by the stove. And boots.
Three pairs.
The first were gray. Soft gray leather, very fine, very clean, with a narrow toe, the kind of boots that are made by hand and cost more than a harvester earns in a month. They stood quite still on the flagstones by the stove. Kross.
The second were white. Long and white, with a little silver buckle at the side. Polished so bright that even in the dim kitchen they shone. Wren's. Standing by the table. Very still. Heels together.
The third were black. New. Stiff. The leather still shiny and unscuffed, the laces still bright. A cadet's boots. They were not still. They shifted. Left. Right. The weight going from one foot to the other and back again, the way a boy's feet shift when he has been told to stand still and cannot.
Rowan Thorne.
"What do we do, sir?" said the young voice. Very correct. Very tight.
"We wait," said Kross. "For our friends to come down the stairs. They're not quick, coming down. And then we look. In every room. Under every bed. Up every chimney." The gray boots turned, a little, toward the scullery door. "And in every cellar."
Corin's heart stopped.
"In the meantime," said Kross, "have a barley sugar."
The gray boots stepped toward the black ones. Corin could not see what happened above the boots. He could only hear it. The rustle of a paper bag. A small, kind, fatherly sound.
"Thank you, sir."
"You did well, coming up those stairs. I know they're not easy. The gap in the rail." A pause. "Your father would have been proud of you."
The black boots went still.
For a long moment, neither of them moved. And then the black boots stepped back. Away from the gray ones. Toward the scullery door.
Into the scullery.
Corin pressed his eye to the crack.
Rowan Thorne was standing in Mrs. Marta's scullery. Right above him. So close that if the trap had not been there, Corin could have reached up and touched the toe of his boot. He could see him now. More of him. The black boots, and above them the gray wool of a cadet's coat, long and plain, buttoned to the throat. And his hands.
They were young hands. Long and thin, with bony wrists that stuck out of the coat sleeves because he had grown since the coat was made. A boy's hands, nearly a man's. There was a scar across the back of the left one, white and new, the kind you get from a sword, in practice, when you are learning.
And they were unwrapping a barley sugar.
They did it slowly. Carefully. One end of the twist of paper. Then the other. The sweet itself, gold and clear like amber, lifted out between finger and thumb, and put into the mouth, out of sight above the crack.
And then the paper.
The young hands folded it.
In half.
And in half again.
And again. And again. Smaller and smaller. Pressing each fold flat with the thumbnail. Neat and careful and exact. Until it was a tiny hard square, no bigger than a fingernail.
Exactly the way Silas Kross did it. Exactly. The same folds. The same number. The same small flat press of the thumbnail at the end.
And then the boy's hand did not put the little square into a pocket. It held it. Turned it over. Looked at it. Corin could see, through the crack, the boy's thumb moving on it. Back and forth. Back and forth. The way you rub something smooth that you are not sure you want.
And under the gray wool of the cadet's coat, over the left side of the chest, there was a shape. Flat. Square-cornered. The size of a folded letter. Pressing against the inside of the cloth.
Corin stared at it through the crack.
A letter. Under his coat. Against his skin. With blood on it, Wren had said. From another boy's nose.
The young hand with the scar on it came up, slowly, still holding the tiny folded square of paper. And pressed flat against the coat. Over the shape. Over the letter. Just for a moment. The way Lord Thorne's thin white hand lay on the blanket over his heart. The way Briggs's big red hand went to his chest when he said Never knew nobody. The way you press your hand against a place that hurts, to hold it still.
Then it came away again.
"Rowan?" said Kross from the kitchen. Gently. "What are you looking at?"
The boy's hand went down to his side at once. The little square of paper vanished into his palm.
"Nothing, sir. The floor." A pause. "There's a rug, sir. By the copper. It's been pushed back."
Corin's blood turned to ice.
The gray boots came to the scullery door. Stood there. Corin saw them, through the crack, in the doorway. Very still. Very clean. And then they turned, slowly, a little, toward the corner by the copper. Toward the rag rug, rucked up against the wall where Corin had pushed it back. Toward the square of old dark wood set flush into the flagstones, with its iron ring.
"So there is," said Silas Kross softly.
And from the very top of the house, five floors up, slow and heavy and patient, came the first thud of the constructs starting down.
The Old Cellars
Corin did not wait to see the trap open.
He turned from the ladder and went back across the cellar in the dark, fast, not feeling for the floor anymore, not caring. His shin hit the sack of potatoes and he went over it and came down on his hands and knees and was up again. His shoulder hit Terrence's barrel. He heard it rock, and slosh, and settle. His hands found the wall. The arch. The hole.
It was as big as his head. Not big enough.
He got his fingers into the edge of it and pulled. A brick came away. Another. They were loose, Wren had been right, the mortar soft and sandy and crumbling like stale cake. He did not lay them down gently now. He let them fall. They thudded on the earth floor at his feet, one after another, and he did not care who heard.
Above him, behind him, the iron ring of the trap scraped in its groove.
He heard it. A small sharp grating sound. And then the long dry groan of the hinges as the old dark square of wood came up.
Light fell into the cellar.
Gray light, dim, from the scullery window above. It fell down through the square hole in a shaft, like water poured into a well, and lit the top of the ladder and the first few rungs and a patch of earth floor at the bottom, scattered with potatoes. It did not reach the back wall. Not quite. Corin was in the shadow, at the edge of it, with his hands in the hole in the bricks and his back to the light.
"Mr. Vane?"
Kross's voice. Coming down through the trap. Very gentle. Very pleasant. The voice of a man calling down into a well to a child who has fallen in.
"Mr. Vane, I know you're there. I can smell you. Did you know that? Harvesters smell of Ash. Very faintly. Like a candle that's just been put out." A small pause. "Do come up. It's very damp down there. You'll catch your death."
Corin pulled another brick out of the wall. And another. The hole was as wide as his shoulders now. He could feel the cold old air coming through it, sour and green.
"I promise you," said Kross, "you won't be harmed. Not in the least. My orders are very clear. Gentle. Whole. Nobody is going to put their hands on you. Nobody is going to take anything out of you. We're only going to go for a little walk, up the Hill. There's somebody who'd very much like to see you." The smile came into his voice. "An old friend."
Corin put his head and his shoulders into the hole.
It was black on the other side. Blacker than the potato cellar. Colder. The air was thick and wet and smelled of stone and still water and something very old, like the inside of a well that nobody has drawn from in a hundred years. He could not see anything at all.
He pushed himself through. His coat caught on the edge of a brick and tore. The canvas bag with Mrs. Marta's flask in it banged against the wall. His knees scraped on brick, and then there was nothing under his hands, and he fell.
Not far. A foot. Two. Onto stone. Wet stone, slippery, with a thin skin of water over it that soaked through the knees of his trousers at once, cold as the river.
The old cellars.
He got up. He stood still for one breath, in the black, and listened.
Behind him, through the hole in the bricks, he could see the potato cellar. A gray patch of light at the bottom of the ladder. And on the ladder, coming down, a pair of black boots. New. Stiff. Shining. Feeling for each rung.
"Gently, Rowan," Kross's voice said from above. "Take the lantern. And your sword, I think. Not to use. Just to have." A pause. "And if you find him, call me. Don't do anything clever. He's not dangerous. He's only frightened. Frightened people do foolish things."
"Yes, sir."
The black boots came down. One rung. Two. A light came down with them. Yellow, swinging. A lantern, held out at arm's length. Its light went flickering over the earth floor and the potatoes and the sacking and Terrence's barrel. Toward the back wall.
Corin turned and ran.
He could not see where he was running. He did not care. He ran with his hands out in front of him into the black, and his boots splashed in the water on the floor, and the sound of the splashing went away from him in every direction and came back, the way sounds do in a big hollow place. The old cellars were big. Much bigger than Mrs. Marta's. He could feel it in the sound. In the air on his face. A great low cold space, going away into the dark on every side, with the weight of the whole street pressing down on it from above.
His hand hit a pillar. Brick. Round. He went round it. Another. He went round that. The floor dipped. The water came up over his ankles. It was very cold. He went on.
Fourth cellar, Wren's voice said in his head. The coal merchant's. Under No. 14 and No. 16. Go along the back. Toward the river end of the street. Downhill.
Downhill. He could feel it. The floor was sloping under his feet. Very slightly. The water was running that way. Toward the river. He went with it.
Behind him, light.
He looked back. He could not help it.
The lantern had come through the hole in the bricks. He saw it, a long way back, a small swinging yellow star in the black. And under it, lit from below, a face. Young. Pale. Thin. With dark hair cut very short in the cadet way, and dark straight brows, and a mouth pressed into a hard line. Looking round. Looking for him.
Lyra's face. Corin saw it at once, even at that distance, even in that light. The same shape. The same eyes. Younger. Harder. A boy's face, pretending to be a man's.
The lantern swung toward him.
Corin ducked behind a pillar.
He stood with his back pressed against the cold wet brick, and his heart hammering, and his breath going in and out of him so loud he was sure the boy could hear it across the whole cellar. The light went past him. He saw it go. A yellow glow on the floor, on the water, on the next pillar along, on the low curved roof of brick overhead, black and dripping, with long pale threads of something hanging from it like hair. Roots, he thought. Or something worse.
The light went on. Away from him. Searching.
And then, behind the boy, back at the hole in the bricks, back in the potato cellar, there was a sound.
Thud.
Corin turned his head.
Through the hole, far back, he could see the shaft of gray light coming down through the trap. And in it, something was coming down too.
Not down the ladder. It was too big for the ladder. Too big for the trap. Corin saw that at once. But it was trying.
A long gray arm. In a sleeve the color of fog, of ash after the fire has gone out. It came down through the square hole in the scullery floor, slowly, slowly, much longer than any arm should be. The hand at the end of it was gray too. A glove, or something that looked like a glove. Long fingers. Too many joints. They opened and closed in the gray light, feeling the air, the way a blind man's fingers feel a table.
And then, after the arm, the head.
It came down through the trap upside down. The way a bat hangs. Bent right over, double, folded at some point in the middle that should not have been able to fold, so that the long gray body was still up in the scullery and only the head and the arm were down in the cellar. Hanging. Turning.
The face.
White. Smooth. Shaped like an egg, a little longer than a man's head. With nothing on it. No eyes. No nose. No mouth. Only the faint pearly shine, like the inside of a shell, like old ivory, like something polished for a very long time by a very patient hand.
It turned. Slowly. Upside down, in the shaft of gray light. Toward the back wall. Toward the hole in the bricks.
Toward him.
It had no eyes. Corin knew it had no eyes. Rook had said so. They do not see, boy. Not the way you mean.
But it was looking at him.
He felt it. Across the whole black length of the old cellar, through the hole and past the pillars and over the boy with the lantern, he felt that smooth white blank egg of a face find him behind his pillar, the way a compass needle finds the north. And stay on him.
And then it began, very slowly, very patiently, to try to come through the trap.
Corin heard the boards of Mrs. Marta's scullery floor begin to crack.
He ran.
