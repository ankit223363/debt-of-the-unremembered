Chapter 29: The Eraser Knocks, Part 1 of 5: Rain on Tallow Street
Rain on Tallow Street
It was raining properly by the time he came up the back alley.
Not the big cold separate drops of the bridge. A steady gray fall, straight down, out of a sky so low and so heavy that it seemed to rest on the chimney pots of the Lower Docks like a lid on a pot. The rain ran off the roofs in sheets and came down the broken gutters in ropes and hit the cobbles of the alley so hard that it bounced. The puddles had joined together into one long brown stream that ran down the middle of the alley toward the river, carrying everything with it. Cabbage leaves. A dead rat, turning slowly. A child's wooden boat with no sail, that went past Corin's boots and on down the hill, bobbing, as if it knew where it was going.
He was wet through before he got to the thirteenth gate. The rain had found its way in at his collar and run down his back, and his borrowed blue shirt was stuck to his skin, and his boots squelched at every step. He did not care. He was listening.
Nothing. Rain. Only rain. On the roofs, on the cobbles, on the water butts, on the lids of the ash pits. A great soft endless roar of it, so loud that a man could have walked right behind him and he would not have heard.
He did not like that.
Fourteen. The gate hanging off one hinge. The little yard with its broken bricks swimming in water. The washing line, empty now, sagging. The back door with the horseshoe over it.
He knocked. Three times. Quickly. Knock fast.
The door was flung open before his hand had come away from it.
"Where have you been?"
Juno did not grab him by the coat this time. She grabbed him by both arms, hard, and dragged him in out of the rain, and slammed the door, and stood with her back against it, panting, glaring up at him. Her face was white. Her eyes were red and swollen, as if she had not slept at all, or had slept and woken crying. She was still wearing yesterday's clothes. The brass key on its string had swung round to the back of her neck.
"Three hours," she said. "I said three hours. It's been eighteen. I went to the inn. On the bridge. At six. And the man said you'd gone. Gone where? He didn't know. I went to Vespera's. She hadn't seen you. I went over every roof in the Ink Quarter. I went to the arches. I went to the Bank. I went—" Her voice broke. "I thought they'd got you. I thought the white faces had got you and you were empty. I came home and sat on the stairs all night waiting for you to come down them and not know who I was."
"Juno—"
"And somebody knocked," said Juno. "Last night. Late. At the front."
Corin went cold.
"Three times?" he said.
"No." She swallowed. "No. Twice. Like a neighbor. But nobody knocks at the front after ten. Nobody. And Gran wouldn't let me look. And when we went out in the morning there was nobody there. Just a twist of paper on the step. Folded very small." She pushed her hand into her pocket and took it out and held it up. A tiny hard square of paper, no bigger than a fingernail, sodden now, going soft at the corners. "Like the man in the gray coat does. With his sweets."
Corin looked at it.
He's not following you, Elara had said. He's walking ahead of you. And looking back, now and then, to make sure you're still coming.
"He was letting you know," he said quietly. "That's all. That he'd been."
"Why?"
"Because he likes people to know."
Juno looked at the little square of paper in her palm. Then she threw it on the floor, hard, and put her foot on it, and ground it into the wet flagstones until it was nothing but a gray smear.
"I hate him," she said.
"I know."
"Where were you?"
Corin looked at her. At her white furious face and her red eyes and her clenched fists.
"At Wren's," he said. "The Inquisitor. On Mercer Street. All night."
Juno stared at him.
"The white coat," she said. "The one with the black books. Her?"
"She sent me a note. She told me not to go home."
"And you listened to her?"
"Yes."
"She's one of them."
"She's on our side, Juno." He said it, and as he said it, he found that he believed it. Not because of anything she had told him. Because of a woman reading names out loud, at three in the morning, to a stranger in an attic. Because of Not one of mine. Kept anyway. "I think she's been on our side for a long time. Longer than she knew."
Juno did not look convinced. But she did not argue.
"Where's your gran?" said Corin.
"Kitchen." Juno jerked her head at the inner door. "Making porridge. Like it's an ordinary day. Like nobody knocked." Her voice wavered. "She made me sit with her all night in the kitchen, with the door bolted and the lamp lit and the poker on the table. She didn't sleep either. She just sat there with her account book and wrote things in it. I looked. It was just numbers. The same numbers, over and over."
Corin took off his wet coat. He hung it on the hook behind the scullery door, where it dripped on the flagstones. He looked down at the floor of the scullery.
There it was. By the big copper, in the corner, half under a rag rug. A square of old dark wood, set flush into the flagstones. With an iron ring in it, lying flat in a little groove. He had walked past it every Friday for six years, on his way through to pay his rent, and never once looked at it. Mrs. Marta's potato cellar.
Ninety-one steps up. Twelve rungs down.
He looked at it for a long moment.
Then he went through into the kitchen.
Mrs. Marta was standing at the stove with her back to him, stirring a pot. Small and stout and square, in her black dress with the white apron, with her sleeves rolled up to her elbows and her gray hair screwed up in its tight knot with the pencil stuck through it. The kitchen was full of steam and the smell of oats and the warm heavy smell of the stove. The account book lay open on the table, with the lamp beside it, still lit, though it was day, and the poker beside the lamp.
She did not turn round.
"You're wet," she said.
"Yes."
"You'll drip on my floor."
"I'm sorry."
"Sit down." She went on stirring. "Porridge in a minute."
He did not sit down.
"Mrs. Marta," he said.
She stopped stirring. She stood with the wooden spoon in the pot and her back to him, very still, for a long moment.
"Here it comes," she said quietly.
"They're coming today. At eleven." He said it as plainly as he could, the way you would tell somebody that their house was on fire. "Kross. An Inquisitor with him. A cadet. And two of the constructs." He swallowed. "They're coming for me. And they've written down that if I'm not here, they'll take you instead. You and Juno. And hold you. Until I come."
Mrs. Marta did not turn round.
She stood with her back to him and the spoon in the pot, and the steam went up round her from the porridge, and the rain roared on the scullery roof behind them. For a long time she said nothing at all.
Then she lifted the pot off the stove, and set it down on the side, and put the lid on it. And wiped her hands on her apron. Slowly. Carefully. Finger by finger.
And turned round.
Her small black currant eyes went over his face. Once. From top to bottom. Missing nothing.
"Well," said Mrs. Marta. "Then we'd better have our porridge first."
The Purple Door
They had their porridge.
Corin did not think he would be able to eat. He sat at the end of Mrs. Marta's kitchen table, in the hard wooden chair that was not the cat's, with a bowl in front of him and a spoon in his hand, and his stomach was a clenched fist. But Mrs. Marta stood over him with her arms folded until he picked up the spoon, and once he had eaten one mouthful he found that he could eat another, and then the whole bowl. It was thick and hot and salty, the way she always made it, with a lump of yellow butter melting in the middle and a sprinkle of brown sugar going dark on top. Juno ate hers standing up by the stove, very fast, without tasting it, the way she had eaten the bread and dripping at Pruett's.
Mrs. Marta did not eat. She sat across the table from him with her account book closed in front of her and her red hands folded on top of it, and she watched him.
When he had finished, she took the bowl away and put it in the sink and came back and sat down again.
"Now," she said. "Tell me properly."
So he told her. Not everything. Not the glass room, or the man with the gentle voice, or the empty column in the ledger at the Bank. But the order. No. 2,107. Wednesday. The eleventh hour. And Wren's plan. The constructs going up the ninety-one steps to his room. The trap in the scullery floor. The twelve rungs. The old cellars underneath, joined up like the cells of a honeycomb. The coal merchant's grating, and the brass key with its paper tag. The drain going down the hill to the big sewer under Wharf Street.
Left. Not right.
Mrs. Marta listened without saying a word. When he got to the trap in her scullery floor, her mouth went tight. When he got to the old cellars, her eyebrows went up. When he finished, she sat for a long time looking at her own folded hands.
"Thirty-four years," she said at last. "Thirty-four years I've kept potatoes down there. And coal. And a barrel of my Terrence's ale that he never finished, that I've never had the heart to pour away." She shook her head slowly. "And I never knew there was a door in the back wall."
"Wren says it's bricked up. Most of them are. But the bricks are loose. You can push them."
"Wren says." Mrs. Marta sniffed. "Your Inquisitor."
"She's not my—"
"I know what she is." The currant eyes came up and fixed on him. "She's the one in the white coat who stood across the street from my house on Saturday afternoon for two hours in the cold, looking up at your window. I saw her. I thought she was one of them. I nearly went out and threw a pan of dishwater at her."
Corin almost laughed.
"I'm glad you didn't."
"So am I, now." Mrs. Marta was quiet for a moment. "So. You'll be under my potatoes, and they'll be up at the top of my house, knocking on your door. And then what?"
"Then they'll find the room empty. And start looking. And I'll be gone, through the cellars, before they get to the scullery."
"If you're lucky."
"Yes."
"And if you're not?"
Corin did not answer.
Mrs. Marta looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at Juno, standing by the stove with her empty bowl in her hands and her face white and set.
"And us?" she said.
"You go," said Corin. "Before ten. Out the back, through the alley. To the Watch house on Rope Street. To Sergeant Moll. Knock fast." He looked at her. "The order says if I'm not here, they take you. But I'll be here. Under the floor. So they won't need to take you. But they'll search. Every room. And if they find you while they're searching, and they're angry because they can't find me—"
He stopped.
"I'm not leaving my house," said Mrs. Marta.
"Mrs. Marta—"
"Thirty-four years, Mr. Vane. I've told you. I've opened that door to the bailiffs and the Bank and the Watch and a man with a knife. I've never once hidden. I've never once run." Her red hands had closed into fists on the account book. "And I'm not starting now, for a gray man with a sweet in his cheek and two great white dolls on stilts."
"Gran," said Juno.
Mrs. Marta did not look at her.
"Gran," said Juno again. Her voice was very small. "They empty people. At the door. Like old Wick. You said so. You said you heard it. Three, slow, up and down the street." She put her bowl down on the side of the stove, very carefully, as if it might break. "I don't want you to be like Wick."
Mrs. Marta turned her head and looked at her granddaughter.
For a long moment neither of them said anything. The rain roared on the scullery roof. The kettle on the stove began, very quietly, to sing.
"I don't want you to be like Wick either," said Mrs. Marta at last. Her voice had changed. It was rough and low. "That's the only thing in the world I don't want."
"Then come with me. To Moll's."
Mrs. Marta looked at her for a long time. Then she looked at Corin.
And Corin saw something go out of her. Not her fierceness. That would never go. But something stiff, something that had been holding her up very straight for thirty-four years like a board down the back of a dress. He saw it bend.
"For her," said Mrs. Marta quietly. "Not for you. Not for them. For her."
"For her," said Corin.
After that, things happened quickly.
Mrs. Marta went upstairs to her bedroom on the first floor and came down ten minutes later in her good black coat and her bonnet with the jet beads on it, the one she wore to church on feast days, with a carpet bag in one hand and her account book under her arm. Juno ran up the ninety-one steps to the top of the house and came down again two minutes later with a bundle wrapped in a blanket, panting.
"Your things," she said, and thrust it at Corin. "From your room. Your other shirt. Socks. The candle stubs off the shelf. The matches. The good knife." She swallowed. "I left the jars. They're too heavy. And I didn't know which ones you'd want."
Corin took the bundle. He did not look in it. He put it on the table.
"Thank you," he said.
Mrs. Marta was at the stove. She had taken a loaf of bread from the crock and was cutting it, very fast, into thick slices, and spreading them with dripping from a bowl, and wrapping them in a cloth. A lump of cheese. Two apples. A flask, which she filled from the kettle and stoppered with a cork. She put it all into a canvas bag with a strap and held it out to him.
"You'll be down there a long time, I expect," she said. "In the wet. You'll want something hot." The flask was tea. He could smell it. Very strong and very sweet. "Don't drink it all at once."
"I won't."
"And don't lose my flask. It was my mother's."
"I won't."
She looked at him for a moment, with the bag held out between them, and her currant eyes very bright. Then she put it into his hands and turned away to the sink and began, very fast, to wash the porridge bowls, as if it were an ordinary morning and they had to be done before she went out.
Juno stood by the back door with her grandmother's carpet bag.
"Corin," she said.
"Yes?"
"The big sewer. Under Wharf Street. Where you come out." Her voice was quick and low. "It comes out under the Customs House. Into the river. There's a big round mouth in the wall, with an iron gate across it, half under the water. I've seen it. From the roofs." She swallowed. "I'll get Wat. After I've left Gran at Moll's. I'll go over the roofs to wherever the barge is and get Wat and his little boat. And we'll be there. At the mouth. At noon. Waiting."
"Juno, you don't have to—"
"At noon," said Juno fiercely. "Promise you'll come out at the river end and not the Hill end. Left. Not right. Promise."
Corin looked at her. Fourteen. In yesterday's clothes, with the brass key on its string round her neck and her grandmother's carpet bag in her arms and her face white and set and wet.
"I promise," he said.
And this time, he meant it all the way through.
Mrs. Marta dried her hands. She took off her apron and hung it on its hook. She looked round her kitchen, once, slowly. At the stove, and the pots, and the bunches of herbs, and the calendar from the coal merchant on the wall with the days crossed off in pencil. At the cat's chair, where the cat was not, because it had gone out into the rain an hour ago and not come back, the way cats do when something is wrong.
Then she went out through the scullery, past the square of wood in the floor with its iron ring, past the copper, to the back door.
She stopped there. With her hand on the latch. And looked back at Corin.
"Mr. Vane," she said.
"Yes?"
"Rent's due Friday."
And she opened the door, and went out into the rain, with Juno beside her, and did not look back again.
Corin stood in the scullery and listened to them go. Their feet on the wet bricks of the yard. The squeal of the broken gate. Then the rain, swallowing everything.
He went back into the kitchen.
It was very quiet without her. He had never been in Mrs. Marta's kitchen without Mrs. Marta. It did not feel like her kitchen anymore. It felt like a room in a house where somebody used to live.
He went down the dark passage that smelled of onions, to the purple door at the end of it, and opened it, and stood in the doorway, and looked back down the passage toward the front of the house, the foot of the stairs, the front door standing open onto Tallow Street and the gray falling rain.
Then he shut the purple door behind him.
The brass knob was cold in his hand. The paint was bright and shining and ugly, the color of a plum, the color of a king's robe in a picture book. So nobody can ever say they couldn't find me.
He left it unlocked.
She would want it left unlocked. He was sure of that. Mrs. Marta had never in thirty-four years let anybody make her lock her own door.
Ninety-One Steps
He went up one last time.
He did not have to. He knew that. Juno had brought down everything he needed. The bundle lay on Mrs. Marta's kitchen table, and the canvas bag with the bread and the flask lay beside it. There was nothing at the top of the house that he could not leave behind.
But Wren had said Kross would send the constructs to his room. Because Fisk had written down, every night: Subject returns to lodging. Goes to top floor. Candle lit. Candle out. And if the constructs were going to go up ninety-one steps and knock on his door, then the room behind the door had better look as if he had only just left it.
And there was something else. Something he could not have explained to Wren, or to Juno, or to anybody. He wanted to see it. Once more. The room where he had lain on six years of bad nights, with the black pressing down on him from the slope of the roof, reading his list by the light of a match.
So he went up.
The staircase at No. 14 Tallow Street had been built by someone who hated people. He had always thought that. He thought it again now, with his hand on the cold iron rail and his wet boots on the first step. It was narrow, so narrow that at the landings, where the rail bulged out, he had to turn sideways to get past. It was steep. And every step was a different height. Some tall, some short, some so short you hardly noticed them and stumbled, some so tall you had to lift your knee like a man climbing a stile. You could never find a rhythm on those stairs. You had to watch your feet the whole way, like a man walking on a beach of loose stones.
And it was dark. There were no windows. There was only the one lamp on each landing, a small blue gemstone lamp in a cage of rusty wire, screwed to the wall at the height of a man's head. Their light was thin and cold, the color of skimmed milk. Between the landings, the stairs went up into a gray dimness where you felt for each step with your toe.
He counted.
One. Two. Three.
The first floor. The landing. The blue lamp in its cage, buzzing faintly, the way gemstone lamps did when they were old. The doors of the two rooms on that floor, both shut. Mrs. Marta's own room, at the front, with its lace curtain. And the room at the back, where old Mr. Penn had lived until the spring, when he went to the Bank one Tuesday to sell an afternoon and came back without the whole of the year he had been married. He had sat on the stairs for three days after that, asking everybody who passed what year it was. And then his nephew had come for him, and taken him away to the country, and the room had been empty ever since.
Twenty-two. Twenty-three.
The second floor. The landing. The blue lamp. Two doors. The Dunns' room, where five children slept in two beds and their mother took in sewing. It was empty now. He had seen them at dawn, Juno said, going off up the street in a line with their bundles, the mother in front with the baby. She heard the knock last week, Juno had said. Three, slow, two streets over. She's gone to her sister's in Saltmarsh. She said she's not coming back till it stops.
Forty. Forty-one.
The third floor. The landing. The blue lamp, flickering. And the banister.
His hand reached for it, the way it always did, and found nothing. There was a gap. A whole length of the iron rail missing, where it had rusted through years ago and fallen down the stairwell in the night with a crash that had woken the whole house. Nobody had ever mended it. His hand reached for it every time he passed, going up and going down, in the dark and in the light, and every time it found the empty air, and every time his heart jumped.
It jumped now. He stood on the landing with his hand out in the air where the rail should have been, and his heart going.
Then he went on.
Sixty-five. Sixty-six.
The fourth floor. The young couple who never spoke to anybody. Their door was shut and there was no sound behind it. He did not know if they had gone, or if they were inside, very still, listening to his feet on the stairs. He did not knock.
Eighty-nine. Ninety.
Ninety-one.
The top of the house. A tiny landing, hardly bigger than a doormat, under the slope of the roof, where the ceiling came down so low that he had to bend his head. No lamp. Only a little gray light coming from somewhere above, through a crack in the slates. And one door.
His.
It was a plain door of gray boards, like Wren's attic door. With a latch, and a keyhole under the latch for a key that he had lost in his second year and never replaced. He had never needed to. There was nothing in his room that anybody would want to steal.
He lifted the latch and went in.
The room was very small. Smaller than he remembered, every time he came back to it. The ceiling sloped down steeply on one side, following the roof, so that he could only stand up straight in the half of the room nearest the door. On the low side, under the slope, there was the bed. A narrow iron bed, painted black once, chipped now, with a thin straw mattress and a gray blanket. On the high side there was a table, small and square, with a candle stub stuck in a saucer on it, and a chair. And a shelf, on the wall above the table. And one window.
The window was small and square and set into the slope of the roof, so that it looked up at the sky more than out at the street. The rain was running down it in long gray streams. Through the streams he could see the chimney pots of the houses opposite, black and dripping, and the gray sky, very low.
And on the shelf, the jars.
There were eleven of them. He had not counted them for a long time. Small glass jars with brass lids, the kind the Ministry gave its Harvesters, the same as the thousands of jars on the shelves in the Third Basement but many fewer. Most of them were empty. Clean and dry and clear, with their brass lids screwed tight, waiting for something to put in them.
Three were not.
In three of them, behind the glass, there was a faint mist. Blue-white. Turning slowly, round and round, like breath on a cold window. Each of those three had a small paper label pasted on the side, in his own hand.
He did not have to read the labels. He knew what they said. He had read them a thousand times.
Unknown woman. Gutter Lane. Last minute. Not claimed.
Unknown child. The river below the old ferry. Last minute. Not claimed.
Unknown man. The Flats. Last minute. Not claimed.
Three of the dead, in six years, that nobody had come for. That the Ministry had said were not worth the cost of a jar in the Third Basement. That he had brought home in his coat pocket, against the rules, because he could not bear to pour them away into the gutter.
He stood in front of the shelf and looked at them.
Then he took them down. One by one. Carefully. He unscrewed the brass lid of the first one, and held it up, and opened the window.
The rain came in at once, cold and wet, onto his face and his hands and the table. The sound of it filled the room. He held the jar out through the window, into the gray falling water.
The mist came out slowly. It hung for a moment above the open mouth of the jar, blue-white and faint, turning. And then the rain took it. He saw it go. Thin and pale, drifting up into the gray, like smoke from a candle going out. Over the chimney pots. Into the low sky.
Into the wind, Vespera had said. Into the river. Into the dreams of people asleep in this city.
He did the second. Then the third.
When they were gone, he stood at the open window with the three empty jars in his hands and the rain running down his face, and he did not know if it was only rain.
Then he shut the window. He put the jars back on the shelf, in a row, with the others. He made the bed. He pulled the gray blanket straight and tucked it in at the sides, the way he never did, so that the room looked tidy. As if somebody had got up early, and made his bed, and gone out.
He looked at the candle stub in its saucer on the table. Cold. Gray. He touched it with one finger. He did not light it.
And then, because he could not help it, he sat down on the edge of the narrow bed, under the slope of the roof, where he had lain so many nights. And put his hand on the gray blanket, and felt the hole in the corner of it, where you could put your toe through.
The same as Tam's.
He had never noticed that before. That his blanket had a hole in the corner too. Exactly where Tam's had. He sat and felt it with his fingers, the frayed edge of it, and he thought that perhaps every narrow bed under every slate roof in Veridia had a gray blanket with a hole in one corner. And a boy lying in it, listening to the rain.
The bell of Saint Bride's began to ring.
He counted.
Ten.
He got up.
He went out of the room and pulled the door shut behind him and dropped the latch, softly, so that it clicked. And he stood on the tiny landing under the roof for one moment more, with his hand flat on the gray boards of his door.
Then he turned, and began to go down.
Ninety-one steps.
He counted them all.
