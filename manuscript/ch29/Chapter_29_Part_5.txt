Chapter 29: The Eraser Knocks, Part 5 of 5: The Word That Is Gone
The Word That Is Gone
It was very dark in the drain.
Not the dark of the cellars, which had been thick and still and dry, like the inside of a shut box. This was a moving dark. A loud dark. The water filled it. It rushed past his legs and slapped against the walls and fell somewhere ahead of him with a hollow booming roar, and every sound it made came back off the brick twice, three times, until the whole drain seemed to be shouting.
He held on to the wall and waited for his breath to come back.
It came slowly. His chest hurt. His fingers hurt, where they were jammed into the cracks between the bricks. His legs did not hurt at all, which was worse, because it meant he could not feel them. The water was up to his waist. It was so cold that it did not feel cold anymore. It felt like a weight. Like two heavy hands, one on each leg, pulling him gently and steadily down the slope.
Boy, said Malakor.
The voice was very small. Very far off. As if it came from the bottom of a well.
Boy, are you still there?
"Yes," said Corin. His teeth were knocking together so hard that the word came out in pieces. "Yes. I'm here."
Do you know your name?
Corin opened his mouth.
And for one moment, one long cold terrible moment, there was nothing there.
Then it came. Corin. Flat and plain. Corin Vane. He said it out loud, into the roaring dark, and it sounded like a name somebody else had given him. Which, perhaps, they had.
"Corin," he said. "Corin Vane."
Good, said Malakor. Good. Hold on to that one. Say it again.
"Corin Vane."
Where were you born?
"Oakhaven." That came at once. That was on the first page. You were born in Oakhaven. He had read it so many times that the words were burned into him like letters cut in wood. But now, saying it, he felt for the first time how empty it was. Oakhaven. It was a word in the city tongue. A Veridian word. Oak. Haven. A safe place by an oak tree. That was what it meant, in the language he spoke now.
But it had not been called that. Not by the people who lived there.
He knew that suddenly. Clearly. The way you know there is a step in the dark before your foot finds it. The people of Oakhaven had had their own word for it. Their own name. In their own soft round tongue with the hills in it. And his mother had said it, and his father, and the little girl with the gap in her teeth. And he had said it. Every day of his life, until he was ten years old.
And now it was gone.
Not hidden. Not behind a locked door. Not written over in chalk. Gone. He had burned it himself, with his own hands, in a coal cellar, to knock a sword out of a frightened boy's hand. Every word of it. The name of his village. The name of the stream. The word his mother had sung. The word for home.
The man with the face that slid away had taken his childhood. And Corin had just burned the last of it himself.
Boy, said Malakor gently. Don't.
"Don't what?"
Don't think about it. Not here. Not now. You can think about it later. You can sit in a warm room with a cup of tea and think about it for a year, if you like. But not here. Here you have to move.
Corin pressed his forehead against the wet brick.
"You knew," he said. "You knew it was there. The language. You knew I had it."
A pause.
Yes.
"Why didn't you tell me?"
Because you would have burned it, said Malakor. Sooner or later. On some ordinary bad night, to light a stair, or open a lock. And it was worth more than that. It was the last thing in you that was older than the Ministry. A pause. I suppose it still was worth more than that. But at least you burned it for something.
"For a boy."
For a boy who let you go, said the voice. I've seen men burn their mothers' faces for less. Now move.
Corin moved.
He let go of the wall with one hand. The water took him at once. It swung him round, off his feet, and he went down the slope on his back, fast, with the dark roaring all round him and the brick scraping his shoulders. He threw out his hands. He hit something. A rung. Another one. Iron, set in the wall. He grabbed it and held on, and the water piled up against his chest and went past him, foaming, and he hung there in the dark and coughed until he thought his ribs would crack.
The water was higher.
He was sure of it. It had been at his waist. Now it was at his chest. The rain. All that rain, falling on the city since before dawn, running off the roofs and down the gutters and through the gratings, all of it coming here. Into the drains. Into the dark. Going down to the river. And it was getting faster.
He had to find the big sewer. He had to find Wharf Street. And then he had to go left.
He got the rod out.
It was hard to do, one-handed, with the water shoving at him. His fingers were too cold to feel the pocket. He found it at last, the long narrow right-hand pocket of his coat, and got his fingers round the thin silver shaft, and pulled it out. It was very cold. Colder than the water. And the voice behind his left ear went quiet, the way it always did when he held the rod, as if Malakor was holding his breath.
"Light," said Corin.
What will you burn?
He thought. It was hard to think. The cold was in his head now. Thick and slow. He went through the rooms. He did not have many left that he was willing to touch. And then he found something small. Very small. Not even a whole memory. A corner of one.
The taste of the bread at the Ministry canteen.
He had eaten it every day for six years. Gray, heavy, a little sour. He had never liked it. He had never once thought about it. And he would not miss it. He took hold of it, the way you take hold of a match, and he burned it.
The tip of the rod lit up. Small. Blue-white. The size of a coin.
It was enough.
He saw the drain. Round, brick-lined, the height of a man stooping. Black and wet and shining. The water filled the bottom half of it, gray and fast, full of leaves and sticks and straw, and things he did not want to look at. It went down ahead of him in a long slope, curving a little to the left. And at the end of the curve, maybe forty paces on, the dark opened out. He could feel it. A wider dark. A bigger sound.
The big sewer.
He went down toward it. Hand over hand, along the rungs set in the wall, and when the rungs ran out, along the cracks between the bricks. The water pulled at him every step. It was at his chest the whole way. Once it was at his chin, where the floor dipped, and he had to lift his head and hold the rod up high and pull himself along by his fingertips with the water slapping at his mouth.
And as he went, he held the barley-sugar wrapper in his left fist. He did not know why. It was only paper. It would be pulp by now. But he held it.
At the end of the drain the brick fell away under his hands, and he was in the big sewer.
It was enormous. Much bigger than he had thought. A great round tunnel of old black brick, so tall that the light from his rod did not reach the roof. And down the middle of it, the river of the city's rain. Wide. Brown. Fast. Roaring. It came down out of the dark on his right, from the Hill, from the high streets and the Ministry and the Bank, and went away into the dark on his left. Down. Toward the water. Toward the Customs House and the river mouth and a little boat, and a girl with her hair in a knot, waiting.
There was a ledge. A narrow stone path along the side of the tunnel, a foot above the water, slimy and broken. He dragged himself up onto it. He lay there on his face, on the cold wet stone, with the rod in one hand and the paper in the other, and breathed.
After a while, he opened his left hand.
The wrapper was a small wet lump. He picked at it with his numb fingers. It came open slowly. Fold by fold. The paper tearing at the edges. And on the inside, on the waxy white side, there was a mark. Black. Smeared. Written with something soft. A lump of coal, maybe, held in a black-dusted hand.
Two words.
I will.
Corin looked at it for a long time, in the small blue light of the rod, with the river of rain going past a foot below him.
Then he folded it up again. Small. Very small. Into a hard tight square, the size of a fingernail. And he put it in the inside pocket of his coat, next to the Ledger, over his heart, where the word Elara had faded.
"Left," he said.
Left, said Malakor.
And he got up, and turned his back on the Hill, and went down into the dark.
Wren Walks Uphill
Wren Halloway walked up the Hill in the rain behind the Inquisitor, and counted her steps.
She always counted. She had counted for as long as she could remember, which was not, if she was honest, very long. Steps. Stairs. Bricks in a wall. Pages in a file. Counting was a thing you could do with the front of your mind while the back of your mind did something else. And the back of her mind, this morning, was very busy.
One hundred and four. One hundred and five.
The street was steep and black and shining. Wharf Street first, then Cooper's Rise, then the long climb of the Ministry Steps, with the gray stone buildings leaning in on either side and the rain running down the middle of the road in a little brown stream. Kross walked ahead of her. He walked quickly, lightly, with his gray coat buttoned to the throat and his collar turned up, and he did not look back. He had not looked back once since the coal cellar. He had not said a word.
That was bad. Wren knew that was bad. Silas Kross talked when he was pleased. He talked when he was bored. He talked, soft and pleasant, when he was about to do something terrible to somebody. The only time he did not talk was when he was thinking. And when Silas Kross was thinking, it was usually about you.
One hundred and thirty.
She kept her face still. She was good at that. She had been good at it for six years, since the day she woke up in the long white room with forty beds, with a gray lady ringing a bell, and found that she did not know her own mother's name. She had learned then that a still face was a kind of wall. People could not climb what they could not see.
But her hands were shaking, inside her gloves. And she could not make them stop.
He went left. She thought it once, very clearly, and then put it away, the way you put a letter in a drawer. He went left. He's in the big sewer now. He's going down to the river. In an hour he'll be at the Customs House. At noon the boat comes. And then he's gone.
If he did not drown first. If the water did not rise too fast. If the rain did not—
She put that away too.
One hundred and sixty-two.
At the top of Cooper's Rise, Kross stopped.
He stopped so suddenly that she nearly walked into his back. He stood in the middle of the street with the rain falling on his hat, and looked down. At the road. At the brown water running past his polished boots. Down the hill. Toward the river.
"Halloway," he said.
"Sir."
"How long have you worked for me?"
"Two years, sir. And four months."
"Two years and four months." He nodded slowly. "And in two years and four months, have you ever once been wrong about a plan? A map? A drain?"
Wren's heart turned over.
"No, sir," she said.
"No." He was still looking at the water. "No, you haven't. You're the best reader of plans in the Office. Everyone says so. You could find your way through the sewers of Veridia blind. You probably have." He turned round. He looked at her. His face was mild and kind and a little sad, the way it always was. "So I'm sure you're right. I'm sure there's a door. I'm sure he'll go right, toward the Hill, toward the Ministry, and we'll meet him coming up the stair."
"Yes, sir."
"And if we don't," said Kross gently, "I'm sure you'll be able to explain why."
He turned and went on up the hill.
Wren stood still for one breath. Then two. The rain ran off the brim of her cap and down the back of her neck, cold as a knife. And then she followed him. Because there was nothing else to do. Because a still face was a wall, and walls do not run.
One hundred and sixty-three.
She thought about the boy.
Rowan Thorne. Cadet. Sixteen years old. With bought hands and a sister on a barge and a letter in his coat that he did not know she had seen. She had not known what he would say, at the grating. She had stood there with the silver light on her face, lying to Silas Kross, and she had felt the boy beside her like a lit match beside a barrel of oil. One word. One word from him, and it would all have come down. Her. Corin Vane. Juno and the boat. Mrs. Marta at Moll's. The whole thin, careful, stupid plan.
I didn't hear, sir. My ears are still ringing.
She did not know why he had said it.
She thought perhaps he did not know either.
One hundred and ninety.
And she thought about the word.
She had heard it. Coming along the old cellars behind Kross, with the Eraser thudding behind them and the silver light bobbing on the brick. A word. One word, out of the dark ahead, in a language she had never heard in her life. And it had gone through her like a bell. Through her chest and her bones and her teeth. Kross had stopped dead. Even the Eraser had stopped. And for the length of that one word, Wren Halloway had stood in the dark between the pillars with her mouth open, and felt something.
Not a memory. She did not have memories, not from before the white room. Just a feeling. Like standing at a window and seeing, very far off, a light in a house you used to live in.
She had known that language once.
She was sure of it. She did not know how she was sure. But she had heard the word, and something at the bottom of her, something under all the empty rooms, had turned over in its sleep and whispered: yes. That one. I know that one.
She put that away too. Very carefully. In the very back of the drawer.
Two hundred and twelve.
The Ministry rose up ahead of them, gray and black in the rain. All its windows lit. All its chimneys smoking. The great bronze doors at the top of the steps, with the old letters cut over them that nobody could read anymore.
Kross went up the steps two at a time. At the top he stopped, and turned, and waited for her.
"The stair from the Archive," he said. "Show me."
"Yes, sir."
She went past him, through the bronze doors, into the warm yellow light and the smell of ink and wax and old paper. She knew the way. She had walked it a hundred times. Down the main hall. Through the door marked RECORDS. Down the long stone stair, four floors, to the Archive. And then further. Down the iron stair at the back, past the drawers, to the very bottom of the Ministry, where the old walls were wet and the lamps were few and the air smelled of the river.
There was a door there. She had not lied about that.
It was bricked up. It had been bricked up for a hundred years. She had lied about the rest.
She would stand in front of it with Kross, and she would look surprised, and she would say: but the plans said, sir. And she would show him the plans, and the plans would say so, because she had changed them herself, last night, by candlelight, with a very fine pen. And he would look at her with his mild kind sad face, for a long time.
And she did not know what would happen then.
Two hundred and forty.
She went down the long stone stair, counting, with Silas Kross's footsteps light and quick behind her. And in her pocket, in her gloved hand, she held the other beach stone. The one that matched the one she had lent Corin Vane. Gray and smooth, with a white line round it, like a ring.
Bring it back, she had said.
She held on to it, and went down.
