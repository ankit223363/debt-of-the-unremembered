Chapter 36: The Duke's Gallery, Part 4 of 5: The Second Price
The Second Price
"How many do you have?" said the Duke.
Lyra did not understand.
"Of her," said the Duke. He was still looking at the little painted window. "Of your mother. How many memories? Real ones. Your own. Not things your nurse told you, or things you made up in bed at night out of wanting. Things you saw with your own eyes, and kept."
Lyra sat very still on the black velvet bench.
She had never told anybody. Not Oren. Not Mrs. Hobb. Not Corin. She had counted them, alone, in the nursery, at ten or eleven, by candlelight, the way a child counts the coins in a money box. And she had counted them so many times since that they had worn smooth. And she had never once said the number out loud.
No lies between you. That was Corin's rule. Vespera's rule. Not hers. Not with this man.
But she found that she could not lie about this. Not here. Not in this room, with the woman in the boat glowing gray in the last case, and the plain silver band on his little finger.
"Five," she said.
The Duke closed his eyes.
He sat like that for a long time. His great soft face did not move. His hands lay folded on his belly. Only, very slowly, his thumb moved on the plain silver ring. Round. And round.
"Five," he said at last. Very softly. "Five. Oh, my dear."
He opened his eyes.
"Tell me them," he said.
"No."
"Not to keep. Just to hear." He did not look at her. "Tell me what they are. Not what's in them. Only what they are. The way the cards say. A Wedding Morning. The Sea." His thumb went round the ring. "Please."
Lyra did not know why she did it.
Perhaps it was the please. She had not thought he was a man who said it. Perhaps it was the room, and the dark, and the gray light. Perhaps it was only that she had carried them alone for so long, and they were so heavy.
"Brushing my hair," she said. "At the nursery window. A hundred strokes. Counting in a language I didn't know."
The Duke's thumb stopped on the ring.
"In the rain," said Lyra. "On Crown Walk. In a blue dress, with no hat. Laughing. And Father on the steps with an umbrella."
The Duke said nothing.
"At the Midwinter table. Touching every chair. And stopping at an empty one."
Silence.
"Singing. In the next room. A song that went up and hung there."
The Duke had shut his eyes again.
"And the last," said Lyra. Her voice had gone very low. "In the front hall. In a traveling cloak. With her gloves on. Kneeling down. Holding my face. Looking at me for a long time." She swallowed. "And saying something. I don't know what. I was too small. I've never been able to remember what she said." A pause. "And then the door. And the carriage. And she didn't come back."
The long dark room was perfectly silent.
Even Nell had stopped counting.
The Duke sat with his eyes shut and his hands folded and his thumb still on the silver ring, and did not move for so long that Lyra began to wonder if he had fallen asleep.
Then he said, without opening his eyes:
"That one."
Lyra's heart stopped.
"The last one," said the Duke. "The front hall. The gloves." His voice was so soft she could barely hear it. "That's my price, Miss Thorne. That's the second thing. That's the real thing." He opened his eyes at last and turned his head and looked at her. "Give me that, and I'll take you to the Bourse on my arm, and I'll stand on the floor with you while you look at your father's last evening on a black page, and I won't go up to the box. And whatever you do after that, I'll never have seen it."
Lyra stared at him.
"Why?" she said. It came out as a whisper. "Why that one?"
The Duke looked at her for a long time.
"Because I've seen her come," he said. "Every night for thirty years. In a boat. In the fog. Coming toward a bridge." He did not look at the gray case. "I've never once seen her go."
Lyra said nothing.
"I was not there," said the Duke softly. "That morning. In your front hall. I was in Ostmark, on the Council's business. I came home a month later and they told me she'd gone. Gone to the coast, they said, for her health. And then they told me she'd died there. And there was a funeral, with a coffin, and nobody was allowed to look in it." His mouth moved. "And I have never known, from that day to this, whether she meant to go. Whether she was sorry. Whether she said goodbye to anybody." A pause. "And now you tell me she knelt down in the hall in her traveling cloak and held your face and said something."
He looked at Lyra.
"I would like to see her say it," he said. "Even if I can't hear what it was. Even only through the glass. I would like to see her face, once, when she was leaving. That's all."
Lyra sat on the bench in her mother's gown and looked at him.
She thought: this is the thing he's wanted since before I walked in. Vespera had said so. Whatever he asks for the second time. He had wanted it for twenty years without knowing it existed. And now he knew, and he would not stop wanting it.
She thought about the gloves. Gray kid. Cool on her cheeks.
She thought about the look. The long, long look, the way you look at something you are trying to learn by heart.
She thought about the words she had never been able to remember. That she had lain awake trying to remember, for years. That were in there somewhere, under the gloves and the look, too small and too far down to reach. The only words her mother had ever said that were for her and nobody else.
If she gave it away, she would have four.
And she would never, ever know what her mother had said.
Fold it small, she thought. Fold it small and keep it close.
She thought about her father under the hay. With his gray eyes on the ceiling. Tapping. Four, two, nine, three. For two years. And then, at last, one word. Bourse. And then, on her palm, in the gold light, when she tapped it back to him: one.
One.
Like an answer.
She thought about Oren by the canvas, with his sword. About Hessa on the hay, saying promise. About Juno's frozen feet. About Corin, with a hole in his leg and the gray running up past his elbows, waiting for the dark.
She thought: Mother would have done it.
She did not know where the thought came from. But it was there, and she knew, the moment it came, that it was true. The woman in the gray gown, in the portrait, with her hands folded and her face listening to something far away. The woman in the boat in the fog, who never turned round. The woman who had knelt in the front hall and held her daughter's face and then got up and walked out of the door and never came back. Whatever she had been, whoever she had been, whatever she had said, she would have done it.
She would have paid.
"Yes," said Lyra Thorne.
The Duke did not move.
"Yes," said Lyra again. Her rough voice did not shake. Her face did not move. She had been taught. "The last one. The hall. You can have it."
The Duke looked at her for a long, long moment.
Then he lifted one ringed hand, without looking away from her face, and said, quite quietly, toward the black door at the far end of the room:
"Pennick."
And the black door opened.
Mr. Pennick's Glass
The man who came in through the black door was small and neat and old, and he was carrying a case.
He walked down the long dark room between the glowing lights without hurrying and without making a sound. Past Nell on the floor, who shrank against the wall. Past the wedding morning and the sea and being forgiven. He was dressed in black, like the steward, but plainer. A black coat, buttoned high. Black trousers. Black shoes, soft-soled. His hair was white and thin and combed very flat. His face was narrow and pale and quite calm, with small round spectacles on a thin nose. He looked like a clerk. He looked like a very good clerk, who had been doing the same job in the same office for forty years and had never once made a mistake.
He wore gloves.
White cotton gloves. Like a footman's. Like the gloves Corin had worn up the Hill in the autumn, to hide his wrists.
He stopped in front of the bench and bowed. A small, exact bow, from the neck.
"Your Grace," he said. "Miss Thorne."
"Mr. Pennick," said the Duke. "Miss Thorne is going to give me something. A memory. One of her own. Will you take it, please?"
Mr. Pennick looked at Lyra.
He did not look at her the way the Duke looked at her, or the way the people in the drawing room had looked at her. He looked at her the way a doctor looks at a patient. Calmly. Carefully. Not unkindly. As if she were a problem that he had solved many times before and would solve again, quite well.
"Of what nature, Your Grace?" he said.
"Early," said the Duke. "Very early. She was small. A few minutes only, I think." He paused. "Her mother. In a front hall. Saying goodbye."
Something happened behind Mr. Pennick's spectacles. Very small. A flicker, like a candle in a draft. Then it was gone.
"Very good," he said.
He knelt.
He knelt on the black stone floor in front of the bench, very neatly, the way he did everything, and set his case down beside him, and opened it. It was lined with black velvet, like the cases on the stands. And in it, in neat rows, each in its own little velvet bed, were his tools.
Lyra had seen a harvester's tools before. Corin's. A silver rod with a spiral on it. A pouch of stones. Jars. These were not like those. These were finer. Smaller. Brighter. A row of empty vials, very small, of clear glass, each with a stopper of silver. A pair of thin silver tongs. A little brass lamp, no bigger than an egg, with no wick in it. And in the middle, in a bed of its own, a glass.
It was a lens. Round, and thick, and slightly cloudy, like the lenses in the tops of the cases. It was set in a ring of brass, and the ring was fixed to a short brass arm, and the arm ended in a padded cup of black leather, shaped to fit against the side of a head.
Mr. Pennick took off his gloves.
He did it slowly. One finger at a time. And Lyra saw his hands.
They were gray.
Gray from the fingertips. Gray over the backs of the hands, in fine dry lines, like frost on a window. Gray up over the wrists. She could not see further, because of his sleeves. But she did not need to. She had seen arms like that this morning, in a dim brick arch, in a thin gray light from a crack in the roof. She knew how far it went.
Most of them retire when it reaches the elbow, Vespera had said. The Ministry gives them a pension and a little house by the sea.
Mr. Pennick saw her looking.
He did not seem to mind. He laid the white gloves neatly on the lid of the case, folded together, the way a woman lays down her gloves at church.
"Forty-one years, Miss Thorne," he said quietly. "At the Ministry. Third Basement. I retired with the gray at my shoulder." He took the brass lens from its bed. "His Grace was kind enough to offer me a house by the sea. In this room." He looked up at her through his spectacles. "It's quite painless. I promise you. You'll feel it go. You won't feel anything else."
Lyra could not speak.
"Sit back, please," said Mr. Pennick. "Against the wall. Turn your head a little. To the left. There."
She sat back. She felt the cold of the black velvet wall through her mother's gown. She turned her head.
Mr. Pennick leaned forward and fitted the padded leather cup against her right temple. It was cool. It smelled of old leather and clove oil. The brass arm held the lens out a little way from her face, so that it hung in the corner of her eye, cloudy and round, like a small moon.
He lit the little brass lamp. There was no flame. There was only a glow, inside the brass, faint and gray, like the light in the last case. He set it on the floor beside the bench.
"Now," he said. "Think of it. Only that. Nothing else. The hall. Your mother. Take as long as you need. Bring it up. All of it. As close as you can. As clear as you can. And when you have it, when it's there, in front of you, close your eyes."
Lyra looked past the lens at the Duke.
He was sitting on the bench beside her, very still, with his hands folded on his belly and his thumb on the silver ring, watching her. His face was soft and pale and quite blank. She could not tell what he was feeling. She did not think she would ever be able to tell.
She looked at Nell, far away down the room, against the wall by the black door, with her hands over her mouth.
Then she closed her eyes, and brought it up.
It came easily. It always came easily. She had taken it out so many times.
The front hall at Crown Walk. Black and white floor. The big brass lamp on its chain. The smell of beeswax, and of the cold coming in under the door. Her own feet, very small, in red slippers. And her mother, coming down the stairs.
In a traveling cloak. Dark blue. With the hood down. And the gloves already on. Gray kid.
Her mother, kneeling down. Right down, on the black and white floor, so that their faces were level. The cloak spreading out round her. And her hands coming up. Gray kid. Cool. On Lyra's cheeks. Both of them.
And the look.
The long, long look. Her mother's eyes, very close. Dark. Dark gray, nearly black, like the water in the canal at Brindle. Going over Lyra's face, slowly, every part of it, the way you read a letter you will not be allowed to keep. Learning it. Learning it by heart.
And her mouth moving.
Saying something.
Lyra had never been able to hear it. Never. In all the years. The words were always just there, just past the edge, like a sound in another room. She had lain awake straining for them. She had tried to read the shape of the lips. She had tried to guess. She never could.
She closed her eyes.
And felt it begin to go.
It did not hurt. Mr. Pennick had told the truth about that. It was not like pain. It was like the feeling of a tooth that has been loose for a long time finally coming away. A pulling. Gentle and steady and slow. From somewhere behind her right eye, toward the cool leather cup at her temple, and out. She felt it go. The black and white floor first, going gray, going thin, like breath on a window. Then the brass lamp. Then her own red slippers.
Then the gloves.
She felt the gloves go. The cool of them on her cheeks. One moment there, and the next, only a word for them. Gray kid. A word with nothing inside it.
Then the look.
And as the look went, as her mother's eyes began to slide away from her, very slowly, into the gray, Lyra heard it.
For the first time. For the only time.
Her mother's voice. Very low. Very close. In a language Lyra did not know, that she had heard once through a nursery door, counting strokes of a brush. And then, at the end, in her own language. In the plain speech of the city. Three words.
"—keep them safe."
And it was gone.
Lyra opened her eyes.
The long dark room swung round her. The two rows of glowing lights. The black velvet wall at her back. The cool leather cup was gone from her temple. Mr. Pennick was kneeling in front of her with the silver tongs in his bare gray hand, holding up a little vial of clear glass.
It was glowing.
A soft, dim, steady light. The color of a winter sky just before snow.
Gray.
Lyra stared at it.
She tried to remember what was in it. She reached for it, the way she had reached for it a thousand times, at night, in the nursery, by candlelight. Her mother in the front hall. Kneeling. Holding her face.
She knew it had happened. She knew the words for it. Front hall. Traveling cloak. Gloves. Kneeling. Said something. She could say them. She could write them. She could have told anybody, now, this minute, that it had happened.
But when she reached for the thing itself, there was nothing there.
Only a smooth, flat, empty place. Like the missing tooth that the tongue keeps finding.
And at the edge of the empty place, very faint, like the last ring on water after a stone has gone down, three words that she did not understand.
Keep them safe.
Keep who safe?
She did not know. She would never know now. She had heard it once, for the length of one breath, as it went. And now it was in a little glass vial in an old harvester's gray fingers, glowing.
And she had four.
So That's What She Said
Mr. Pennick stoppered the vial with a tiny silver stopper, and sealed it with a drop of black wax from a stick he warmed over the gray glow of the lamp, and pressed the wax flat with the end of the silver tongs. Then he held it out to the Duke on the flat of his bare gray palm.
The Duke did not take it at once.
He looked at it. Lying there in the old man's hand, glowing its soft winter gray. For a long time.
Then he put out his great soft hand with the ten rings, and took it, very gently, between his finger and thumb. The way you would pick up a bird's egg.
"The glass, Pennick," he said.
Mr. Pennick unscrewed the cloudy lens from its brass arm, and handed it to him.
The Duke held the vial up in front of his face, in the dim colored light from the cases, and put the lens between them, and bent his great head, and looked.
Lyra watched him.
She could not stop herself. She sat on the black velvet bench with her back against the wall and her hands in her lap and the smooth empty place behind her right eye, and she watched the Duke of Marrow watch her mother leave.
His face did not change at first. It stayed soft and pale and blank. The heavy lids drooped over the pale eyes. He breathed slowly through his nose.
Then, very slowly, something happened to it.
Lyra did not know how to say what. It was as if a great house with all its shutters closed had, one by one, from the top floor down, begun to open them. Not wide. Just a crack. Just enough to let in a little light. The heavy face went softer. The mouth, the full red girl's mouth, came a little open. The pale eyes behind the lens went very wide, and very still, and very bright.
And then they went wet.
The Duke did not move. He did not make a sound. He held the little gray vial up in front of the lens with his great soft fingers quite steady, and looked, and looked, and the tears ran out of the corners of his pale eyes and down over his plump cheeks and into the white lace at his throat, and he did not wipe them away. He did not seem to know they were there.
Lyra had seen him laugh until his belly shook. She had seen him be rude to sixty people at once. She had seen him in his gallery, gentle and strange, talking about best days.
She had not thought he could do this.
It went on for a long time. Longer than the memory could possibly have been. He must be watching it over and over, she thought. The way he had watched the boat in the fog every night for thirty years. Waiting for the end, and then starting again at the beginning. The stairs. The cloak. The kneeling. The gloves.
The look.
At last he lowered the lens.
He sat with the vial in one hand and the lens in the other, resting on his knees. He looked at nothing. At the black floor. His face was wet.
"So that's what she said," said the Duke of Marrow.
Very softly. To nobody.
Lyra's heart turned over.
"What?" she said.
The Duke did not answer.
"You can't hear," said Lyra. "Through the glass. You said. You can only see." Her voice was shaking. "You can't know what she said. You can't."
The Duke turned his head and looked at her.
"I can read lips, my dear," he said gently. "I learned. In Marrowmere. The year I was there. On the canals, in the fog, everybody learns. You can't hear a thing across the water, so you learn to watch." He looked down at the vial. "I've read hers before. A long time ago. On a bridge."
Lyra stared at him.
"Tell me," she said.
The Duke said nothing.
"Tell me." She did not care that her voice cracked. She did not care that Mr. Pennick was kneeling there, or that Nell was crying again by the door, or that the whole of the Hill was drinking tea in the white room downstairs. "She was my mother. I gave it to you. I gave you the only thing she ever said to me, and I never even knew what it was. Tell me what she said."
The Duke looked at her for a long time. His wet face was very still.
"You heard it," he said at last. "Didn't you. At the end. As it went."
Lyra did not answer.
"They always do," said the Duke. "Pennick will tell you. They always hear the last of it, as it goes. Like a door closing. You catch what's said in the room just as the door shuts." He was quiet. "What did you hear, Miss Thorne?"
"Keep them safe," said Lyra.
It came out as a whisper.
The Duke closed his eyes.
He stayed like that a moment. Then he opened them, and looked down at the little glowing vial in his fingers, and something moved in his great soft face. Something Lyra could not read at all.
"Then you have the end of it," he said. "And I have the rest." He closed his hand round the vial. "That's fair, I think. That's a fair trade."
"It isn't fair. It isn't anything like—"
"No," agreed the Duke. "It isn't." He looked at her. "Nothing in this room is fair, my dear. That's why it's in this room."
He stood up.
It took him a long time. He put his ringed hand on the arm of the bench and heaved, and grunted, and rose, slowly, like a ship coming up off a sandbank on the tide. He stood over her in the dim light. He did not offer her his hand.
He turned, and went to the last case. The gray one. Marrowmere. He lifted its glass lid, very carefully, on its brass hinges. And he laid the new vial down on the black velvet cushion inside it. Beside the old one.
Two soft gray lights. Side by side. The woman coming, and the woman going.
He closed the lid.
He stood looking down at them for a moment through the glass.
Then he put his hand into the inside of his blue velvet coat, and took out a card, and held it out to her without turning round.
It was small and stiff and heavy, the size of a playing card. Cream, edged in gold. And in the middle of it, in the Duke's big soft round brown hand, three lines.
Midwinter. The Bourse.
His Grace the Duke of Marrow
and guest.
And under it, pressed into the card in gold, a small seal. A tower on a hill, and a river running at its foot.
"My carriage will come for you at the eighth bell," said the Duke. He did not turn round. "Wherever you tell Pennick. Not your barge. I don't want to know where your barge is. I have already forgotten that I ever heard there was a barge." A pause. "Wear the gray. Vespera will make your mask, I suppose. Tell her to make it white. White is what nobody wears to the Bourse. Everyone will look at you."
Lyra took the card.
Her gloved fingers were shaking. She held it in her lap and looked at it.
"And Miss Thorne," said the Duke softly, to the glass.
"Yes?"
"There will be somebody else in white that night. Somebody from the Bank. A young woman, with a gold tooth. She audits the Bourse's books every Midwinter, and walks the floor, and looks at every lot." His voice did not change. "Don't let her look at you for too long."
Lyra went very still.
"Why?"
"Because she looks at things the way I do," said the Duke of Marrow. "And she never forgets what she sees."
He did not say anything else. He stood with his great soft back to her and his hands folded in front of him, looking down through the glass at the two gray lights. And after a while, Lyra understood that he had finished with her. That he would stand there until she had gone, and for a long time after.
She got up.
She put the card into the pocket that Madame Hesper had sewn into the left side of her mother's gown, inside the waist. It fitted exactly. As if it had been made for it.
Perhaps it had.
Then she walked down the long dark room between the two rows of best days, toward the black door, and Nell.
