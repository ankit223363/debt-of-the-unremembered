Chapter 36: The Duke's Gallery, Part 3 of 5: A Canal in the Fog
A Canal in the Fog
There was no name on the card.
All the other cards had names. Not people's names. The names of the days. A Wedding Morning. The Sea. Being Forgiven. Written in the Duke's big soft round brown hand, propped on their little brass stands. This card was the same. The same brass stand, the same cream card, the same brown ink.
But it said only:
Marrowmere.
And nothing else.
Lyra stood in front of the case. The gray light came up out of the glass and lay on her mother's gray gown, so that the wool seemed to glow a little, faintly, as if it remembered something.
"Look," said the Duke.
He did not say it the way he had said it at the sea. He said it very quietly. Almost as if he did not want her to.
Lyra bent to the lens.
Gray. Soft and shifting and blurred, like the blue had been. Like looking up through water. She waited.
And slowly, the gray took shape.
It was fog. She knew that before she knew anything else. Thick white-gray fog, like the fog on the canal at Brindle, the night Corin came. It filled the picture. It moved. And then, out of it, the way the Patient Grace had come out of it, black and sudden, something dark.
Water. Dark water, flat and still, very close. And on the water, a boat. Long and narrow and black, with a high curved prow like the neck of a swan. Moving slowly through the fog. And beyond it, rising up out of the water on both sides, walls. Tall walls of old stone, stained green at the bottom where the water touched them, with doors in them that opened straight onto the canal. Steps going down into the water. Iron rings for tying boats. Windows above, small and dark and shuttered. And far ahead, just visible through the fog, the curve of a bridge. A high narrow stone bridge, with a lamp on it, glowing yellow in the gray.
A canal city. A city built on water.
And in the prow of the black boat, standing, with her back half turned, there was a woman.
Lyra held her breath.
The woman was tall and slim, in a long dark cloak with the hood down. Her hair was dark, and pinned up, and there were drops of fog on it, like tiny pearls. She was standing very straight in the prow of the boat, with one hand on the curved wood beside her, looking ahead. Toward the bridge. Toward the lamp. Her face was turned away. Lyra could see only the line of her cheek, and the curve of her ear, and the back of her neck under the pinned-up hair.
And at her collar, where the cloak was fastened at the throat, a brooch.
Silver. Small. Shaped like a half-mask. Two round eyes, and the curve of a nose between them.
Lyra did not move.
She had seen that brooch. In a portrait, in her own narrow room over the stable, on the collar of a woman in gray. She had seen its shape in a keyhole, in the long gallery at Crown Walk, behind the place where the clock had stood. She had seen it in the bottom of a cup, a Marrowmere cup, in her aunt's white drawing room, turning round and round under her aunt's gloved fingertip like a face looking for something.
Every girl in Marrowmere is given a mask on the day she's named.
The woman in the boat did not turn round. She stood there in the prow, very still, with the fog going past her and the bridge coming nearer, and nearer, and Lyra watched her the way you watch somebody walking away from you down a long road, waiting for them to look back.
She did not look back.
The bridge came closer. The lamp on it grew brighter. And there was somebody on the bridge. Lyra saw them now. A dark shape, standing under the lamp, leaning on the parapet, looking down at the water. Waiting for the boat.
And the picture began to fade.
It went from the edges first. The walls went gray. The steps went gray. The water. The boat. Until there was only the woman's back, and the line of her cheek, and the small silver brooch at her throat, glinting.
And then only the gray.
Lyra straightened up.
She did not know how long she had been bent over the case. Her back ached. Her eyes were wet. She did not wipe them. She stood in the gray light at the end of the long dark room and looked at nothing.
The Duke was standing very close behind her. She could feel the heat of him, the way you feel the heat of a stove. He had not said anything all the time she was looking.
"Whose is it?" said Lyra.
Her voice came out very low and rough.
The Duke did not answer at once.
"Mine," he said.
She turned round.
He was standing with his great soft hands folded in front of him and his face turned toward the gray case, not toward her. In the dim light, his face looked older than it had downstairs. Heavier. The sleepy eyes were not sleepy now, and they were not watching her. They were looking at the gray light. The way the eyes of the woman in the boat had been looking at the bridge.
"Mine," said the Duke again, softly. "It's the only one in this room that was ever mine. I had it drawn out of me in the spring of the year I came home from Marrowmere. I was twenty-six." He paused. "I couldn't bear to keep it, and couldn't bear to burn it, and couldn't bear to sell it. So I put it here. With the others. Where nobody would ever open it." A pause. "Not even me."
Lyra stared at him.
"Then you don't remember it," she said slowly. "You had it taken out. You don't remember that morning at all."
"No."
"Then how do you know what it is?"
The Duke smiled. The small, tired, private smile.
"Because I have looked through that lens every night for thirty years, Miss Thorne," he said. "Every night. Before I go to bed. I come in here, and I bend down, and I look. And I watch a woman in a boat in the fog who never turns round. And I wait for her to." He was quiet. "I don't remember the morning. But I remember looking. That's what I have instead. Thirty years of looking."
He lifted one hand and touched the little finger of the other. The plain thin band of silver among all the rubies and the gold.
"Was it—" said Lyra.
She could not finish.
The Duke looked at her at last.
"You know who it was," he said. "You've seen the brooch. Don't ask me to say it. I haven't said her name out loud in this room in thirty years, and I'm not going to start for a Thorne." His soft voice did not change. "Least of all for one with her eyes."
Lyra stood very still.
Behind her, the gray light came up out of the last case and lay on her mother's gown.
And behind the Duke, very far away down the long dark room, by the black door, Nell had sat down on the floor with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up and the empty velvet bag in her lap, and was crying without making any sound at all, because she did not know where she was, or what any of this was, and she was fourteen.
Lot Forty-One, Withdrawn
The Duke saw Nell.
Lyra did not know how. He had not turned round. But he lifted one ringed hand a little, without looking, toward the black door at the far end of the room, and said in his soft voice, "Your girl is crying, Miss Thorne. Go and tell her she's not going to be eaten. Then come back. I'll wait."
Lyra went.
She went down the long dark room between the two rows of glowing cases, past all the best days, and knelt down on the black stone floor beside Nell, in her mother's gown, and put her gloved hand on the girl's shoulder.
"Nell."
Nell looked up. Her face was wet and red and crumpled, and her white cap had slipped over one ear.
"I'm sorry, miss," she whispered. "I'm sorry. I don't know what's wrong with me. I don't know what this place is. All the little lights. It's like a graveyard. It's like a graveyard with candles on." She hiccupped. "I want to go home. I want to go home to the arches."
"I know," said Lyra. "Soon. I promise. Very soon."
"Is he going to hurt you?"
Lyra looked back down the room. At the great soft shape of the Duke, standing at the far end by the gray case, with his back to them and his hands folded, looking at the light.
"No," she said. "I don't think he hurts people. I think he just keeps them."
She did not know where that had come from. She did not know if it was true. But Nell seemed to take some comfort from it. She wiped her face on her sleeve, and straightened her cap, and nodded.
"Sit here," said Lyra. "Don't touch anything. Don't look at anything. Count the stones on the floor if you have to. I'll be back."
She went back down the room.
The Duke had moved. He was standing a little way from the gray case now, beside a low black velvet bench that ran along the end wall, under a painting Lyra had not noticed in the dark. He lowered himself onto the bench, slowly, with a long grunt, the way a big man sits down in a small boat. He patted the velvet beside him.
Lyra did not sit.
She stood in front of him, in the faint glow of the cases, with her hands folded.
The Duke looked up at her. He did not seem offended. He seemed, if anything, pleased.
"You've seen the catalogue," he said.
It was not a question. Lyra did not answer it.
"Of course you have," said the Duke. "Or you wouldn't be here. You'd be on your barge, or in your stable, or wherever it is you've been hiding, waiting for the ice to melt." He folded his hands on his belly. "Lot 41."
Lyra said nothing.
"A Lord's Last Evening," said the Duke softly. "From a lord of the first rank. Taken whole before the fire." He tilted his great head. "There are eleven lords of the first rank left in Veridia, Miss Thorne. I know them all. Nine of them were in my drawing room an hour ago, eating my cake. One of them is me. And the eleventh—"
He did not finish.
He did not need to.
"I'm going to bid on it," said the Duke.
Lyra went cold.
"Of course I am," said the Duke. "How could I not? It's the finest thing that's been offered at the Bourse in my lifetime. Nobody has ever sold a whole evening of the fourth tier before. Not a whole one. Not from a lord." His pale eyes rested on her face. "And I knew him, you see. Your father. I knew him when we were boys. We were at school together. He was the best of us. The cleverest, and the kindest, and the bravest, and the best at everything, and he never once made you feel it." He was quiet. "I would like to have his last evening. Here. With the others. Where nobody can ever eat it."
"You'd never open it," said Lyra.
"Never."
"So nobody would ever know what was in it."
"No."
"Not even you."
"Not even me." The Duke smiled. "That's rather the point, my dear."
Lyra stood very still.
She thought about her father under the hay. Tapping. For two years. On walls and blankets and the backs of hands. Four, two, nine, three. Trying to tell somebody something. And saying, at last, one word.
She thought about that word, and everything it had cost him to bring it up the long stair, being put into a glass case in this black room. On a black velvet cushion. Beside a woman in a boat who never turned round. Glowing softly, forever, unopened. While a fat man in a blue velvet coat bent over it every night before bed and looked at the light.
"No," she said.
The Duke raised his eyebrows.
"No?"
"He didn't burn himself to the walls so that you could put him in a box," said Lyra. Her voice was very low and very rough. "He did it to hide something. Something that mattered more to him than his own mind. More than me. More than Ro. And somebody stole it from him, and now they're selling it, and you want to buy it and put it on a cushion and look at it." She heard her voice shake, and stopped it. "That's not keeping it safe. That's just another way of burying it."
The Duke looked at her for a long time.
He did not seem angry. His great soft face did not change at all.
"You want to open it," he said.
"Yes."
"You want to know what he was thinking. That night. When he shut the door."
"Yes."
"And when you've opened it, Miss Thorne, it will be gone." His soft voice was quite gentle. "Gone. The last of him. You understand that? Once opened. You'll know, and he'll be gone, and there'll be nothing left in all the world of the man I went to school with but the thing in the hay that taps."
Lyra did not answer.
She did not trust her voice.
The Duke sighed. He looked away from her, at last. Up, at the painting on the wall above the bench, in the dark.
"Do you know," he said, in a different voice, lighter, almost idle, "I came to the auction. At Crown Walk. The day they sold your house."
Lyra had not known that.
"I came for one thing," said the Duke. "Only one. I didn't want the house. I didn't want the furniture, or the silver, or the horses. My agent bought those. I didn't care about any of it." His eyes stayed on the painting. "I came for her portrait."
Lyra's heart stopped.
"The one in the long gallery," said the Duke. "Over the clock. In the gray. With the brooch." He was quiet. "It was in the catalogue. Lot forty-one. I sat in the front row for three hours, through every chair and every candlestick and every bad painting of every bad horse your grandfather ever owned, waiting for Lot forty-one." His soft mouth twisted. "And when it came, the auctioneer stood up, and said it had been withdrawn. That morning. By the family."
He looked down at her.
"By the family," he said again. "There was only you, by then. And your brother, who was fourteen. And your aunt." A pause. "I've always wondered which of you it was."
Lyra said nothing. She thought of the portrait, with its face to the wall, in her narrow room over the stable. Of the chalk on the back of the frame. Lot 41. Portrait, Lady T. Withdrawn. She had never asked who had withdrawn it. She had only been glad that someone had. She had found it waiting for her, the week after, in the stable loft, with no note.
She had thought it was Madame Hesper.
She was not sure, any more, of anything.
"Lot forty-one," said the Duke softly. "Twice." He smiled at nothing. "Somebody at the Bourse has a sense of humor, Miss Thorne. I'd very much like to know who."
The gray light came up out of the last case and lay on the black floor between them.
Lyra looked at the Duke. At his great soft face, and his ten rings, and his sleepy, sharp, pale eyes.
Then she did something she had not planned to do.
She sat down. On the black velvet bench. Beside him.
Not close. At the far end. With the whole width of the bench between them. But she sat.
"Then don't buy it," she said.
The Duke turned his head.
"Take me in," said Lyra. "On your arm. As your guest. Let me walk round the tables and look at the lots and stand on the floor where everyone can see me. And then—" She stopped. She chose her next words very carefully, the way Corin chose his. "And then, when the bell rings at the eleventh hour, Your Grace, don't go up to the box."
The Duke looked at her for a long, long time.
"Don't bid," he said slowly.
"Don't bid on Lot 41."
"And let somebody else have it. Somebody who will open it. Eat it."
"Let me worry about who has it," said Lyra.
She did not say anything else. She did not need to. She saw him hear it. Everything she had not said. She saw it go into the pale eyes under the heavy lids and be taken, and weighed, and put away somewhere very deep.
And the Duke of Marrow began, very softly, to laugh.
The First Price
It was not the laugh from the drawing room. That one had been loud, and rolling, and made for an audience. This one was very quiet. A soft, shaking, wheezing sound, deep in his great chest, that went on for a long time. He put one ringed hand over his eyes. His belly shook under the silver leaves.
Lyra sat at the far end of the bench and waited.
At last he took his hand away. His pale eyes were wet.
"Oh, my dear," he said. "Oh, my dear. You've been spending time with low company. That was a very low thing to say. Let me worry about who has it." He shook his head slowly. "Your mother would have said it exactly like that. Exactly. With her chin up. As if she were asking for the salt."
Lyra said nothing.
"You're going to steal it," said the Duke softly. "Aren't you."
She did not answer.
"No. Don't tell me. I don't want to know. If you tell me, I'll have to decide whether to tell Vivienne, and I don't want to decide anything today. I've had a very tiring afternoon." He sighed comfortably and folded his hands. "I'll only say this. Vivienne's vault has three doors, and two of them are not doors. And she keeps the fourth tier in a room that has no floor plan, because she had the man who built it put into a crate." He looked at the ceiling. "That's all. That's all I know. That's all I shall ever know, if anyone asks."
Lyra sat very still.
She did not thank him. She knew better than that. A thing said like that, sideways, to the ceiling, was not a gift. It was a hook. It was a thing he would come back to one day, and pull.
"Will you take me?" she said.
The Duke looked at her.
"Ah," he said. "Now we come to it."
He was quiet for a moment. The gray light from the last case lay on his great soft face, and on the ten rings, and on the plain silver band.
"I will take you," he said, "on Midwinter Night. In my carriage. On my arm. Up the steps of the Bourse, past the Weigher, past Gorse, past Vivienne in her violet, past every gossiping fool on the Hill. I will walk you round every table on that floor and I will stand beside you while you look at Lot 41, and I will not let one of them say one word to you that you do not wish to hear." He paused. "And I will not go up to the box at the eleventh bell."
Lyra's heart was going very hard.
"For a price," said the Duke.
"Yes," said Lyra.
"You knew that."
"Yes."
"Good. I'd have thought less of you if you hadn't." He smiled his small smile. "Everything in this room has a price, Miss Thorne. Even the things I was given. Especially the things I was given."
He turned his great head and looked up at the painting on the wall above the bench.
Lyra had not looked at it. It had been too dark, and she had been looking at him. She looked now.
It was a small painting. Not much bigger than a book. In a plain dark frame. It showed a window. Only a window. A tall narrow window in a dark room, with the shutters folded back, and through it, very small and far away, a stretch of water, gray and still, and the curve of a bridge.
There was nobody in it. Just the window, and the water, and the bridge, and a great deal of dark.
"I want her portrait," said the Duke.
Lyra went very still.
"The one from Crown Walk," said the Duke. His voice was quite gentle. "Lot forty-one. Withdrawn. In the gray, with the brooch. I know you have it. I've always known. A carter took it from the house the morning of the auction, wrapped in a blanket, and drove it down the Hill to the Lower Quarter, and up a ladder into a hayloft over a stable. My man followed the cart." He did not look at her. "I want it. Here. On this wall. Under this window. Where she can look out."
Lyra did not breathe.
She thought of the portrait. With its face to the wall, in her narrow room over the stable. She had turned it round only at night, when she could not sleep, and lit a candle, and sat on the end of her bed and looked at it. A woman in gray, with her hands folded, and a brooch at her collar, and a face that was listening to something far away. She had looked at it so many times that she knew every crack in the varnish. It was the only picture of her mother that she owned. It was the only picture of her mother that existed, as far as she knew, in the whole of the world.
It was the face she checked her five coins against. Every time. To be sure she had kept them true.
Don't give him the first thing he asks for, Vespera had said. The first thing is never the real thing.
"No," said Lyra.
The Duke turned his head and looked at her.
"No," said Lyra again. Her voice shook, and she let it. "Not that. I'll give you a great many things, Your Grace. But not that. It's the only one there is. If I give you that, I won't have her face any more. I'll only have what I remember of it." She swallowed. "And I don't remember enough."
The Duke looked at her for a long time.
She could not read his face. She had never been able to read it. Not when she was sixteen, at his dinners, and not now. It was too soft, and too still, and the eyes were too far back in it.
Then he sighed.
"No," he said. "I didn't think you would." He looked back at the little painting of the window. "I had to ask. You understand. I've wanted that portrait for twenty-two years. I'd have been a fool not to ask."
"I understand."
"Do you." He was quiet. "I wonder."
The gray light lay between them on the black floor.
Far down the long room, by the black door, Lyra could hear Nell counting under her breath. Forty-one. Forty-two. Forty-three. Counting the stones in the floor.
"Then I'll ask for something else," said the Duke.
He did not turn his head. He went on looking at the painted window, and the water, and the bridge.
"And this time, Miss Thorne," he said, "I'd think very hard before you say no."
