Chapter 36: The Duke's Gallery, Part 5 of 5: The Mews
The Mews
They went out by a side door.
The steward took them. He met them at the black door of the gallery without a word, and led them down a narrow back stair that Lyra had not known was there, and along a stone passage that smelled of boot blacking and candle grease, and out through a door into the Crescent mews. He did not take them back through the drawing room. Lyra did not know if that was a kindness, or an insult, or simply the Duke's orders. She did not ask.
At the door, the steward bowed. The same small exact bow as Mr. Pennick.
"Miss Thorne," he said.
And shut the door.
It was nearly dark.
The short winter day had gone while they were in the gallery. The sky over the Hill was a deep, clear, cold blue, going black in the east, with one star in it. The gas lamps along the Crescent drive were bright now, a long curving row of yellow lights, like a necklace laid down on the frozen lawn. Carriages were leaving. Lyra could hear them, on the other side of the house. Wheels on gravel. Coachmen calling. The Duke's Saturday was over.
She stood in the mews in her mother's gray gown and breathed.
The air was so cold it hurt.
The mews was a long cobbled lane behind the Crescent, with the backs of the great white houses on one side and a row of coach houses and stables on the other. It smelled of horses, and straw, and the river far below. There was a lamp at the far end, on a bracket, and a little yellow light in a window over one of the stables. And in the middle of the lane, under the lamp, there was a man holding a horse.
Lyra saw the horse first.
She did not mean to. She would have looked away, if she had been quicker. But she saw it before she could stop herself. A tall gray mare, dappled like a winter sky, with a pale mane and a pale tail and a long fine head held a little low, and a blanket of dark red wool thrown over her back against the cold. She was standing quite still in the lamplight with her breath smoking out of her nostrils in two long white plumes. One ear was turned toward the man who held her. The other was turned toward Lyra.
Lyra stopped.
The mare lifted her head.
She lifted it high, very suddenly, the way a horse does when it hears a sound it knows. And both her ears came round, and pointed, and she stood there under the lamp with her head up and her nostrils wide, looking down the mews at the girl in the gray gown.
And she called.
Not a whinny. A whinny was loud, and high, and wild. This was low. A deep, soft, fluttering sound, from somewhere in the mare's great chest, that went on and on. The sound a mare makes to her foal. The sound a horse makes when the hand it knew for six years comes into the stable in the morning with an apple.
"Mist," said Lyra.
She did not know she had said it.
The man holding the mare turned his head.
He was small and bandy-legged and old, in a groom's brown coat and gaiters and a cap pulled down over his ears. He had a face like a walnut, brown and lined, and a gray stubble on his chin. And when he saw Lyra, standing there by the door in the lamplight, his face did something that made her want to sit down on the cobbles and cry.
"Miss Lyra," he said.
"Tibbs."
She had not seen him in two years. Not since the auction. Not since the morning the Duke's groom had come into the stable yard at Crown Walk with a rope, and Tibbs had stood by the mounting block with his cap in his hands and his face like stone, and said nothing at all.
She had thought he had gone to the country. Like Bertram. Like Doctor Aske. To a sister.
"His Grace sent down," said Tibbs. His voice was rough. "Half an hour since. Said to bring her round to the side door, and wait. Said you might be coming out that way." He swallowed. "Said you might want to say goodbye."
Lyra stood very still.
She thought of the Duke in the drawing room, with sixty people watching. Would you like to see her? Before you go? I could have her brought round.
She had said no.
He had brought her round anyway.
She did not know if it was the cruellest thing anyone had ever done to her, or the kindest. She thought, standing there in the cold, that perhaps with the Duke of Marrow there was no difference.
She went down the mews.
She went slowly, in her mother's thin gray slippers, on the frozen cobbles. And the mare watched her come the whole way, with her head up and her ears forward and that low soft sound still coming out of her, on and on. And when Lyra was close enough, Mist put her long gray nose down and pushed it, very hard, into the front of Lyra's mother's gown, just under her ribs, the way she had always done, the way that had always made Lyra stagger. And blew.
Lyra staggered.
She put her arms up round the mare's neck. She did not care about Madame Hesper's gloves, or the gown, or the horsehair that would be all over the gray wool by the time she let go. She put her arms round the great warm gray neck and her face against the pale mane, and held on.
The mare stood quite still and let her.
She smelled the same. That was what undid Lyra, in the end. Not the sound, or the push. The smell. Warm horse and clean straw and the leather of a well-kept bridle and, under it all, something that was only Mist, that had always been only Mist, since the morning of Lyra's twelfth birthday, when her father had led a gangling gray yearling into the stable yard on a halter and said, She's yours, Ly. Look after her.
She had not looked after her. She had let a man come with a rope.
"She's well, miss," said Tibbs. Very low. Behind her. "I want you to know that. She's well. I came with her. His Grace took me on with her, the same day. Said nobody else would know how to handle her." He cleared his throat. "She pines a bit. Off her feed in the winter. But she's well. I see to it."
"He rides her," said Lyra into the mane. "On Sundays. He said."
Tibbs made a small sound. It might have been a laugh.
"His Grace sits on her," he said. "On Sundays. In the park. At a walk. With me at her head the whole way and a hand on the rein." A pause. "He's never once asked her to do anything she didn't want."
Lyra did not answer.
She stood with her face in the mare's neck for a long time. The lamp hissed on its bracket. Somewhere down the mews, a door banged. Nell stood by the side door of Marrow House with the gloves and the empty bag, and did not move.
At last Lyra let go.
She stepped back. She wiped her face with the back of her gloved hand. She put her hand flat on Mist's long gray nose, the way she always had, and the mare pushed against it, gently this time, and blew warm breath into her palm.
"Tibbs," said Lyra.
"Miss?"
"Do you drive for him? For the Duke?"
Tibbs looked at her. His walnut face went very careful.
"I drive the second pair," he said. "On the big carriage. When he goes out of an evening. Grays, both of them. Matched. Not as good as her." He jerked his chin at Mist. "He likes grays. He likes them very much."
Lyra looked at him.
"Midwinter," she said. "Midwinter Night. Will you be driving?"
Tibbs did not answer at once. He looked at her face for a long moment, in the lamplight. At her mother's gown. At the hay that was still, after everything, behind her left ear.
"I'll be on the box," he said. "Midwinter. If you're in the carriage, miss." He touched his cap. "I'll be on the box."
That was all.
He did not ask why. He did not say anything else. He turned, with the mare's lead rope in his hand, and clicked his tongue to her, softly, and began to walk her back up the mews toward the stable with the yellow window.
Mist went with him.
But she turned her head as she went. Once. And looked back down the mews at Lyra, with her ears pricked and her dark eyes shining in the lamplight. And made the sound again. Very low. The sound a mare makes to a foal.
Then the stable door opened, and the yellow light took her, and the door shut.
Four Coins
"Miss," said Nell, beside her. Her voice was small and hoarse from crying. "Miss, are you all right?"
"Yes," said Lyra.
She did not know if it was true. She thought it might be. She thought she might be all right, and that the thing she felt, the smooth flat empty place behind her right eye, was what all right felt like now.
"Come on," she said. "We have to get back before dark."
They went down the Hill.
They went the way they had come. Along the Crescent, past the last of the carriages. Past the fountain at the top of the Gardens, where the frozen bronze boy stood in his frozen basin with the lamplight on him. Lyra did not look down Crown Walk this time. She looked at the pavement in front of her feet.
She was counting.
She could not help it. She had always done it, every night, since she was ten. It was like touching the coins in your pocket to make sure they are still there.
One.
Her mother's hands in her hair, at the nursery window. A hundred strokes. The words in the language she did not know. The window open a little. The smell of the river.
Yes. There. Warm and smooth, the way it always was.
Two.
The rain on Crown Walk. The blue dress. No hat. Laughing. Father on the steps. Isolde, Isolde, you'll catch your death.
There.
Three.
The Midwinter table. Every candle lit. Her mother walking all the way round, touching the back of every chair. Stopping at the empty one. Standing there with her hand on it for a long, long time.
For someone who couldn't come.
There.
Four.
The song through the half-open door. The tune that went up and up and hung there, asking.
There.
Five.
She reached for it.
She reached for it the way she had reached for it every night for ten years, and her hand closed on nothing.
She stopped walking.
She stood in the middle of the pavement on Lily Hill, under a gas lamp, with the cold coming up through her mother's thin gray slippers, and reached again.
The front hall. She knew that. The words were there. The front hall. A traveling cloak. Gloves. Kneeling. She knew all the words. She could have said them out loud. She could have drawn the hall, the black and white floor, the brass lamp, from what she knew of it, from all the other times she had stood in it.
But the thing itself was not there.
There was no cloak. No gloves. No face coming close to hers, dark-eyed, learning her by heart. There was a place where those things had been. A shape. An outline. Like the pale square on a wall where a picture has hung for many years, and has been taken down.
There are pale squares on all the walls now, Vespera had said to Corin. And you're not even looking at them any more. You walk past them.
Lyra did not walk past it.
She stood under the gas lamp and looked at it. The pale square. The empty place. And at the very edge of it, faint as the last ring on water, three words.
Keep them safe.
She did not know whose voice they had been in. She knew it had been her mother's. She knew that, the way she knew the words for the cloak and the gloves. But she could not hear it any more. She could only hear the words, in her own head, in her own rough voice. Keep them safe. As if she had read them in a book.
Keep who?
She did not know. She would never know. The Duke knew. The Duke had read it off her mother's lips through a cloudy glass, and closed his great soft hand over it, and put it in a case in the dark, beside a woman in a boat who never turned round.
"Miss?"
Nell had stopped too. She was standing a little way down the hill, looking back, with the empty velvet bag clutched to her chest.
"I'm coming," said Lyra.
She began to walk again.
Four, she thought.
It was very dark by the time they reached the river.
The lamps were lit along the embankment, and the dye in the water under the Dye-Works had gone black in the dark, all the colors together, so that only where the lamplight fell on it could you see a ribbon of red or a thread of blue, twisting. The arches were black holes in the bank. Red light showed in some of them, far back. Braziers.
And in the mouth of the third one, there was a figure.
It was not sitting on an upturned bucket. It was standing. Leaning, rather, on something. A stick, Lyra thought. A broom handle, with a rag wound round the top. It was leaning on it hard, with all its weight on one leg, the right, and the other held a little off the ground, stiff and straight.
It was looking up the embankment toward the steps.
It had been looking, Lyra thought, for a long time.
She stopped at the top of the slimy steps between the warehouses and looked down at it.
And it saw her.
She saw it see her. She saw it straighten. Not much. As much as it could, on one leg and a broom handle. She saw its head come up. And even from here, even in the dark, with only the lamplight on the black water and the red glow from the arch behind it, she saw the left side of its mouth go down. At the corner.
She came down the steps.
She did not hurry. She did not run. She came down the slimy steps one at a time, in her dead mother's gray gown and her dead mother's gray slippers, holding the hem of the gown up out of the green weed with one gloved hand, the way she had been taught. And she walked along the black riverbank to the mouth of the third arch.
And stopped in front of him.
Corin looked at her. His face was his own again. The wax was gone from his nose. There was only the mole, small and brown, under his left eye. He was very white. He was leaning on the broom handle so hard that his knuckles stood out.
"It's dark," he said.
"I know. I'm sorry."
"I said if you weren't back by dark—" He stopped.
"What?" said Lyra. "What were you going to do?"
He did not answer. She did not think he knew. She thought he had been standing here on one leg in the cold for an hour, at least, trying to think of the end of that sentence, and had not been able to.
"He'll take me," she said. "The Duke. On his arm. In his carriage. At the eighth bell on Midwinter." She put her gloved hand to the pocket in the side of her gown, and felt the stiff card in it. "And he won't go up to the box."
Corin's face did not change.
"What did it cost?" he said.
No lies between you.
She looked at him. At his white face, and his tired eyes, and the mole. At the gray she could not see, under his gloves and his sleeves, running up past his elbows.
"One of my mother," she said.
She told him. Standing there on the black riverbank, with the dye running under the lamps, and Nell behind her with the empty bag. She told him all of it. The gallery, and the best days, and the woman in the boat in the fog. The portrait he had asked for, and she had refused. The five coins. The one he had chosen. Mr. Pennick's gray hands, and the brass glass, and the feeling of it going, like a tooth.
And the three words, at the end, as the door closed.
"Keep them safe," she said. "That's all I've got. That's all that's left of it. I don't even know who she meant."
Corin did not say anything.
He did not say I'm sorry. She was glad. She did not think she could have borne it.
He let go of the broom handle with one hand. He swayed. He caught himself. And with the free hand, he reached inside his coat, and took out the Ledger.
He opened it. Right there, on the riverbank, in the dark, by the light of the lamps on the water. He found a clean page. He took the stub of pencil from the spine. And he held it out to her.
The book and the pencil. On his open hand.
"Write it," he said.
Lyra looked at him.
"What you've got left," said Corin. "The words. The hall, and the cloak, and the gloves, and what she said. Whatever you can still say about it." His voice was very quiet. "Write it down. In your own hand. And I'll keep it."
He paused.
"You said I keep things," he said. "Let me keep this."
Lyra stood very still.
Then she took off Madame Hesper's gray kid gloves, one finger at a time, and gave them to Nell to hold. And she took the pencil in her bare, red, blistered hand. And she bent over the Ledger, open on his palm, in the lamplight, and wrote.
In small, fierce letters, slanting hard to the right, as if they were leaning into a wind.
My mother knelt down in the front hall at Crown Walk in a dark blue traveling cloak and gray kid gloves, and held my face, and looked at me for a long time. Then she said something. The end of it was: keep them safe.
I gave the rest to the Duke of Marrow, on Saturday, so that I could go to the Bourse and get Father back.
I had five. Now I have four.
Lyra Thorne.
She gave him back the pencil.
Corin looked at the page. He did not read it. Not properly. She saw him not read it. He looked at it the way you look at something you have been given to hold, without opening it. Then he closed the Ledger, very gently, and put it back inside his coat. Against his heart. Where it lived.
"Come in," he said. "It's cold. Mother Grue's made soup."
She went in.
And at the far end of the third arch, on his sack, in the red glow of the brazier, old Wick looked up from his paper as they passed. Just for a moment. His faded eyes went to Lyra's face, and rested there, the way they never rested on anybody's.
Then he looked down again, and picked up a scrap, and laid it on a pile.
Tap. Tap.
