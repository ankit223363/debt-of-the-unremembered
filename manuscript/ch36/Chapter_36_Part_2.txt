Chapter 36: The Duke's Gallery, Part 2 of 5: Duke Ansel Marrow
Duke Ansel Marrow
She walked down the room.
It was a long way. It was the longest walk of her life. Past the first chandelier and the second and the third. Past Lady Harriet Langley with her cake. Past the Fennimore boys by their pillar. Past old Lady Ashcombe's turned back. Past the mirrors, where a hundred gray-gowned girls walked beside her, all with their chins up and their faces still. She did not hurry. She did not look to the left or the right. She walked the way she had been taught to walk at fourteen, with a book on her head, up and down the long gallery at Crown Walk, while the governess counted.
The crowd opened in front of her. Nobody spoke. She could hear her mother's slippers on the polished floor. Click. Click. Click. And behind her, Nell's boots, much louder, trying to be quiet.
At the far end of the room there was a fire.
It was in a fireplace so big that a man could have stood up in it. White marble, carved with fruit and flowers and fat stone children. The fire in it was enormous, a roaring heap of whole logs, and the heat came off it in waves. In front of the fire there was a chair.
And in the chair there was the Duke.
He was very big. That was the first thing. Not tall, exactly, though he was tall. Big all over, the way a great old tree is big, or a bear. He filled the chair. It was a large chair, a gold one with red velvet cushions, and he filled it from side to side and spilled a little over the arms. His shoulders were round and heavy. His chest was deep. His belly, under a waistcoat of dove-gray silk embroidered all over with tiny silver leaves, was large and smooth and comfortable, like a feather pillow.
And he was soft. Soft-looking. His face was large and pale and smooth, with plump cheeks and a small soft chin and a mouth that was full and red, like a girl's. His hair was fair, going silver, and curled, and very carefully arranged. He wore a coat of dark blue velvet so fine that it seemed to drink the firelight. His neckcloth was a fall of white lace. And his hands, which lay folded on top of his belly, were large and white and soft, with dimples where the knuckles should have been.
There was a ring on every finger.
Lyra counted them without meaning to. Ten. Gold, and silver, and one of something black. Rubies and emeralds and a great square sapphire on the first finger of his right hand, as blue as the stained glass in the hall. And on his left little finger, a plain thin band of silver, so plain that it looked strange among the others. Like a weed in a flower bed.
His eyes were half shut.
They were heavy-lidded and pale, a washed-out gray-green, like water in a pond in winter. They looked sleepy. They looked as if he might drop off at any moment, there in front of his roaring fire, with sixty people watching. But they were not sleepy. Lyra saw that as soon as she came close. Under the heavy lids, the pale eyes were moving. Very slowly. Over her face. Over her gown. Over her gloves, and her slippers, and the way she was standing, and Nell behind her. Taking everything. Missing nothing. The way a cat lying in the sun takes in every bird in the garden without once seeming to open its eyes.
She stopped in front of his chair.
She did not curtsy.
She saw him notice that. She saw the heavy lids lift, just a fraction.
"Your Grace," she said.
"Miss Thorne." The soft, purring voice. "You came on foot."
"Yes."
"All the way up the Hill. In Isolde's gray." He looked at the gown. "Hesper's work. The cut at the shoulder. Nobody else in this city can cut a shoulder like that." His pale eyes came back to her face. "She let it out once, you know. That gown. At the waist. And took it in again. Did she tell you?"
Lyra did not answer.
"No," said the Duke. "I don't suppose she did." He smiled. It was a lovely smile. Warm and soft and kind, like a fat old uncle's. It went all the way up into his pale eyes and stopped there, and did not go any further. "You have hay behind your left ear."
Somebody behind her laughed. A short, sharp, nervous laugh, quickly stopped.
Lyra did not put her hand up. She kept her gloved hands folded in front of her and her face perfectly still.
"Yes," she said.
The Duke looked at her a moment longer.
Then he threw back his great soft head and laughed. A real laugh, deep and rich and delighted, that went rolling down the long white room and came back off the mirrors. He laughed until his belly shook under the silver leaves.
"Oh, good," he said. "Oh, very good. She doesn't touch it. Did you see? She doesn't even touch it." He wiped his eyes with the back of one ringed hand. "Harriet, did you see? You'd have screamed. You'd have run from the room. Fennimore, your mother would have fainted." He looked round at the silent crowd with his sleepy pale eyes, enjoying them. "There is more breeding in that one bit of hay than in this whole room. Somebody write that down. I want it in the papers."
Nobody wrote it down.
Nobody said anything at all.
The Duke turned back to Lyra. His laugh had gone, all at once, the way a fire goes out when you shut the door of the stove. His face was soft and pleasant and quite still.
"Sit down, Miss Thorne," he said. "There. That chair. And your girl may stand behind you and look at my ceiling with her mouth open, which is what my ceiling is for." He did not look at Nell. "Somebody bring Miss Thorne some tea."
Somebody did. A footman in gold, with a silver tray. Lyra sat on the edge of the little gold chair the Duke had pointed to, opposite his own, so close to the fire that she could feel it on her cheek. She took the cup. It was so thin she could see the firelight through it. She did not drink.
The room was still silent. Sixty people, with their cups and their cakes, standing in their groups up and down the long white room, watching.
The Duke did not seem to notice them.
"How is your father?" he said.
"The same," said Lyra.
"Still tapping?"
She went cold.
She did not let it show. She did not let anything show. She sat on the gold chair with the thin cup in her gloved hands and her face as still as the stone girl in the garden on Crown Walk.
"I don't know what you mean," she said.
"Don't you." The pale eyes rested on her. "No. Of course you don't." He smiled his soft smile again. "I hear things, Miss Thorne. It's one of my vices. I hear a great many things. I heard he had been taken out of Pruett's. I heard the Inquisition would very much like to know where. I heard a little bird say a barge." He spread his ringed hands. "But I hear a great deal of nonsense, too. I never know which is which until it's too late to matter."
Lyra said nothing.
"And how is my mare?" said the Duke. "No. Your mare. I beg your pardon. I always forget. The gray. What did you call her?"
Lyra's fingers tightened on the cup.
"Mist," she said.
"Mist. That's it. A pretty name." He nodded slowly. "She's very well. I ride her myself, on Sundays, in the park. She's a little thin in the neck. She pines, I think. They do, horses. For the hand they knew." He looked at her over his folded hands. "Would you like to see her? Before you go? She's in the mews. I could have her brought round."
"No," said Lyra. "Thank you."
"No." He sighed. "No, I suppose not."
He was quiet for a moment. The fire roared. Somewhere at the far end of the room, very softly, somebody coughed.
"So," said the Duke. "Miss Thorne. You walk up my steps on a Saturday afternoon, in your mother's gown, with hay in your hair and a girl from the arches carrying an empty bag. In front of all these people. Who have, I believe, been rather unkind to you." He did not look at them. "And you sit in my chair. And you don't drink my tea. And you don't curtsy." A pause. "You must want something very badly."
"Yes," said Lyra.
"What?"
She looked at him across the little space in front of the fire. At the great soft face. At the sleepy eyes that were not sleepy.
She thought about Vespera. Make him ask twice.
She thought about doing this somewhere quiet. In a little room, with a door that shut. Where nobody would see.
And then she thought: no. That's why I came on a Saturday.
"I want you to take me to the Masquerade," said Lyra Thorne. "At the Bourse. On Midwinter Night. On your arm. As your guest."
She did not lower her voice.
She heard it go out into the long white room. She heard it reach the first chandelier, and the second, and the third. She heard, behind her, sixty people draw in their breath at the same moment, like a wave pulling back from a beach. She heard a cup go down on a saucer, very hard, somewhere near the door. She heard Lady Harriet Langley say oh in a small high voice, and stop.
The Duke did not move.
He sat in his gold chair with his ringed hands folded on his belly and looked at her. For a long, long time. His pale eyes did not blink.
Then, slowly, very slowly, the soft red mouth began to smile.
Not the uncle's smile. A different one. Smaller. Much more real.
"Ah," said Duke Ansel Marrow, very softly. "There she is."
He put his great soft hands on the arms of the chair, and heaved, and stood up. He was even bigger standing. He stood over her, between her and the fire, with the light behind him, so that his shadow fell across her lap.
"Not here," he said. "Not in front of all these people. They've had quite enough for one afternoon. Look at them. Poor dears. Harriet's dropped her cake." He held out one hand to her. The one with the great square sapphire. "Come and see my gallery, Miss Thorne. It's very quiet in my gallery. And I think you and I have a great deal to say to each other that is nobody else's business at all."
Lyra looked at the hand.
She put down the thin cup, untouched, on the little table beside her chair. She stood up. She did not take the Duke's hand.
But she went with him.
The Gallery of Best Days
The Duke walked slowly. He was too big to walk any other way. He went down the long white room with Lyra beside him and Nell behind, and the crowd parted in front of him the way water parts in front of a barge, and closed again behind him in a rush of whispers. He did not look at any of them. He nodded, now and then, at nothing. He hummed a little, under his breath.
They went out into the round hall, with its colored light and its banks of lilies. And up the great curved white stairs, one step at a time, the Duke breathing heavily, his ringed hand sliding along the gilded rail. And along the gallery at the top, under the dome of colored glass, with the whole of the hall laid out below them like a chessboard. And through a door at the far end.
The door was black.
It was the only black thing Lyra had seen in Marrow House. Plain black wood, with a black iron handle and no knocker, set into the white wall like a hole. A footman stood beside it. Not in gold. In black, like the steward. He opened it without a word as the Duke came near, and stood back, and did not look at them.
They went through.
It was dark inside. Not quite dark. Dim. After the white and gold of the rest of the house, Lyra's eyes could not make anything out at first. Only shapes. A long room, she thought. Long and narrow, like a corridor, or like the long gallery at Crown Walk. With a low ceiling. And a smell of beeswax and cold stone and something else, very faint, that she knew and could not name.
The footman shut the door behind them.
The noise of the drawing room stopped. All of it. The voices, the violin, which had started up again, the clink of cups. Gone, as if a hand had been laid over a mouth. The silence in the long dim room was so thick that Lyra could hear her own heart. And the Duke's breathing. And, behind her, very small, Nell swallowing.
Then her eyes began to see.
The walls were black. Painted black, or hung with black velvet, she could not tell. The floor was black stone, polished, so that it shone faintly like still water. There were no windows. The only light came from the cases.
There were dozens of them. Running down both sides of the long room, in two straight rows, on black wooden stands as high as Lyra's waist. Glass boxes, each about the size of a jewel case, with brass corners and brass feet. And inside each one, on a little cushion of black velvet, a vial.
The vials were glowing.
Each one gave off its own faint light. That was all the light there was. Soft and dim and steady, like the light of a candle seen through a curtain. Each one a different color. Pale gold. Rose. A clear, clean blue, like the sky at noon in summer. A soft green, like new leaves. A warm amber, like honey held up to a window. They stood in their glass cases in two long rows down the black room, glowing, one after another, like a street of lit windows at night seen from very far away.
Lyra stood still in the doorway and looked at them.
She had seen vials before. At the Bourse, at seventeen. On plum velvet, under the glowing dome. But they had been in rows, on long tables, hundreds of them, with cards beside them, like goods in a shop.
These were not like goods in a shop.
These were like the candles in a chapel.
"My gallery," said the Duke softly.
He had stopped beside her. In the dim colored light from the cases, his great soft face looked different. Older. Gentler. The sleepy eyes were wide open now, for the first time, and they were moving along the two rows of glowing cases the way a man's eyes move over the faces of his children.
"Two hundred and eleven," he said. "I've been collecting for thirty years. Since I was younger than you." He began to walk, slowly, down the middle of the room, between the rows. "Come. I'll show you."
Lyra went with him.
The first case on the left held a vial of pale gold. There was a card in front of it, propped on a little brass stand. Not printed, like the Bourse cards. Handwritten. In brown ink, in a big, soft, round hand.
A Wedding Morning. Ostmark. The first of May. The bride's.
"She sold it to pay her husband's debts," said the Duke. "Twenty years later. He'd gambled away her dowry. She came to the Bank with it, and the Bank sent for me, because they know what I like." He looked at the soft gold light. "She didn't need it any more, she said. She said she'd rather not remember being that happy with a man who'd turned out like that. So I bought it." He paused. "I paid her twice what she asked."
He moved on.
The Sea. First sight of it. A boy of six.
A clear, clean blue.
Winning the Race. The Long Mile at Ashcombe Downs. The winner, aged nineteen.
A hot, bright red, like a coal.
Being Forgiven. A daughter, by her mother, on her mother's deathbed.
A soft, pale rose.
Lyra stopped in front of that one. She did not mean to. She stood and looked at the rose-colored light for a long time.
"That one I did not buy," said the Duke behind her. "That one was given to me. By the daughter. She said she couldn't bear to keep it, and couldn't bear to burn it, and couldn't bear to sell it. So she gave it to the one man in the city who would never, ever open it." He was quiet. "She was right. I never shall."
Lyra turned and looked at him.
"You don't open them?"
"Never." The Duke smiled. Not the uncle's smile. A small, tired, private one. "Opening is for people who want to have something, Miss Thorne. To put it in their heads and pretend it's theirs. Like Harriet Langley, buying some fisher girl's first kiss so that she can lie in bed at night and pretend she had it." He made a small face. "That's not collecting. That's eating."
He bent, with a grunt, and put his great soft face close to the glass of the nearest case. The blue one. The sea.
"Look," he said. "Here. In the top of the case. There's a lens."
There was. Lyra saw it now. Set into the brass frame of the case, at the top, was a small round window of glass, thicker than the rest, curved like the bottom of a bottle.
"Put your eye to it," said the Duke. "Go on."
Lyra hesitated.
Then she bent, in her mother's gray gown, and put her eye to the lens.
At first she saw only blue. A soft, blurred, shifting blue, like looking up through water. Then, slowly, the blue began to move. To shape itself. To have edges. And she saw, very faintly, the way you see a face through rain on a window, a line of white. Moving. Coming toward her and falling away. And above it, a great flat brightness, going on and on. And very close, so close it might have been her own, a small hand, a child's hand, held up against the light, with the fingers spread.
The sea. A boy of six, seeing it for the first time.
She could not hear anything. She could not feel anything. It was only a picture, faint and silent and blurred, like a picture in a dream. But she could see it. She could see the hand.
She straightened up. Her eyes were stinging.
"You can only see," said the Duke softly. "Never hear. Never feel. Never know what they were thinking. Only the edges. Only the light." He straightened too, with another grunt. "That's enough. That's all I want. I don't need to have their best days, Miss Thorne. I only need to know that they're here. That they're safe. That nobody is ever going to eat them, or burn them, or let them rot in a crate in somebody's cellar." His soft voice did not change. "Two hundred and eleven of the best days that anybody in this world has ever had. In one room. Where nobody can hurt them."
Lyra looked at him.
She did not know what she had expected. Not this. She had expected a man like Harriet Langley, only richer. A man who collected other people's lives the way some men collected snuffboxes, to show off. A man who ate.
She did not know what this was.
Careful, she thought. It came to her in Corin's voice, oddly. Dry and quiet. Careful. Kind is not the same as good.
"Come," said the Duke. "There's one more thing I want you to see. Down at the end."
He walked on down the long dark room, between the two rows of glowing lights. And Lyra followed him, with Nell creeping behind her, past a hundred other people's best days. A first child. A last dance. A house finished, after forty years of building. A friend found again. A song sung perfectly, once, in an empty church, with nobody listening.
And at the very end of the room, on the left, in the last case of all, a vial that was not gold, or rose, or blue.
It was gray.
The soft, dim, shifting gray of a canal in a fog.
