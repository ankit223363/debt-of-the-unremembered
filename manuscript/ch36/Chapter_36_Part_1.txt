Chapter 36: The Duke's Gallery, Part 1 of 5: The Needle and Thimble
The Needle and Thimble
The back door of the Needle and Thimble was in a mews behind Velvet Street, between a coach house and a wall with broken glass along the top of it, and it was painted the same plum color as the front.
Lyra had never used it. She had always come in by the front, under the gold thimble that swung on its bracket over the door, with the bell ringing over her head. That had been a long time ago. Before the auction. Before the stable. Before everything.
She knocked.
Nell stood behind her in the mews, in her one good shawl, with her hands knotted together and her eyes going everywhere. At the coach house. At the wall with the glass on it. At the backs of the tall houses rising up on every side, with their neat black drainpipes and their scrubbed back steps and their curtained windows. Nell had been born in the arches. She had never been further up the Hill than the fish market. She had walked all the way up behind Lyra in silence, with her mouth a little open, as if she were climbing a mountain in a dream.
The door opened.
Madame Hesper stood in it.
She was just the same. That was the first thing Lyra thought, and it went through her like warm water. Tall and thin as a heron. Black dress buttoned to the chin. Brown linen apron with its bulging pockets. Iron-gray hair pulled back tight, with two long pins through the knot in a cross. Round spectacles on a silver chain. And in the corner of her mouth, four pins, sticking out like a cat's whiskers.
She looked at Lyra.
She looked at her for a long time, from the top of her damp head to the bottom of her borrowed boots. At the too-big brown dress from the arches, with its sleeves rolled up. At the red, rough, blistered hands. At the face.
She took the pins out of her mouth, one at a time.
"You," said Madame Hesper, "have been forking hay."
Lyra opened her mouth. Nothing came out.
"Your hands," said Madame Hesper. "The blisters are in the wrong places for a needle and the right places for a fork. And there is hay in your hair. Behind your left ear. A great deal of it." She pushed the pins into the pincushion on her wrist. "And you smell of horse, and woodsmoke, and something that I believe came out of a dye vat. And you have brought me a child who has never been up the Hill in her life." She looked at Nell. Nell shrank. "Good afternoon, child. Come in. Both of you. Quickly. The mews has eyes."
They went in.
The back room was just as Lyra remembered it. Long and low and warm, with a stove at one end, red at the seams, and a long table down the middle covered in bolts of cloth. Silk and velvet and wool and linen, in every color, heaped up and spilling over. Tailor's dummies stood about the room like headless guests at a party, wearing half-finished gowns pinned at the seams. There were patterns hanging from hooks, and tape measures hanging from nails, and a shelf of pattern books, fat and battered, with their spines cracked. And over everything, the smell of the room. Steam and starch and new cloth and warm iron.
Nell stood just inside the door and stared at the dummies as if they might come for her.
Madame Hesper shut the door behind them and turned the key.
"Well," she said. "I suppose you want the gray."
"Yes," said Lyra.
"And I suppose you want it today."
"In an hour."
"An hour." Madame Hesper put her spectacles on. She looked at Lyra through them. "And where, may I ask, are you going in your mother's evening gown at two o'clock on a Saturday afternoon?"
Lyra told her.
She told her quickly and plainly, standing by the stove in her borrowed boots. Not all of it. Not the barge, or the hay, or Brindle. Not the Bourse. Only that she must call on the Duke of Marrow this afternoon, at his house on the Crescent, during his Saturday. And that she must ask him for something. And that she must look, when she asked, like a Thorne.
Madame Hesper listened without a word.
When Lyra had finished, she took off her spectacles and let them fall on their chain. She was quiet for a moment.
"Ansel Marrow," she said.
"Yes."
"You know what he did. To your father. To your house."
"Yes."
"And you will go to him, in his own drawing room, in front of the whole of the Hill, and ask him for a favor." Madame Hesper's long face did not move. "In your mother's gown."
"Yes," said Lyra.
Madame Hesper looked at her a moment longer.
Then she turned away and went to a tall cupboard at the far end of the room, a plain wooden cupboard with a lock, and took a key from her apron pocket and opened it. And took out the gray.
It hung from her hands like smoke.
Lyra had worn it once before. In the autumn. To go into her own house in the dark. She remembered the feel of it. Soft, heavy, gray wool, so fine it was almost silk, falling straight from a high narrow waist to the floor, with long close sleeves and a collar that stood up a little at the back of the neck. No lace. No trimming. Nothing at all but the cut, and the color, which was the gray of a winter sky just before snow.
She had promised to bring it back. She had. Juno had carried it here in a parcel, the week after.
"Take that off," said Madame Hesper. "All of it. Child, the screen. Hold up the screen. Don't look at me like that, I have seen more ladies in their shifts than you have had hot dinners."
Nell held up the screen, a tall folding thing of painted silk with birds on it, her face scarlet.
Lyra took off the brown dress from the arches. She took off the boots that pinched. She stood in her shift by the stove, with her red hands and her blistered feet and the hay behind her ear, and Madame Hesper came round the screen with the gray gown over her arm and pins in her mouth again.
She dressed her.
She did it the way she did everything. Quickly and without fuss, with her long cold fingers moving so fast that Lyra could not follow them. The gown went over her head. It fell. It settled. Madame Hesper turned her round by the shoulders, and pinned, and tugged, and smoothed, and pinned again, muttering round the pins.
"Thinner," she said. "You are thinner than you were. In the autumn. Half an inch at the waist. More at the shoulder. You are not eating."
"I eat."
"You eat on a barge," said Madame Hesper. "Bread and cheese and raw onions, by the smell of you. Hold still."
Lyra held still.
She had not said barge.
She did not say anything now. She stood by the stove in her mother's gown with the old woman's cold fingers working at her waist, and she did not ask how Madame Hesper knew. She thought she would rather not hear the answer.
"Shoes," said Madame Hesper. She went to a shelf. She came back with a pair of gray kid slippers, soft and plain, with flat heels. "These were hers too. I bought them back from the same thief on Silk Street. He wanted them for a dancer." She knelt, very stiffly, with her knees cracking, and put them on Lyra's feet. They fitted. Of course they fitted. "And gloves."
She took a pair from her apron pocket.
They were gray kid too. Long ones, to above the elbow, soft as a cat's ear. Lyra looked at them in the old woman's hands, and something happened in her chest, and she could not breathe.
Gray kid. Cool on her cheeks.
Her mother kneeling in the front hall, in a traveling cloak, with her gloves already on. Holding Lyra's face in both hands.
"Are those—" Lyra began.
"No," said Madame Hesper. "Those are mine. I have had them forty years. I wore them to my own wedding." She held them out. "Your mother's went with her. Wherever she went."
Lyra took them.
She put them on. Slowly. One finger at a time. They hid the blisters and the red, rough skin, and the broken nails, and all the hay. They made her hands into a lady's hands again. Smooth and pale and gray.
Madame Hesper stood back.
She looked at Lyra for a long time, in the warm low room, with the headless dummies all around them and Nell peering over the top of the silk screen with her mouth open. And her long stern face did something Lyra had only seen it do once before. In the autumn. By the curtain.
It softened.
"Now your hair," said Madame Hesper. "And then, my girl, you are going to sit down at my table and eat a bowl of soup. All of it. I will not send you to Ansel Marrow on an empty stomach. He can smell hunger. He likes it."
She went to get the soup.
And at the door to the front of the shop, she stopped, and turned, with her hand on the frame.
"Lyra," she said.
"Yes, Madame?"
"There is a pocket," said Madame Hesper. "In the gown. Inside the left side of the waist, where the seam is. I sewed it in last night." She paused. "I sew a pocket into everything now. Since the autumn. For a list."
She went out.
Lyra stood by the stove.
After a moment, she put her gloved hand to the left side of her waist. And felt it. A little flat place in the seam, under the wool, no bigger than a playing card. Empty.
Waiting.
The Crescent
They went up through the High Quarter on foot, because there was no money for a hackney, and because Madame Hesper said a Thorne on foot was a Thorne with nothing left to lose, and the Hill was always a little afraid of those.
It was a bright, hard, cold afternoon. The sky had cleared while Lyra ate her soup. It was a pale, washed blue now, very high, with thin white clouds pulled out across it like wool on a carding comb, and the sun, low in the south, threw long blue shadows across the streets and made every window in the High Quarter flash gold.
The High Quarter was clean. That was what Nell kept saying, under her breath, behind Lyra's shoulder. It's so clean. Miss, it's so clean. And it was. The streets were wide and paved with smooth gray stone, swept every morning. The gutters ran with clear water. The houses stood back from the road behind black iron railings, tall and pale and quiet, with their brass knockers shining and their windows polished and their front steps scrubbed white. There was no smoke here. Or there was, from the chimneys, but it went straight up into the blue and away, and did not come down on you as soot.
And it was quiet. That was the other thing. In the arches there was always noise. Coughing, and crying, and arguing, and the drip of dye. On the river there was the slap of water and the creak of ropes. Here there was nothing. A carriage going by, its wheels hissing on the stone. A dog barking, very far away, behind a wall. A footman in a doorway clearing his throat. The click of Lyra's mother's gray slippers on the pavement. That was all.
Nell walked two steps behind her, the way Madame Hesper had told her to, in a plain black dress and a white cap and apron that Madame Hesper had found for her in a cupboard and pinned onto her in five minutes flat. She was carrying a small velvet bag with nothing in it. A lady's maid always carries something, Madame Hesper had said. It doesn't matter what. She held the bag in front of her in both hands as if it were full of eggs.
They went up Lily Hill, and along the top of the Gardens, where the trees were bare and black and the paths were raked gravel. And at the end of the Gardens, where four roads met round a stone fountain with a frozen bronze boy in the middle of it, Lyra stopped.
She had not meant to.
The road to the left was Crown Walk.
She could see it from here. A long, quiet, straight street of tall gray houses, older than the ones around the Gardens, with steeper roofs and smaller windows and lead on their gutters gone white with age. Trees down both sides, limes, bare now. And about halfway down on the right-hand side, a house that was not like the others.
Its shutters were closed.
All of them. Every window on every floor, from the area railings to the attics, shut tight behind gray wooden shutters, like eyes that would not open. The brass knocker on the door had been taken off. There were four marks on the black paint where it had been screwed on. The front steps had not been scrubbed. They were gray and green with old leaves and old rain. And in the front garden, behind the railings, there was a stone girl with a jug. A small statue, on a plinth, among dead roses. The jug was tipped as if to pour. Nothing came out of it.
Lyra stood by the fountain and looked at it.
She had grown up behind those shutters. She had climbed that stone girl's plinth when she was six and been smacked for it. She had stood at the nursery window at the top of the house, on the right, the second one from the end, and watched her mother stand there before her.
"Miss?" said Nell. Very small. "Miss, are you all right?"
"Yes," said Lyra.
She turned away from Crown Walk.
She took the road to the right.
The Crescent was at the very top of the Hill. It was the highest street in Veridia, and the newest, and the whitest. A great sweeping curve of tall houses, all exactly alike, all joined together in one long smooth bend like a white wall, five floors high, with a hundred tall windows all in a row, and a hundred black iron balconies, and a hundred front doors at the top of a hundred flights of white steps. In front of it, inside the curve, was a lawn. A great half-moon of grass, white with frost, with a gravel drive going round the edge of it and gas lamps along the drive on tall black posts, already lit, though it was hardly past the second bell. The city paid for those lamps. Everybody knew. The city paid for them to burn all night, every night, on the Crescent, so that the people who lived there need never once walk in the dark.
From the top of the Crescent you could see the whole of Veridia. Lyra did not look. She knew what she would see. The roofs and the chimneys and the spires going down and down to the brown river, and the smoke lying over the Lower Docks, and somewhere, too far to make out, the Dye-Works with its colored streams, and the arches.
Marrow House was in the middle of the curve.
It was easy to find. It was the only one with a crowd outside.
There were carriages all along the gravel drive in front of it. Nine of them. Ten. Their horses stood steaming in the cold with blankets over their backs, and their coachmen stood in a knot by the railings, stamping and blowing on their fingers and passing a flask. There were coats of arms painted on the carriage doors. Lyra knew most of them. The Langley swan. The three keys of Ashcombe. The black bull of the Fennimores. The tower of Ost. She had danced with the sons of half of those doors when she was sixteen.
The front door of Marrow House stood open at the top of its white steps. It was painted gold. Not yellow. Gold. Real gold leaf, laid on thin, so that it shone in the low sun like the inside of a jewel box. And on either side of it stood a footman in livery the same color, gold coats with white facings and white powdered wigs, as still as the stone horses at the Bridewell gates.
Lyra stopped at the bottom of the steps.
She looked up.
It was a long way up. Fourteen steps. White. Scrubbed so clean that they shone. And at the top, the gold door, and the two gold footmen, and beyond them, through the open door, light. A great deal of light. And noise. Voices. Laughter. The clink of cups. A violin, playing something quick and pretty.
The whole of the Hill was in there.
Every one of the people who had shut their doors on her. Who had stood behind their own gold footmen and said Miss Thorne is not expected. Miss Thorne is not at home to callers. Miss Thorne will understand. Every one of them was in there, drinking the Duke's tea, and laughing at the Duke's wit.
And she was going to walk in among them, in her dead mother's gown, with a girl from the arches behind her carrying an empty bag.
She felt Nell come up close behind her. She felt the girl's breath on her shoulder, quick and frightened.
"Miss," whispered Nell. "Miss, do we have to?"
Lyra did not answer at once.
She thought about her father, lying in the hay, with his gray eyes on the ceiling. Saying one word. Bourse.
She thought about Oren, standing by the canvas all night with his hand on his sword.
She thought about Corin in the broken chair, with the wax still on his nose, watching her go and saying nothing at all, because there was nothing he could say that would be true.
"Yes," she said. "We do."
She lifted the hem of her mother's gray gown in one gloved hand, the way she had been taught. She put her chin up, the way she had been taught. She made her face still, the way she had been taught, so still that nothing at all showed in it.
And she went up the fourteen white steps of Marrow House, one at a time, without hurrying, toward the gold.
The Drawing Room
The gold footmen did not move as she came up the last step. They did not look at her. Their powdered heads stayed exactly where they were, and their eyes stayed fixed on the air above the frozen lawn, as if she were not there at all.
But the doors behind them were open. And in the doorway, as her foot touched the top step, a man appeared.
He was old and thin and very upright, in a black coat and a white neckcloth, with a long gold chain across his waistcoat and a face like a closed book. The Duke's steward. Lyra had seen him before, she thought. Years ago. Behind the Duke's chair at some dinner, pouring wine. He looked at her for a long moment, from her mother's gray slippers to her mother's gray-gowned shoulders to her face. Nothing at all happened in his own.
"Miss Thorne," he said.
Not a question. Not a welcome. A fact, set down on a table.
"I have come to call on the Duke," said Lyra. "If he is at home."
"His Grace is always at home on Saturdays, Miss Thorne." The steward stood aside. "To everyone."
She went in.
The hall was round, and very tall, and white. White marble floor, white marble pillars, white marble stairs going up in a great curve to a gallery, and at the top of the stairs, a dome of colored glass with the winter sun coming through it, so that the white floor was splashed with patches of red and blue and gold. There were flowers. In winter. Great banks of them, on stands, all along the walls. White lilies and hothouse roses and something tall and blue that Lyra did not know. Their smell was very strong in the warm air. Sweet and heavy, almost too much, like a church at a funeral.
She did not like lilies.
She had not always not liked them. She did now.
The noise came from the left. A pair of tall white doors, standing open, and beyond them, light, and voices, and the violin. The steward went ahead of her. Lyra followed. Nell followed Lyra, two steps behind, so close that Lyra could hear her breathing.
The steward stopped in the doorway. He drew himself up.
"Miss Lyra Thorne," he said.
He did not say it loudly. He did not need to.
The drawing room went quiet.
Not all at once. It went quiet the way a pond goes still when you throw a stone in it, from the middle outward. The people nearest the door stopped talking first. Then the people beyond them, turning to see why. Then the people beyond them. The quiet went out across the long room in a ring, wider and wider, until it reached the far end, by the fire. And the violin, last of all, faltered, and played two more notes, and stopped.
Lyra stood in the doorway.
It was the longest room she had ever seen in a private house. Longer than the long gallery at Crown Walk. It went the whole depth of Marrow House, from the windows on the Crescent at the front to tall windows at the back looking out over a garden, and it was white and gold all the way. White walls, with gold on the moldings. A white ceiling, painted with gold stars. Three chandeliers, hanging in a row down the middle, each one a great frozen waterfall of crystal, lit even now in the afternoon so that the drops threw little rainbows on the walls. Mirrors between the windows, tall ones, in gold frames, so that the room seemed to go on and on, and the chandeliers seemed to be thirty, and the guests seemed to be a thousand.
There were perhaps sixty of them, really.
They stood in groups, the way people stand at a Saturday, with cups of tea in their hands and little plates of cake. The ladies in afternoon dresses of silk and velvet, in every color of the winter fashion, plum and dove and bottle green. The men in dark coats and pale waistcoats. Young and old. Lyra knew almost every face.
And every face was turned to her.
She saw them all, in one long look, the way you see a whole field in one flash of lightning. Lady Harriet Langley, by the first window, round and pink in rose-colored silk, with a piece of cake halfway to her open mouth. The two Fennimore boys, whom she had danced with at sixteen, standing together by a pillar, looking at her as if she were a ghost. Old Lady Ashcombe, in black, with her three keys in diamonds on her bosom, sitting very upright on a gold sofa with her stick in front of her. Lord Ost and his wife. The Pellow sisters. Young Captain Dray, in his regimentals, who had once sent her a poem.
All of them.
All looking.
She stood very still in the doorway with her chin up and her gloved hands folded in front of her and her face showing nothing at all. And she let them look.
They'll be watching your face, Vespera had said. Not your mask. Your face. Waiting for it to crack.
She had no mask today. Only her face. Only her mother's gown and Madame Hesper's gloves and twenty years of being taught to stand like this.
It held.
She had stood in rooms like this before. Many times. She had stood in this very room, at sixteen, in a white dress with blue ribbons, on her father's arm, at her first Saturday. She remembered it. Not as a coin, not as one of the things she counted. Just as a thing that had happened, a long time ago, to a girl she did not know very well any more. That girl had been so frightened that she had not eaten for two days before. She had stood in this doorway and looked at the chandeliers and the mirrors and the faces, and thought that she would die of it. And her father had bent down and said into her ear, very quietly, so that nobody else could hear, Pick one face, Ly. Just one. The kindest one in the room. And look at that one, and pretend the rest are furniture.
She had picked old Lady Ashcombe, that night. Because Lady Ashcombe had smiled at her.
Lady Ashcombe was not smiling now.
Lyra looked for another face. A kind one. Just one.
There was not one in the room.
So she looked at the chandeliers instead, and pretended that the faces were furniture, and it held.
Somewhere to her left, very quietly, somebody said, "Well."
And somebody else, a woman, said, "In that gown. Is that—? It is. That's Isolde's gray. I'd know it anywhere."
And a third voice, lower: "Over a stable, they said. In the Lower Quarter. And then she simply vanished. And now—"
"Cressida's niece," said someone else. "Does Cressida know she's here?"
"Cressida never comes to Ansel's Saturdays. You know that. Not since—"
"Hush."
Old Lady Ashcombe, on the gold sofa, put both her hands on the top of her stick. She looked at Lyra for a long moment down the length of her nose. Then, slowly and deliberately, she turned her head away, and looked out of the window at the frozen lawn instead.
Three or four people near her did the same.
Lyra did not move.
She felt Nell, behind her, shrink. She felt the girl's fingers, through the gray wool, take hold of a fold of her skirt and hang on.
She did not look round.
Let them, she thought. Let them. It's what they're for. Let them turn their backs. Let them whisper. Let them watch.
The more they watch me, the less anyone will ever watch a waiter.
And then, from the far end of the long white room, from beside the fire, a voice said:
"Oh, how wonderful."
It was a man's voice. Deep and soft and lazy, with a kind of purr in it, like a big cat stretching in the sun. It did not shout. It did not need to. It came down the length of the room through the silence the way warm water comes through cold.
"Oh, how perfectly wonderful," it said. "Everybody be quiet. No, you are all quiet already. How obliging of you." A pause. A soft laugh. "Miss Thorne. Come here. Come all the way down here to me, past all these people pretending not to look at you, so that I can see you properly. I have been hoping you would come for weeks."
