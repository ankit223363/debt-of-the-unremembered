Chapter 44: Sterling's Gift, Part 2 of 5: The Knee
The Knee
Doctor Silas came through from the third arch with his coat on over his nightshirt and his crooked spectacles in his hand, and his gray hair standing up on one side where he had been lying on it.
He had been asleep on a pile of sacks by the far brazier, the way he had been asleep every night since Sunday, for an hour or two at a time, between the arches and the crypt. Tobin had gone to wake him. Tobin was eight and very serious and had a voice like a squeaking door, and he had stood beside the doctor's sacks and said Doctor, doctor, he's back and he's bleeding until the doctor sat up.
Now Silas stood at the foot of the pallet, and put his spectacles on, and looked down.
He did not say anything for a while.
He was a tall man, and thin, so thin that his coat hung on him like a coat on a hook, and in the red light of the brazier, with his long neck bent and his long arms hanging, he looked like a heron standing in a ditch, looking down into the water for a fish. He had a tired face. It had been tired the first time Lyra saw it, on Saturday night, and it had got more tired every day since, and now, at one o'clock in the morning, it looked like a face that had been drawn in pencil and then half rubbed out.
"Light," he said.
Lyra brought the lamp. She knelt by the pallet and held it up. Silas knelt on the other side, slowly, with a crack from his own knees, and took out his little knife and cut the binding.
He cut it all the way. From the hip to the ankle. The gray strips of Hessa's mother's dress, and the white linen under them, and the dark stiff place where the linen had soaked through and dried and soaked through again. He peeled it back the way you peel a bandage off a child, slowly, talking all the time in a low voice about nothing. About the snow. About the price of lint. About a cat he had once had that would only drink out of a teacup.
Corin lay with his eyes shut and his jaw tight and let him.
Then the leg was bare in the lamplight.
Lyra had seen it before. She had held it on Saturday night, when the doctor opened it, and on Monday, when he washed the coal out of it. She knew the wound. The small deep hole in the back of the thigh above the knee, where the rod had gone in at the Bridewell stables, with the red road running up from it toward the hip. She had thought she knew the worst of it.
She had not seen the knee.
It was swollen to twice its size, round and tight and shining, and the color of a plum. The skin over it was stretched so thin it looked as if it would split if you touched it. And the leg did not lie the way a leg lies. It lay dead straight, from hip to heel, like a log, like the leg of a wooden doll, and the foot at the end of it stood up, toes to the roof, and did not fall to the side the way a sleeping foot falls.
The wound had torn. She could see where. The stitches the doctor had put in on Monday afternoon had pulled through the edge of the skin, two of them, three, and the hole was open again, and bleeding slowly, dark and thick.
The ninth step, she thought. He sat down on the ninth step with his leg across it, and the wound opened, and he left his blood there for his sister to find.
Silas looked at it for a long time.
Then he put out one long careful hand, and laid it flat on the front of the swollen knee, very lightly, and pressed.
Corin made no sound. His whole body went stiff on the pallet, from his shoulders to his heels, and his hand on the Ledger went white.
Silas took his hand away.
Then he put it under Corin's heel, and lifted the heel an inch from the blanket. Not the knee. The heel. And held it there. And watched the knee.
The knee did not bend. Not at all. The whole leg came up off the blanket in one piece, from the heel to the hip, straight as a plank.
Silas put the heel down again. Gently.
"Well," he said.
He sat back on his heels and took his spectacles off and rubbed his eyes with his finger and thumb. He did it for a long time.
"I told you," he said. Not angry. Very tired. "On Tuesday. I told you, if you walk on it tonight, the knee will set. It will never bend again." He put his spectacles back on. "You walked on it. Four hundred steps, I should think. Up and down. And sat on it, on stone, in the cold." He looked at the leg. "It's set."
Nobody said anything.
"The tendon behind the knee," said Silas, "the big cord that bends it, the rod cut it half through. On Saturday. I told you that too. Half a cord can heal, if you let it lie still and knit. You didn't let it. You walked on it, and the half that was left tore, and the leg locked straight to stop itself breaking, the way a body does. And now the joint has filled up with blood, and the blood is going hard inside it, and by the end of the week it will be bone." He spread his long hands. "There. That's the doctor speaking. I'm sorry."
"Can you open it?" said Lyra. "Like you opened the wound. Let the blood out."
"I could." Silas looked at her. "And he might lose the leg. Or his life. A knee is a box with a lot of things in it, and if you open the box in a place like this, with coal dust in the air and a dead rat in the next arch, things get in. Nasty things." He shook his head. "No. It's set. It stays set."
Lyra looked at Corin.
He had opened his eyes. He was looking at the black brick roof again, the way he had looked at it when he told her about the glass.
"What does it mean," he said. "For walking."
"It means you'll walk." Silas began to fold the old binding, slowly, into a bundle. "In a month, with a stick. In a year, without one, maybe, on a flat road. You'll swing it from the hip, like a man with a wooden leg. Stairs, sideways, one at a time, holding the rail. Sitting down, you'll have to sit with it out in front of you, so you'll want the end of a bench. Or the corner of a room." He put the bundle down. "You won't run. Ever. You won't kneel. You won't climb a ladder, or a wall, or a rope. You won't ride, unless somebody ties you on." He paused. "And if you're ever in a fight, you'll want to be standing with your back against something, because you won't be able to turn round fast."
Corin did not say anything.
"That's all," said Silas. "It's not nothing. But it's not the leg. And it's not your life. I've seen boys come back from the war with less than that and live to be eighty."
"I know," said Corin.
He lay still for a moment. Then he lifted the Ledger off the blanket, and opened it, and held out his other hand.
Lyra gave him the pencil.
He found the page without looking. Lyra knew it by now. The page with the word SPENT at the top, in his square careful capitals, and the long list underneath, in his own hand and sometimes in hers. Wed cellar: a word (mother). Wed drain: three winters. Thu Saltmarsh: rest of first language. Fri sluice: first night at No. 14. All the things he had burned. All the things he had paid.
He wrote under the last line. Slowly. The pencil scratched.
Tue Bourse: the bend in my left knee. For good. Silas told me before. I went anyway.
He looked at it. Then he added, smaller:
Not a memory. Still counts.
He gave the pencil back to Lyra.
She took it. She did not trust her voice, so she did not use it. She held the lamp while Silas cleaned the wound and put four new stitches in it, slowly, with his long careful fingers, and Corin held the edge of the pallet and breathed through his nose, and Oren stood by the wall with his arms folded and his face like a shut door. She held the lamp while Silas bound the leg again, fresh linen, hip to ankle, with two splints of ash wood down either side, because now there was nothing left to save in the knee and the only thing that mattered was that it should not knock.
Then Silas stood up, with another crack from his own knees, and wiped his hands on a rag, and looked down at the bound leg on its blanket.
"Why, though?" he said. It was not a doctor's question. It was a tired man's. "I've been wondering all day. I've been wondering since Tuesday. What was in that house that was worth a knee?"
Corin looked up at him.
"A passage," he said. "That had to learn my face."
Silas looked at him for a long moment, over his crooked spectacles.
"And did it?"
"Yes."
"Then I hope," said the doctor, "that it was worth it. Because I can't give you the knee back. Nobody can." He turned away, toward the third arch and his pile of sacks. "I'll sleep an hour. Then we go. All of us. Before the dark goes."
He went.
Lyra sat by the pallet with the lamp in her lap. The flame stood up very straight and still. She looked at the stiff leg in its splints, and at Corin's face, gray and old under the soot and the false skin and the white wild eyebrows of a lame lamp boy who had never existed.
"You knew," she said. "On Tuesday. When he told you. You knew you'd go anyway."
"Yes."
"And you didn't ask me."
Corin turned his head and looked at her.
"No," he said. "It's my leg."
Lyra looked at him for a long time.
Then she said, "Yes. It is." And put the lamp down on the floor, and reached out, and took the hand that was lying on the Ledger, and held it. The black oily lamp-boy glove, with the fingers worn through. She held it hard.
She did not say what she was thinking. That she had said the very same thing to him, on Monday night, in this arch, when he told her about his sister and said he was sorry he had kept it from her. She had been angry. She had said it hard.
That's not your choice what I carry.
And now he had given it back to her, without meaning to, and it fitted him just as well. She held his hand, and did not let go, and did not say it.
Forty-One People
At the fourth bell, Mother Grue banged her stick three times on the iron rim of the first brazier, and the arches woke up.
They woke up the way the poor wake up, which is all at once and without any fuss, because there is nothing to linger for. No warm bed. No breakfast waiting. People sat up on their sacks and their heaps of straw and put their boots on, if they had boots, and tied rags round their feet if they did not. Mothers lifted babies. Old men coughed. Children who were too young to understand anything stood about in the red light rubbing their eyes and were told to hold somebody's coat and not let go.
Lyra had not slept. She had sat by Corin's pallet with her back to the wall and the lamp turned low and listened to him breathing, and somewhere around the third bell her eyes had shut on their own, and she had dreamed of nothing at all for what felt like a moment. Then the stick banged on the iron, and she was awake again, with a crick in her neck and the Ledger still in her lap.
Corin was asleep. Properly asleep, at last, with his mouth a little open and his face turned to the wall. The doctor had given him a spoonful of something brown out of a bottle at the second bell. For the pain, he had said. And so he'll lie still while you carry him. He won't like being carried. Lyra had seen Corin swallow it without a word. That was how she knew how bad the pain was.
She got up, and went to help.
Mother Grue had a way of doing it. She had done it before. Lyra understood that at once, watching her. Not this exact thing, perhaps. Not forty-one people out of the arches before a raid. But something like it. More than once. She stood by the first brazier in her heap of shawls, leaning on her stick, and she did not shout and she did not hurry, and the arches emptied around her like water running out of a basin through a hole.
"Fives," she said. "Nobody walks in more than five. Five is a family. Six is a crowd, and a constable looks at a crowd." She pointed with her stick. "Abel. You take the Pinches and the two Ferrises. Down the river path to the Fish Locks, and over by the little bridge, and out the Salt Road. Go now."
A man in a sack apron nodded, and gathered up four people with his eyes, and went out under the canvas into the dark. Lyra knew him, a little. He was the big man who sat by the first brazier and said nothing. She had never heard him speak.
"Bet," said Mother Grue. "You stay with the doctor. You go to Saint Bride's. Your Ned is there."
The little girl from the fifth arch, ten years old, with her chapped red cheeks and her big serious eyes, stood with a bundle in her arms and did not move. Her Ned was there. Her Ned was in a box in the crypt under the church, waiting for the ground to thaw enough to be dug. Lyra did not think Bet had cried yet. She did not think Bet knew how.
"Nell. Tobin. You're with the Hobbses. Up Tanner's Steps and through the Shambles. If anybody stops you, you're going to the early market for your mother. You've been sent for bones."
Nell nodded. She had her broom with her. She always had her broom. Fourteen years old, all elbows and knees, with her hair tied back in a rag. She looked at Lyra across the arch, and Lyra looked back, and something passed between them that did not need saying. Nell had held the Ledger in Cinder Lane on Sunday night. She had stood in the snow and held it against her chest for two hours, waiting, the way Lyra had held it tonight.
Five. Five. Five. Out into the dark, two minutes apart, by different ways. The river path. The Steps. The alley behind the dye-works, where the streams ran red and blue and yellow through the snow. Lyra counted them out without meaning to. Ten. Fifteen. Twenty. Twenty-five.
Vespera went at the half bell, alone, with her carpet bag of pots and needles and a shawl over her head. She did not say goodbye to anybody. At the canvas she turned and looked back, once, at Corin asleep on his pallet in his old man's face. Her own work. Then she looked at Lyra.
"The paint on his hands will run by tomorrow night," she said. "Tell him. I'll do it again Friday, if I'm still where I'm going." She looked at the stiff leg in its splints. "Tell him the price hasn't changed."
Then she was gone.
Thirty. Thirty-five.
And then Mother Grue stopped.
She stood by the brazier, leaning on her stick, and looked down the long tunnel of the arches, past the second brazier and the third, into the dark at the far end. Lyra looked too.
At the far end of the third arch, on his sack against the wall, an old man was sitting with his back to the brick and a pile of paper beside him.
Wick.
He had not moved. Everyone else in the third arch was gone. Their straw lay flattened and empty. Their blankets were rolled and gone. The brazier at that end had burned down to a dull red eye. And Wick sat on his sack in the middle of it all, in his long ragged coat with the string for buttons, with his faded eyes on nothing, and picked up a scrap of paper from the pile beside him, and looked at it, and laid it on the other pile.
And tapped. Twice. With one finger. On the brick floor beside his knee.
Tap. Tap.
Then he picked up another scrap of paper.
Mother Grue sighed. A long sigh, out through her beak of a nose.
"Every time," she said. "Every time we move." She began to walk down the arches toward him, slowly, leaning on her stick. Lyra went after her.
Wick did not look up as they came. He did not look up when Mother Grue stood over him. He picked up a scrap of paper and looked at it. It was a piece of an old handbill, gray with damp. Lyra could read part of it upside down. —TENTION ALL PERSONS— and below it, —NG CLOSED BY ORDER—. He looked at it for a long time, as if it said something very important. Then he laid it on the pile.
"Wick," said Mother Grue. "Up, now. Time to go."
Nothing.
"Wick." She bent, with a groan, and put her hand on his shoulder. "Up. We're going to Mary's. You like Mary's. There's a fire, and she makes the soup with barley in it."
Wick's finger tapped on the brick. Twice.
Tap. Tap.
Mother Grue put her other hand under his arm and lifted.
And Wick made a sound.
Lyra had never heard a sound like it. It was not a word. Wick did not have words. They had been taken out of him, the Ledger said, along with everything else, after he found the dead scribe in the lane and told everybody the harvester would find her, and was proud of it, and someone had decided that pride was worth taking. It was a high thin sound, like a kettle, or a dog shut out in the snow. It went on and on. And Wick's hands came up and caught hold of the edge of his sack, and held it, the knuckles going white, and his whole thin body went stiff and heavy, the way a child's body goes heavy when it does not want to be picked up.
Mother Grue let go.
The sound stopped. At once. Like a door shutting.
Wick picked up a scrap of paper. Looked at it. Laid it on the pile.
Tap. Tap.
"Every time," said Mother Grue again. Very quietly. "Every time. And every time it takes an hour, and three of us, and he screams the whole way, all through the streets, and the whole river hears him." She straightened up, slowly, with her hand on her back. "We haven't got an hour. And we can't have screaming. Not today."
"Oren could carry him," said Lyra.
"Oren is carrying Mr. Vane." Mother Grue did not look at her. "Up the hill to the doctor's. A grown man with a leg like a plank, all the way up to Saint Bride's, in the snow, with constables on every corner. And you think Oren should do that with Wick screaming under his other arm?"
Lyra did not answer.
Wick tapped. Twice.
Lyra looked down at the finger. The thin old finger with the black under the nail, tapping on the brick. Two. Always two. Never four, or nine, or three. Corin had written it in the Ledger. Wick taps. Twice. Always twice. And Moll had read the drum calls on Monday, on the barge, and Oren had carried them into the city in his head and said them over by the brazier, slowly, while Corin wrote them down. Four is Witness. Two is Hold. Nine is Fold. Three is Home.
Two is Hold.
"He's saying it," said Lyra. "Mother Grue. He's saying hold. Stay where you are. Don't move." She heard her own voice go up. "It's a drum call. From the war. It's the only thing he's got left, and he's saying it, every time you try to move him, he's been saying it for—"
"I know what he's saying, girl."
Lyra stopped.
Mother Grue was looking at her. Not angry. Old. Very old, in the dull red light of the dying brazier, with the shawls heaped round her and the snow-light coming gray down the long tunnel from the canvas at the far end.
"I've known for twenty years what he's saying," said Mother Grue. "My husband tapped it too. Before he died. Two and two and two. In his sleep. On my back, with his finger, when he had the fever." She looked down at Wick. "Hold. Hold the line. Don't run. That's what they taught them, the poor lambs. And they held. And look at them."
Wick laid a scrap of paper on the pile.
"Gant's not after Wick," said Mother Grue. "Gant's after your Mr. Vane, and you, and the boy with the bitten arm. When Gant comes in here at first light with his warrant and his six men, he'll find an empty arch, and one old man on a sack who can't tell him anything, because he hasn't got anything to tell. Not a word. Not a name. Not his own." She put her hand on Wick's head. Lightly. For a moment. "They'll shout at him. Maybe they'll knock him about a bit. And then they'll go away. And tomorrow night, when it's quiet, I'll send Abel back for him with a barrow and a blanket over his head, and he'll scream all the way to the Saltmarsh, and that'll be that."
"And if they take him?"
"Then they take him." Mother Grue took her hand off his head. "And I'll go and ask for him back. I've done that before, too."
She turned, and began to walk back up the arches toward the first brazier, slowly, leaning on her stick.
Lyra stood by Wick's sack.
She stood there for a long moment. Then she bent down, and took the bowl from beside his pile of papers, the tin bowl he ate his soup from, and went to the pot on the dying brazier, and scraped what was left of last night's soup into it, cold and thick, with the barley in it. And brought it back. And put it down beside him, and put the spoon in his hand, and closed his fingers round it, the way she had seen Mother Grue do on Sunday.
Wick looked at the spoon in his hand for a long time.
Then he looked up at her.
Just for a moment. His faded eyes came up off the spoon and rested on her face, the way they had rested there on Saturday night when she came in with the Duke's card in her pocket. The way they rested on nobody else's.
Then they went away again.
He put the spoon down in the bowl. And picked up a scrap of paper. And looked at it.
Tap. Tap.
Lyra went back up the arches to the fire, and did not look round.
