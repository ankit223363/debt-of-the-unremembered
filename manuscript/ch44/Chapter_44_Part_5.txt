Chapter 44: Sterling's Gift, Part 5 of 5: The Treasurer
The Treasurer
They were back at Saint Bride's before the fourth bell, with the box under Oren's coat.
Nobody followed them. Oren made sure of that. He took them home a long way round, through the Ink Quarter, where the printing shops stood shoulder to shoulder down narrow lanes and the air smelled of hot metal and lampblack, and the gutters ran black instead of pink. He stopped three times at corners and looked at the reflections in shop windows. He went into a printer's yard by one gate and out by another. He said nothing the whole way. Only once, at the bottom of Saint Bride's Hill, he stopped and stood very still, listening, while the drums rattled somewhere off to the east, toward the river, and then went quiet.
"Nothing," he said. And they went on up.
In the side vault, under the skulls of the old monks, Lyra put the box on the stone bench beside Corin's stiff leg, and opened it, and told him.
She told it the way Corin had told her about the glass. Plainly. All of it. The livery, and the seal with no name, and the card. The mask. The key in the lining. The gold thread. Aurum. A christening gown twenty years ago. Mr. Dorian Sterling, with his silver hair and his charm and his watch that he never looked at. And her mother, at the end, by the stove, in her gray gloves. If ever my girl comes to your door, let her in.
She had meant to tell that part last, and quickly, and then stop. She found she could not stop. She told him about the gray gloves and the traveling cloak, and how Madame Hesper's hands had been held in them, and how she, Lyra, had stood there in the back room of the shop holding a brown cup and listening, and had felt for her own memory of the same gloves, and the same cloak, holding her own face, in the front hall at Crown Walk, the day the carriage came.
And it was not there.
She had given it to the Duke. On Saturday. In his gallery. For a card that said His Grace the Duke of Marrow, and guest. It was in one of his black glass vials now, glowing, behind a lens, on a shelf, where nobody would ever open it. And Madame Hesper had the same gloves in her memory, holding her hands, and kept them. And Lyra did not.
She stopped talking.
Corin did not say anything. He reached out and took her hand, the way she had taken his last night, and held it, and did not let go. The black lamp-boy glove was gone. Mother Grue had cut it off him in the morning, and the paint on his hands was going too, the way Vespera had said it would, in patches, so that the gray of the Ash showed through underneath, all the way from the fingertips to past the wrist. She held the gray hand and looked at the key.
After a while, Gideon got up from the floor.
He had been carving all afternoon. Lyra saw what it was now, as he put it down on the floor by the wall: a small wooden boat, the size of his palm, with a mast made of a splinter. He came and stood by the bench and looked down into the box. At the mask. At the key lying beside it on the white silk.
He did not touch it either. He looked at it for a long time, with his head on one side, the way he looked at a crate when he was working out how heavy it would be.
"That's ours," he said.
"Ours?"
"The Bourse." Gideon pointed with his good hand, not quite touching. "The V. All the house keys have that. Mrs. Pratt's got a ring of them on her belt as big as a dinner plate. Coal store, wine cellar, linen room, gallery doors. They've all got a V." He looked at it a moment longer. "And numbers. One to twelve. For the doors upstairs."
"And three?"
"Three's the back stair." Gideon frowned. "From the kitchen yard. The one the waiters go up with the trays on the big nights. Up behind the gallery. To the little rooms at the top where the Lady's special guests sit." He paused. "I've never been up it. You'd need a coat. And a face."
"The private rooms," said Corin slowly. "Behind the gallery. Up a back stair. Remy said. The Fourth Tier. By invitation."
Gideon nodded.
Then he frowned again, deeper, and bent closer to the key, so close his nose nearly touched it.
"That's wrong, though," he said.
"What's wrong?"
"The teeth." He pointed, very carefully, with his dirty fingernail, an inch above the brass. "Look. Old key. The bow's all worn. Thumbs. Years of thumbs. But the teeth are bright. New." He straightened up. "Mrs. Pratt says the Lady has all the upstairs locks changed every Midwinter, the week before. So nobody that's been sacked in the year can come back in. Locksmith comes Monday, Mrs. Pratt says. Little man with a bag. Changes every lock from one to twelve and goes away again and nobody sees the new keys but the Lady and Fane."
Nobody said anything.
"So that," said Gideon, "is an old key, with new teeth, for a new lock. That hasn't been changed yet." He looked at Corin. "Somebody knows what the new lock's going to be. Before the locksmith's done it."
The crypt was very quiet. Out in the main hall, Mrs. Doyle was talking in her loud kind voice to somebody who did not answer. There's my lamb. Just a sip.
"Or somebody," said Oren, from the arch of the vault, "is the man who changes them."
They looked at him.
Oren did not unfold his arms.
"A man at the Bank," he said, "who knows the Lady's locks before she does. Who has her keys cut and sends them out across the city in a dressmaker's box. With a thread from across the sea in the lining." He looked at the key. "That's a man who owns the locksmith. Or the Lady. Or both."
Corin lay still on the stone bench, under the skulls.
Then he lifted the Ledger off his chest and opened it. Not to the first page. To the back, where he kept the pages of names. THOSE I OWE. THOSE WHO OWE ME. And a third list, that he had started on the barge, in the autumn, and that had been getting longer every week since. It had no heading. Only a line drawn across the top of the page, very hard, so that the pencil had nearly gone through.
Lyra had read it. Silas Kross was on it. The Lady Vivienne. Gant. Ambrose Pallister.
Corin wrote, under the last name, in his square careful capitals:
DORIAN STERLING. Treasurer, the Bank. Silver hair. Watch he doesn't look at. Sent L. a mask (white) with a key (V3, back stair to the 4th Tier rooms, new teeth) in the lining, sewn with Aurum gold. No note. Knows L.'s name. Knows Hesper. Knows the Duke said white.
He stopped. He held the pencil over the page.
Then he wrote, smaller:
Elara works for him.
And he lay there looking at that line, and did not write anything else.
And from somewhere very deep down, under the Ledger, under the bench, under the old stone of the crypt and the old bones of the monks in their niche, Lyra saw his face change. The way it changed when the voice spoke. The voice he never talked about, that had been quiet for days now, so quiet that once or twice she had wondered if it was gone.
His lips moved.
"What?" said Lyra. "What did he say?"
Corin did not answer at once. He was listening. His eyes were on the skulls, and his face was turned a little to one side, the way you turn your head toward a voice in another room.
"Sterling," he said at last. His own voice. But slow, and careful, as if he were copying the word off a slate. "Alistair's boy's boy."
"Who is Alistair?"
"I don't know." Corin shut his eyes. "He's gone again. That's all he said. Alistair's boy's boy. And then—" He stopped.
"And then?"
Corin opened his eyes and looked at her.
"He laughed," he said. "Very quietly. The way you laugh when you see an old enemy across a room, and you're glad, because now at least you know where he is."
Lyra looked at the key.
It lay in the bottom of the box on the white silk, beside the white mask, small and plain and yellow, with its worn bow and its bright new teeth. It did not look like anything. It looked like a key off a housekeeper's ring.
"Why?" she said. "Why give it to us? To me?"
"Nobody gives you a key for nothing," said Gideon. He had gone back to his boat, and was sitting on the floor with it in his lap, turning it over. "Not in this city. Somebody gives you a key, it's because there's a door they want opened and they don't want to be seen opening it."
"Or," said Oren, "because they want to know who comes through it."
Corin closed the Ledger.
"Then we keep it," he said. "And we don't use it. Not until we know which." He put the Ledger back on his chest, flat, under his gray hand. "And we don't tell anybody we've got it. Not Remy. Not Vespera. Not Silas." He looked at Lyra. "And we don't send him anything back. No thank you. No note. Nothing."
"He'll know I've got it," said Lyra. "Hesper will tell nobody. But the box is gone from her shop. He'll know I came."
"Yes," said Corin. "That's his first move. He wants to see if you come when he calls." His mouth went down at the left corner. "Well. You came. Now it's our move. And our move is to do nothing at all, and make him wonder."
Gideon looked up from his boat.
"That's a game," he said. "Isn't it? You're talking like it's a game."
Nobody answered him.
Gideon looked at them for a moment longer. Then he went back to his boat. And after a while, very softly, almost without a sound, he began to hum. The carrying tune. The one that went down at the end, like a man setting a heavy box on the ground.
Quarter to Midnight
The crypt slept the way the arches had slept. All at once, and lightly, and never all of it at the same time.
By the tenth bell the widows had gone home, all but Mrs. Doyle, who slept in a chair by the blanketed door with her shawl over her head and her feet on a hot brick, and snored like a kettle on the boil. The lamps on the pillars had been turned down to small blue beads. The braziers glowed. Down the long low hall between the pillars, the children lay on their pallets in the dark and breathed, slow and sweet, and every so often one of them said something in its sleep, a word or half a word, and was quiet again.
Silas had come in at the ninth bell with snow on his hat and gone round them all, one by one, with a candle, bending over each pallet like a heron over water. Then he had sat down on the floor by the far brazier with his back to a pillar and his chin on his chest and fallen asleep in the middle of taking off his boots.
In the side vault, Corin slept on his stone bench under the skulls, with the Ledger on his chest and his gray hand on it. Gideon slept on the floor at the foot of the bench, curled round his wooden boat like a dog round a bone. Bet slept by the wall with her head on her bundle. Oren did not sleep. He sat in the arch of the vault on an upturned crate, with his back to the stone and the iron bar across his knees, facing the hall. Every so often Lyra saw his eyes close. Then open again.
Lyra could not sleep.
She sat on the floor by the bench with her back against the cold stone, and the box on her knees, and the lid off.
She had not meant to take the mask out again. She had put the lid on the box at the sixth bell, after supper, and tied the gray silk ribbon round it in a flat neat bow, and pushed it under the bench beside Corin's boots. And she had lain down on a pile of sacks by the wall and shut her eyes. And lain there. And lain there.
At the eleventh bell she had got up and pulled the box out again.
Now the mask lay in her lap on its bed of white silk, face down, with the loosened flap of lining lifted where Madame Hesper had unpicked it, and the empty kid pocket showing underneath where the key had been. The key itself was gone. Corin had it. It was inside the Ledger, between the pages, pressed flat. She had watched him put it there.
She looked at the lining.
The small gray quilted diamonds, row on row, each one no bigger than a fingernail, each one stitched round with a thread of gold so fine it was nearly not there. In the daylight, in the back room of the shop, it had only looked like quilting. A pattern, like the pattern on the inside of a jewel case. Even. Regular. The same all over.
But it was not daylight now. It was the light of one candle, a stub of tallow on a tin lid on the floor beside her, and the blue bead of the lamp on the pillar outside, and the red glow of the brazier through the arch. Low light. Slanting light. The kind of light that shows you the scratches on a table that you never see by day.
And by that light, the gold thread was not even.
Lyra bent closer.
Most of the diamonds were stitched with one thread of gold. Just one. Round and round, a single fine line. But some of them were not. Some of them were stitched with two threads, side by side, so close together that in daylight they would have looked like one. And in the candlelight, those caught the flame a little more. Burned a little brighter. Stood up out of the gray silk, very faintly, like the lines on the palm of a hand when you hold it to the fire.
She looked at them.
They made a shape.
Not a big one. Small. In the middle of the lining, over the place where the mask would sit on the forehead, just above the bridge of the nose. Just above where the key had been. A ring of diamonds, stitched double, round and round, in a circle the size of a coin. And inside the ring, twelve tiny points. Where the corners of the diamonds met, at the edge of the circle, twelve of them, evenly spaced, each one marked with a single tiny knot of gold, no bigger than a grain of sand.
And from the middle of the circle, two lines. Stitched double, gold on gray. One long. One short.
Lyra sat very still.
It was a clock.
A clock face. Stitched into the lining of a mask, in gold thread from the far side of the sea, so fine and so faint that you would only ever see it by candlelight, at night, if you were sitting very still, and looking.
She counted the knots. Twelve. She found the top one, the one that would be twelve o'clock, by the way the circle sat on the mask: the top of the face, toward the hair.
The short line pointed nearly straight up. Just before the top knot. Just before twelve.
The long line did not. She followed it with her fingertip, not touching the silk, just over it. It pointed back from the top, to the left, to the ninth knot. A quarter turn back. Fifteen minutes before the hour.
She counted it twice more, to be sure, because she was tired and the candle was moving. It came out the same each time.
A quarter to midnight.
Lyra put her hand over her mouth.
She did not know why. Nobody was going to hear her. Oren was asleep on his crate, or nearly. Corin was asleep on his bench. The skulls were not going to tell anyone. She sat with her hand over her mouth and the mask in her lap and the candle guttering on its tin lid, and looked at the little gold clock in the lining, with its hands at a quarter to midnight, sitting over the place where the key had been.
The bidding at the Masquerade opened at the eleventh bell. She knew that. It was in the catalogue. Viewing from the ninth bell. Bidding at the eleventh. And at midnight the Lady opened the brass box under the dome, and read out the winners, one by one, and everyone in the great hall turned to look at the gallery, and clapped.
At midnight, everyone would be looking at the gallery.
At a quarter to midnight, the waiters would be going up the back stairs with the last of the wine. Door three. From the kitchen yard, up behind the gallery, to the little rooms where the Lady's special guests sat with their sealed bids.
At a quarter to midnight, a girl in a white mask could go up a back stair with a tray, and nobody would look at her at all.
At a quarter to midnight.
Lyra looked at the clock in the lining for a long time.
Then she lifted the mask out of the box, by the edges, and turned it over in her hands, so that the white face was toward her and the clock was underneath, against her fingers. The pearls round the edge caught the candle. The silver thread round the eyes flickered and went out. It weighed nothing. It weighed less than a feather.
She held it up, and put it to her face.
It was cold for a moment, the silk. Then it was not. It sat on her face as if it had been made for it. As if somebody had measured her, the bridge of her nose and the bones of her cheeks and the place where her eyebrows began, without her knowing, and made it to fit. The frame did not press anywhere. The lining lay against her forehead, soft and cool. Against the skin just above the bridge of her nose, where the key had been, she could feel, very faintly, if she thought about it, the tiny knots of the clock.
Twelve of them.
She sat in the dark of the crypt of Saint Bride's, under the bones of the old monks, with a white mask on her face that a man she had never met had made to fit her, and looked out through the eye holes at the candle, at Corin asleep on his bench, at Oren on his crate in the arch.
Oren's eyes were open.
He was looking at her.
He did not say anything. He looked at her in the white mask for a long time, with the iron bar across his knees, and his face did not move at all. And Lyra understood, without either of them saying a word, that he was not seeing her. He was seeing someone else. A woman with the same dark hair and the same eyes, who had once looked down at him from a carriage window on a gray morning, and said goodbye, and been driven away.
She took the mask off.
She put it back in the box, face down, with the clock on top, and laid the tissue over it, all the layers, one by one, and put the lid on, and tied the gray silk ribbon round it in a flat neat bow.
"Oren," she said, very low.
"Lady."
"You were thinking of my mother."
Oren did not answer for a while.
"Yes," he said at last.
"Did she wear a mask like that? At Midwinter?"
"No." Oren looked at the box on her knees. "She never wore a mask. Not once. Not at any party, all the years I knew her." He paused. "Your father asked her why, once. In the carriage, coming home. I was on the box. I heard."
"What did she say?"
Oren was quiet for so long that she thought he had forgotten. Or that it was one of the things he had given away. Then he said, slowly, in his rough low voice, as if he were reading it off the inside of his own head:
"She said, Where I come from, Aurelian, we only put on masks for people we mean to leave."
The brazier ticked.
Lyra sat with the box on her knees and did not move.
Outside, far off, down the hill, toward the river, the drums began again. A dry quick rattle, coming and going over the roofs in the dark. Calling street to street. Saying something that nobody in the crypt could understand any more.
And under the drums, very faint, from up the long low hall between the pillars, from one of the pallets in the dark, a child's voice. Small. Half asleep. Saying the same thing over and over, the way children do when they are dreaming of something they have been promised.
"Friday. Friday. The peppermint man is coming Friday."
Then it was quiet.
Lyra pushed the box back under the bench, beside Corin's boots, where she could reach it in the dark. And lay down on her pile of sacks by the wall. And did not sleep for a long time.
