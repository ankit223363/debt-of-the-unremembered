Chapter 44: Sterling's Gift, Part 4 of 5: The Searched City
The Searched City
The city had changed since Saturday.
Lyra saw it at the first corner. On Saturday, when she had walked to Hesper's with Nell, the streets had been the streets she knew. Cold, and busy, and dirty, and loud. Carts and barrows and sedan chairs. Boys selling broadsheets. Women with baskets. The ordinary noise of a city getting on with its day.
The noise was still there. But there was something under it now, like a second sound under the first. A sound that came and went. A dry quick rattle, far off, then nearer, then far off again, so that you could not tell which way it was coming from.
Drums.
"Harlan's boys," said Oren, beside her. Very low. He did not slow down. "Calling to each other. Street to street."
"What are they saying?"
Oren walked on for a while without answering.
"I don't know," he said at last. His voice was flat. "I used to. I knew every call they had. My brother taught me, one winter, on the kitchen table, with two of the—the things you eat soup with." He stopped. His mouth worked, the way it did now when he came to the edge of a word that was not there any more. "The things they beat. The name of them. I gave it away on the Saltmarsh, with the calls, to make the—" Another stop. "To make the river hold. Now it's just noise."
Lyra did not say anything. She walked beside him with her cap pulled down and her hands in the pockets of the too-big coat, and listened to the rattle coming and going over the roofs, and tried not to think about what it might be saying. There. Over there. Her. Him.
They went down Saint Bride's Hill, and along Chandler Row, and across the top of the Shambles, where the butchers were hanging out the day's meat on their iron hooks, red and white in the snow, and the gutters ran pink. At the corner of the Shambles and Long Acre there was a crowd.
Not a big one. Twenty people, perhaps. Standing in a ring, the way people stand round a fight or a dancing bear. And in the middle of the ring, a cart. A coal cart, with a thin old horse between the shafts, its head down. And round the cart, four men in long black coats, with flat black caps, and on each cap a small white badge that caught the light.
An open eye.
Two of the men had the long iron hooks. Twelve feet long, with a curved point at the end like the claw of a great bird. They were driving them down into the coal in the back of the cart. Not gently. Down, all the way to the boards, so that you heard the iron hit the wood, chock. And pulled them out. And moved along a foot. And drove them down again.
Chock.
The carter stood by the horse's head with his cap in his hands. He was an old man. He did not say anything. He watched the hooks go into his coal.
A third man stood at the tail of the cart with a slate. A drum boy. Lyra saw that he was a boy, when she looked properly. Thirteen, perhaps. Fourteen. Tall for his age and very thin, in the long black coat that was made for a man and came down nearly to his boots. He had a drum on a strap over his shoulder, a small flat one, and two sticks pushed through the strap. And he was writing on the slate. Long Acre. Coal. One horse. Searched. Lyra could see the chalk moving.
He looked up. Not at her. At the crowd. A quick sweep of the eyes from face to face, the way Mother Grue had swept the arches. Then down again.
Chock.
"Walk," said Oren. "Don't stop. Nobody stops to watch the searches who hasn't got a reason."
They walked.
On the wall at the corner, past the crowd, there were papers pasted up. A great many of them, one over another, the old ones peeling at the edges and the new ones still wet with paste. Lyra read them as she went by, without turning her head. She could not help it. She had always read every word she passed, every sign, every label, every shop board, ever since she was small. Her mother had laughed at her for it. Lyra reads the street like a book.
NOTICE. By Order of the Archon's Council. All boats, barges, carts and wagons entering or leaving the city to be SEARCHED.
WANTED for questioning in the matter of the THORNE ABDUCTION: a man aged about thirty, dark, with gray marks upon the hands.
And under it, newer, the paste still shining:
THE OFFICE OF PUBLIC HEALTH. A GENTLEMAN from the Office will be IN PERSON at the FISH STAIRS on FRIDAY at the EIGHTH BELL. All children of the parish are to attend. Sweets will be given.
Lyra looked at that one for a moment longer than she should have.
Sweets will be given.
That was new. That had not been on the one Oren tore down on Monday. She knew, because Corin had read it to her twice, lying on his pallet with the paper held up to the brazier.
Oren's hand came down on her shoulder. Heavy. Steering.
She walked.
They went by Comb Street, as Gideon had said. It was a narrow crooked street of little shops that ran down the side of the Hill between high walls, so steep that it was half steps. Barbers, and wigmakers, and the sellers of combs and pins and ribbons and false curls. In the windows, on wooden heads with painted faces, the wigs. Fair, and dark, and red, and gray, curled and piled and powdered for the Hill ladies, or plain and pinned for the shop girls who wanted to look like the Hill ladies.
Lyra had come here on Saturday night, with the doctor's knife still in her hand and her hair in a bag, and sold it for two crowns and four shillings to a little bald man who had held it up to his lamp and said Very fine. Very fine. Dark, with red in it. That's rare.
She did not look in his window as they passed.
She looked anyway.
It was there. On a wooden head, in the middle of the window, on a stand of green velvet. Not made up yet. Only washed, and combed out, and hung over the head to dry, long and dark and shining, with the red in it, so that the wooden face with its painted pink cheeks and painted blue eyes looked out through it as if through a curtain.
There was a small card propped in front of it.
Real Hair. Very Fine. From a Lady.
Lyra stopped.
She did not mean to. Her feet stopped on their own, on the steep cobbles, in the snow, and she stood there and looked at her own hair on a wooden head behind a pane of glass.
Oren did not pull her on. Not at once. He stood beside her. And after a moment he said, very low, without looking at her:
"There's a boy behind us."
Lyra did not turn round.
"In a gray cap," said Oren. "About twelve. He was at the corner of the Shambles, by the coal cart. Now he's at the top of Comb Street, buying a ribbon he doesn't want." Oren paused. "He followed me on Monday. From the Bank steps to the Ink Quarter. I lost him there."
"Whose is he?"
"I don't know." Oren looked at the wig in the window. "Somebody who folds his notes small, I'd guess."
Lyra looked at her hair.
Then she reached into the pocket of the too-big coat, and took out the two crowns and four shillings that she had been carrying since Saturday night, because she had not been able to make herself spend them. And went up the step, and opened the door of the little shop, and the bell over it jangled.
The little bald man looked up from his bench.
"The hair in the window," said Lyra, in her doctor's boy's voice, low and rough. "How much?"
The little man looked at her. At the cap, and the coat, and the face under the cap. And something came into his eyes. He looked at the window. He looked back at her.
"Four crowns," he said slowly.
"I've got two and four."
"Then you can't have it." He looked at her a moment longer. "Lad."
Lyra stood in the shop with the money in her hand.
Then she put it back in her pocket.
"Is there a back way out?" she said.
The little man looked at her for a long moment. Then he jerked his head, without a word, at a curtain behind his bench.
They went out through the back of the wigmaker's shop, past shelves of wooden heads with no hair on them at all, bald and painted and staring, into a yard full of snow and barrels, and over a low wall, and down an alley so narrow that Oren had to turn sideways, and out at the bottom into the mews behind Velvet Street.
Nobody came after them.
Lyra did not look back. She had looked back too many times today. She walked down the mews behind Oren with her hands in her pockets, feeling the coins there, two crowns and four shillings, heavy and cold.
She did not know why she had tried to buy it back. She would never wear it. It was no use to her. It was hair.
She thought perhaps it was because it was the only thing she had sold that she could still see.
The Parcel
The back door of the Needle and Thimble was painted dark green, and it had a brass knocker in the shape of a thimble, and the step in front of it had been scrubbed that morning so hard that the stone was nearly white. Bridie Kane's work. Lyra could see the marks of the brush in it, in little curved lines, under the new snow.
She knocked. Three times, and then once.
The door opened at once, as if somebody had been standing behind it with a hand on the latch.
Madame Hesper looked at her.
She was the same. Tall and thin as a heron, in her black dress buttoned to the chin and her brown linen apron with its bulging pockets. Iron-gray hair pulled back tight, with the two long pins through the knot in a cross. Round spectacles on a silver chain. Four pins in the corner of her mouth.
She looked at Lyra's cap. Then she put out one long cold finger and lifted the edge of it, and looked under it, at the cropped dark hair that stopped just below the ears.
She let the cap fall back.
She took the pins out of her mouth, one at a time.
"Comb Street," she said. Not a question. "Saturday night. The little man with no hair of his own." She put the pins in the cushion on her wrist. "How much did he give you?"
"Two crowns and four."
"He has it in his window for four." Madame Hesper's long face did not move. "I passed it on Monday. I knew it at once. I have dressed that hair for you since you were seven years old." She stood back from the door. "Come in. Both of you. Quickly. Mr. Oren, you will have to bend."
Oren bent.
The back room of the shop was as Lyra remembered it from Saturday. The long table, covered in a white sheet, with the bolts of cloth laid out on it in rows. The iron stove in the corner, red, with a kettle on it. The tall cheval glass on its stand, turned to the wall. The dressmaker's dummies, three of them, standing about in the shadows in half-made gowns like guests at a party who have forgotten why they came. The smell of starch, and warm iron, and lavender, and dust.
And on the end of the long table, by itself, a box.
Lyra stopped.
It was not a big box. Flat and wide, the kind of box that a hat comes in, or a pair of good gloves, or a lace collar. It was covered in thick soft paper the color of a dove's breast, gray with a little pink in it. It was tied with a ribbon of gray silk, in a flat neat bow, and where the ribbon crossed on the top of the lid there was a seal. A round blob of wax, silver-gray, the size of a coin. Plain. No crest in it. No letters. Nothing at all pressed into it but a ring of tiny dots round the edge, like the dots round the face of a clock.
Under the ribbon, tucked in, a card. White. The edges gilt.
For the Lady Lyra Thorne. To be called for.
Copperplate. Perfect. Every loop the same size as every other loop. The hand of a clerk who had been taught to write by a man who was paid to teach it.
"It has been in the yard," said Madame Hesper, behind her. "Since half past ten yesterday morning. All night. In the coal bunker, under a sack. I did not want it in the shop." She shut the back door and turned the key. "I brought it in when Bridie came back today and said she had found you. So that it would not be cold for you to open."
"Why not in the shop?"
Madame Hesper looked at her over the round spectacles.
"Because I have been dressing the Hill for forty-one years, Lady Lyra," she said, "and in forty-one years, nothing good has ever come into my shop by the front door in gray paper with a seal that has no name on it." She came and stood by the table. "Open it, or don't. But don't stand there looking at it as if it might bite. It's a box."
Lyra took off her cap.
She did not know why she did that. She put it on the table beside the box. Then she pulled the end of the gray silk ribbon, and the bow came undone, slowly, all at once, the way a good bow does. The ribbon slid off. The seal stayed on the lid, on its own, a little gray island.
She lifted the lid.
Tissue paper. Many layers of it, white, soft as breath, folded over and over. She lifted it away a layer at a time. Oren stood behind her at her shoulder, so close she could feel the cold coming off Terrence's coat. Madame Hesper stood at her other side, very still, with her hands folded in front of her apron.
The last layer of tissue came away.
It was a mask.
A half-mask, the kind that covers the eyes and the nose and the tops of the cheeks, and leaves the mouth bare. It lay in the bottom of the box on a bed of white silk, face up, with its ribbons coiled beside it. And it was white. Not white like paper. White like the inside of a shell, or the first snow on a windowsill before anyone has breathed on it. The silk was stretched over a frame so fine and so light that the mask seemed to have no weight at all, as if it might lift out of the box on its own if you breathed on it.
It had no feathers. No jewels. No paint. Nothing on it at all, except, all round the edge, where the silk turned under, a single row of seed pearls, each one no bigger than a grain of rice. And round each of the two eye holes, a line of silver thread, so fine that you could only see it when the light moved. It caught the red light of the stove as Lyra bent over it, and flickered, and went out.
The ribbons were white silk too. Long. Very long. Long enough to tie at the back of the head and fall nearly to the waist.
Lyra looked at it.
She did not touch it.
She thought of the Duke of Marrow in his gallery on Saturday, with his sleepy pale eyes, saying Wear gray. Your mask white. She had not told anyone that but Corin. And it was written in the Ledger, and nowhere else.
"There's no note," she said.
Madame Hesper bent and looked into the box. She lifted the white silk bed the mask lay on, by one corner, and looked under it. She ran her long finger round the inside of the box, along every edge, into every corner. She turned the lid over and looked inside it. She picked up the card from the table and turned it over and looked at the back.
Nothing.
"No," she said. "There is no note." She put the card down. "There doesn't need to be."
She put out her hand toward the mask. Then she stopped, with her fingers above it, not touching.
"May I?"
Lyra nodded.
Madame Hesper lifted the mask out of the box. She did it the way she did everything, quickly and without fuss, with her long cold fingers, but there was something different about the way she held it. She held it the way Lyra had seen the old man at the Duke's gallery, Mr. Pennick, hold one of his glowing vials: as if it were something that would break if you thought about it too hard.
She turned it over in the lamplight. She held it up close to her round spectacles, and looked at the seed pearls, one by one. She ran her thumbnail very lightly along the silver thread round one eye hole. She turned it to the side and looked at the edge of the frame, where the silk was folded under.
"This was not made in this city," she said quietly.
"How do you know?"
"Because there is nobody in this city who can do it. Not me. Not Pellam on Silk Street. Not the old Lacquer woman on the Crescent, who made masks for the Archon's own wife." Madame Hesper turned the mask over again, in her long fingers. "Look at the frame. It is not wire. It is not whalebone. It is not anything I know. It weighs less than a feather, and it does not bend." She pressed it, very gently, between finger and thumb. "And the silk is stretched on it without one seam. Not one. A single piece. Over the nose, and round the eyes, and under, and back. I have been forty-one years at this table and I could not do that if you gave me a year and paid me in gold."
She turned it over, so that the inside of the mask lay face up on her palm.
The lining.
It was a different silk. Gray. A pale soft gray, like the gray of the paper on the box, quilted all over in tiny diamonds, the way the inside of a good jewel case is quilted. Each diamond no bigger than a fingernail. And each one stitched round with a thread so fine that it was nearly invisible against the gray. A thread that was not gray.
Madame Hesper looked at it for a long moment.
Then she put her thumb flat on the lining, over the bridge of the nose, where a mask sits closest to the face.
And pressed.
And stopped.
Lyra saw her stop. She saw the long cold fingers go still, and the long face go still, and the round spectacles catch the lamplight and go white so that you could not see the eyes behind them. And she heard Madame Hesper breathe in, through her nose, very slowly, the way you breathe in when you have put your hand into a drawer in the dark and touched something you did not expect.
"There is something in here," said Madame Hesper.
The Lining
Madame Hesper did not ask this time.
She carried the mask to the stove, where the light was best, and laid it face down on a square of clean white linen on the corner of the long table, so that the gray lining was uppermost. She took her spectacles off their silver chain and polished them on the hem of her apron and put them on again. She took a pair of scissors out of one of the bulging pockets. Tiny scissors, no longer than her little finger, with handles shaped like the wings of a bird and blades so thin that the light came through them.
Then she bent over the mask, so close that her long nose nearly touched the silk, and felt along the lining with the tip of one finger.
"Here," she said. "Feel."
Lyra put her finger where Madame Hesper's had been. Over the bridge of the nose, where the two eye holes came closest together. The gray silk was soft and cool. And under it, under the quilting, under the tiny diamonds, there was something hard. Flat. Thin. A little longer than the top joint of her thumb. She could feel its edges through the silk, square at one end, and something with teeth at the other.
"A key," said Lyra.
"A key," said Madame Hesper. "Sewn in. Between the lining and the frame, in a pocket made for it. Somebody quilted the lining over it afterward, so that it would not move, and would not show, and would sit against the wearer's forehead all night." She turned the mask a little, toward the light. "Do you want it out?"
Lyra looked at the mask on the white linen. The pearls. The silver thread. The long white ribbons coiled beside it like two sleeping snakes.
"Yes."
Madame Hesper bent to it.
She did not cut the silk. Lyra saw that at once. She did not put the point of the little scissors into the lining and slit it, the way Lyra would have done. She found the end of one quilting thread, where it went down into the silk at the corner of a diamond, and caught it with the very tip of the scissors, and drew it up. One stitch. Then the next. Then the next. Slowly, slowly, with her long face bent over the work and her breath held, she unpicked the stitches round three of the tiny diamonds, one at a time, and laid each thread as it came free on the white linen beside the mask, in a neat line, like a hair.
It took her nearly a quarter of an hour.
Nobody said anything. The stove ticked. The kettle sang, very faintly, on the hob. Outside in the mews, a horse went by, and someone shouted to someone else about a wheel. Oren stood by the back door with his arms folded and his eyes on the window, and did not look round once.
Then Madame Hesper put down the scissors, and lifted the loosened flap of gray silk with the tip of her finger, and there it was.
A small flat key of yellow brass.
It lay in a pocket of thin gray kid, sewn to the inside of the frame, and it fitted the pocket so exactly that it might have grown there. Madame Hesper drew it out with two fingers and laid it on the linen.
Lyra looked at it.
It was a plain key. That was the first thing. Not grand. Not a key to a front door or a strongroom or a jewel case. The kind of key that hangs on a nail in a housekeeper's room, on a ring with forty others. A flat bow, a round hole in it for the ring. A short shank. Three plain teeth. The brass was worn smooth and pale at the bow, where a great many thumbs had held it. But the teeth were bright. New cut. As if somebody had taken an old key and filed it, very recently, very carefully, to fit a different lock.
And on the bow, stamped into the brass, very small, two marks.
A letter. V.
And under it, a number. 3.
Lyra did not touch it.
"V," said Oren, from the door. He had turned round. He was looking at the key on the linen, and his face had gone very still. "That's the Lady's mark. On her lamps. On her door. On that boy's hand."
"Yes," said Lyra.
"And three?"
"I don't know."
Madame Hesper did not say anything. She was not looking at the key. She was looking at the threads.
The little line of unpicked quilting threads, laid out on the white linen beside the mask, one after another, each one curled a little from the stitch it had been in. Lyra looked at them too, now. She had not looked at them properly before. She had been looking at the key.
They were gold.
Not yellow. Not brass. Not the dull gilt thread that the Hill ladies had on their stomachers and their slippers, that went green at the edges after a winter. Gold. Real gold. So fine that a single thread of it lay on the linen like a single hair from a fair child's head, and caught the red light from the stove, and burned.
Madame Hesper picked one up. She held it between finger and thumb, up to her spectacles, and drew it out to its length, very slowly, and looked along it, the way a man looks along a gun barrel.
"Do you know," she said, "how gold thread is made?"
Lyra shook her head.
"You take a bar of gold. Pure. And you pull it through a hole in a steel plate. And then through a smaller hole. And a smaller. A hundred holes, a thousand, each one smaller than the last, until the bar is a wire, and the wire is a thread, and the thread is finer than a hair." Madame Hesper turned the thread in the light. "In this city, the best drawers on Gilt Street can get it to the thickness of a horsehair. No finer. It breaks. I have bought it from them for forty years. I know it the way I know my own needles."
She laid the thread down on the linen.
"That," she said, "is finer than a horsehair. That is finer than a baby's hair. There is only one place in the world where they can draw gold as fine as that, and it is not this side of the sea."
"Where?"
"Aurum." Madame Hesper took off her spectacles. Her eyes without them were smaller and older and very tired. "Across the Narrow Sea. East. The Gilded League. The merchant cities." She folded the spectacles. "I have seen it twice in my life. Once on the coat of a prince-merchant who came to the Archon's wedding, thirty years ago, and stood in the cathedral like a sunrise. And once on a christening gown that came to me to be mended. Twenty years ago. For a child of the Hill." She paused. "I mended it. And gave it back. And never knew whose it was."
The stove ticked.
Lyra looked at the threads on the linen, and the key, and the mask with its loosened lining, lying face down.
"The Bank," she said slowly. "Bridie said the footman was in Bank livery. Gray and silver. The inside servants. The ones who work upstairs." She looked at Madame Hesper. "Who at the Bank would have a thread from across the sea? And a key to the Lady's house? And my name?"
Madame Hesper did not answer at once.
She put her spectacles back on their chain. She folded her hands in front of her apron. And she looked at Lyra for a long time, the way she had looked at her on Saturday when she came to the back door with hay in her hair.
"There are two men at the Bank of Veridia who keep footmen of their own," she said. "In their own livery. Upstairs. One is the Archon. The Bank is his, as everything is his. And the Archon does not send parcels to young ladies in dressmakers' shops." She paused. "The other is the Treasurer."
"Who is the Treasurer?"
"Mr. Dorian Sterling." Madame Hesper said the name as if she were laying a pin down on a table, very exactly, in its place. "I made a waistcoat for him once. Eight years ago. Gray silk, with a silver thread. He came for the fitting himself, which they never do, the Bank men. Silver-haired already, though he was not forty. Very charming. Very easy. He talked to me the whole time about my roses in the yard, and asked their names, and remembered them all, every one, when he came back a week later." Her long face did not move. "He asked for a watch pocket in the waistcoat. On the left. And he said, Make it deep, Madame. I never take it out."
"His watch?"
"His watch. Gold. A big round one, on a chain." Madame Hesper's mouth went thin. "I watched him for an hour, at that fitting, and the next one. He never looked at it. Not once. A man who carries a gold watch and never looks at it, Lady Lyra, is a man who always knows what time it is."
Lyra did not say anything.
"That is all I know," said Madame Hesper. "Except that he paid his bill on the day it was due, to the penny, which no man on the Hill has ever done before or since. And that I did not like him." She turned away to the stove and picked up the kettle. "Now. You'll take tea before you go. And then you'll take that box out of my shop, and I shall forget I ever saw it."
She poured. The steam went up between them.
"And you will come back tomorrow," she said, without turning round. "Thursday. At the fourth bell. By the back. For the last fitting of your mother's gown."
"I thought it was finished."
"It was finished on Saturday." Madame Hesper put the kettle down. "For the girl you were on Saturday." She turned round with the cup in her hand and looked at Lyra over the steam, at the cropped hair and the thinner face and the wrists sticking out of the too-big sleeves. "You've lost half a stone since then. You'll swim in it. The Hill will see. I will not have the Hill see a Thorne swim in her mother's gown." She held out the cup. "Fourth bell. Don't be late."
Lyra took the cup.
She held it in both hands, the way she had held the thin white cup at Aunt Cressida's in the autumn, with the little silver mask looking up at her out of the bottom. This one was plain brown china, with a chip in the rim. There was nothing in the bottom of it but tea.
"Madame Hesper," she said. "Why are you doing this?"
Madame Hesper looked at her.
"Because I dressed your mother," she said, "for eleven years. Every gown she ever wore on the Hill. And the last time she came to this shop, she stood where you're standing now, by that stove, in her traveling cloak, and held both my hands in her gray gloves, and said, If ever my girl comes to your door, Hesper, let her in." She turned back to the stove. "I didn't know what she meant. Not then. She went to the coast the next week, and didn't come back." She picked up the poker and pushed at the coals. "Drink your tea."
