Chapter 91: Snow on the Free Valleys, Part 1 of 5: The Edge at Dawn
The Edge at Dawn
The snow had stopped some time in the night, and in the gray before morning the whole world was white and still.
Lyra Thorne stood on a flat rock at the top of a low hill and looked north. Behind her, a long way off, the Glass lay along the edge of the sky like a strip of dull steel. It did not shine at this hour. It only lay there, smooth and endless and quiet, with the white land running up to it and stopping, as if the snow did not dare go any farther. She could see where the snow ended. It ended in a line so clean you could have drawn it with a ruler. Snow on this side. Nothing on that side. Not even frost.
She had walked out of there. Four days ago. Five. She had lost count somewhere in the cold.
She turned her back on it.
South, the land fell away in long soft folds, white on white, with here and there a black line of a stone wall half buried, or a dark smudge of thorn trees bent over by the wind. There were no roads that she could see. There were no houses. There was a frozen stream in the bottom of the nearest valley, and she could see it only because the snow over it was a little flatter and a little bluer than the snow around it. The sky over everything was the color of an old tin plate. Low. Heavy. It would snow again before noon.
The cold was the kind that got into your teeth. She had a scarf wound three times round her face, up over her nose, and her breath had frozen on the wool in a stiff white crust. Her boots were wet through and had been wet through for two days. Her feet did not hurt anymore, which was worse than when they had hurt.
She pulled the scarf down off her mouth and breathed the air in, sharp as glass, and let it out in a long white cloud.
Then she did the cut.
She did it empty-handed, the way she had done it every dawn since the Eye. Her right hand was still bound in a strip of linen, wrapped round and round the palm and tied at the wrist with a knot she had pulled tight with her teeth. The cut under it had stopped weeping two days ago. It had started to itch instead. She could close the hand now, a little. Not round a hilt. Not yet. So she did not hold anything. She held the shape of a sword instead, the way her father had shown her when she was nine and too small to lift a real one.
Back foot.
She set it in the snow behind her, turned out, flat. She felt the rock under the snow, under her heel. She sank her weight into it. Front foot forward, light, the toe pointing where the blade would go. Hands together at her right shoulder, the left one under the bound right one, the way they would be on a grip. She breathed in.
And then she let it go. The whole of her. From the back heel up through the leg and the hip and the long muscles of the back, round through the shoulders, out along the arms, into the empty air where a blade would be. A long low curve, rising at the end. The Vance cut. It ended with her arms out straight in front of her and her weight all forward on the front foot, and the snow sprayed up from under her boot in a little white fan and fell back down.
She held it there. Breathing.
The cut was still in her. That was the thing. She checked every morning, the way you would touch a sore tooth with your tongue to see if it was still there. Every morning she stood up and set her feet and let it go, and every morning it came. Clean. Whole. As if nothing had happened under the Eye. As if a tired man in a gray coat had not knelt over her with his bare hand and looked at it, at the cut, in her, and chosen to leave it where it was.
She did not know why he had done that. She tried not to think about it, and thought about it every morning.
Keep counting.
She set her feet and did it again.
"Back foot," said a voice behind her.
She did not turn round. "I know."
"Then do it."
She looked down. Her back foot had come off its edge, a little, on the second cut. The heel had lifted. Only a finger's width. Nobody else in the world would have seen it.
She set it down again. Flat. She felt the rock.
"Like a post," said Oren Vance.
"Like a post," said Lyra.
She did it a third time, and this time it was right, and she knew it was right before she had finished. She felt it go all the way through. She held the end of it, arms out, breath held, and then let her arms come down and turned round.
Oren was sitting on a stone a little way down the slope, with a blanket round his shoulders like a cloak and the hood of his old army coat pulled up over his head. He had his sword across his knees. He always had it across his knees when he watched her. He had a small gray whetstone in his right hand, and he was drawing it along the edge of the blade, slowly, from the hilt to the point, and then lifting it off and going back to the hilt and doing it again. The sound it made was very small in the white silence. A long soft whisper, and a pause, and another long soft whisper.
His hands knew how to do that. They knew it all on their own. She had watched him do it every morning for four mornings, and every morning his face had the same look on it while he did it. A careful look. A little puzzled. The look of a man listening to a song in a language he used to speak.
He could sharpen the sword. He could hold it. He could carry it on his back, and stand up with it, and stand in a doorway with it so that nobody could get past. He could not use it. Not anymore. Kross had taken that from him, under the Eye, with his bare hand on the back of Oren's neck. All of it. Every year of it. Every drill in every yard, every fight, every morning in the cold with a wooden sword and a sergeant shouting. Gone.
And yet his eyes still knew the cut. He could not do it. But he could see it. He could see when her back foot was a finger's width off the ground, from twenty paces, in the gray light, with snow in the air.
She did not understand that either.
She came down off the rock and walked to him through the snow. It came up over her ankles. It was dry, powdery stuff on the top, and under that there was a crust that broke with a crunch at every step, and under that it was soft again. Walking in it was like walking on a pie.
"Better," said Oren, without looking up from the blade.
"The third one."
"The third one." Whisper went the stone. "The first two, your back foot was thinking about breakfast."
"It was thinking about my feet," said Lyra. "Which I can't feel."
"Can't feel them is good," said Oren. "Means you're not wasting time on them." He lifted the stone off the blade and looked along the edge with one eye shut. Then he went back to the hilt. "When you can't feel your hands, that's bad. Feet, nobody cares."
"I care."
"Nobody else."
She sat down on the stone beside him. It was cold through her coat, through her skirt and her leggings and everything. She tucked her bound hand in under her other arm, inside her coat, where it was warm.
Below them, in a hollow out of the wind, the others were waking up.
They had slept in a heap, the way the Ashborn slept on the Glass. A great pile of felt blankets and hides with the warm stones from the fire in the middle and bodies all round them, back to back, and every cover they had over the top. It had looked like a small brown hill in the snow. Now the hill was coming apart. She watched a pair of arms come out of the top of it and stretch. A head came out after it. A short, wiry woman, not young and not old, with a sharp brown face and quick black eyes. Oda. She sat up in the snow with the blankets falling off her and yawned so wide that Lyra heard her jaw crack from up the hill.
Then Sef came out. He was young and thin, with a line of gray down his face that stopped just past the tip of his nose, and he had a way of coming out of things all at once, as if somebody had pulled him. He stood up and looked all round with his hand shading his eyes, as if there might be something to see. There was nothing to see but snow. He seemed pleased about it.
And then Kestrel.
She did not come out of the heap. She had never been in it. She had sat up all night beside it, on a folded hide, with her spear across her knees and her back to the wind, and she was sitting there still, very straight, with frost on her eyebrows and on the smooth gray line that ran over her shaved head from the back of her neck to the tip of her nose. The tall Ashborn woman did not seem to feel the cold at all. She did not seem to feel anything much. She sat and watched the south the way an owl watches a field.
Lyra looked for the last one.
He was a little way off from the heap. He had his back against a stone, and he was sitting with his knees drawn up and a book open on them. A big book. Black leather, scuffed gray at the corners, with a strap round it. He had one gloved finger on the page, moving slowly along the lines. And his lips were moving. She could not hear him from here. But she knew what he was saying. She had heard it every morning for five days.
He was reading himself.
She watched him do it. Corin Vane, sitting in the snow at the bottom of the world, with his hood back and the frost in his dark hair, reading his own life out loud to himself so that he would not forget it.
She could see the gray from here.
It was on the left side of his face. It went up his neck from his collar, over the line of his jaw, across his cheek. It was the color of ash, of old candle wax, smooth and dull, with no shine to it. It went to the corner of his mouth. It had been at the corner of his mouth for three days now. It had not moved. She looked at it every morning, before she looked at anything else. She looked at it the way she touched the cut in herself. To see if it had moved.
It had not moved.
Vespera had said, before she went on ahead. The next one goes in at the mouth. You understand me? The next big burn. It will take words. Not memories. Words. The words for things.
And she had looked at Lyra, then, with her sharp black eyes in her old wrinkled face, and said: Don't let him.
Lyra had not asked how she was meant to stop him. She was not sure anybody had ever stopped Corin Vane from doing anything.
"He's been at it since before light," said Oren. He had not looked up. He did not need to. "Reads the whole first part twice. Then he does the new bit. Then he shuts it and sits."
"I know."
"He's slower than he was." Whisper. Pause. "At the reading."
Lyra did not answer that. She had noticed it too. She did not want to talk about it.
Below them Corin closed the book. He did up the strap. Then he sat with both hands flat on the cover, one in its glove and one in its glove, and looked at nothing. Only for a moment. Then he looked up the hill. Straight at her. As if he had known all along exactly where she was.
He lifted his hand.
She lifted hers.
"We should go," she said. "It'll snow by noon."
Oren nodded. He drew the stone down the blade one last time, from the hilt to the point. Then he held the sword up in front of him in the gray light and looked at it. Just looked. As if he were trying to remember what it was for.
Then he put it back in its sheath, and put the sheath on his back, and stood up. Slowly. With one hand on the stone. And they went down the hill together to the others.
The Walk South
They packed the heap in the way the Ashborn had taught them, quickly and without talking. The felt blankets were rolled tight and tied with thongs. The hides were folded hair-side in. The warm stones were tipped out of the middle onto the snow, where they hissed and sank and sent up little threads of steam, and then Oda picked them up one by one with a fold of leather and put them back in the iron pot to carry, because there would be no stones like that where they were going. There was a little cold porridge left from last night in the bottom of the other pot. They shared it round with their fingers. It tasted of smoke and oats and nothing else, and it was the best thing Lyra had eaten in her life, the way every meal had been the best thing she had eaten in her life since they came off the Glass.
Then they walked.
Kestrel went first. She always went first. She walked with her spear held loose in one hand, upright, the butt end striking the snow every second step, and she did not look back to see if anyone was keeping up. She did not need to. Sef walked behind her, then Oren, then Lyra and Corin side by side, and Oda came last, with the pot and the stones on her back in a sling, humming something under her breath that had no tune Lyra could follow.
The snow on the slope going down was deep and soft, and in the hollows it was deeper. Twice Sef went in up to his waist and had to be pulled out by Oren, who reached down and took him by the collar of his coat and lifted him like a pup. Sef laughed both times. Oren did not. He set him down on the crust and went on walking.
They came down into the valley with the frozen stream, and walked along the side of it, because the snow was thinner there, and the wind had swept the ice in places so that it showed through, black and clear, with white bubbles trapped in it like little moons. Under the ice, Lyra could hear the water still moving. A small, cold, busy sound. Going south, the same as them.
Corin walked beside her with his head down, leaning on the ash crutch Silas had made for him, a long time ago now, before the Glass. He did not need it the way he had. But his leg was still weak on the hills, and he had not given it up.
She kept the space between them. A hand's width. She had kept it so long now that her body kept it on its own, the way you keep your hand away from a hot stove without thinking about it. When the path went narrow along the stream bank and they had to walk closer, she slowed down and let him go first. When it went wide again, she came up beside him. A hand's width. Never closer. He had his gloves on. He always had his gloves on now, even to sleep. But she kept the space anyway.
It was not because she was afraid of him.
She had tried to work out, more than once, on the long walk over the Glass, exactly what it was because of. She had not got very far. It was something to do with the way he had looked at his own bare hands, in the Eye, after. As if they belonged to someone else. As if they might go off and do something without asking him first.
She thought perhaps the space was for him, not for her.
"You're quiet," she said.
He looked up. His eyes were gray. They had always been gray, as far as she knew. They were the only part of his face that the gray on his skin had not touched. On the right side his face was a young man's face, thin and cold and red at the cheekbone from the wind. On the left side it was smooth and pale and still, like the face on a stone saint in a church. It did not move when he talked. When he smiled, only half of his mouth went up.
He did not smile now.
"I'm thinking," he said.
"About what?"
"How many words I've got."
She walked on for a few steps before she answered.
"Don't," she said.
"I can't help it." He looked down at the snow again. "I keep finding myself doing it. Counting. I'll be walking along and I'll see a thorn tree and I'll think, thorn tree, and I'll hold onto it. As if somebody were going to come along and take it." He breathed out a long cloud. "Thorn tree. Snow. Stream. Ice. Stone. Boot."
"You've got them all," said Lyra. "Every one."
"Today."
"Today is what we've got."
He was quiet again. The stream talked to itself under the ice. Up ahead, Kestrel's spear went thock into the snow, and thock, and thock.
"Vespera said the next big one goes in at the mouth," he said.
"I know what she said. I was there."
"So there won't be a next big one."
She looked at him sideways. He was looking straight ahead.
"Do you mean that?" she said.
"I mean I'll try."
"That's not the same."
"No." Half his mouth went up. "It's not."
She did not push it. There was no point. She knew him well enough by now to know that he would not lie to her about it, and he would not promise her something he did not think he could keep. I'll try was as far as he would go. It was a long way, for him.
She found she was holding her bound hand in her coat pocket very tightly. She let it go.
"Tell me something," he said.
"What?"
"Anything. About you. You said you would. A bit at a time."
She had said that. In the Eye. When Kestrel had offered to read her page aloud to him, the page in the Ledger where he had written down what he knew about her, so that if he lost her he would have it. She had said no. I'll tell you myself. A bit at a time. No lies. And so far she had told him about Biscuit, the fat brown pony she had when she was seven, who bit everybody but her and once ate her father's hat. And that was all. Five days and one pony.
"All right," she said.
She thought about it. They walked. A crow went over, very high, black against the gray, going north, and called once. It was the first bird she had seen since the Edge.
"My mother had a garden," she said. "At the house on the Hill. Behind the house. It had a high brick wall all the way round it, old red brick, with moss in between the bricks, and a little green gate at the end that nobody ever used. She grew herbs, mostly. Rosemary. Sage. Thyme. Mint, in pots, because she said mint was a bully and would take over the whole garden if you let it out. And roses, up the wall. Pink ones. They smelled like tea."
She stopped. She had not thought about the garden in a long time. She found she could see it very clearly. The way the light came over the wall in the afternoon and lay on the gravel paths. The bees in the lavender. Her mother on her knees in the dirt, in an old blue dress with the sleeves pushed up, and a man's straw hat on her head with a hole in the brim.
"She was always dirty," Lyra said. "My mother. That's the thing I remember most. Every other lady on the Hill was so clean. White gloves. Gloves to go out in the carriage, gloves to eat cake. And my mother would come in to dinner with dirt under her nails and a leaf in her hair, and my father would look at her down the table and say, Isolde, you have a garden on your head, and she would laugh and not take it out."
She walked for a while.
"She used to sing out there," she said. "In the garden. While she worked. She sang very well. Much better than me."
"You sing flat," said Corin.
"I know I sing flat. You don't have to tell me."
"I wrote it down," he said. "In the Ledger. Lyra sings flat. Her mother sang. Before." He looked at the snow. "I didn't know about the garden."
"Well, now you do."
"Will you let me write it down?"
She thought about that. About her mother's garden, in black ink, in his square careful capitals, in the big book with the strap round it. About him reading it to himself, some gray morning a long way from here, because he had forgotten. HER MOTHER HAD A GARDEN WITH A WALL ROUND IT. PINK ROSES THAT SMELLED LIKE TEA. SHE CAME IN TO DINNER WITH DIRT ON HER HANDS.
"Yes," she said. "Write it down."
He nodded. He did not take the Ledger out. He would do it tonight, she knew, by the fire, slowly, with his tongue between his teeth. He wrote very slowly now. Slower than he used to.
The snow began a little before noon, just as she had said it would.
It came in on the wind from the north, behind them, so at least it was not in their faces. Small hard flakes at first, that stung where they hit and bounced off the wool of her scarf. Then bigger and softer. Then so many of them that the far side of the valley went gray and then went away altogether, and there was only the stream, and the snow, and the backs of the people in front of her, dark shapes that got paler and paler the farther ahead they were. Kestrel at the front was hardly more than a shadow. Lyra could hear her spear. Thock. Thock. She walked toward the sound.
They did not stop. Kestrel did not stop. The Ashborn did not stop for snow. Lyra had asked Oda about it, on the Glass, and Oda had looked at her in surprise and said, Why stop? It is only water. Water falling down. And then laughed, as if Lyra had asked why they did not stop for sky.
So they went on.
The world got very small. Lyra's world was the next step, and the next, and the sound of the spear ahead, and the shape of Oren's broad back with the sword on it, and the snow in her eyelashes, and the cold. And Corin on her left, a hand's width away, walking with his head down. She could hear him breathing. She could hear, sometimes, under the wind, that he was saying something. Very quietly. To himself.
She leaned a little closer. Not much. Not past the hand's width. Just enough to hear.
"Snow," Corin was saying. "Snow. Stream. Boot. Oren. Lyra. Snow."
She straightened up again and walked on and did not say anything.
In the middle of the afternoon the valley turned, and the stream turned with it, and went off to the east through a gap in the hills. Kestrel did not turn. She went straight on, south, up a long slope with a broken stone wall running along the top of it, and stopped at the wall and waited for them. When they came up to her she was standing with one hand on the stones, looking at something on the other side.
Lyra came up beside her and looked.
On the other side of the wall the land went down again, and at the bottom of it, half buried in snow, there was a sheepfold. Four stone walls in a square, as high as a man's chest, with a gap in one side for a gate. And in one corner of it, a little roofed shelter, built against the wall, with a turf roof heavy with snow. There was no smoke. There were no sheep. There was nobody there.
"There," said Kestrel. "Tonight."
"It's still light," said Lyra. "We could go on another hour."
Kestrel turned her head and looked at her. Her eyes were the color of weak tea, and very calm, with frost in the lashes.
"The heron is tired," she said.
Lyra looked at Corin. He was standing a little way back, with snow on his hood and his shoulders, and he was swaying. Only a little. On his feet. The way a tree sways when it has been cut almost through and has not decided yet which way to fall. His face, the right side of it, the side that still had color, had gone very white.
She had not seen. She had been walking beside him all afternoon, a hand's width away, and she had not seen.
"Yes," she said. "Tonight."
