Chapter 91: Snow on the Free Valleys, Part 4 of 5: The Long Hall
The Long Hall
The hall was just as she remembered it, and it was not.
It was long and low and dark, with a floor of beaten earth strewn with rushes, and a roof held up on great black beams so old they had gone hard as iron. Down the middle of the floor there was a long stone hearth, and on the hearth a peat fire burned low and red, and the smoke went up and found its own way out through a hole in the roof, mostly. The rest of it hung under the beams in a soft brown layer, so that the top of the hall was always a little dim, a little blue, like the top of a wood on a summer evening. It smelled of peat and old smoke and tallow candles and wet wool.
On the walls there were the things she remembered. Pikes. Long ash poles with rusty iron heads, a whole row of them, hung up on pegs. Iron caps, the old round kind with a rim, gone brown with rust and polished on top where hands had touched them. A drum with a cracked skin, painted once in red and yellow, the paint mostly gone. A pair of child's boots, small and black and very worn, nailed up over the door with a bunch of dried heather tied between them. And over the hearth, at the far end, on two long poles fixed into the wall, the banner. Torn and faded. Brown cloth that had been some other color once. And on it, very faint, a yellow sun.
The last time she had been here, she had not known what the banner was. Now she did. She had seen that sun on a wall under the Glass. She had seen it carved over a stair, and painted on the shield of a dead woman who had been a general.
Sola's sun.
What was different was the people.
The last time there had been children everywhere. Running about in the rushes, climbing on the benches, hiding under the long tables. A girl with two fair plaits who had touched Corin's gray hand. Old men playing a game with stones on a board by the fire. Women spinning. Noise.
Tonight there were only a few people, and they were quiet. Two old men on a bench by the fire, not playing anything, only sitting. A woman Lyra did not know, mending a net. A young man at the door end, with a stave across his knees, who stood up when they came in and sat down again when he saw it was Hild. And at the far end, by the hearth, in a high wooden chair with arms carved into the shape of two hounds, the smallest old woman Lyra had ever seen.
Granny Abbe had not changed at all.
She was tiny. Her feet did not reach the floor from the chair, and she had them on a little stool. She had a face like a walnut, brown and lined all over, every line going somewhere. Her hair was white and thin and pulled back under a black cap. And she was wearing, as she had been the last time, a man's army coat, much too big for her, of some old faded red cloth, with the sleeves rolled back four times at the wrists so that her small brown hands could come out. There was one brass button on it, done up at the throat. The rest were gone.
She had a clay pipe in her mouth. It was not lit. Lyra did not think it had been lit in years. Abbe held it between her teeth and moved it from one side of her mouth to the other when she was thinking.
She was looking at Lyra now. She had been looking at her since the door.
"The Hill girl," she said. Her voice was very dry and very strong, like a branch that has been in the sun a long time. "Back again."
"Yes, Granny."
"You came by the top."
"Davin told us."
"Davin should be in his bed." Abbe moved the pipe from one side to the other. "Sit. You look like a drowned cat. Hild, give her something hot."
Lyra sat on the end of the bench nearest the chair. Hild went away and came back with a wooden cup of something that steamed, and pushed it into Lyra's good hand. It was milk, hot, with honey in it, and something else, something sharp and brown that went straight down into Lyra's middle like a lit coal and spread out from there.
"You saw the post," said Abbe.
"Yes."
"What did you think of the picture?"
Lyra looked down into the cup.
"They drew him too fat," she said.
One of the old men by the fire laughed. A short, rough laugh, like a cough. Abbe did not laugh. But her pipe moved.
"That's what Davin said," she said. "Is he with you? The gray boy?"
"In Hild's byre."
"With the cows." The pipe moved again. "Good. Cows don't read." She leaned back in the big chair, and it seemed to swallow her up. "And the captain?"
"Yes."
"And the others? The spear-folk?"
"Three of them. Kestrel, and Oda, and Sef."
At the name Kestrel, both of the old men on the bench looked round. Abbe's eyebrows went up a very little way.
"Kestrel herself," she said. "Out of the Glass. On this side." She took the pipe out of her mouth and looked at it, as if it had done something surprising. "Well. I didn't think I'd live to see that."
"You know her?"
"Everybody in the free valleys knows of her. Nobody knows her." She put the pipe back. "She'll be wanting to go on. To the city."
"We all are."
Abbe looked at her a long time. The fire ticked. The woman with the net had stopped mending and was listening, with her hands still in her lap.
"The men who came," said Abbe at last. "The gray ones and the one in black. Do you know what they said to me, Hill girl? When I sat on my step and would not stand up for them?"
Lyra shook her head.
"The one in black did the talking," said Abbe. "Thin man. Young. Very clean boots. He had a paper, and he read it out, and then he rolled it up and he leaned down to me. Like this." She leaned forward in her chair, a little, so that her walnut face came close to Lyra's. "And he said, very kind, Mother, you must understand. This is for your own good. By the spring, the free valleys will be on the Ministry's books like everywhere else. You will have an office in Haverstock. You will have lamps. You will have a weighing day once a month, and a list, and a man to keep it. You will be safe."
She sat back.
"Safe," she said.
The old men on the bench did not laugh this time.
"We have been free in these valleys," said Abbe, "since my grandmother's grandmother. Do you know why? Not because we fought for it. We did fight for it, once. But that's not why. We have been free because nobody wanted us. We were too far. Too poor. Too close to the Glass. The tax men came up the south road once, when I was a little girl, and looked at us, and went back down again, because it would have cost them more to count us than we were worth." She moved the pipe. "It's a fine thing, to be not worth counting. You can live a long time that way."
"And now they want to count you," said Lyra.
"Now they want to count everybody," said Abbe. "Every last one. Every sheep and every child and every old woman on a step." She looked at the fire. "I asked him why. The young man in black. I said, what do you want with us? We have nothing. And he smiled at me, very nice. And he said: Mother, you have more than you know."
She was quiet.
"I've thought about that every night since," she said. "I'm ninety-one years old, girl. I've got more in my head than anybody in this valley. I've got my mother's songs, and her mother's. I've got the old war. I was a young wife with two babies when my man came home from it with one arm. I've got the night the river came up over the ford and took Rob Tullie's house with Rob Tullie in it. I've got every child I ever helped into the world in this valley, and there's been a fair few." She tapped the side of her head with the stem of the pipe. "More than you know. That's what he meant. Not the sheep."
Lyra put down her cup.
She thought about the jars in Corin's padded box. The little glass jars with the gray ash in them, swirling. She thought about the vaults under the Bank, which she had never seen, where people said the jars went up on shelves in rows that went on for a mile. She thought about a valley of old people who had been too poor to count, and who were not poor at all, if you counted the right thing.
"Yes," she said quietly. "That's what he meant."
Nobody said anything for a while.
"Hild says the spring," said Abbe. "The young man said the spring. I'll say before. I'll say they'll be back up that road when the thaw comes, with their list, and their lamps, and their weighing. Unless." She looked at Lyra, very sharp. "Unless something happens in that city first, that keeps them busy at home."
Lyra met her eyes.
"Something will," she said.
She did not know where it came from. She had not planned to say it. It came up out of her the way the anger had come up at the ford post, hot and sudden and from a long way down. And she found, once she had said it, that she meant it.
Abbe looked at her for a long time. Then she took the pipe out of her mouth, and pointed the stem of it at Lyra, the way you would point a finger.
"Then go and make it," she said. "And be quick about it. I'm old." She put the pipe back. "But not tonight. Tonight you sleep. You look like something the cat wouldn't bring in."
"I've been told," said Lyra.
"Then believe it." Abbe waved one small brown hand out of the end of her rolled-up sleeve. "Hild will put you in the loft. Tomorrow, before light, bring me the captain. And the gray boy. I want to look at him." She paused. "I have something for you. For the road."
"What?"
"Tomorrow," said Abbe, and shut her eyes.
And that was the end of it. She sat in the big chair with her feet on the stool and her eyes shut and the dead pipe in her teeth, and did not move again, and after a moment Lyra understood that she had been sent to bed.
She got up. Hild was waiting at the door with the lantern.
As Lyra went down the hall toward her, one of the old men on the bench by the fire put out his hand and caught her sleeve. Just for a moment. He had a long white beard, stained yellow round the mouth, and a face that had been out in a great many winters. She remembered him. He was the one who had touched Oren's sleeve, the last time, when Lyra said Oren's name out loud in this hall, and everybody went quiet.
"The captain," said the old man. His voice was hoarse and very low. "The Vance. Did he find her?"
Lyra looked at him.
"Find who?"
"The general," said the old man. "In the Glass. My father said she was still in there. Holding it. Under the sun." His old eyes went to the banner over the hearth, and back. "Did he find her?"
Lyra did not know what to say. She thought about a room under the Eye. A woman in armor sitting very straight in a chair, with her hands on a map, who had been dead for sixty years and was not dead, and then was.
"Yes," she said. "He found her."
The old man let go of her sleeve. He did not say anything else. He turned back to the fire, and put both his hands over his face, and sat like that.
Lyra went out into the cold with Hild, and did not look back.
Goose Fat and Yarrow
Hild fed them in the byre.
She brought it out from the house in a great black iron pot, carrying it by the handle with a folded cloth, and set it down on the barrel by the lantern. Mutton stew, thick, with barley and turnip and onion in it, and a skin of yellow fat on the top. And a round flat loaf, still warm, wrapped in a cloth. And a jug of the sour milk, and a little crock of salt. And six wooden bowls stacked one inside the other, which she handed out one at a time, looking at each person as she gave them a bowl, as if she were counting them in.
They ate sitting on the straw, with their backs against the stalls. Nobody talked. The cows chewed and watched them. Sef ate three bowls and then lay down in the straw with his hands on his stomach and his eyes shut and a look on his face like a saint in a window. Oda ate two and then went round and wiped out everyone else's bowls with the last of the bread and ate that too. Kestrel ate one bowl, slowly, sitting very straight with her spear beside her, and when she had finished she put the bowl down and bowed her head to Hild, a little, the way Lyra had seen her bow to the old ones in the Ashborn camp.
Hild did not seem to know what to do with that. She went pink across the cheeks and said, "There's more," and went to fuss with the lantern.
Corin ate half a bowl.
Lyra watched him. He ate it slowly, the way he had eaten the porridge, as if each spoonful were a job of work. When he had eaten half, he put the bowl down on the straw beside him and sat looking at it.
Hild saw. She did not say anything. She went out of the byre, and came back a little later with a small brown clay pot in one hand and a roll of clean linen in the other. She knelt down in the straw in front of Corin.
"Leg," she said.
Corin looked at her.
"It's better," he said. "It's healed. Mostly."
"I'll be the judge of mostly. Leg."
He rolled up the leg of his trousers. Slowly. It was his left leg. There was a long red scar down the outside of it, below the knee, as long as Lyra's hand. It had been a cut once, deep and ugly, and it had gone bad on the walk north, and Hild had dressed it in this byre with goose fat and yarrow and told him to change it every night. He had changed it every night. Lyra had watched him do it, every night, even on the Glass. Even the night after the Eye, when he could hardly hold the pot.
It had closed now. It was pink and shiny and puckered at the edges, and the skin all round it was clean.
Hild looked at it for a long time. She did not touch it. She seemed to know, somehow, without being told, that she should not touch it. She looked at it the way a farmer looks at a field after rain.
"Well," she said. "That's healed. You can let go of that crutch soon. Not yet. But soon."
"I told you."
"You did." She sat back on her heels. "You did what I said, too. Every night. I can see it." She looked up at his face. "Nobody ever does what I say."
"You said it very loud," said Corin.
Hild laughed. It came out of her suddenly, a big loud laugh, and the brown cow jumped in her stall. Hild put her hand over her own mouth. Then she took it away again and shook her head.
"Saints," she said. "Saints alive."
She held out the clay pot to him.
"Take it anyway," she said. "It's fresh. Put it on the scar, nights, so it doesn't pull. And on your hands, if they crack. And on hers." She nodded at Lyra's bound hand. "What's under there?"
"A cut," said Lyra.
"Let me see."
Lyra unwound the linen. It took a while, one-handed, and Hild watched her do it and did not offer to help, which Lyra was grateful for. When the last of it came away, she turned her right hand over in the lantern light, palm up.
The cut went across the whole palm from one side to the other, where the edge of a grip had opened it. And all round it the skin had torn away in patches, the way skin tears when you have held on to something so long and so hard that the blood dries and glues you to it. It was healing now. Red and shiny and tight, with new skin growing in pink and thin over the raw places. It was the mark of a sword hilt, held under the Eye with a bent blade on the end of it, for longer than any hand should hold anything.
Hild looked at it.
"How did you do that?"
"I held on to something," said Lyra.
Hild opened the little pot and dug two fingers into it and held them out. The ointment was pale yellow and smelled of fat and green things.
"I can touch you, can I?"
"Yes."
Hild laid the stuff on very gently with the tips of her fingers. It was cold for a moment and then warm. It stopped the itching at once. Lyra let out a breath she had not known she was holding.
"Leave it open tonight," said Hild. "Let it breathe. Bind it again in the morning, loose." She put the lid back on the pot and pushed it into Corin's hands. "There. Now you've got a job. Do hers and do yours."
Corin held the pot in his gloved hands and looked at it.
"Thank you," he said.
Hild stood up. She brushed the straw off her knees. She looked down at him for a while, sitting in the straw with the pot, and the lantern light on the gray side of his face.
"It's gone up," she said. "Since last time. The gray."
"Yes."
"To your mouth."
"Yes."
She nodded slowly. Then she said, without looking at anybody, "My brother's went to the mouth."
The byre went very quiet.
Lyra remembered. Hild had told them, the last time, kneeling in this same straw with the goose fat. Her brother Rab. Who went south to the docks when he was nineteen, to work, and burned for someone. For someone else's tax. And came home nine winters ago gray past the elbow, and sat by Hild's fire all one winter not saying much. And then one morning in the spring he got up early and walked north, onto the Glass, and never came back.
"He didn't talk much," said Hild, "by the end. He'd lost a lot of words. Little ones. He'd say the thing for a cup, and the other thing for a plate. He'd point." She was looking at the lantern. "He called me you. At the end. Just you. Like he'd lost my name too."
Nobody said anything.
"I'm not telling you to make you sorry," said Hild. "I'm telling you because you've been over it. The Glass. And come back." She looked at Corin. "Twice now."
"Yes."
"So I want to know." Her voice had gone very flat. "Is there anything out there. For them. For the gray ones that go."
Corin did not answer at once.
Lyra watched him. She watched him think about it. She knew what he was thinking about. The first night on the Glass, going north. Lying on his back on that endless smooth darkness, in the heap, and turning his head and looking down. Into it. And the lights.
She had seen them too. They all had. Soft gray-blue lights, deep down under the Glass, the size of a hand, drifting very slowly like pale fish in dark water. Some on their own. Some in twos and threes. Thousands of them, going away under the dark as far as you could look. The war's dead, Vespera had said. And the gray ones. Everyone who ever went into the Glass and did not come out. Kept. Not gone. Only kept, the way the cold keeps things.
And one of them had come up, out of all of them, and stopped right under Corin's gray hand. And stayed. Because it was his.
"There's light," Corin said.
Hild looked at him.
"Under the Glass," he said. "A long way down. At night you can see it. Lights. A great many. They move about. Slowly. They don't go anywhere. They just... stay." He was choosing every word, Lyra could see. Picking them up and turning them over before he used them. "The Ashborn say they're the ones who went in. Who stayed. Nobody knows if they know anything. If they think. But they're there. They're not gone."
Hild did not move.
"And they come up," said Corin, "if somebody of theirs is on the Glass. Somebody of their own blood. They come up under your hand and stay there." He looked down at his own left hand, in its glove. "One came up for me."
Hild stood very still in the lantern light, with her red arms at her sides.
"If I went out there," she said slowly. "If I went out on the Glass. And lay down."
"He'd come up," said Corin. "I think he'd come up."
Hild closed her eyes.
She stood like that for a long time. The cows chewed. The lantern hissed. Outside, the wind had got up, and was moving the snow on the roof over their heads with a soft dry sound like somebody brushing a coat.
Then Hild opened her eyes and wiped them both, quickly, with the heel of her hand, one and then the other. The way you would wipe flour off.
"Well," she said. "That's something."
She picked up the empty stew pot by the handle.
"I'll fetch more blankets," she said. "The loft's cold. You'll need more than you think." And she went out, and the byre door banged shut behind her in the wind.
Nobody said anything for a while.
"That was kind," said Kestrel.
Corin looked at her.
"It was true," he said.
"It was both," said Kestrel. "That is the best kind. This one did not know a city man could do both."
Corin did not answer. He sat in the straw with the little clay pot in his gloved hands. Then he took the lid off and held it out toward Lyra, at arm's length. Not closer.
"Hand," he said.
She held out her burned hand, palm up, and he dipped two gloved fingers in the pot and laid the ointment on, in the air above her palm, so that it dropped. It fell in a little cold blob in the middle of the burn. She spread it with her own thumb.
It was a stupid way to do it. They both knew that. It took twice as long as it needed to, and half of it went on her sleeve.
Neither of them said so.
