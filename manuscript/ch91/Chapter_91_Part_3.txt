Chapter 91: Snow on the Free Valleys, Part 3 of 5: The Two Sisters
The Two Sisters
They came to the Two Sisters a little after noon.
Lyra saw them from a long way off. Two cairns, on a bare round hilltop, standing side by side against the blue sky like two old women stopped on the road to talk. They were as tall as a man, and made of flat gray stones piled up very carefully, each one a little smaller than the one below, so that they went up in a point. There was snow on them now, on every ledge of every stone, so that they looked striped, gray and white, gray and white. The last time she had seen them there had been no snow. Only frost, and a wind.
The old road ran up between them. And on the far side, at the top of the hill, it split in two.
The right-hand road went on south. That was the one they had come up. Down off the hill and along the valleys to Thistle Ford, and after that Haverstock, and after that the farms and the fens and the city. The left-hand road went off east, along the top of the hills, and then bent round, slowly, north. Toward the Glass. It was hardly a road at all. A line of old stones sticking up through the snow on either side, a cart's width apart, going away into the white. And at the start of it, a little way along, there was a standing stone. Taller than the cairns. Thin and rough and leaning a little. With a round sun carved into the face of it, very worn, so that you could only see it when the light came at it sideways. Facing north.
She remembered all of it. She remembered Corin standing here, the last time, at the top of the hill where the roads split, and turning his head slowly to the left. And his feet going after his head. As if something were pulling him by a string. My feet went left. He had written it in the Ledger that night, at Thistle Ford. I did not tell them to.
They had not gone left, that day. They had gone right, the long way, by Thistle Ford and the fens, because that was the way Kross had told them, and Lyra had been reciting Kross's directions to herself in her head all the way, north by the old road, and then north again. They had not walked the Ashborn road. Not then.
She watched Corin now as they came up between the cairns.
He walked slowly, with his head down. Kestrel had stopped at the top, at the fork, and was standing there with her spear, waiting. Corin came up to her and stopped. And looked down the left-hand road for a long moment. At the line of stones going off into the snow. At the sun stone.
His feet did not move.
"Nothing," he said. He sounded surprised. "It doesn't pull."
"No," said Kestrel.
"Last time it pulled. My feet just went." He looked down at his boots, as if they might start on their own. "Now, nothing."
"Last time you were going home," said Kestrel. She said it simply. "Now you have been."
Corin looked at her.
Then he looked back down the road, the other road, going south. Down off the hill into the blue shadows of the valley. And Lyra saw something go across his face. The half of it that could still show things.
"Then that way's home too," he said quietly. "Now."
Kestrel did not say anything. But she put out her hand, the one that was not holding the spear, and laid it very lightly on his shoulder, on the coat, for a moment. And then took it away.
Lyra went to the cairns.
She went to the left-hand one. It had a flat stone on top, wider than the rest, set on like a hat. Somebody, a long time ago, had tied a red rag to a stick and pushed the stick in between the stones near the top, and the rag was still there, faded almost pink, stiff with frost. And at the foot of the cairn, half under the snow, there was a little wooden bird. She remembered it. A plain carved bird, no longer than her finger, with its wings folded, the wood gone gray and split with the weather. Somebody had left it there for somebody.
She knelt in the snow. She dug with her good hand, the left one, until she found a stone under the snow at the foot of the cairn. A small flat one. Gray, with a white line of quartz across it like a scar. It was frozen to the ground and she had to work it loose with her fingers.
She held it in her hand.
She had promised. At the shore cairn, on the edge of the Glass, the morning they buried Arran. Everybody had put a stone on, and said a name. Kestrel had said Hask, who taught us the notes. Oren had said Sola. My father's sister. She was a Vance. And Lyra had put her stone on and opened her mouth, and found she had nothing to say. Because the man she wanted to say was a man she had killed. On a stair under the Eye. A big square man in black, with a beard, who had been going to throw a stone at an old woman. And Lyra had brought up the Vance cut from her right hip, with her right hand, with the burn in it, and the man had gone down and not got up.
She did not know his name.
She had heard it. That was the worst of it. She had been there when the other Enforcer, Harrow, walked out past the Ashborn spears and knelt down by the bearded man and said something to him, very low. A name. She had heard the sound of it and not the word. And then they had picked him up and carried him away.
So at the shore cairn she had put her stone on and said, The man I cut. And she had said, to herself, very quietly, I'll find out his name. And I'll come back and say it.
She had not found out. She was nowhere near finding out. Harrow was somewhere south of the Glass, with whatever was left of his company, going back to the city.
She looked at the stone in her hand.
Then she reached up and set it on the cairn. On one of the ledges, near the top, just under the hat stone. She pushed it in firm so it would not fall.
"I still don't know your name," she said. Not loudly. "I'm sorry. I'm going to find out."
She stayed there on her knees for a moment, with her hand on the cold stones. She felt a little stupid, talking to a heap of rocks on a hill. She did not care very much.
When she stood up, Oren was beside her. She had not heard him come. He was standing with his hands at his sides, looking at the stone she had put on.
"He had a family, probably," said Lyra. "Didn't he. They all do."
"Probably."
"I keep thinking about that. That there's a woman somewhere in the city, or a mother, or a little boy. And somebody's gone round to the house and knocked and said, I'm sorry. And she's asked how. And they've said, a girl did it. With a sword."
Oren did not say anything for a while.
"The first man I killed," he said, "was on the border, on a bridge, in the rain. I was nineteen. He was older. He had red hair. I never knew his name either." He looked at the cairn. "I used to think about him every night. For a year. Then every week. Then I stopped."
"Is that supposed to make me feel better?"
"No," said Oren. "I just thought you should know it stops. And that you should be sorry when it does."
She looked at him.
"That's an odd thing to say."
"I'm an odd man," said Oren. He reached out and touched the stone she had put on, with two fingers, very lightly. "Find out his name. Then you can stop."
They went down the hill on the south side, all of them. Kestrel first, and the rest behind.
Corin walked beside her. A hand's width.
The sun was going over toward the west now, and the shadows of the stones and the thorn trees were getting long and blue on the snow. A wind came up from somewhere and moved the snow on the tops of the walls, so that it blew off in thin white smoke. It was very quiet. Their boots crunched. Kestrel's spear went thock.
"Lyra," said Corin.
"Mm."
"Up there. On the hill. Before I looked down the other road." He was quiet for a few steps. "I felt the hand again."
She looked at him. "The small one?"
"The small one in the big one."
He had told her about it, going north. At the same place. A feeling he could not put a picture to. A small hand, a child's hand, held inside a big dry warm one, walking. No face. No voice. Only the hand. And he had not known, then, whose hand it was. Or whose the big one was.
"Is it the same?" she said.
"No." He frowned. "It's... last time I was the small one. I'm sure of it. I was the small one and somebody had my hand. Leading me." He looked down at his own gloved right hand, as if he could see it there. "This time it was the other way round."
"You were the big one?"
"I was the big one. And somebody little had hold of me. Very tight. The way you hold on when you're frightened you'll be pulled off." He stopped walking. "Somebody with very small fingers. Who kept looking back."
Lyra stopped too.
She did not have to ask who he meant. She could see it in his face. He was not sure. He did not want to say it, in case saying it made it go away. But he knew. They both knew. A boy of ten and a girl of six, with two braids, walking on a road. Before the blue wagon. Before anything.
The girl was kept. Her name is Elara.
"It's a memory," she said. "Isn't it. Not a feeling. A real one. Coming back."
"I don't know." He started walking again, slowly. "Vespera said some things come back. The ones that were taken badly. The ones that left a hook in." He shook his head. "I don't know if it's mine, or if I'm making it up because I want it. I can't tell the difference anymore. That's the worst of it, Lyra. I can't tell."
She walked beside him and did not answer for a long time.
"Write it down," she said at last. "Tonight. Write down that you don't know. And then if it comes again, write that. And if it comes again after that." She looked at the snow. "If you write it enough times, it doesn't matter whether you made it up. It's in the book. It's yours."
"That's not how memory works."
"It's how books work."
Half his mouth went up.
"My sister's in the city," he said. As if he were trying out the words. To see if they would hold his weight. "Somewhere. Kept."
"Yes."
"I don't know where. I don't know what she looks like. I don't know if she knows about me." He breathed out. "I don't know anything."
"You know her name."
"I know her name."
"Then that's where you start," said Lyra. "That's what Vespera would say. You start with the name. And you find the next thing."
"And then the next thing."
"And then the next thing."
They went on down the hill into the blue shadow, side by side, and in the bottom of the valley, a long way off still, she could see a thin gray thread of smoke going straight up into the cold still air. And then another. And then a line of them, close together, along a dark smudge that was a river.
Thistle Ford.
The Ford Post
They went by the top, the way the boy had said.
There was an old wall along the side of the hill above the village, so old that it was not much more than a long hump under the snow, with a stone poking out of it here and there like a bone. They walked along beside it in the last of the light, keeping low, with the village below them on the left. Lyra could see it all from up there. A double row of low houses on either side of a track, with thick turf roofs, so heavy with snow now that they looked like loaves rising in a tin. Peat smoke going up from every one, straight up, gray-brown, smelling of earth. Little square windows with yellow light behind them. A river at the bottom, black and fast, with ice along both banks, and the ford across it, marked by white posts. And at the top end of the track, bigger than the rest, the long hall, with its oak door facing the ford.
There were no Ash lamps. There had been no Ash lamps the last time either. The light in the windows was oil light, and candlelight, and firelight. Soft and yellow and moving. It was the first thing she had liked about the place, the last time. It was the first thing she liked about it now.
They came down the hill at the back of the last house, the one nearest the hall. It had a byre built on the end of it, low and long, with a loft over the top and a wooden ladder going up the outside. Lyra remembered the loft. She remembered the apple racks, and the frosted window, and the warm smell of the cows coming up through the floor. She remembered lying awake up there in the dark, listening to Corin breathe.
They stopped by the byre wall. Kestrel looked at Lyra.
Lyra went to the back door of the house and knocked.
She knocked softly. Not the knock. Only a knock. Three times, with her good hand. She heard someone moving about inside, and a chair scrape, and heavy steps on a stone floor. Then the door opened a crack, and light came out round it, and a face.
A big face. Broad and red, with a heavy jaw and pale blue eyes, and fair hair going gray, scraped back very tight. A big woman, in a brown wool dress with the sleeves rolled up over thick red forearms, and an apron with flour on it.
Hild looked at Lyra for a long time without saying anything.
Then she looked past her. At Kestrel by the byre wall, with her spear. At Oren. At Oda and Sef. And at Corin, a little behind the rest, with his hood down and the last of the daylight on the gray side of his face.
Hild breathed out through her nose. A long breath, like a horse.
"Well," she said. "Saints keep us."
And she opened the door wide.
She did not let them into the house. She took them round into the byre instead, and shut the byre door behind them, and lit a lantern that hung on a hook from a beam. The two cows were in their stalls at the far end, a red one and a brown one, chewing slowly and looking round at the newcomers with their big wet eyes. The byre was warm. It smelled of hay and cow and dung, and it was the warmest place Lyra had been in nine days.
"You can't go up to the hall," said Hild. "Not tonight. Not all of you."
"We know," said Lyra. "The boy told us. Davin. On the ridge."
"He's a good lad." Hild set the lantern down on a barrel. In its light her face looked hard and tired. "He should be in bed. He's been up on that ridge every day since they came, watching the south road. I've told him and told him." She looked at Corin. Then away from him. "You'll have seen the paper, then."
"Not yet."
"There's one on the hall door. Or there was. Old Tam pulled that one down the minute they'd gone, and Abbe made him go and stand in the river up to his knees for it. Not because he pulled it down. Because he did it where people could see him." She shook her head. "The one on the ford post, Abbe's had left. She says it's good for us to look at. To remember what they look like."
"I want to see it," said Lyra.
Hild looked at her.
"It's dark."
"I know."
"It'll keep till morning."
"I want to see it now."
Hild looked at her a while longer. Then she nodded, once, the way people nod when they have decided it is not worth arguing. She took the lantern down off the barrel.
"Just you," she said. "The rest of you stay in here. Don't go out the front. Don't go up the ladder till I come back. There's milk in the pail by the brown cow, it's this morning's, it'll be sour but you're welcome to it."
Corin had stepped forward.
"I'll come," he said.
"You will not," said Hild and Lyra at the same moment.
They looked at each other. Hild's mouth twitched at one corner. Corin stepped back.
The track through the village was trodden hard in the middle and deep at the sides. Hild walked down the middle of it with the lantern held low, and Lyra walked beside her, with her hood up and her scarf round her face. Doors were shut. Shutters were closed over the windows on the side of the track, so that the light only showed in thin yellow lines round the edges. Somewhere a dog barked, once, and somebody told it to hush. Somewhere else, a baby was crying, high and thin.
They walked past the hall. The big oak door was shut. Lyra could see, in the lantern light, the old carved sun on it, worn almost flat, and four bright little marks in the wood round the edge, in a square. Nail holes. Where the paper had been.
And under them, a dark wet patch on the step, where somebody had poured out a bucket of something and scrubbed.
"What's that?"
"One of the gray ones spat on the step," said Hild. "When Abbe wouldn't stand up. Spat right by her hand. Mags scrubbed it with lye after. Twice." She did not look at it. "We don't spit on that step."
They went on down to the river.
The ford was a wide shallow place where the river ran over stones, fast and black and very loud in the cold. There was ice along both banks, thick and white, and in the middle the water was open, and jumped and foamed over the stones, and threw up spray that froze on the ford posts. There were white posts on both sides, two on each bank, painted with lime, to show the edges of the safe way across. The paint was old and flaking. The nearest post on this side was taller than the others, and had a crosspiece at the top, like a little gallows, from which a bell hung. A small iron bell, for ringing when the river was in flood.
The paper was nailed to the post under the bell.
Hild held the lantern up.
It was a big sheet, as long as Lyra's arm, printed in thick black letters on cheap gray paper. The kind of paper they used for notices in the city, that went soft in the rain and fell apart in a week. It had gone soft here, with the spray off the river. The edges had curled and frozen. One corner had torn away. But the middle was still there. And it was still easy to read.
At the top, in the biggest letters, it said:
BY ORDER OF THE COUNCIL OF VERIDIA
And under that, smaller:
UNDER THE OLD LAW OF PLAGUE AND FIRE
And under that, very big again:
A CLEANSING
Lyra read the rest slowly. The lantern swung a little in Hild's hand, and the light swung with it, and the letters seemed to move.
All persons who keep ink upon the skin, or who write in books not on the Ministry's list, or who take, keep, sell or burn memory without the Ministry's leave, are to come in to the nearest Ministry office to be weighed, with their books, needles and jars. They will be treated gently. All persons who shelter, hide, feed or help such persons are to come in with them. Those who come in freely will be treated kindly. Those who must be fetched will be treated as the law allows.
The Ministry thanks you for your help in keeping a clean house.
And under that, in a box with a thick black line round it:
SOUGHT
And a picture.
It was a woodcut. Black lines on gray. A young man's face, from the front, with dark hair, and a long straight nose, and a thin mouth. The boy had been right. They had drawn him too fat in the face. They had drawn him clean and young and ordinary, with both sides of his face the same. There was no gray. The eyes were wrong. But it was him. If you knew him, it was him. It had his chin.
Under the picture it said:
CORIN VANE. Once a Harvester of the Ministry. A thief of memory. Of no fixed home. Dangerous to touch.
And under that:
And those who walk with him.
Lyra stood and looked at it for a long time.
The river was very loud. The spray came off it in little gusts and pattered on the paper, and on her hood, and froze on her scarf. Behind her, Hild held the lantern up and did not say anything.
Dangerous to touch.
She thought about a man sitting in the snow inside a sheep pen with his hands lying open in his lap. Too tired to take off his own pack. I'm sorry. For being slow.
She found that she was very angry. It came up in her all at once, from somewhere low down, hot and sudden, like the cut. She wanted to tear the paper off the post. She wanted to tear it into little pieces and throw them in the river. She had her good hand up, halfway to the post, before she knew it.
She stopped.
Abbe says it's good for us to look at.
She let her hand down.
"When did they put it up?" she said. Her voice came out quite calm. That surprised her.
"Four days back. In the afternoon."
Lyra counted. Four days back from today. She counted it again, the other way, from the Glass, from the Eye, from the morning they had come down the stair and Kross had gone away south with his gray coat over his arm.
"That can't be right," she said.
"It's right. It was the day Mags's goat had twins. I'm not likely to get it wrong."
"Then they printed this before the Council met." Lyra looked at the big black words at the top. BY ORDER OF THE COUNCIL. "Kross was still on the Glass then. He hadn't even sent his report. Nobody in the city could have known yet what he would say." She looked at the date at the bottom of the paper, in tiny letters, half washed away. It had been printed in the city. In the Frost month. On a day before any report could have reached it.
"They hadn't decided anything," she said. "Not yet. Not when this was printed. And the riders were already on the road with it."
Hild was quiet.
"So somebody had decided already," said Lyra. "Before anyone asked the Council. Somebody had it all printed and nailed up and ready. And then he walked into the Council and asked them what they thought. And they said yes."
She heard her own voice go thin and hard at the end.
Hild lowered the lantern a little.
"I don't know about councils," she said. "We don't have one. We have Abbe." She looked at the paper. "But I'll tell you something, girl. A man who prints his answer before he asks the question isn't asking."
"No," said Lyra.
She took one last look at the face on the post. The wrong eyes. The right chin.
Then she turned her back on it, and they walked up the track together to the hall.
