Chapter 91: Snow on the Free Valleys, Part 2 of 5: The Sheepfold
The Sheepfold
The shelter in the corner of the fold was not much. Three walls of piled stone and the wall of the fold for the fourth, with a roof of old poles laid across and turf on top of them, and a low gap at the front to crawl in by. Inside it was dark and smelled of sheep, and of old wet wool, and of the cold earth floor. Somebody, a long time ago, had put down a layer of bracken on the floor, and it had gone brown and brittle and crackled when you knelt on it. There was room in there for four people lying down, if none of them minded the others' feet.
There were six of them.
Oda did not seem to think this was a problem. She crawled in first, on her hands and knees, and looked all round, and crawled out again, and said, "Good," in the voice of somebody who has been shown a fine room in an inn.
Then she built the fire.
She built it in the open, just in front of the gap, in the lee of the fold wall, where the wind could not get at it. She did it with almost nothing. A handful of dry heather she had been carrying inside her coat all day, against her body, to keep it dry. Some twigs off a dead thorn bush in the corner of the fold. A few old sheep droppings, dry as cork, that she picked out of the bracken inside the shelter and crumbled up between her fingers. And then a little lump of black stuff out of a pouch on her belt, that she said was peat from the south side of the Glass, and that she had been saving.
She struck sparks into it with a flint and a bit of iron. It took her eleven tries. Lyra counted. On the twelfth, a little curl of smoke went up out of the heather, and Oda bent down with her face almost in the snow and blew on it, very softly, the way you would blow on a baby's hair. And it caught.
Sef had gone out over the wall to look for more wood. He came back with an armful of broken thorn branches and a face full of snow, very pleased with himself. Oren went with the pot to the stream at the bottom of the slope and broke a hole in the ice with the pommel of his sword and filled the pot with water. Lyra watched him do it from the top of the fold wall. He knelt in the snow by the hole and lifted the sword in both hands, by the blade, with the hilt down, and brought it down on the ice. Once. Twice. A crack, and a black spurt of water.
He did not look at the sword when he did it. As if it were only a hammer. As if it had only ever been a hammer.
And Corin sat down.
He sat down on the snow just inside the gate of the fold, with his back against the wall, and did not get up again. He did not say anything. He did not take his pack off. He only sat there, with snow on his hood and his shoulders and his knees, and his gloved hands lying in his lap, open, palms up. And looked at nothing.
Lyra got down off the wall and went to him.
She knelt beside him in the snow. A hand's width. She looked at his face. The right side of it was gray-white now, almost the same color as the left side. His lips were blue. His eyes were open. They moved when she moved, so he was there. But they were slow.
"Corin."
"I'm all right."
"You're not all right. You're the color of a fish."
"A fish isn't a color." Half his mouth went up. "Fish are lots of colors. Some of them are red."
"You're the color of a dead one."
"That's fair."
She looked at his pack. It was a big one. Oren had been carrying it for him on the Glass, his and Corin's both, and had only given it back yesterday morning, when Corin said he could manage. It had the Ledger in it, in its wrapping of oilcloth. And his spare shirt, and some dried goat's meat from the Ashborn, and a padded wooden box with the jars in it, the little glass jars he kept the Ash in. And the honey jar from Silas, which was empty now.
"Take that off," she said.
He tried. He got one arm out of one strap and then stopped, as if he had forgotten what came next.
She did not touch him. She could not touch him. She took hold of the strap instead, where it went over his shoulder, through all the layers of his coat, and drew it down his other arm, slowly. The coat was between them. And her own glove, on her good hand. Two layers of wool and one of leather. She pulled the pack off his back and set it down in the snow.
He let her.
She sat down next to him, on the snow, with her back against the wall, a hand's width away. And the two of them sat there and watched Oda's fire grow, and Sef feed it twigs one by one, and Oren come back up the hill with the pot of water, walking slowly, carefully, with his shoulders bent forward, so as not to spill any.
"I'm sorry," said Corin.
"What for?"
"For being slow."
"You're not slow. You're tired." She pushed her bound hand down deep into her coat. "We walked over the Glass. We walked over the Glass twice. You burned half your life in the Eye. Of course you're tired."
"Kestrel saw."
"Kestrel sees everything."
"You didn't."
She did not say anything to that for a while.
"No," she said at last. "I didn't."
He turned his head and looked at her, the slow way.
"That's all right," he said. "I didn't want you to."
"Then that was stupid of you."
"Yes."
"Next time, you tell me. You say Lyra, I'm going to fall over. Out loud. With your mouth. Do you hear me?"
"Lyra, I'm going to fall over."
"Not now."
"I'm practicing."
She looked at him. He was not quite smiling. But the half of his mouth that could still do it was thinking about it.
She let out a long breath and looked back at the fire.
Oda put the pot on to boil. She did it on three flat stones she had set round the fire in a little triangle, so that the pot sat on them like a hen on a nest, with the flames coming up underneath. When the water was hot she put in a handful of oats and some of the dried meat cut up small with her knife, and a pinch of something gray-green out of another of her pouches that smelled, when the steam came up, like a hillside in summer. She stirred it with a stick. She hummed while she did it. The same tuneless humming.
Kestrel came and stood over them.
She had been at the far side of the fold, standing on a stone with her spear, looking south into the falling snow. Now she came across and stood over Corin, tall in her long hide coat and white fur cape, with the snow settling on the smooth gray skin of her bare head. She looked down at him for a long time.
Then she knelt in the snow in front of him. On one knee, with her spear upright beside her and both hands on the shaft.
"Heron," she said.
"Kestrel."
"Tomorrow, we walk slower."
"You don't have to."
"This one does not have to do anything," said Kestrel. "This one chooses." She looked at his face. At the gray, going up into the corner of his mouth. "The city is not going anywhere. The city has been there a long time. It will be there in four days. It will be there in six."
"Vespera said be quick."
"Vespera is not here." Kestrel's face did not change. But something round her eyes did. "And if Vespera were here she would say walk slower, you stupid boy, or you will fall down dead in a sheep pen and then what use will you be to anybody. And then she would hit you with something."
Corin laughed. It came out of him all at once, short and surprised. It was his own laugh, Lyra thought. Not the other one. Not the bark like a fox that had come out of him at the Ashborn camp, that was not his. His own. Small and dry and a little rusty.
Kestrel looked pleased. That is, her face did not move at all, but she looked pleased.
"We walk slower," she said again. And got up, and went back to her stone.
They ate the porridge out of the pot, passing it round, each of them dipping in with the one wooden spoon they had and then passing it on. It was thick and salty and hot. Lyra burned her tongue on the first mouthful and did not care. When it came to Corin he held the spoon in his gloved hand and looked at it for a moment before he ate. Then he ate, slowly. He ate four spoonfuls, which was more than he had eaten in two days, and then passed it on to Oren. And Oren took the spoon and turned it over in his big fingers, and frowned at it, and dipped it in, and ate.
Lyra watched him do it. He did not say anything. He never said anything about that. He did not have the word for it anymore.
Afterward, when the pot was empty and Oda had scrubbed it out with snow, and Sef had crawled into the shelter to sleep, and the fire had burned down to a red heap, Corin took the Ledger out.
He unwrapped the oilcloth. He undid the strap. He opened it on his knees to the last page with writing on it, and took the pencil out of the spine. And he sat with the pencil over the page in the red light, for a long time.
Then he wrote. Slowly. In big square capitals. Pressing hard.
Lyra did not look. She had promised herself she would not look at the Ledger, ever, unless he asked her to. She looked at the fire instead. She listened to the pencil. It made a small scratching sound, and stopped, and scratched, and stopped. She could tell where the words ended by the stops.
When he had finished he sat for a while with the book open. Then he turned it on his knees, toward her, and held it a little way out. Into the light.
She looked.
SUNDAY. THE SHEEP PEN.
LYRA'S MOTHER HAD A GARDEN WITH A RED BRICK WALL. ROSES UP THE WALL THAT SMELLED LIKE TEA. MINT IN POTS BECAUSE IT IS A BULLY.
SHE CAME IN TO DINNER WITH DIRT ON HER HANDS AND LEAVES IN HER HAIR. HER NAME WAS ISOLDE.
SHE SANG IN THE GARDEN.
I WAS GOING TO FALL OVER AND I DID NOT TELL LYRA. NEXT TIME, TELL HER.
She read it twice. Then she looked up at him.
"Is it right?" he said.
"Yes," said Lyra. Her voice did something strange in the middle of the word, and she had to stop and swallow. "Yes. It's right."
He nodded, and turned the book back, and shut it, and did up the strap.
The Boy on the Ridge
Lyra took the first watch, and Oren took it with her, because Oren always did.
They sat side by side on a flat stone by the gap in the fold wall, with a blanket round both of them and the red heap of the fire at their feet. The snow had stopped again. The clouds had pulled apart in the north, and through the gap there were stars, very small and very sharp, and under them, low down on the edge of the world, a faint pale line that was the Glass catching the starlight. Behind them in the shelter the others were asleep. She could hear Sef snoring, a small whistling snore like a kettle a long way off. She could hear Oda talk in her sleep, once, a few words in the Ashborn tongue, quick and cross, and then nothing.
She could not hear Corin at all. She never could. He slept as if he were trying not to take up any room.
Oren had his sword across his knees again. He was not sharpening it. He had his hands resting on it, on the flat of the blade, one on each side of the hilt.
"Can I ask you something?" said Lyra.
"You're going to."
"Does it hurt?"
He did not ask what. He looked down at the sword on his knees.
"No," he said. "That's the strange part. It doesn't hurt. I thought it would. When you lose something, it hurts. When my mother died it hurt. When I gave up the colors it hurt like a broken arm." His thumbs moved, a little, on the steel. "This doesn't. It's like a room in a house that you don't go into anymore. You know it's there. You walk past the... you walk past the way in, every day. You just don't go in."
She heard him stop on the word. She heard him go round it. She did not say anything.
"And sometimes," he said, "I hear somebody moving about in there."
"When you watch me."
"When I watch you." He nodded slowly. "I see your foot come up and something in that room says, back foot. Clear as anything. As if there's a man in there still, doing his drills. And I say it out loud before I know I'm going to." He was quiet. "I don't know who he is. But he's a better swordsman than me."
"He's you."
"He was."
She pulled the blanket tighter round her shoulders.
"Kross could have taken it from me too," she said. "The cut. He had his hand on me. He was looking right at it. I felt him looking. And he left it."
"I know."
"Why?"
Oren did not answer for a long time. The fire ticked and settled.
"When I was a boy," he said at last, "there was a man in my father's company. An old sergeant. He had the cut. Not as well as my father. But he had it. And when my father died, the sergeant went round to the young ones in the yard, one at a time, and showed them. Over and over. Because he said a cut that's only in one man is a cut that dies with him." He moved his thumbs again. "Kross took it from me. He had to. I was in the way. But a cut that's only in Kross..."
He did not finish.
"You think he left it in me on purpose," said Lyra. "So it wouldn't die."
"I don't think anything about Kross," said Oren. "Nobody does. That's how he likes it."
They sat a while longer. A star fell, low in the north, a short white scratch on the dark. Neither of them said anything about it.
"My brother's in the city," said Oren.
She turned her head. He was looking at the fire.
"Harlan," he said. "You saw him. On the bridge, in the autumn, with the boys and the round things they beat, and the carts with the hooks. He's got the whole battalion now. The river side, at the old Gate Barracks." He breathed out. "On that bridge he told me never to knock in his city. Never do that here, he said. And then he knocked back at me. With one finger. On the stone. Where nobody could see."
"You never told me that part."
"No."
"Why are you saying now?"
Oren looked at her. His face in the red light was square and tired and very still. There was frost in his short gray beard.
"Because they've put up papers in the free valleys," he said, "with his face on them," and he tipped his head back toward the shelter, toward Corin. "And if they're putting up papers, they'll be sending men. And if they're sending men, they'll be sending the best they've got. And the best they've got is my brother."
"You don't know they've put up papers."
"No," said Oren. "Not yet."
They found out the next morning.
It was a fine day, cold and clear, with a hard blue sky and the sun low in the south, very bright on the snow. It hurt to look at anything. Oda tore strips off the bottom of her shirt and showed them how to tie them over their eyes with a thin slit cut in the cloth to see through, the way the Ashborn did on the Glass in summer. Through the slit the world was a narrow white line with a blue line on top of it.
They walked slower, the way Kestrel had said. She did not make a thing of it. She only went a little shorter at each step, and stopped a little more often, on the tops of the rises, to look round. And when she stopped, they all stopped. And Corin leaned on the nearest stone, or wall, or thorn tree, and breathed, and nobody said anything about it.
In the middle of the morning they came over a ridge and saw sheep.
They were in the valley bottom on the far side, a long way off, a gray-white smudge against the white, moving slowly along the edge of a stream where the wind had blown the snow thin and there was a little brown grass showing through. A great many of them. And on the ridge across the valley, standing on a rock against the blue sky with a long crook in his hand, there was a boy. And beside him, sitting very straight, a black-and-white dog.
Lyra knew him. She knew the shape of him, thin and straight, and the way he stood with his weight on one hip and the crook out in front of him like a flag. She had seen him before. Going north, on the way to the Glass. He had stood on a ridge like that one and watched them go past, and looked at Corin's hands.
"Davin," she said.
The boy had seen them. He stood very still on his rock for a long moment. Then he lifted the crook over his head, high, and waved it from side to side. Once, twice.
And then he came down off the rock and came toward them. Running, across the valley, through the snow, with the dog running beside him and in front of him and round him in circles. He went straight through the middle of the sheep. They parted in front of him like water and closed up again behind.
He came up the slope on their side of the valley at a run and stopped in front of Kestrel, breathing hard, with his cheeks red and his breath going up in clouds. He was about twelve. He had a sheepskin coat on, with the wool inside, and a knitted hat pulled down over his ears, and big boots that had been somebody else's. The dog sat down at his feet and looked up at all of them with its pink tongue out, very interested.
The boy looked at Kestrel. At the spear. At the gray line over her bare head. He did not seem afraid. Only careful.
Then he looked past her, at Corin.
He looked at Corin for a long time. At his face. At the left side of it, gray, from the collar to the corner of the mouth. At the gloves on his hands.
"You came back," said the boy.
"We came back," said Corin.
"Nobody comes back." The boy said it plainly, the way you would say it's cold. "My granda says. The gray ones go north and that's it. That's the last you see." He looked at the gray on Corin's face again. "You've got more of it."
"Yes."
"Did it hurt?"
"Not much," said Corin. "Not the gray. Other things."
The boy nodded slowly, as if that made sense to him. Then he seemed to remember something, and turned to Lyra, because she was the one he had decided was in charge, the way people often did.
"Don't go to the ford by the road," he said. "Go by the top. By the old wall. Come down behind Abbe's."
"Why?"
"There's a paper on the ford post." He looked at Corin again, quickly, and away. "Men came. Four days back. On horses. Three in gray, and one in black with a hat. They came in by the south road, by Haverstock, and they nailed paper up on the post at the ford and on the door of the hall. And they read out what it said. Out loud. In the yard. They made everybody come out and listen." His face went tight. "Granny Abbe wouldn't come out. They went in and got her. And she still wouldn't stand up. She sat on the step."
"What did the paper say?" said Lyra.
The boy chewed his lip.
"Something about a cleaning," he said. "A Cleansing. And about anybody who's got ink on them, or keeps old books, or does what he does." He nodded at Corin's gloves. "Taking. Without the Ministry. That they've got to be brought in. And anybody who hides them has to be brought in too. And there's a picture."
Nobody said anything.
"It's him," said the boy, to Lyra. "It's not very good. They've drawn him too fat. And they didn't know about the gray. But it's him."
Oren let out a long breath through his nose.
Kestrel looked down at the boy. "The men," she said. "Where did they go?"
"North a bit, to look at the Glass. Then they came back and went south again. The same day." He shrugged. "They didn't like the Glass. One of the gray ones was sick in the snow."
"Good," said Kestrel.
The boy looked at her in surprise. Then he grinned. It was a sudden grin, all teeth, and it made him look younger.
"That's what Granny Abbe said," he said.
Corin had not moved. He was standing a little behind Lyra, with his hands in his pockets and the strip of white cloth over his eyes, and his face had not changed at all. She could not see his eyes behind the cloth.
She turned to the boy.
"Thank you, Davin," she said.
The boy blinked. "How do you know my name?"
"You told us," she said. "Last time."
He thought about that. Then he nodded. "So I did." He looked at Corin. "Do you remember it? My name?"
Corin was quiet for a moment.
"I wrote it down," he said. "So, yes."
The boy seemed to think this was a good answer. He whistled to the dog, a short sharp whistle with two notes in it, and the dog leaped up and went off down the slope like a black-and-white arrow. And the boy went after it, back toward his sheep. Halfway down, he turned round and waved his crook again.
Corin lifted his hand and waved back.
