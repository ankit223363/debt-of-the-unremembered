Chapter 91: Snow on the Free Valleys, Part 5 of 5: The Little One for Soup
The Little One for Soup
They went up to the hall in the dark before morning, the three of them. Lyra and Oren and Corin.
The village was asleep. The snow had stopped again in the night, and the sky had cleared, and it was colder than it had been since the Glass. The stars were out, hard and white, more of them than Lyra had ever seen over the city. The snow on the track squeaked under their boots. Their breath went up in clouds and hung there behind them, in the still air, like a line of ghosts following them up the hill.
The young man with the stave was at the hall door. He was asleep sitting up, with his back against the wall and his chin on his chest. He woke when they came up the step, all at once, and grabbed for his stave, and then saw who it was and let it go again and stood up, red in the face. He opened the door for them without a word.
Inside, the fire had been built up. Somebody had been awake for a while. The hall was warm and full of the brown smell of peat, and red light moved on the black beams and the old pikes and the faded banner.
Granny Abbe was in her chair.
She was not alone. The two old men from last night were on their bench, wide awake, with blankets round their shoulders. And there were others. A dozen or so. More came in behind Lyra as she stood there, quietly, one at a time, through the door: old men, old women, a few younger ones. Hild. The woman with the net. They did not say anything. They came in and stood along the walls under the pikes, and on either side of the long hearth, and looked.
They were looking at Oren.
Oren had stopped just inside the door. Lyra saw him see them all. She saw his shoulders go back and his chin come up, the way they always did when there were a lot of people looking at him. A soldier's way of standing. He did not know he did it.
"Captain," said Abbe from her chair. "Come here."
Oren walked down the hall to her. Slowly. Past the people along the walls. He stopped in front of the chair. He was very big, standing there in front of her. Abbe had to tip her head right back to look up at him, and the black cap slid back on her white hair.
"Stand under it," she said.
Oren looked at her. Then he looked up, over the hearth. At the banner.
He went and stood under it, on the far side of the fire, with his back to the wall. The torn brown cloth hung over his head and a little to one side, and the light from the fire came up from below, and the faded yellow sun on it seemed to move.
Abbe looked at him for a long time. Everybody did. Nobody said a word.
"Yes," said Abbe at last. Very quietly. "Yes. You've the look."
The old man with the long white beard had stood up off the bench. He was holding his blanket round himself with both hands. He was crying, without any sound, the tears going down into his beard.
"My father carried that," he said to Oren. His voice shook. "That cloth. From the Ford to the Eye. At the back, with the pikes. He was fifteen." He wiped his face. "And he carried it back. The ones who came back. There weren't many. He said she sent them back. She stood on the steps of that place with her sword and sent them home. Every one of them she could spare."
Oren did not answer. He stood under the banner and looked at the old man, and his face, the square tired face with the scar from the temple to the mouth, was doing something Lyra had never seen it do. It was not quite crying. It was the face of a man holding something very heavy very still.
"She was my father's sister," he said. His voice was rough. "Sola. I didn't know. Not till the Glass."
"We knew," said Abbe. "We knew the minute your girl said your name. The first time." She moved her pipe. "We just didn't say. In case."
"In case of what?"
"In case you were the other kind of Vance," said Abbe simply. "The city kind. There's been a few."
Oren looked at her for a long moment. Then something in his face eased. Only a little.
"There's been a few," he said. "Yes."
Abbe nodded. Then she turned her head and looked past him. At Corin, who had stayed back by the door with his hands in his pockets and his hood down, as if he were trying to be part of the wall.
"And you," she said. "Gray boy. Come here where I can see you. My eyes are as old as the rest of me."
Corin came down the hall. He stopped in front of her chair, where Oren had stood, and she looked up at him. At the left side of his face. At the gray.
"Give me your hand," she said.
Corin hesitated. He looked at Lyra.
"With the glove on," said Abbe. "I'm old, I'm not stupid."
He held out his left hand, the gray one, in its glove. Abbe took it in both of hers. Her small brown hands came out of the rolled-up red sleeves and went round his, and held it. She did not look at it. She looked at his face.
"Cold," she said. "Like a stone in the river. That's what the little one said."
And from somewhere along the wall, there was a giggle.
Lyra looked round. There, in the shadow by the hearth, half behind Hild's skirts, was a small girl in a nightgown with a shawl over it and two fair plaits. She had her hand over her mouth. Her eyes were very big. She was looking at Corin.
"Wenna," said Hild. "Bed."
The girl did not go to bed. She came out from behind Hild's skirts, very fast, before anybody could stop her, and ran across the rushes in her bare feet, and stopped in front of Corin. She held something up. Something small and brown and round, with a hole in the middle.
A dried apple ring.
Corin looked at it. Then he looked at the girl. And Lyra saw his face change.
"Eat it," said the girl. "Not keep it."
"I ate the last one," said Corin.
"I know. Granny said." She pushed it at him. "So here's another one."
He took it in his right hand. His glove. He held it up and looked at it in the firelight, as if it were something very rare. Then he put it in his mouth, the whole thing, and ate it. Slowly. Chewing.
The girl watched him do it with great satisfaction. When he had finished she nodded, once, very firmly, like a cook when the cake has risen. And then Hild came and picked her up under the arms and carried her away, and she went without fighting, waving at Corin over Hild's shoulder.
Corin waved back.
Abbe let go of his hand.
"Well," she said. "You'll do." She leaned back in the chair. "Now. The Hill girl."
Lyra came forward.
Abbe put her hand into the front of the old red coat, under the one brass button, and felt about in there for a while, and brought something out. She held it in her lap for a moment, in both hands, and looked at it. Then she held it out.
It was a spoon.
A wooden spoon. Small. A child's spoon, for porridge, or soup, no longer than Lyra's hand from the wrist to the tip of the middle finger. It was made of some pale wood, pear perhaps, or apple, and it had gone the color of honey with age and handling, and very smooth. The bowl of it was worn thin at one side, where somebody had scraped it, over and over, along the bottom of a bowl.
And on the handle, carved very small and very fine, there was a tree.
Lyra took it and held it close to the firelight to see. A little tree, with a straight trunk, and roots going down, and branches going up and out. And the tops of the branches came together, at the very end of the handle, into a shape. Points. A row of little points, like the teeth of a comb, or the top of a crown.
A tree, with a crown on it.
She did not know what it was. She knew she had seen it before. Somewhere. In some book in her father's library, perhaps, or carved on some old house on the Hill. She could not think where. It tugged at her, like a word on the tip of the tongue.
"Where did you get it?" she said.
"Found it," said Abbe. "In the snow. By the ford post. Some winters back." She moved the pipe from one side of her mouth to the other. "A cart went through in the night. Going north. Fast. A closed cart, with the cover tied down, and two horses, and they didn't stop at the ford, they went straight through it in the dark with the water up to the axles. I heard it from my bed. Everybody did. And in the morning there was this, in the snow on the far bank. As if somebody had dropped it out of the back." She looked at the spoon in Lyra's hand. "Or thrown it."
Lyra was very still.
"I kept it," said Abbe, "in case somebody came back for it. Nobody did." She looked past Lyra at Corin. Only for a moment. Then back. "I'm giving it to you, Hill girl. Not to him. You understand me? You keep it. You give it to him when he's ready for it."
"How will I know?"
"You'll know." Abbe shut her eyes. "Or you won't, and then it won't matter."
Lyra closed her hand round the spoon.
She turned. Corin was standing a little way off, by the hearth. He was looking at her hand. At the spoon in it. His face had gone very still, the way it went when something was pulling at him from far off, a long way down. Like his feet at the Two Sisters. Like the rod.
He did not come closer. He did not ask to see it. He only looked.
And then Oren came round from the far side of the fire and stopped beside her and looked down at the spoon too, in her open hand. He frowned at it. He bent closer.
"That's nice," he said. "That's nicely made." He touched the little carved tree, very lightly, with the tip of one big finger. "For a child. A little one for soup." He frowned again. "What do they call that? The little one for soup?"
Nobody answered him.
Lyra wrapped the spoon in the end of her scarf and put it inside her coat, against her shirt, over her heart, where she could feel it. Small and hard and warm.
The Loft
Kestrel said they would leave at noon.
She said it in the byre, when they came back from the hall in the gray first light, and she said it in the voice she used for things that were not going to be talked about. Not before noon. The heron would sleep. They would all sleep. And at noon they would go on south, by the top road, the way the boy had told them, and be at Haverstock by the next night. Slower. But there.
Nobody argued. Oda yawned and climbed the ladder to the loft and lay down on the boards in her coat and was asleep before anybody else had got up the ladder at all. Sef went after her. Oren sat down at the bottom of the ladder, in the byre, with his back against the wall and his sword across his knees, and said he would take the first watch, and nobody argued with that either.
Lyra went up the ladder last.
The loft was as she remembered it. Low, so that even she had to bend her head under the roof beams, and long, running the whole length of the byre. The floor was rough boards, with gaps between them, and through the gaps the warmth came up from the cows below, and the smell of hay. There was hay in heaps along one side. Along the other side there were the apple racks: wooden frames with slats, like shelves, one above the other, where Hild kept the apples through the winter. They were mostly empty now. A few wrinkled apples were left on the bottom rack, small and red-brown and soft, and they filled the loft with a sweet old smell like cider.
At the far end there was the window. One small square window, with four little panes of thick greenish glass, and frost on all four of them in feathers and ferns. The first light was coming through it, gray and pale, and lying on the floor in a square.
Corin was sitting in the square of light, with his back against the end wall, and the Ledger on his knees.
Lyra had thought he would be asleep. She had thought he would fall asleep standing up, after the hall. He had looked like it, walking back down the track. But he was sitting up, with the book open and the pencil in his hand. He was not writing. He was looking at the window.
She went and sat down on the boards, near him. A hand's width. Her back against the apple rack. She took her hood down and shook the frost out of her hair. It was getting long again. Not long like it used to be, long enough to sit on, before she sold it. But down past her ears now. It got in her eyes.
They sat there for a while without saying anything. Below them the cows shifted in their stalls. Somewhere in the hay Oda was snoring, very softly, a little clicking snore.
"I want to see it," said Corin.
She had known he would say it. She had known since the hall. She put her hand inside her coat, on her shirt, over the spoon, without meaning to.
"Abbe said not yet."
"Abbe doesn't know me."
"She knew enough to know you'd ask."
He turned his head and looked at her. The slow way. In the gray light from the window his eyes looked very pale.
"It pulled," he said. "In the hall. When she took it out. Lyra, it pulled like the road did. Like the rod did. I felt it come all the way up my arm, and I don't even know what it is. I haven't even seen it properly. Only a bit of wood in your hand."
"I know."
"So I want to see it."
She was quiet for a moment.
"What happens if you do?" she said.
"I don't know."
"Do you think it's Ash? Do you think something's in it?"
"No. It's only wood. You can't put Ash in wood." He shook his head. "But it doesn't have to have anything in it. Things don't have to have anything in them to pull. You only have to have something in you, that knows them." He looked down at his gloved hands on the Ledger. "That's what Vespera said. Some things leave a hook in. Even after they're taken. And when you see the thing again, the hook pulls."
"And then what?"
"And then something comes up."
"Or it doesn't."
"Or it doesn't."
"Or something comes up," said Lyra, "and it's the wrong thing. Or it's half a thing. Or it's a thing that hurts so much you go out the next morning and burn something, to make it stop." She looked at him. "Your hands shook for an hour after the rod. And you never even touched it."
He did not answer that.
She had not seen the rod. But she had seen his hands afterward. Under the Eye, he had gone down the bunker stair alone, with a lamp, to the place at the bottom where Ferrand died. And he had come back up white as paper, with the lamp swinging, and both his hands shaking. The gray one and the other one. There was a rod lying at the foot of the stair, he said. A silver rod, the length of a man's forearm, with a twist down it like a stick of barley sugar. Turning to the left. Malakor's rods had always turned to the right. He had not touched it. He had left it there in the dark. And his hands had shaken so hard that he could not hold the pencil to write it down, and had to try three times. MY HANDS SHAKE WHEN I SEE IT. I DO NOT KNOW WHY.
And under that: FIND OUT.
"That was different," he said at last.
"How?"
"I don't know. It was." He rubbed his gloved thumb across the page of the Ledger. "The rod was... I was frightened of the rod. I didn't know why, but I was. The spoon isn't like that. It's not frightening." He frowned. "It's like the hand. On the hill. It's like somebody's hand."
Lyra closed her own hand over the spoon through her shirt. She could feel the shape of it. The thin bowl. The little carved tree.
"Then it'll keep," she said. "If it's a good thing. A good thing will keep. Till you're not so tired. Till we're somewhere warm, and you've slept, and eaten more than half a bowl of anything." She looked at him. "Till you're ready."
"And who says when I'm ready?"
"Me," said Lyra. "Abbe said. So, me."
He looked at her for a long moment.
Then half his mouth went up. It was not quite a smile. It was a tired, crooked, gray-edged thing. But it was there.
"All right," he said.
"All right?"
"All right. You say when." He looked down at the Ledger. "I'll write it down. So I don't keep asking."
He wrote. She did not look. She looked at the window instead, at the frost on the glass, at the light coming slowly whiter. She listened to the pencil. Scratch and stop. Scratch and stop.
When he had finished he did not turn the book to show her. He closed it on the pencil and sat with his hands on the cover.
"What did you write?" she said, before she could stop herself.
He did not seem to mind.
"Abbe gave Lyra a spoon. It has a tree on it. It pulled. Lyra says when. Do not keep asking." He paused. "And then, I want to keep asking."
She laughed. She did not mean to. It came out of her short and surprised, the way his had come out of him in the sheepfold, and she put her good hand over her mouth to keep it in, because of the others sleeping.
He looked pleased.
They sat in the gray light a while longer.
"Lyra," he said. "When we get to the city."
"Saint Bride's," she said. "Vespera said. The crypt. Gideon's people. We go there first, and we wait for her, and she tells us what she's found."
"And then?"
She did not answer at once.
She thought about the city. About the paper on the ford post, and the riders on the road with it before anyone had decided anything. About the Hill, and the cream house where her aunt lived, with every curtain drawn. About her brother, Rowan. Sixteen, and alone in it, with all of their father's papers and nobody to tell him what to do with them. About a list of names coming to the Bank every week, and somebody at a desk ticking them off.
She thought about a valley of old people too poor to count.
"And then we find your sister," she said. "And we find out about the rod. And we find out who printed that paper before he asked. And we stop him."
Corin was quiet for a long time.
"That's a lot of things," he said.
"It's one thing," said Lyra. "I think it's all one thing. I don't know how. But I think it is."
He turned his head and looked at her.
"You sound like Vespera," he said.
"Vespera's usually right."
"Don't tell her that."
"I'm not stupid."
He smiled. The half smile. Then he let his head go back against the end wall of the loft and closed his eyes.
"Wake me at noon," he said.
"You won't sleep."
"I'll try."
"That's not the same."
"No," he said, with his eyes shut. "It's not."
But he did sleep. Lyra watched him do it. She watched his breathing go slow, and his hands go loose on the cover of the Ledger, and his head go a little sideways, against the wall, so that the gray side of his face was in the light from the window and the other side was in shadow. She watched the frost on the window. She watched the square of light on the floor move, very slowly, across the boards toward the apple racks, as the sun came up somewhere outside.
She did not sleep.
She sat against the rack with her knees drawn up and her hand inside her coat, on the spoon, and she thought about a closed cart going through the ford in the dark, fast, with the water up to the axles. Going north. Toward the Edge. And something small and wooden falling out of the back of it into the snow.
Or being thrown.
She thought about the tree on the handle. The tree with the crown on top of it. And she tried, for a long time, to remember where she had seen it before.
She could not.
Blue Hands Went By
Hild woke them a little before noon, with bread and hot milk, and a message.
She came up the ladder with the jug in one hand and the bread under her arm, and set them down on the boards, and stayed at the top of the ladder with only her head and shoulders in the loft, like somebody looking over a wall. She waited while they ate. Then she said:
"I forgot. Last night. With all the fuss." She looked at Corin. "There was a woman came through. Two days back. Before you."
Corin had a cup halfway to his mouth. He stopped.
"Small," said Hild. "Old. Gray hair cut short, all anyhow, like she'd done it herself with a knife. And her hands blue. Right up to here." She touched her own red wrist. "Blue like a jay. And writing all up her arms, I saw it when she pushed her sleeves up to drink. And a big man with her. Very big. Slow-spoken. Ate like a horse."
"Vespera," said Lyra. "And Brin."
"Didn't give names. Didn't stop. Came in by the top, same as you, at dusk, and knocked on my back door, and ate what I gave them standing up in my kitchen, and were gone again before full dark. South. Fast." Hild sniffed. "She asked me if I'd seen a gray man go north, some time back, with a girl and a captain. I said I had. She said you'd be coming back this way, and when you did, I was to tell you something."
"What?" said Corin.
Hild screwed up her face, remembering it exactly.
"Tell the heron to be quick," she said. "And tell him not to be stupid. He'll know which one matters more."
Nobody said anything for a moment.
Then Oda laughed, in the hay, very loudly, and slapped her own knee. Sef grinned. Even Kestrel's mouth moved.
Corin put his cup down. He looked at Lyra.
"Which one matters more?" he said.
"The second one," said Lyra.
"I thought you'd say that."
"Then why did you ask?"
"I wanted to see if Vespera and you thought the same." Half his mouth went up. "You do. It's frightening."
Hild went back down the ladder, shaking her head.
They left at noon, the way Kestrel had said.
They did not go out by the track. They went out the back, behind Hild's byre, and up the hill to the old wall, the way they had come in. Hild came with them as far as the wall. She had a sack for them. More bread. A lump of hard cheese wrapped in a cloth. A twist of salt. A dozen of the wrinkled apples from the loft, which she said were no good for anything but she could not bear to throw away. And the little clay pot of goose fat and yarrow, which Corin already had, but she pushed another one into Oda's hands anyway, in case.
At the wall she stopped.
She looked at them all, one at a time, the way she had looked at them when she handed out the bowls. Counting them out, Lyra thought, the way she had counted them in.
Then she looked at Corin.
"If you see my brother," she said. "Under there." She did not say where. She did not need to. "Tell him Hild says he's a great fool. And to wait for me."
"I will," said Corin.
Hild nodded, once. She turned and went back down the hill to her byre, very fast, without looking back, with her red arms folded tight across her chest. They watched her go in. The door banged.
They went on along the old wall.
The day was gray again, and still, and not so cold. The sky had come down low over the hills. There was no wind at all. The smoke from the village went up behind them in thin straight lines and spread out under the clouds. Up ahead, at the end of the old wall, the top road went on south along the side of the hill, a line of thorn trees and broken walls going away into the gray.
They had walked for perhaps a quarter of an hour when they heard the whistle.
It came from the ridge on their right. High and sharp. Two notes. The same two notes Davin had whistled to his dog.
Kestrel stopped dead. Everybody stopped.
Lyra looked up at the ridge. She saw him. The boy. Standing on his rock against the gray sky with his crook, and the dog beside him. He was not looking at them. He was looking south. Down the valley. At the road.
He whistled again. And this time he lifted his crook and pointed with it. Down. At the south road. Twice.
Kestrel said something in the Ashborn tongue. It was short. And all at once Oda and Sef were down on the ground behind the old wall, flat in the snow, and Kestrel was down too, on one knee, with her spear laid flat beside her. And Oren had Corin by the back of the coat, through the coat, and was pulling him down. And Lyra went down after them. On her front, in the snow, behind the hump of the old wall, with her chin on the stones and the cold coming up through her coat into her stomach.
She lifted her head, very slowly, until she could just see over the top.
Below them, a long way down, the south road came up the valley from Haverstock. A white track between two lines of black walls. And on it, coming toward the village at a walk, there were horses.
Four of them. And a cart.
Three of the riders were in gray. Long gray coats, flat gray caps. One of them carried a long pole with something on the end of it. A little flag, she thought, or a lamp. The fourth rider was in black, at the front, very upright in the saddle, with a tall hat. And behind them came the cart. A small closed cart, with a black cover tied down tight over a frame, pulled by a single heavy horse. There was a man on the box, in gray, with a whip.
On the side of the black cover, painted in blue, there was a hand.
An open hand. Fingers spread. As big as a cartwheel.
Lyra did not know what it meant. She felt Oren go very still beside her.
"What is it?" she whispered.
Oren did not answer at once.
"The Spire," he said at last, very low. "That's the Spire's mark. The open hand. The church's." He was quiet. "I've seen those carts in the city. Not often. At night, mostly. Going up the Spire Road."
"What are they for?"
"They say they're for the Choir." His voice had gone flat. "The quiet ones. Children, mostly. Ones that can't speak right, or won't. Or that have had too much taken. The Spire takes them in. Gives them a home. Teaches them to sing." He breathed out through his nose. "That's what they say."
Lyra looked down at the cart. At the blue hand.
She thought about a little girl with two fair plaits, running in bare feet across the rushes with an apple ring held up in her hand. Eat it. Not keep it. She thought about a whole valley of people too poor to be worth counting. And a young man in black, leaning down to an old woman on a step. Mother, you have more than you know.
She felt her body begin to move before she knew she had decided anything. Her knees came up under her. Her good hand went to her belt, where there was no sword.
A hand came down on her back. Flat. Heavy. Through her coat. And held her there, pressed down in the snow.
Kestrel's.
"No," said Kestrel. Very softly.
"They're going to take them."
"We do not know that."
"They've brought a cart."
"A cart can be empty." Kestrel did not take her hand away. "A cart can come to count. A cart can come to frighten. This one does not know which. Neither do you." Her tea-colored eyes were very calm. "Look."
Lyra looked.
The riders had come up to the ford. They stopped there, by the post with the bell on it, where the paper was. The man in black got down off his horse. He went and looked at the paper on the post for a while, with his hands behind his back. Then he turned round and looked up the track. At the village. At the hall.
And the hall door opened.
Lyra could just see it from up here. A small dark shape came out of it, very slowly, and stood on the step. And then sat down. On the step. In the snow. Very small. With something red about it. A red coat.
Granny Abbe.
She sat on her step, the way she had sat before, and did not get up. And then, one by one, out of the houses along the track, other people came out. Old men. Old women. Hild, with her red arms. The woman with the net. The young man with the stave, without his stave. They came out and stood in their doorways, along both sides of the track. Not doing anything. Not saying anything. Only standing there, in the snow, in a long double line, looking down the track at the riders by the ford.
And very faintly, so faintly that at first Lyra thought she was making it up, there was singing.
It came up the hill on the still air. One old voice, high and cracked and thin. Then another, under it. Then more. A slow song. A very old one. Lyra did not know the words. She did not know the tune. It went up and down like the hills, and round and round like a wheel, and it did not stop.
The man in black stood by the ford and looked up the track at them.
He stood there a long time.
Then he turned and said something to the gray riders, and one of them got down and went to the cart and untied a corner of the black cover and took out a book. A big book, with a black cover. And a stool, folding, the kind clerks carry. And he set the stool down in the snow by the ford post, and sat on it, and opened the book on his knees. And the man in black took a pen out of his coat.
"They're counting," said Oren, very low. "They've come to count."
"This time," said Lyra.
"This time."
Kestrel took her hand off Lyra's back.
They lay there behind the old wall in the snow, all six of them, and watched the man in black walk slowly up the track toward the hall, with his pen, between the two lines of silent people. And the singing went on. And the old woman in the red coat sat on her step, very straight, and did not get up.
Lyra put her hand inside her coat and closed it round the spoon.
She had told Abbe something would happen. In the city. Something to keep them busy at home. She had said it without knowing she was going to, from somewhere low down. And Abbe had pointed her pipe at her and said, Then go and make it. And be quick about it.
She slid back down behind the wall.
"Let's go," she said.
Kestrel looked at her. Then she nodded. And they went on south along the top road, keeping low behind the walls, one behind the other, with the old song following them a long way through the still gray air. Until the hill came between them and it, and they could not hear it anymore.
Corin walked beside her. A hand's width.
He did not say anything for a long time.
Then, very quietly, so quietly that she nearly did not hear it, he said: "Hall. Ford. Bell. Abbe. Wenna. Apple."
And after a moment: "Spoon."
Lyra looked at him sideways.
"You said it," she said.
"I said it." He did not look at her. "I've still got it. The word." He walked on a few steps. "Somebody should have it."
She did not say anything to that. She did not need to. She walked beside him through the snow, with the spoon warm against her heart, toward the city.
