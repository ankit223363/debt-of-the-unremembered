Chapter 34: What Lord Thorne Taps, Part 1 of 5: Hay
Hay
Lyra Thorne had never forked hay in her life until four days ago, and now she was good at it.
Not very good. Hessa was very good. Hessa could stick the long wooden fork into a stack, and turn it, and lift a load as big as herself up over her head and onto the barge in one smooth swing, without seeming to try. Lyra could not do that. But she could lift a load half as big. And she could do it again, and again, and again, all morning, without stopping, which was more than she could have done on Monday.
She had blisters on both hands. They had broken on Tuesday and bled on Wednesday, and Mrs. Hobb had bound them in strips of linen soaked in something that smelled of pine, and now they were hard and yellow and did not hurt so much. Her back hurt. Her shoulders hurt. There was hay down the back of her neck and inside her boots and in her hair, which she had tied up in a knot with a bit of string, like Hessa's. There was hay, she was quite sure, in her ears.
She did not mind.
It was something to do. It was something to do with her hands that was not tapping, and not waiting, and not lying awake under the canvas in the dark, listening for a knock on the hull.
The farm at Brindle was on the hill above the wharf. It was a big farm, with three great barns of gray stone roofed with slate, and a yard in the middle where the carts came and went, and a farmhouse with smoke coming out of all four of its chimneys. Below the barns, on the slope going down to the canal, were the stacks. Lyra had counted them on Tuesday. Thirty-one. Big round towers of hay, taller than a house, each one with a pointed cap of thatch on top to keep the rain out, so that they looked like a village of giants' huts standing in the frozen grass.
There were only nine left now.
All morning, the farmer's men had been pulling them down. They climbed up the sides of the stacks on long ladders and cut the thatch off, and then they forked the hay down onto the backs of big flat carts, and the carts came creaking down the frozen ramp to the wharf, pulled by heavy brown horses with feathery feet and steam coming out of their noses. And on the wharf, the bargemen and their wives forked the hay off the carts and up onto the barges, and trod it down, and roped it under the canvas.
It was loud. It was cold. It was very ordinary.
The men on the carts shouted at the horses. The bargewomen shouted at the men. A boy with a dog ran up and down the landing stage, getting in everyone's way, until somebody's mother caught him by the ear. On the barge next along, the thin gray woman in the red shawl, Nan, was singing a song about a sailor and a mermaid, very loudly and very badly, and every time she got to the chorus the men on the carts joined in with words that were not the words of the song. Lyra did not understand all of them. She understood enough.
She forked hay and listened, and it was like listening to a different country.
She had grown up on Crown Walk, in the High Quarter, in a house with forty rooms. She had grown up with servants who spoke only when spoken to, and parties where nobody ever said what they meant, and a governess who had taught her to sit with her back straight and her hands folded and her face showing nothing at all. She had never in her life heard a woman shout across a wharf that another woman's husband had the brains of a turnip, and the other woman shout back that at least her turnip came home at night.
She liked it. She had not expected to.
"Lyra."
She looked up. Hessa was standing at the top of the load on the deck, with a fork in her hands and her feet sunk to the ankles in the loose hay.
"Slower," said Hessa.
"What?"
"Slower. You're working too fast. You look like you're in a hurry." Hessa stuck her fork into the hay and leaned on it. "Nobody on this wharf is in a hurry. It's Friday. We're paid by the load, not by the hour, and the farmer's men are paid by the day, and nobody wants to finish before Saturday, because then they'll have to go and find more work." She looked at Lyra. "Slower."
Lyra looked along the wharf. At the bargemen forking hay off the carts, slowly, and stopping to talk, and stopping to blow on their hands, and stopping to watch the boy with the dog. At the farmer's men on the stacks, who were sitting on the top of one with their legs dangling over the edge, passing a bottle.
She slowed down.
Hessa nodded and went back to treading the hay. She had not slept, Lyra thought. Her eyes were red-rimmed and her face was gray under the windburn. But she had been up at first light with the rest of them, forking hay, as if nothing had happened. As if an old man had not been taken away in a closed cart. As if a dog had not been shot.
Lyra had heard. She had been in the cabin, with Mrs. Hobb, when Hessa came down the ladder. Hessa had not said anything. She had just sat down at the little table with the tide book in her hands, and Mrs. Hobb had looked at her face and put the kettle on without a word, and after a while Hessa had said, very quietly, Jory. Once. And Mrs. Hobb had put a hand on her shoulder. And that had been all.
Now she was treading hay.
Lyra forked another load. Slowly. She lifted it, and swung it up onto the deck, and Hessa caught it with her own fork and spread it out.
Below her, on the edge of the wharf, Oren was forking hay off a cart.
He did not do it slowly. He could not. It was not in him. He stuck the fork into the hay as if he were sticking it into an enemy, and heaved, and the load came up off the cart and flew through the air and landed on the deck with a great soft whump, and Hessa had to jump out of the way. The farmer's man driving the cart looked at Oren, and then at the empty cart, and then at Oren again, and said something to the man next to him, and they both laughed.
Oren did not laugh. He did not seem to hear them. He was looking up the wharf, toward the cart ramp, toward the road that came down from the farm. He had been looking at it all morning, between every load. Lyra had watched him do it.
He was waiting for gray coats.
She did not know if they would come. Nobody did. Hessa said they would not, not today, not to a hay wharf where seven crews were watching. Oren said they would, and that when they did, there would be no warning. And Corin—
Corin was asleep.
He had gone to sleep at last, about an hour after the fog lifted, sitting on the upturned bucket by the cabin door with the Ledger on his knee. Just gone, all at once, the way a candle goes out. Mrs. Hobb had found him there, and said oh, the poor lamb, and between her and Oren they had carried him down the ladder and put him in the cupboard bed, under the bright patchwork quilt, with Wat snoring on the sacks below. He had not woken up. He had not even moved. Lyra had gone down at the mid-morning bell to look at him, and he had been lying exactly as they had left him, on his back, with his mouth a little open and one hand on his chest, over the place where the Ledger lived.
She had stood and looked at him for a long time.
He looked younger asleep. That was the strange thing. Awake, he looked older than he was. Twenty-eight, he had told her once, but she would have said forty. There were lines round his eyes and at the corners of his mouth that should not have been there yet, and gray at his temples, and a way of holding himself very still, as if he were always listening for something. Asleep, all of that went away. He just looked tired. And thin. And young.
And there were scratches all over his face from the brambles at Jory's, and the cut on his hand had opened again in the night and bled into the quilt.
She had thought about the moment on the deck, in the fog. When she had come out from under the canvas and he had looked at her, and for a breath, for less than a breath, there had been nothing in his eyes. Nothing at all. As if he were looking at a stranger.
Then he had known her. She had seen it come back. It had come back so hard that he had stumbled.
She had not said anything about it. She did not know what she would have said. For a moment you didn't know me. He knew that. She had seen that he knew.
She forked another load of hay. Slowly.
The little folded wrapper was in his pocket, she thought. The one from Ro. I will. She had given it back to him to keep. She had told him it was because he kept things. And that was true. But there was another reason too, which she had not told him, and which she had not quite told herself until now, with the fork in her blistered hands and the hay down her neck.
She had given it to him so that he would have a reason to remember her.
Keep this one for me. Until I see him. Then you can give it back.
As if a scrap of paper could do that. As if anything could.
She lifted the fork.
Below her, on the wharf, Oren had stopped working. He was standing very still beside the empty cart with the fork in his hands, looking up the road.
Lyra turned to look.
But it was only the farmer. A big red-faced man on a gray cob, riding slowly down the ramp to see how the loading was going. He waved to the bargemen. The bargemen waved back. Somebody called out a joke about his horse, and he called back a worse one about their barges.
Oren let out his breath. He stuck his fork into the next cart.
And Lyra went back to work, slowly, the way you were supposed to on a Friday, and tried not to count how many loads of hay were left before Saturday, and how many hours, and how many knocks on the hull she would lie awake listening for before then.
The Thing You Sit On
At the noon bell, everybody stopped.
It happened all along the wharf at once, as if somebody had blown a whistle. The farmer's men climbed down off the stacks. The carts stood still at the bottom of the ramp, and the big brown horses put their noses into nosebags and blew steam out of the sides of them. On every barge, people sat down where they were, on the hay or the cabin roofs or the edge of the deck, and got out bread and cheese and cold sausage wrapped in cloths, and bottles of cold tea, and ate.
On the Patient Grace, they ate on the cabin roof.
It was the only flat place on the deck that was not covered in hay. Hessa spread a piece of old sailcloth on it, and Mrs. Hobb came up the ladder with a basket, and they sat round it in a ring with their legs hanging over the edge. Hessa, and Mrs. Hobb with Robin in her lap, and Lyra, and Oren. Wat was still asleep below. So was Corin.
There was a round brown loaf, and a lump of hard yellow cheese with the rind on, and two raw onions, and a jar of pickled walnuts that Mrs. Hobb had brought from Pruett's and was very proud of. Hessa cut the bread with her clasp knife, in thick slices, against her chest, the way bargewomen did. She cut the cheese. She cut the onions into quarters and handed them round, and everybody ate them raw, with the bread, and Lyra's eyes watered so badly that she could not see, and nobody laughed at her except Robin.
Robin laughed at everything. He was a fat, solemn baby with a round red face like his mother's and a fluff of brown hair, and he sat in Mrs. Hobb's lap chewing on a crust and watching each of them in turn with great serious eyes. And then, quite suddenly, for no reason that Lyra could see, he would laugh. A loud, wet, delighted shriek, with his whole body. And then he would go solemn again, and chew his crust.
"He's laughing at your onion face," said Hessa.
"I haven't got an onion face."
"You have. Look at her, Robin. Look at her onion face."
Robin looked at Lyra. He laughed.
Lyra wiped her eyes on her sleeve and found that she was smiling. It hurt a little, like using a muscle she had not used for a long time.
Oren did not smile. He sat at the end of the sailcloth with his bread in one hand and his onion in the other, not eating either, looking up the wharf toward the cart ramp.
"Eat," said Mrs. Hobb to him. "You'll be no use to anybody with an empty belly."
Oren looked at the bread in his hand as if he had forgotten it was there. He took a bite. He chewed. He swallowed. Then he put the bread down on the sailcloth and turned to Hessa.
"We should go today," he said.
Hessa went on cutting cheese.
"This afternoon," said Oren. "After the noon break. While the carts are coming down and everybody's looking at the hay. We cast off quietly. We go north. Withy Reach. You said four days."
"I said no," said Hessa.
"You said it to Vane."
"I'm saying it to you."
Oren's jaw moved under the beard. "Mistress Doon. The man who followed Vane up the Long Pond has had a whole night to think about it. By now he has told Kross. By now Kross knows that a harvester went through a sluice under a lock-keeper's garden and came out above the Coldwater lock. And Kross can read a map as well as you or I. There are only so many places a man can go on this canal, north of Coldwater, in a little boat, in the dark, in one night." He stopped. "Brindle is one of them."
"So are six others," said Hessa.
"Then he will search all six."
"Then let him." Hessa put down her knife. "Let him come. Let him search seven hay barges one at a time with a fork, in front of seven crews who already think he's taken an old man and shot a dog for nothing. Let him try." She looked at Oren. "I'll tell you what he'll find. He'll find a cold welcome and a lot of hay."
"And under the hay?"
"He won't get that far."
"You don't know that."
"I know the river," said Hessa Doon. "I know these people. You don't."
They looked at each other across the sailcloth.
Lyra put down her bread.
She had seen Oren angry before. She had seen him angry at men twice his size, and at her father's creditors, and at her aunt's footmen, and once, when she was twelve, at a horse that had kicked a stable boy. She had seen him angry at her. But she did not think she had ever seen him like this. Not shouting. Not red. White. With his big scarred hands lying very still on his knees, and the old crack of the scar down his cheek standing out pale against the beard.
He was afraid, she thought. That was what it was. Oren Vance was afraid.
"Then we take my lord off the barge," said Oren. "Tonight. My lord, and Mrs. Hobb, and my lady. I will carry him. There are farms. There are barns. There is a whole country out there that is not this canal."
"Carry him where?" said Mrs. Hobb gently. "In this cold? An old man who can't walk, who can't feed himself, who doesn't know his own name?" She shook her head. "He'd not last three nights in a barn, Captain. You know that."
Oren did not answer.
"And who would feed him?" said Mrs. Hobb. "Who would wash him? You?"
"If I must."
"And where would you sit him, when you fed him? On the frozen ground?"
Oren opened his mouth.
Then he closed it again.
Lyra saw it happen. She saw him reach for something, a word, and find nothing there. She saw his face change. Not much. A small, quick tightening round the eyes, like a man who has put his foot on a stair in the dark and found that it is not there.
"I would find him a—" said Oren.
He stopped.
He looked down at the cabin roof under him. At the sailcloth. At the place where he was sitting. His hand moved, a small, helpless movement, as if he wanted to point at something and did not know what.
"A thing," he said. "To sit on. With legs. Wood. A back to it." He stopped again. His voice had gone very quiet. "You know what I mean."
Nobody said anything.
Robin, in Mrs. Hobb's lap, stopped chewing his crust and looked at Oren with great solemn eyes.
Lyra knew what he meant. Of course she knew. She had known since Pruett's. Since the night he had gone out into the yard alone, with his sword, to meet the four men who came over the wall. Since he had put up his hand and said a word she did not hear, and the air had gone hard in front of them like glass, and the club with the nails in it had broken against nothing.
She had asked him afterward what he had paid for it. And he had told her. Two words. Chair. And spoon. He had said them to her one last time, carefully, so that he would know he had said them, and then he had burned them.
And now he did not have them.
He knew there was a thing with four legs and a back that you sat on. He knew what it looked like. He had sat on hundreds. But there was nothing to call it. The word had gone, and left a hole the exact shape of itself, and every time he came to the edge of the hole he fell in.
"A chair," said Mrs. Hobb quietly.
"Yes." Oren did not look at her. "That."
He picked up his bread again. He looked at it. He did not eat it.
"My lady," he said. Not to Hessa. Not to Mrs. Hobb. To Lyra. "You decide."
Lyra looked at him.
"It is your father," said Oren. "It is your house. What is left of it." His voice was steady. "I have served the Thornes for twenty-two years. I served your father when he was a young man, and I will serve you. If you say we stay, we stay. If you say we go, I will carry him on my back to the end of the world, and I will find him a—" The smallest pause. "A chair to sit on when we get there."
The wharf was very loud around them. Nan on the next barge had started singing again. A horse was stamping. Somebody was laughing.
And on the cabin roof of the Patient Grace, it was very quiet.
Lyra looked at Hessa. Hessa was looking at the sailcloth, at her knife, at the cheese. Not at Lyra. She was not going to ask. Lyra understood that. Hessa had saved her father's life, and taken him into her home, and put her own baby in danger every hour of every day to do it, and she was not going to ask for one thing in return.
Lyra looked at Mrs. Hobb, with Robin in her lap.
She looked at Oren.
Then she looked down at the deck. At the great mound of hay, roped down under its canvas. And under it, she knew, in the little plank room in the middle, her father was lying on his back with his gray eyes open, tapping.
"We stay," said Lyra Thorne.
Oren did not move.
"We stay," said Lyra again. Her rough voice shook, and she made it stop shaking. "Hessa is right. If we run, they'll know we ran. And Father can't run. He can't go anywhere. He can barely—" She stopped. "We stay. We load hay. Slowly. Like everybody else." She swallowed. "And if they come, Oren, then they come. And you'll be here."
Oren looked at her for a long moment.
Then he bowed his head. Just a little. The way he used to bow to her father, in the great hall on Crown Walk, when her father gave an order he did not agree with.
"My lady," he said.
He put the bread down on the sailcloth, uneaten, and stood up, and climbed down off the cabin roof, and went back along the deck to the wharf. And Lyra watched him pick up his fork and stick it into the next cart as if he were sticking it into an enemy.
Nobody said anything for a while.
Then Hessa picked up her knife, and cut a slice of cheese, and put it on a slice of bread, and put a pickled walnut on top of it, and held it out to Lyra.
"Eat that," she said. "Then help me with the canvas."
She said it once.
And Lyra, who had learned in four days on a hay barge what that meant, took it and ate it.
