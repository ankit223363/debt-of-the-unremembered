Chapter 34: What Lord Thorne Taps, Part 2 of 5: Canvas
Canvas
The canvas sheets were old and stiff and heavy, and there were nine of them.
They had to come off the load and go back on again every afternoon, Hessa said, because the hay underneath had to be trodden down and the new hay laid on top and the whole thing roped tight before the dew came, or it would sweat and go moldy and the stable masters in the city would pay half price for it. So Lyra and Hessa went along the deck from the bow to the stern, one sheet at a time, folding each one back, treading the hay under it, and pulling it forward again.
It was hard work. Harder than the fork. The canvas was thick as a horse blanket and stiff with old tar and cold, and it did not want to fold, and it did not want to unfold. Lyra's blistered hands burned. Her arms ached to the shoulder.
Hessa did not talk for the first four sheets.
She worked the way she did everything, quickly and neatly and without waste, and Lyra did her best to keep up. They pulled, and folded, and trod, and pulled. Below them, on the wharf, the carts came and went. Oren forked hay. Nan sang. The light began, very slowly, to go out of the afternoon.
At the fifth sheet, Hessa stopped.
It was the one over the hole. The tunnel. Lyra could see the lifted corner of the lower sheet, where the hay was packed hard round the mouth of it. Hessa stood on the load above it with her hands on her hips and looked down at it. Then she looked along the wharf. At the carts. At the farm lane going up the hill. At the north road beyond it, a pale line across the frozen fields.
"I was nine," she said. "When I went in for Davy."
Lyra stopped pulling.
"I've told it a hundred times," said Hessa. "Everybody on the river's heard it. Little Hessa Doon and the lock. My father used to make me tell it in taverns, for the drinks." She was not looking at Lyra. "I always tell it the same. The water. The gates open. All the men on the side, shouting. Me going in." A pause. "I never tell the part before."
Lyra waited.
"I stood there," said Hessa. "On the edge. With all the men. For a long time. Longer than I say." She looked down at the hay. "I could see him in the water. Going round and round. His red hair. I could see him and I stood there and I thought, somebody will go in. One of the men. Somebody big." She was quiet. "And nobody did. And he went under, and came up, and went under again. And I thought, if I go in, I'll die. I'm small. I'll die. And I stood there."
"But you went," said Lyra.
"I went." Hessa shrugged. "In the end. When I saw that nobody else was going to." She bent and took hold of the edge of the canvas. "That's the part I never tell. That I stood there. That I waited for somebody else, first. That I very nearly didn't."
She pulled. Lyra pulled with her. The heavy sheet came forward over the hole and lay flat.
"Jory knew," said Hessa. "He was on the other side of the lock. He saw me stand there." She trod the canvas down along the edge, very hard, with her heel. "He never once said. Sixteen years. Not once. He just greased that wheel every Sunday and let me through his garden and asked after Robin."
She stopped treading.
"And now they've got him," she said. "In a cart. Because I asked him for a thing, once, when I was a girl, and he said yes, and he never stopped saying it."
Lyra did not know what to say. So she said nothing. She stood on the cold hay in the thin afternoon light and looked at the small square woman beside her, and waited, the way Corin waited. The way you waited when someone was holding something very heavy and might put it down.
Hessa did not put it down.
She picked up the edge of the next sheet instead.
"I want you to promise me something," she said.
"Anything."
"Don't say anything." Hessa looked at her for the first time. Her eyes were red-rimmed and very steady. "You don't know what it is yet. Never say anything before you know."
Lyra swallowed. "What is it?"
Hessa looked along the deck. At the cabin. At the tin chimney with its thread of smoke. At the small yellow window, where, inside, Mrs. Hobb would be sitting by the stove with Robin in her lap.
"If they come," said Hessa. "The gray coats. If they come up that ramp one day, or down that canal, or across those fields. And there's a fight, and there's fire, and there's no time." Her voice was perfectly level. "And I'm on the deck, and you're on the deck, and Robin's in the cabin."
"Hessa—"
"You don't stand on the edge," said Hessa Doon. "You don't wait to see if somebody bigger goes in first. You go in. You get Robin. You give him to Wat, or to Mrs. Hobb, or to anybody with two arms and a pair of legs that can run. And then you run." She held Lyra's eyes. "You don't come back for me. You don't come back for anybody. You run."
The wind moved across the canal. The canvas lifted a little under their feet and settled.
"And my father?" said Lyra. Very low.
Hessa did not look away.
"Your father's a grown man," she said. "Whatever's left of him. He had his life. Robin's had eleven months." A pause. "I'd go back for your father. Me. That's my business. That's my choice to make, and I'll make it. But you get Robin out first. Before anything. Before me. Before him. Promise."
Lyra stood very still.
She thought of her father under the hay, a few feet below them, tapping on a red square with white stars. She thought of Oren by the canvas with his sword. She thought of Robin, laughing at her onion face.
"I promise," she said.
Hessa looked at her a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and bent, and took hold of the edge of the sixth sheet.
"Good," she said. "Pull."
She said it once.
They pulled.
They did the sixth sheet, and the seventh, and the eighth, and the ninth, and neither of them said anything else. And when they were done, and the whole load was roped down tight under the canvas for the night, and the light had gone thin and gold over the frozen fields, Hessa climbed down off the hay without a word and went into the cabin, and Lyra heard her say something to Mrs. Hobb in a perfectly ordinary voice about the price of chicken.
Lyra stayed on the load.
She stood there for a long time, on top of the hay, above the hidden room where her father lay. And she held the promise in her mind the way she held her five worn coins, turning it over and over, feeling its edges.
Then she climbed down and went to find Mrs. Hobb, because it was nearly the third bell, and her father would want his broth.
Five Coins
At the third bell after noon, Mrs. Hobb took a bowl of broth into the hay, and Lyra went with her.
She did it every day. Twice a day, at noon and at dusk, and once in the night if he was awake. She could not do much else for him. She could not wash him, because he did not like it when she did, and became restless and frightened and pulled away from her hands, though he let Mrs. Hobb do it without a murmur. She could not shave him, because her hands shook. But she could hold the bowl. And she could talk to him while Mrs. Hobb fed him, so that he would hear a voice he might know.
It was warm in the hay room. Warmer than the cabin. The hay all around kept the cold out like a great thick coat, and the little lamp on its hook made the plank walls glow gold. Mrs. Hobb knelt on one side of the mattress with the spoon. Lyra knelt on the other with the bowl.
The broth was made from the bones of a chicken that Hessa had bought from the farm, with barley in it, and carrots, and a great deal of pepper. It smelled wonderful. Lord Thorne did not seem to notice the smell. He lay on his back on the patchwork cover with his gray eyes on the ceiling and his right hand going tap tap tap tap on the red square with the white stars, and when Mrs. Hobb put the spoon to his lips, his mouth opened, the way a baby's does, without his eyes moving at all.
He swallowed.
Mrs. Hobb dipped the spoon again.
"There," she said. "There now. That's good. That's my lord." Her voice was soft and low and steady, the same voice she used for Robin. "Another one. There."
Tap tap.
Lyra held the bowl.
She did not talk. Not at first. She watched the spoon go up and down, and her father's mouth open and close, and the tapping finger, and she thought about the morning. About Corin's hand palm-up on the cover. About the long fingers coming down on it. About her father's head turning on the pillow, and the gray eyes finding Corin's face, and the person behind them coming all the way up to the window to look out.
At Corin. Not at her.
She was not jealous. She told herself that very firmly. She was not jealous of a man her father did not know. She was only—
She did not know what she was.
"He's eating well today," said Mrs. Hobb. "Better than yesterday."
"Is he?"
"Half the bowl, near enough. Yesterday it was a quarter." Mrs. Hobb wiped his chin with the corner of her apron. "Something's woken him a bit. You can see it. There's more color in him."
Lyra looked. She could not see any color. Her father's face was the same gray-white as always, the color of old paper. But Mrs. Hobb had nursed him every day for two years. Mrs. Hobb would know.
"Talk to him, love," said Mrs. Hobb. "He likes your voice. I can tell."
Lyra did not believe that either. But she tried.
"Father," she said. "It's Lyra. We're still at Brindle. We're loading hay. I forked forty-one loads this morning. Hessa says I'm getting better." She stopped. It sounded foolish, out loud. Her rough voice in the little gold room. "Corin's back. He's asleep. He's been asleep since the fog lifted. Mrs. Hobb says he'll sleep till dark."
Tap tap tap.
"And I heard from Ro."
She had not meant to say that. It came out.
"Rowan," she said. "Your son. He sent me two words. On a sweet paper. I will." Her voice cracked. "He read my letter, Father. He read it. I don't know what he's going to do. But he read it."
The gray eyes looked at the ceiling.
The spoon went up. The mouth opened. Swallowed.
Lyra looked down at the bowl in her hands.
She thought about her mother.
She did not know why. She did not often let herself. It was a thing she had learned to do very young, after the funeral, when the house on Crown Walk had gone quiet and the portrait had been hung in the long gallery and everyone had stopped saying her mother's name. You did not think about it. You folded it small, and kept it close, and did not take it out.
But she took it out now. Kneeling in the hay with the warm bowl in her hands.
She had five.
That was all. Five memories of her mother that were really hers. Not things people had told her, or things she had seen in the portrait, or things she had made up later out of wishing. Five things she had seen with her own eyes and kept. She had counted them, years ago, at the age of ten or eleven, lying awake in the nursery, the way a child counts the coins in a money box. And she had counted them so many times since that they had worn smooth.
One. Her mother's hands in her hair, by the nursery window, brushing it out at night. A hundred strokes. Her mother counting them under her breath in a language Lyra did not know. And the window open a little, and the smell of the river coming in.
Two. Her mother in the rain on Crown Walk, in a blue dress, with no hat, laughing, with her face turned up to it and her eyes shut. And Father on the steps behind her with an umbrella, saying Isolde, Isolde, you'll catch your death, and laughing too.
Three. The Midwinter table. The long table in the great hall, with every candle lit and the silver shining. And her mother walking all the way round it before the guests came, very slowly, touching the back of every chair. And stopping at one at the far end. An empty one. With a plate and a glass and a folded napkin set in front of it, the same as all the others. And her mother standing there with her hand on the back of it for a long, long time.
Who's that for, Mama?
For someone who couldn't come.
Four. A song. Her mother singing, low, in the next room, through a half-open door. Not a song Lyra had ever heard anyone else sing. A slow one, with a tune that went up and up and hung there, as if it were asking a question.
Five. The last one. Her mother kneeling down in front of her in the front hall, in a traveling cloak, with her gloves already on. Holding Lyra's face in both her gloved hands. Looking at her for a long time, the way you look at something you are trying to learn by heart. Saying something. Lyra had never been able to remember what. She had been very small. She remembered only the gloves, gray kid, cool on her cheeks. And the look.
And then the door, and the cold air, and the carriage.
And she had not come back.
Five coins. Worn smooth.
"Mrs. Hobb," said Lyra.
"Mm?"
"You knew her. My mother."
Mrs. Hobb's spoon stopped, halfway to the bowl.
"I did," she said, after a moment. "Not well. I came to the house to nurse your brother when he was born, and I stayed on after. She was—" She stopped. She dipped the spoon. "She was kind to me. Very kind. She was kind to all of us below stairs. Some of the great ladies weren't. She was."
"What was she like?"
Mrs. Hobb fed Lord Thorne the spoonful of broth. She wiped his chin. She seemed to be thinking.
"Quiet," she said at last. "Very quiet, for a great lady. She didn't go to the balls much. Your father would go, and she'd stay home and read to you two in the nursery. She liked the nursery best of all the rooms. Said it had the best window." Mrs. Hobb smiled a little. "She'd stand at that window by the hour, some evenings. Looking down at the river. Just standing. Like she was waiting for a boat."
Lyra looked at her.
"A boat?"
"That's what it looked like. To me." Mrs. Hobb dipped the spoon. "I asked her once. Being bold. I said, Are you expecting somebody, my lady? And she laughed and said, No, Hobb. I'm remembering somebody. And she wouldn't say any more."
The tapping finger came down on the red cloth.
Tap tap tap tap.
"She was from away," said Mrs. Hobb. "You know that, I expect. Not from Veridia. From somewhere over the water, in the west. She had an accent when she was tired. Just a little. And she couldn't abide the cold." She fed him the spoonful. "She used to say the winters here were the only thing she'd never forgive your father for." She smiled. "Teasing, of course."
Lyra did not say anything. She was holding the bowl very tightly.
She had known where. Only lately. Only since her aunt had said it, over the Marrowmere cups in the white drawing room, in a voice like a closing door: a girl from the canals, with a mask on her collar. But she had not known the rest. The accent. The cold. The window. Nobody had ever told her those things. In the house on Crown Walk, her mother had simply been Lady Thorne, and then she had been her late ladyship, and then she had been nothing. A portrait with its face to the wall, chalked Lot 41. Withdrawn.
Over the water. In the west.
I'm remembering somebody.
"There," said Mrs. Hobb. "That's the bowl done. Well done, my lord. Well done." She put down the spoon and patted his still left hand. "Now you have a nice sleep."
Lord Thorne did not sleep. His gray eyes stayed on the ceiling. His finger went on tapping.
Mrs. Hobb took the empty bowl from Lyra and went out through the tunnel on her hands and knees, humming.
Lyra stayed.
She sat back on her heels in the gold light and looked at her father, and thought about five worn coins, and a woman at a window, and a sixth thing she had not counted, because it was not a memory. It was a hole. A scratched-out name on a height chart in the nursery, beside lines that started at two and stopped at six. A chair at a table. A cup in a cupboard with a scratch on it, put away in the dark.
For someone who couldn't come.
"Father," she said quietly. "Who was she waiting for?"
The finger tapped.
She had not expected an answer. She did not get one.
One Word
She did not go out after Mrs. Hobb.
She should have. There was hay to load, and Hessa would want her, and Oren would be watching the road and pretending not to watch the barge. But she stayed kneeling on the planks beside the mattress, in the warm gold light, and did not move.
After a while she took her father's hand.
Not the still one. The tapping one. The right.
She had not done that before. She did not know why. It had always seemed to her that the tapping was his, the only thing that was still his, and that she should not get in its way. So she had always held the other hand, the left, the quiet one, and let the right go on.
Now she took the right. She lifted it off the red square with the white stars, gently, and held it between both of hers, the way Corin had held out his palm.
The fingers went on moving. In her hands. Against her palm.
Tap tap tap tap.
They were cold. They were always cold. Thin and dry and cold, like the twigs of a tree in winter, with the knuckles standing up like knots. She remembered these hands. She remembered them warm. She remembered them holding the reins of a gray horse in the park, and turning the pages of a book by the fire, and lifting her up, very high, so that she could touch the crystal drops of the chandelier in the great hall and make them ring.
Tap tap.
"Father," she said.
The long one. Against her palm. Seven. Eight. Nine.
Tap tap tap.
And the pause.
And in the pause, the fingers closed.
Lyra went very still.
They closed round her hand. Slowly. All four fingers and the thumb. Not tight. There was no strength in them. But they closed, the way a baby's hand closes round your finger, without being told.
She looked at his face.
The gray eyes had left the ceiling.
They were turning. Slowly. So slowly. Toward her. The long white hair sliding on the blanket. And she watched them come, and she did not breathe, and she did not dare to hope, because she had hoped before. Twice before, in two years. And both times the eyes had come round and looked straight through her, as if she were a window, and gone back.
They came round.
They found her face.
And there was somebody there.
She knew it at once. The way you know, in a dark house, that the person in the next room has woken up. Nothing changed that you could point to. The eyes were the same eyes. The face was the same face, thin and gray and sunk in on itself. But somebody had come up the long stair. Somebody was standing at the window. Looking out. At her.
At her.
"Father," she whispered.
The dry lips moved.
No sound came. Just the shape. She watched it. She did not know how to read lips any better than Corin did. But she had watched these lips all her life. She had watched them laugh, and say her name, and say Isolde in the rain.
They moved again.
And this time a sound came with them.
It was not much of a sound. A dry, cracked whisper, like a page turning in an old book, so faint that if the barge had creaked at that moment she would have lost it. A voice that had not been used in a very long time, and had forgotten how.
One word.
"Bourse."
Lyra stared at him.
The gray eyes held hers. The fingers held her hand. For one breath. Two. As if he were waiting to see if she had heard. As if he were making sure.
Then his lips moved again. No sound this time. Just the shape. And she thought—she was not sure—she thought it was her name. The first part of it. The L.
And then he went.
She saw him go. The way you see a lamp carried away down a long corridor. The somebody at the window turned, and went back down the stair, back into the dark, and the eyes were empty again. They stayed on her face a moment longer, empty. Then they began to turn back toward the ceiling.
The fingers loosened round her hand.
And began, against her palm, very softly, to tap.
Tap tap tap tap.
Lyra did not move for a long time.
She knelt there on the planks with his hand in hers and the tapping going on against her skin, and stared at his face, and felt her heart going so hard that she thought it would come out through her ribs.
Bourse.
The Memory Bourse.
She had been there once. Only once. When she was seventeen, the spring before everything fell apart. Her father had taken her, at night, in the closed carriage, with masks in a box on the seat between them. You should see how the world really works, he had said. Before you marry into it. She had been excited. She had thought it would be a ball.
It had been a market.
She remembered the dome. Green copper on the outside, like an old penny. But inside, under the copper, hung a second dome, all of glass, lit from behind by a thousand lamps, so that when you looked up it glowed like the inside of a lantern. And under it, a floor of black and white marble, and chandeliers, a hundred of them, hanging so low that the crystal drops brushed the tops of the ladies' feathers. And everywhere, people in masks. Silk masks, velvet masks, masks of silver and gold and painted paper. Walking slowly round long glass tables. Looking at what was on the tables. Bending close.
Vials.
Thousands of them. Small glass vials, each one stoppered with wax, each one glowing faintly from inside with a colored light. Blue. Amber. Rose. Green. Laid out on velvet in rows, like jewels in a jeweler's window. And beside each one, a small card, in a beautiful sloping hand.
A Summer at the Sea, Age Eight.
The Violin, Twenty Years.
First Kiss, Behind the Mill.
A Mother's Voice, Singing.
She had stopped at that one. She remembered. She had stopped and stood looking at it for so long that her father had come back for her and put his hand on her arm.
Whose was it? she had asked him.
Somebody who needed the money more than the memory, her father had said. That's always the answer here. Come away, Ly.
And she had come away. And they had gone home in the closed carriage, and she had not said anything all the way, and neither had he. And she had never gone back.
And a year later, he had burned his own mind down to the walls.
Bourse.
After two years of nothing. After two years of four, two, nine, three. After weeks without a single word, except once, at sundown, a name. He had come all the way up the dark stair, and found her at the window, and said the one word he had kept.
Not Lyra. Not Rowan. Not help me or I'm sorry or where am I.
Bourse.
She looked down at the thin hand tapping in hers.
"What's at the Bourse, Father?" she said. Her rough voice was very small. "What did you leave there?"
Tap tap.
She waited.
The long one.
The pause.
And then, from outside, through the hay, through the planks, faint and far off, she heard Hessa's voice, calling across the wharf. Not to her. To someone on the towpath. Sharp and loud and full of something Lyra had not heard in it all day.
"Juno? Juno, is that you? Get down here, you daft girl, before you freeze. Get down here!"
