Chapter 34: What Lord Thorne Taps, Part 5 of 5: Who Goes In
Who Goes In
They talked until the ninth bell.
They sat round the stove in a ring, the way they had sat round the sailcloth at noon, because there was nowhere else to sit. Lyra on the edge of the cupboard bed with the catalogue. Juno beside her, in the quilt, with her feet out of the basin now and wrapped in a dry blanket. Corin on the floor with his Ledger. Wat in the corner, saying nothing, with his good hand on Hessa's shoulder. Hessa on the bottom step. Mrs. Hobb on a stool by the kettle, with Robin asleep against her chest. And Oren, standing, because he would not sit, with his head bent under the beams and his arms hanging at his sides.
Corin did most of the talking. Lyra had not known he could talk so much. He talked the way he wrote, in short, plain, careful lines, one after another, and after each one he stopped and waited, as if he were giving it time to dry.
"The catalogue tells us the rules," he said. He opened it at the first page and laid it on the floor where they could all see. "Masks, all night. Every guest presented by a sponsor of the old houses. Viewing from the ninth bell. Bidding from the eleventh." He tapped the page. "So there are two ways in. The front door, as a guest, in a mask, on somebody's arm. Or the kitchen door. With this."
He put the black card down beside the catalogue.
ADMIT ONE. SERVICE.
"Servants," he said. "Porters. Footmen. Waiters. Girls to take the cloaks. Men to carry the lots up from the vaults. They don't need masks. They don't need sponsors. Nobody looks at them." He glanced at Juno. "Bridie proved that this morning."
"I know what you're thinking," said Lyra.
"I've been in the Bourse kitchen," said Corin. "Once. In the autumn. I went to the kitchen door and asked for a boy called Tam." His face did not change. "I don't remember much of it now. I've got it written down. Stone floors. A lot of copper. A stair going down to the vaults and a stair going up to the floor. Men in black coats with silver trays." He paused. "If I'm in a black coat with a silver tray, I can be anywhere in that building. Upstairs. Downstairs. In the room where they keep the fourth tier."
"And when somebody looks at your face?" said Oren.
"Nobody looks at a waiter's face."
"Vivienne will," said Oren. "Vivienne knows your face. So does her man Gant. So does every one of the four who came over Pruett's wall."
"Then I'll need a different face," said Corin.
Nobody said anything.
"Vespera," said Juno suddenly.
They looked at her.
"Vespera," said Juno again. "At Mother Grue's. In the arches. She went there the night you went into the sewer, with all her inks and her needles. Gideon says she's been doing faces. For the people down there. The ones the Inquisition's looking for." She sat up a little in the quilt. "Gideon says there's a man down there who was on a broadsheet last week, and now his own mother wouldn't know him. Vespera did it with ink and a bit of wax and some cotton in his cheeks." She looked at Corin. "She can do yours."
Corin was quiet for a moment.
"And masks," he said slowly. "For the floor. For whoever goes in at the front."
"Who goes in at the front?" said Hessa.
She had not spoken in a long time. She asked it plainly, the way she would ask how much a load of hay weighed.
Nobody answered.
Lyra looked down at the first page of the catalogue. Each guest to be presented by a sponsor of the old houses.
She knew who would go in at the front. She had known since she read the rules. She thought they all knew. Corin knew. She could see it in the way he was not looking at her.
"I will," she said.
Oren made a sound.
"I will," said Lyra again. "I'm the only one who can. I'm of the old houses. I'm a Thorne. It's the only thing I've got left that's worth anything, but it's worth this." She lifted her chin. "I've been there before. I know how to walk in a ballroom. I know how to wear a mask and say nothing and look at things on a table as if I could buy them all and couldn't be bothered. I was taught. It's the one thing I was taught."
"And your sponsor?" said Oren. His voice was very rough. "Who presents you, my lady? Your aunt?"
Lyra felt something cold go down her back. Like a draft from a shut door.
"No," she said. "Not my aunt."
"Then who? Every old house that's left in this city has closed its doors on you. Every one. You know that. You stood on their steps. You know." Oren's hands had made fists at his sides. "There is not one of them who would put your hand on their arm and walk you into that room."
"There's one," said Lyra.
She looked at the stove.
"Duke Marrow," she said.
The cabin went very quiet.
Oren stared at her. For a moment, Lyra thought he was going to shout. She had never heard Oren shout at her. Not once, in twenty years. She thought he might now.
He did not shout.
"Ansel Marrow," he said, very low, "bought your father's seat on the Council. For less than it was worth. On the day after the auction. He bought your father's debts from the Bank, every one of them, at a quarter of their face, and called them in, and took Crown Walk. He took the house. He took the furniture. He took the horses." His voice shook. "He took the gray mare your father gave you on your twelfth birthday. I saw his groom lead her out of our stable yard with a rope round her neck."
"I know," said Lyra.
"And you would go to him."
"He collects things," said Lyra. "Everybody knows that. He has a gallery. Other people's memories. Their best days. In glass." She made herself keep looking at Oren. "He'll want to see Lot 41. Of course he will. Every collector in the city will. And he'll want to be seen walking in with the last Thorne on his arm, because it'll be the talk of the Masquerade, and he likes to be talked about." She swallowed. "And he'll want something for it."
"What?"
"I don't know," said Lyra. "I'll find out tomorrow."
"Tomorrow?"
"There's no time, Oren. Ten days. I have to see him tomorrow. And Vespera tomorrow. And Gideon, if we're going down into the vaults." She looked at Corin. "Aren't we?"
Corin met her eyes.
"Yes," he said. "The bulk lots are in the lower rooms. Viewing by appointment. I want to see the lower rooms." His voice went flat again. "I want to see the crates."
"And how will you get to the city?" said Hessa. "Tonight? With every lock between here and the river watched?"
Nobody had an answer to that.
Then Wat spoke from the corner. For the first time all evening. His voice was low and slow and thick with whatever Mrs. Hobb had put in his tea.
"Ellis's wain," he said.
Hessa turned her head to look at him.
"Farmer's carter," said Wat. "Ellis. Takes the first load up to the city stables by the north road. Every Friday night, loading week. Leaves at the tenth bell from the top of the ramp. Gets to the Bridewell stables at dawn." He breathed. "Big wain. Six horses. You could hide a cow in that hay."
"No locks on the road," said Hessa slowly.
"No locks on the road," said Wat.
Hessa looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at Lyra. And Lyra saw something in her face that she had not seen before. Not fear, exactly. Something older. Something tired. The look of a woman who has spent her whole life on the water, watching people step off her boat onto the land, and not knowing if they would ever step back.
"Ellis owes me," said Hessa Doon.
"Everybody owes you," said Corin.
"Most of the river," said Hessa. She almost smiled. "I'll go up and speak to him."
She stood up.
"I'm coming with you," said Oren.
He said it to Lyra, not to Hessa.
Lyra looked at him. At the big scarred face, and the gray in the beard, and the hands that had made fists at his sides. She thought of him in the yard at Pruett's, alone, with his sword, against four men. She thought of the two words he had burned to keep her alive. Chair. Spoon. And of the thing with four legs and a back that he could no longer name.
"No," she said.
"My lady—"
"No." Her rough voice did not shake this time. "You're staying here, Oren. With Father."
He opened his mouth.
"If the gray coats come," said Lyra, "if they come up that ramp tomorrow, or the next day, or any day while I'm gone, somebody has to be here who will stand in front of the hay with a sword. Somebody who won't run. Somebody Father would know, if he could know anybody." She held his eyes. "That's you. That's always been you. You said it yourself. A captain's place is with his lord."
Oren said nothing.
"I'll have Corin," said Lyra. "And Vespera. And Juno, when her feet are better."
"My feet are better now," said Juno.
"They're not," said Mrs. Hobb, without looking up from Robin.
Oren stood under the low beams for a long time.
Then he turned, without a word, and went up the ladder into the dark. And Lyra heard his boots go along the deck, slowly, past the cabin window, to the side of the hay. And stop there.
And stay there.
She did not go after him. She knew where he was. He was standing by the canvas, over the hole in the hay, with his hand on his sword. Where he would stand, she thought, all night, and the next night, and every night until she came back.
If she came back.
A Letter to Ro
Hessa came back from the farm at half past the ninth bell, with frost on her shawl, and said only, "Tenth bell. Top of the ramp. He'll not stop, so be quick." Then she went down the ladder and began to cut bread and cheese and wrap it in cloths, for the road, without being asked.
That left half an hour.
Lyra sat down at the little table in the cabin, under the lamp, and took out her pencil.
She had not meant to. She had meant to pack. But there was nothing to pack. Everything she owned in the world was on her back: the too-big gray coat that had been Wat's, and a dress that had been Hessa's mother's, let down at the hem, and a pair of boots that had been nobody's in particular and pinched at the toes. Her own clothes were in a sack at Pruett's, or in a room on Crown Walk that belonged to Ansel Marrow now. Her mother's gray gown was at Madame Hesper's shop on the Hill. She had nothing to pack.
So she sat down, and took out her pencil, and a sheet of paper from the back of the tide book that Hessa had said she could have, and wrote.
She had always been able to write. It was the one thing she had never had to be taught. Her governess had taught her to sit straight and fold her hands and keep her face still, and Lyra had hated every minute of it. But writing had simply come to her, the way swimming comes to some children, or riding. She had been writing letters since she was six. To her mother, after the carriage, every week, for a year, until somebody told her to stop. To her father, when he was away. To Ro, at the Academy, every month for two years, though he never once wrote back.
It was how she talked to people. Properly. Not with her voice, which had always been rough, and which went rougher when she was frightened, and which never seemed to say what she meant. With her hand. On paper. Small and fierce and slanting hard to the right, the way it had always slanted, as if the words were leaning forward into a wind.
She wrote:
Ro,
I have your two words. I can't tell you how I got them. Someone is keeping them for me, someone careful, until I can see you and give them back.
She stopped. She looked at that for a while. Then she went on.
I don't know what you will. I don't need to. I know you will, and that's enough for now.
Ask. And watch. But don't let him see you watching. He watches people watching. He's very good at it. Better than you. Better than me.
She stopped again. Her hand was shaking. She put the pencil down and pressed her palms flat on the table until it stopped, and picked the pencil up again.
Father said a word today. Out loud. The first one in a very long time. He looked at me when he said it, Ro. He knew me. Only for a moment. But he knew me.
I won't write the word. I'll tell you when I see you.
I'm going to try to see you before Midwinter. I don't know how yet. I don't know where. I'll find a way. Wait for it.
I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm still not asking you to come.
But I'm asking you to be careful. Please. Be so careful. They took Old Jory this morning. You don't know who he is. He's nobody. He's an old man who kept a lock and kept bees and greased a wheel every Sunday for sixteen years. They took him away in a closed cart, and he sang the whole way.
Don't let them put you in a cart, Ro.
Fold it small.
Ly.
She read it through. Twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way Corin read things, turning every word over and looking at it from underneath. Was there anything in it that would hurt anybody, if somebody else read it? If it was opened at the gate, or taken from a pocket, or found under a mattress?
No places. No names but Jory, and Jory was already taken. No barge. No Brindle. No Bourse.
It would have to do.
She began to fold it.
She folded it in half. Then in half again. She was going to go on. Smaller. Smaller. The way her mother used to. The way she had folded every letter she had ever sent him, since she was little. Fold it small.
And then she stopped, with the paper half-folded in her fingers.
She was thinking of the little hard white square in Corin's palm, on the deck, in the fog. Soft at the edges. Torn at one corner. That's Kross's fold. And of the cadets at the Academy gate, Juno had said, all folding their papers into tiny hard squares, because the Inquisitor did, and they all wanted to be like him.
Isn't it strange, she had said to Corin, that they should both say the same thing?
She did not know why it frightened her so much. It was only a fold. It was only a thing people said. Half the mothers in Veridia probably said it to their children. It meant nothing.
But she did not fold it any smaller.
She opened it out again, and folded it in three instead, like an ordinary letter. Flat. Plain. The way a clerk folds a bill, or a servant folds a note to the grocer. The way nobody would ever notice.
Then she sat and looked at it on the table under the lamp.
"Juno," she said.
Juno was asleep. She had gone to sleep in the patchwork quilt by the stove, sitting up, with her head on Wat's good shoulder and her mouth open. Wat had not moved. He looked down at Lyra over Juno's head, very gravely, and did not wake her.
"When her feet are better," said Lyra quietly.
Wat nodded.
Lyra got up and put the letter into the deep inside pocket of Juno's grandfather's coat, where it hung steaming on its hook by the stove. Next to the brass key on its string. Right at the bottom, where Juno had carried the catalogue for fourteen miles and twenty-three bridges and three locks she had gone round through the fields.
She stood for a moment with her hand in the pocket.
Then she took it out, and turned round, and found Corin watching her from the floor by the stove, with his Ledger closed on his knee.
He did not ask what she had written. He did not ask why she had stopped folding it. He just looked at her, in the lamplight, with his tired eyes.
And she thought, he saw. He saw me stop.
And she thought, he'll write it down. Tonight, or tomorrow, in that small sloping hand. Lyra stopped folding her letter. She did not fold it small. And one day, perhaps, he would open the Ledger and read that line, and not remember why it mattered. And neither, perhaps, by then, would she.
"It's nearly the tenth bell," said Corin.
"I know."
"Do you want to see him first?"
Lyra looked at the curtain of sacking at the end of the cabin.
"Yes," she said.
Tapping Back
Oren was standing by the hay, as she had known he would be.
He did not move when she came along the deck. He stood beside the lifted corner of the canvas with his hand on the hilt of his sword and his breath smoking in the lantern light, looking out at the dark wharf and the darker canal. He did not look at her. But when she knelt down by the tunnel, he bent, without a word, and held the canvas up higher so that she would not catch her hair.
"Thank you," said Lyra.
He did not answer.
She went in on her hands and knees. Corin came behind her. The warm dark closed round them, and the smell of hay, and the creak of the planks under her knees, and then the gold glow of the little room.
Her father was awake.
He was lying exactly as he always lay, on his back, on the straw mattress under the patchwork cover, with his gray eyes on the plank ceiling. Mrs. Hobb had brushed his hair. It lay spread on the folded blanket under his head, long and white and soft. Someone had put a hot stone wrapped in flannel at his feet, under the cover. She could see the shape of it.
His right hand was moving on the red square with the white stars.
Tap tap tap tap.
Lyra knelt down beside him. Corin knelt at the foot of the mattress, a little way back, in the shadow, where the lamplight did not reach. She understood why. He did not want to get in the way. He did not want the eyes to come up the stair and find him again, instead of her.
Tap tap.
She took the tapping hand. Gently. Off the red cloth. Into both of hers.
The fingers went on, against her palm. The long one. Seven. Eight. Nine. The pause. Tap tap tap.
The long pause.
"Father," said Lyra. "I'm going away for a little while. Only a few days. I'm going into the city with Corin." She swallowed. "I'm going to the Bourse."
Nothing. The gray eyes looked at the ceiling.
The fingers began again. Tap tap tap tap.
She had thought about this all through the letter, without knowing she was thinking about it. She had thought about Moll's fingers on the Watch house desk. About Oren in the yard at Pruett's, a long time ago, before the fight, standing at the door of the white room and listening to the tapping on the wall, and saying, It sounds like the drums. The army drums. Harlan could have told you. About Corin, in the white room, the first time, putting his own hand flat on the wall beside her father's and tapping it back. Four. Two. Nine. Three. And her father's lips making a shape.
She had never done it. Not once. In two years.
She had been afraid to.
She did not know what she had been afraid of. That he would not notice. That he would. That he would stop, and never start again, and the last thing that was still his would be gone, and it would be her fault.
The fingers finished. Tap tap tap. The long pause.
And in the pause, Lyra turned his hand over.
Palm up, in her two hands. The way Corin had held out his own. And with the first finger of her right hand, very lightly, she tapped on her father's palm.
Tap tap tap tap.
Four.
She felt him go still.
Not his face. His face did not change. The gray eyes did not move from the ceiling. But his hand went still in hers. Completely still. The way an animal goes still in long grass when it hears a step.
Tap tap.
Two.
She was shaking. She made her finger stop shaking. She made it go on.
Seven. Eight. Nine.
Nine.
Tap tap tap.
Three.
And then she stopped, and held his hand, and waited.
The little room was utterly silent. She could not hear the canal. She could not hear the barge creak. She could not hear Corin breathing at the foot of the mattress, though he was right there. She could hear nothing at all but her own heart.
Her father's hand lay open in hers.
It did not move.
It did not move for so long that she began to be afraid. That she had done it. That she had broken something. That the last thing had gone. She looked at his face, and it was the same face, gray and still, with the eyes on the ceiling, and she thought, I've stopped him, I've stopped him, oh Saints, I've stopped him—
And then his first finger moved.
Once.
It lifted, very slowly, off her palm. Not far. Just the width of a hair. And came down again.
Tap.
One.
Just one. Soft and dry and light, like a bird's foot. Right in the middle of her palm.
And then nothing.
She waited. He did not tap again. Not the four. Not the two. Nothing. The hand lay open in hers and the finger lay where it had come down, and did not lift.
And slowly, very slowly, the long fingers closed. Round hers. The way they had closed in the afternoon, in the gold light. Not tight. There was no strength in them. But they closed.
And held.
Lyra did not know how long she knelt there. She did not know that she was crying until a tear fell on the back of his hand, and she saw it shine there in the lamplight, and did not wipe it away.
At last she looked up.
Corin was still at the foot of the mattress, in the shadow. He had not moved. But his face had gone very white, and his eyes were fixed on her father's hand, on the one finger, with an expression she had never seen on him before. As if he had just heard a word spoken in a language he used to know.
"One," he said. Almost no sound at all.
"What does it mean?" whispered Lyra.
Corin shook his head slowly.
"I don't know," he said. "But Moll did it. In the Watch house. Juno told me. When old Terrence came down the stairs and said three, at the fourth bell, Moll knocked once on the table. Just once." He was quiet. "Like an answer."
Lyra looked down at her father's hand, closed round hers.
Like an answer.
Outside, far away up the hill, a bell began to ring. The church in the village. Slow and muffled across the frozen fields. And at the top of the cart ramp, closer, a man's voice shouted walk on, and there was the jingle of harness, and the deep, heavy creak of great wooden wheels beginning to turn.
The tenth bell.
"Lyra," said Corin.
"I know."
She did not move.
Then, very gently, one finger at a time, she took her hand out of her father's. His fingers did not hold on. They let go, the way a sleeping child lets go. She laid his hand back on the red square with the white stars, palm down, the way it had been.
She bent and kissed his forehead. It was cool and dry, like paper.
"I'll be back," she said. "Before Midwinter. I promise."
She crawled out through the tunnel without looking back.
And she did not see what Corin saw, kneeling behind her in the gold light for one moment longer before he followed. The long right hand on the patchwork cover. Lying still. Not tapping. For the first time in two years, for the length of ten slow breaths, not tapping at all.
Then, softly, it began again.
Tap tap tap tap.
Ellis's wain was the biggest cart Lyra had ever seen.
It stood at the top of the ramp in the dark, with a lantern hanging from a pole at the front and another at the back, and six great horses in harness in front of it, two by two, stamping and blowing in the frost. The load of hay on its back was as high as a house. Higher. It rose up above the lanterns into the night like a hill, roped down under brown canvas, and it smelled of summer, and it creaked.
Ellis was a small bent man in a sheepskin coat, with a whip in his hand and a pipe in his mouth. He did not look at them. He did not look at anything but his horses. When Hessa brought them up the ramp in the dark, he only took the pipe out of his mouth and said, "Back. Left side. There's a hole. Don't come out till I bang three times on the boards, and don't come out at all if I don't." And put the pipe back.
There was a hole. Low down at the back of the load, on the left, under a flap of canvas. A hollow in the hay, like the one on the barge, but smaller. Big enough for two people lying close.
Corin went in first. Lyra went in after him.
The hay closed round them. Warm and dark and scratchy and sweet. She lay on her side with her back against Corin's chest and her face an inch from a wall of hay, and felt the wain lurch, and the wheels begin to turn, and the whole great hill of it start to sway, slowly, from side to side, like a ship.
She could not see out. But she could hear.
She heard the jingle of the harness, and the stamp of the horses, and Ellis's voice, low and steady, talking to them. She heard the wheels crunch on the frozen ruts of the farm lane. She heard, very faint, down on the wharf, a baby crying, and a woman's voice hushing it. And then, as the wain turned onto the north road, she heard something else.
Oren's voice. From the wharf. Not loud. Not calling after them. Just saying something, very low, to nobody. To the dark. To the hay. She could not hear the words.
And then the road took them, and the sound of it was gone.
She lay in the warm dark with her face against the hay and Corin's breathing at her back, slow and steady, and the wain swaying, and the hooves going clop, clop, clop on the frozen road toward the city.
And in her palm, where she had closed her fingers over it, she could still feel it. Light and dry. A bird's foot. One.
Tap.
