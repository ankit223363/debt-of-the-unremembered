Chapter 34: What Lord Thorne Taps, Part 3 of 5: Juno on the Towpath
Juno on the Towpath
Lyra came out of the hay tunnel so fast that she hit her head on the edge of the planking and saw stars.
She did not stop. She came out from under the canvas on her hands and knees and stood up on the deck, with hay falling off her, and looked.
It was nearly dusk. The short winter afternoon was over already, and the light had gone thin and blue and cold. The last of the carts was standing at the bottom of the ramp with nobody on it. The farmer's men had gone up to the farmhouse, where the windows were yellow and smoke was pouring out of all four chimneys. On the barges, lamps were being lit in the cabin windows, one by one, small and gold against the darkening water.
And on the towpath on the far side of the canal, coming slowly along the frozen track under the black poplars, there was a small dark figure.
It was walking very badly. Lyra could see that even from here. It walked the way a very old woman walks, or a very tired child. Bent forward. Slow. Each foot put down carefully, as if it did not quite trust the ground. It was wearing a coat much too big for it, a huge dark coat that came down to its ankles and flapped round its legs, and it had something wrapped round its head and face, a scarf or a shawl, so that only the eyes showed.
But Lyra would have known that coat anywhere.
It was Juno's grandfather's coat. The one she had worn on every errand, in every kind of weather, for as long as Lyra had known her. The one with the deep pockets, and the torn lining, and the brass key on a string in the left-hand one.
Hessa was already on the wharf. She had a lantern in one hand and the other cupped round her mouth.
"The bridge!" she shouted. "Juno! Go up to the bridge! Two hundred yards! Cross at the bridge and come down this side! Don't you dare try the ice! Don't you dare!"
The small figure on the far towpath stopped. It looked across the canal at them. Then it lifted one arm, slowly, as if the arm were very heavy, and waved. And turned, and went on along the towpath toward the little humped bridge.
Lyra jumped down off the barge onto the landing stage.
She did not think about it. She ran. Along the frozen planks, past the line of barges, past Nan in her red shawl standing at her cabin door with her mouth open, past the empty cart and the steaming horses. Up the bank. Along the near towpath toward the bridge. The cold air burned in her throat and her boots slid on the frozen mud and she did not care.
She met Juno at the top of the bridge.
Juno had stopped there. She was standing in the middle of the hump, holding on to the wooden rail with both hands, swaying a little. Up close, she looked worse. Much worse. The scarf round her face was white with frost where her breath had frozen on it. Her eyebrows were white. Her lashes were white. The bits of hair that stuck out from under the scarf were stiff with ice, like the bristles of a brush. And her eyes, above the scarf, were huge and dark and very tired.
"Hello," said Juno. Her voice came out muffled through the frozen scarf. "I walked."
"I can see you walked." Lyra took hold of her by both arms. Through the huge coat, Juno felt very small and very thin and very cold. "From the city? Juno, it's twelve miles. More. It's—"
"Fourteen," said Juno. "I counted the bridges. Corin taught me. Twenty-three bridges and three locks." She swayed. "I went round the locks. Through the fields. There's gray coats on all of them. Even the little ones." She swallowed. "I fell in a ditch."
Lyra looked down. Juno's boots and the bottom of the coat were caked in frozen mud, gray and stiff, up to the knee.
"Come on," said Lyra. "Come on. Hessa's got the stove going. Come on."
She put her arm round Juno's shoulders and they went down off the bridge together, slowly, along the towpath, with Juno leaning on her harder and harder at every step. By the time they reached the wharf, Lyra was nearly carrying her. Hessa came running along the landing stage with the lantern and took her other arm, and between them they got her along the frozen planks to the Patient Grace, and up the ladder, and across the deck.
The cabin door was open. Light and warmth were pouring out of it.
And in the doorway, holding on to the frame with one hand and rubbing his eyes with the other, his hair sticking up on one side and the marks of the patchwork quilt pressed into his cheek, was Corin.
He looked at Juno.
Juno looked at him.
"You're asleep," said Juno. "You look asleep."
"I was." Corin's voice was thick. "I heard Hessa shouting."
"Everybody heard Hessa shouting," said Juno. "They heard her at Coldwater."
And then her knees gave way.
Corin caught her. Lyra saw him do it, and saw how quick he was, for a man who had been asleep a moment before. He got his arms under hers as she went down and held her up against the door frame, and Hessa took her legs, and between them they carried her down the ladder into the cabin and put her on the floor in front of the stove, on the sacks where Wat had been.
Wat was not there. Wat was sitting up in the corner, propped against the wall with his bound arm in a sling, gray-faced, watching. He shifted over to make room. He did not say anything. But his good hand came out and pulled the bright patchwork quilt off the cupboard bed and dropped it over Juno, all of it, in a heap.
Mrs. Hobb was already at the kettle.
"Boots," she said. "Get her boots off. Quick. And that coat. It's soaked through, look at it. Lyra, love, the blankets from the shelf. All of them. Hessa, the brandy. Not much. A spoonful." She was everywhere at once. "Oh, you poor lamb. Oh, you silly, brave, poor lamb."
They got her boots off. They were frozen to her feet and had to be pulled, and Juno made a sound when they came. Her stockings underneath were wet and gray. Her feet were white. Dead white, like candles, and Mrs. Hobb looked at them and said nothing and began to rub them between her two big soft hands, slowly, very gently, while Lyra brought the blankets.
They got the coat off. It was so heavy with wet and frozen mud that Lyra could barely lift it. She went to hang it on a hook by the stove.
"No," said Juno.
She had opened her eyes. She was lying under the quilt and three blankets with only her face showing, and the frost on her eyebrows was melting now in the heat of the stove and running down her face like tears.
"The pocket," she said. "The inside one. On the left. Don't let it get wet."
Lyra put her hand into the coat.
The inside left pocket was very deep. It went down almost to the hem. Her fingers found the brass key on its string. And below the key, right at the bottom, something flat and stiff. Wrapped in oilcloth. Tied with string.
She took it out.
It was a parcel about the size of a small book. The oilcloth was cold and greasy and smelled of fish. Somebody had tied it up very tightly, with knots on knots, the way a person ties a thing when they are afraid of losing it.
She held it out to Juno. But Juno shook her head, a tiny movement on the sacks.
"Not me," she said. "Him." Her eyes went to Corin. "And you. Both of you. Moll said." She shut her eyes again. "Moll said, give it to the harvester and the Thorne girl, and nobody else, and don't open it on the road. I didn't open it. I didn't."
"We know you didn't," said Corin. He had knelt down beside her, on the other side from Mrs. Hobb. "What is it, Juno?"
Juno did not answer at once.
Mrs. Hobb had finished with one foot and started on the other. A little pink was coming back into the first one, round the toes. Juno's face twisted when Mrs. Hobb touched the second. She bit her lip.
"It's the Bourse," she said.
The cabin went very quiet.
Lyra stood still with the oilcloth parcel in her hands. She looked down at it. She looked across at the curtain of sacking at the end of the cabin, and beyond it the wall that backed onto the hay, and beyond that the little plank room where her father lay tapping.
Bourse.
She looked at Corin.
Corin was looking at her. He had seen her face. He did not know why it had gone the way it had. She saw him not know, and wonder, and file it away somewhere behind his eyes to ask about later.
"Tea," said Mrs. Hobb firmly into the silence. "Tea first. Then the Bourse. Nobody opens anything in my kitchen until this child's got something hot inside her."
Nobody argued with her.
And Lyra sat down on the edge of the cupboard bed with the cold parcel in her lap, and held it in both hands, and waited, and listened through the wall to the tapping. Four. Two. Nine. Three. And thought: he knew. Somehow, down there in the dark, he knew it was coming.
News from Rope Street
Juno drank two mugs of tea with sugar in them and a spoonful of Hessa's brandy in the first one, and ate a slice of bread and dripping, and then another, and then most of a third. Then she sat up against Wat's good side, wrapped in the patchwork quilt like a caterpillar in a leaf, with her pink feet in a basin of warm water and Robin asleep in the crook of her arm, and told them.
She told it the way Juno told everything. Fast and flat and in the wrong order, with bits left out and bits put back in, so that you had to hold on to it with both hands.
"Gran won't go," she said. "I told you she wouldn't. I said, Gran, the man in the gray cape knows it's your cloth. He's been in the Watch house. He showed it to Moll. And Gran said, Well, it is my cloth, and I'd like it back, it's my good one." Juno rolled her eyes. "And Moll won't go either. He says a sergeant doesn't leave his post. He says he's been at that desk twenty years and he'll be at it when they come for him, and they can come for him there, with his boots polished."
"And have they?" said Corin. "Come for him?"
"No." Juno hesitated. "Not yet. But a paper came. This morning. From the Ministry. With a red seal." She swallowed. "Sergeant Ebenezer Moll is to present himself at the Office of the Inquisition on Monday at the tenth bell, with his papers of service from the Commonwealth Foot. That's what it said. Moll read it out. Then he folded it up and put it in his drawer and went on with his tea."
Nobody said anything.
"He said it's nothing," said Juno. "He said they do that. Check papers. He said he's got nothing to hide." She looked down at Robin in her arm. "He was tapping on the desk all the time he said it. With his fingers. Like he does."
Lyra saw Corin's face change.
She did not know why. She did not know Moll well. A big slow man with a bad leg and a cold pipe, who had sat in the arches with them once, and had let them through the back door of the Watch house more than once, and had never asked any questions. She did not know about his fingers.
Corin did. She could see that he did. She saw him look at the curtain of sacking at the end of the cabin, and the wall beyond it, where the tapping was. And then look back at Juno.
"What else?" he said.
"The doors." Juno took a breath. "Your list. All the doors in your Ledger. The ones you wrote down Tuesday night. Gran and Moll did it like they said. The step women. Win Barrow and the Pole sisters and Bridie and Granny Sedge. They went round to every one. All yesterday and all this morning. Scrubbing steps." She almost smiled. "You should see Hook Lane. It's never been so clean. And at every door, when somebody came out to see who was scrubbing their step, they said the thing. The landlady says the rent is late, and not to call round."
"Every door?"
"Every one on the list but two." Juno counted on her fingers under the quilt. "Mother Grue's, because she's in the arches and you can't scrub an arch. Gideon took it to her. And the Lamplighter's Rest." She looked at Corin. "Bess Pole went there this morning. The old man with the blue fingers. Your teacher. He wasn't there. The landlord said he'd gone out early. With his books. He didn't say where."
Corin did not move.
Lyra watched him not move. She had learned to watch for that. When something hit Corin hard, he did not flinch, or shout, or put his hand to his face. He went still. Stiller than anybody she had ever known. As if somebody had stopped a clock.
"He's all right," said Juno quickly. "Bess says. He went out on his own feet. With his books. The landlord saw him go. Nobody took him."
"Yes," said Corin. "All right."
He did not say anything else. After a moment the clock started again.
"And the gray cape?" said Oren.
He had come down the ladder while Juno was talking. He was standing at the bottom of it, bent under the low beams, with his arms folded.
"He came to the Watch house," said Juno. "Yesterday, at four. With Gran's cloth. Moll told you." She looked at Corin. "I told you. On the boat." Then she frowned. "And this morning. Early. He came back."
"This morning?" said Corin sharply.
"At the eighth bell. I was in the back room with Gran. I heard him." Juno shivered under the quilt, though it was very hot in the cabin. "He didn't ask for anything. He didn't say anything. He just came in the front and asked Moll if he could sit by the fire for a bit, because he'd been out all night on the river and he was cold. And Moll said yes. And he sat. On the bench in the front room, by the stove, with his hat on his knee. For an hour." She looked round at them. "He didn't say a word. Not one. He just sat there. And then he got up and said thank you, Sergeant, and went away."
"What did he look like?" said Corin.
"Like nothing," said Juno. "Like he wasn't there. Gran says he's the sort of man you could stand next to in a queue for an hour and not remember." She paused. "His boots were wet. Up to the ankle. And there was mud on his coat. Gray mud. Like the mud at Coldwater."
Nobody said anything for a while.
Hessa was sitting on the bottom step of the ladder, beside Oren's feet. She had not said anything at all since Juno started talking. She was looking at the stove.
"And Jory?" she said at last. Very quietly.
Juno's face changed.
"I heard," she said. "Everybody's heard. They brought him through the city in a closed cart this morning. Up Wharf Street and over the Lantern Bridge and up the Hill. Gideon saw it go by. He said there was an old man inside, you could see him through the little window, and he was—" She stopped.
"He was what?" said Hessa.
Juno looked at her.
"He was singing," she said. "Gideon said. He couldn't hear the words. But the old man was sitting up in the cart with his hands on his knees, singing."
Hessa did not move.
Then, very slowly, she put her face down into her hands again. The way she had last night, after the stitches. And stayed like that. And Wat, in the corner, reached out with his good hand, past Juno and the baby, and laid it on the back of his wife's neck, and left it there.
Nobody said anything for a long time.
The kettle ticked on the stove. Robin snuffled in his sleep. Through the wall, the tapping went on.
Then Corin said, "Juno. The parcel."
Juno looked at him. She looked at Lyra, on the edge of the cupboard bed, with the oilcloth bundle still in her lap.
"It's from Moll," she said. "Like I said. He gave it to me at noon, before I set out. He said—" She frowned, trying to get it right. "He said there's two things in it. One's yours. You gave it to him to keep. Ages ago. The night you brought the Thorne girl to the Watch house the first time, wet through. He said you gave it him and said keep this, Sergeant, it's evidence, and don't let anybody see it. And he put it in his locked drawer and he's had it there ever since."
Corin's face did not change.
But Lyra, who was watching him, saw his hand move. Very slightly. Toward the inside of his coat. Toward the Ledger. As if to check something. And then stop.
He did not remember. She could see that he did not.
"And the other thing?" said Lyra.
"Bridie got it," said Juno. "Bridie Kane. She's sixteen. She scrubs steps on Silk Street. Moll gave her your thing, the first thing, this morning, and sent her up the hill with it to a door." She almost grinned. "A kitchen door. Round the back of a great big round building with a green roof. Bridie said there were so many chandeliers through the windows she nearly went blind. And she knocked, and a man in a black coat opened it, and she held up the thing and said, Please, sir, I'm the new girl, my lady sent me for her catalogue for Midwinter, and he looked at the thing and not at her, and went away, and came back, and gave her a book." Juno shook her head. "Just like that. He didn't even ask which lady. Bridie said he never once looked at her face. Nobody ever looks at a step girl's face."
The cabin was very quiet.
"Open it, love," said Mrs. Hobb softly.
Lyra looked down at the parcel.
Her hands were shaking. She did not know why. It was only oilcloth and string. She picked at the knots with her nails. They would not come. Hessa leaned over without a word and cut the string with her clasp knife, and the oilcloth fell open in Lyra's lap.
There were two things in it.
The first was a card. Small and stiff and black, the size of a playing card, edged in gold. Lyra knew it before she had turned it to the light. She had seen it before, in the back room of a printing shop, at three in the morning, in Corin's hand, the night they met.
THE MEMORY BOURSE.
ADMIT ONE.
SERVICE.
And under the card, wrapped separately in a sheet of brown paper, the second thing.
A book. Thin and tall, bound in soft black leather, with gold on the edges of the pages. No title on the front. Only, pressed into the leather without any ink, so that you could only see it when the lamplight slid across it, the small blind shape of a half-mask. Smooth and curved, like the inside of a shell.
