Chapter 34: What Lord Thorne Taps, Part 4 of 5: The Black Catalogue
The Black Catalogue
Lyra opened it on her knee.
The leather was soft and cold and smelled faintly of something sweet. Violets, she thought. Or the powder great ladies put on their faces. The first page was thick cream paper, heavy as card, with a deckled edge like the edge of a torn letter, and in the middle of it, in gold ink, in a hand so beautiful that it looked as if it had been painted rather than written, there were five lines.
The Memory Bourse
by invitation of
The Lady Vivienne
begs the honor of your company at the
Midwinter Masquerade
And under that, smaller:
On Midwinter Night, from the ninth bell until the last guest has gone. Masks to be worn at all times. Each guest to be presented by a sponsor of the old houses. Lots to be viewed from the ninth bell; bidding from the eleventh. Payment in coin, in vials, or in kind.
In kind. Lyra read it twice. She knew what it meant. Everyone who had ever been to the Bourse knew what it meant.
She turned the page.
The next page was a list. And the next. And the next. Page after page of them, in the same beautiful gold hand, on the same heavy cream paper, laid out in neat columns like the menu at a very grand dinner. A number on the left. A name in the middle. A few words under the name, in smaller writing, describing it. And on the right, a price. Or not a price. A starting bid.
The first section was headed Childhoods.
Lot 1. A Summer by the Sea. Age seven to eight. Sand, gulls, a red kite, a grandmother's kitchen. Very bright. Complete. Starting at forty crowns.
Lot 2. The First Snow. Age four. One afternoon. Sledging, a dog, hot milk after. Short but exceptionally clear. Starting at twelve crowns.
Lot 3. Being Read To. Age five to nine. A father's voice, every evening. Some three hundred stories, partly complete. The voice itself is very fine. Starting at sixty crowns.
Lyra stopped reading.
She looked at the third one for a long time. A father's voice, every evening. Somebody had sold that. Somebody had sat in a chair at the Black Bank, or in the back room of a pawnshop on Gutter Street, and let a harvester draw out of their head three hundred evenings of their father reading to them, and had gone home without them. With money in their pocket. And now they did not know that they had ever been read to. They did not know that their father had a voice.
And on Midwinter Night, under a glass dome, a woman in a silk mask would pay sixty crowns for it, and take it home, and have it put into her own child's head. So that her child would remember a father reading. A father who was not theirs.
She turned the page.
Talents.
Lot 9. The Violin. Twenty-two years of practice, to concert standard. Includes sight-reading. Starting at four hundred crowns.
Lot 10. Cookery, Southern Style. Forty years. A whole kitchen's worth. Starting at ninety crowns.
Lot 11. Swimming. Lifetime. Very strong. From a fisherman. Starting at thirty crowns.
From a fisherman. Lyra thought of a man going out in his boat the next morning, the morning after he had sold it, and the boat turning over in a squall, and the man in the water, thrashing, not knowing how. Not knowing that he had ever known.
She turned the page faster.
Loves.
Lot 17. A Courtship. Two years. Letters, a dance, a proposal in the rain. Both sides available separately. Starting at seventy crowns each.
Both sides available separately. She did not let herself think about that.
Lot 22. A Mother's Love. Infancy. Being held. No faces, only warmth. Most suitable for gifting. Starting at one hundred and ten crowns.
She felt sick.
Juno was leaning over her shoulder. Lyra had not noticed her move. She was out of the quilt, kneeling on the cupboard bed beside her with a blanket round her shoulders and her hair still wet from the melted frost, reading. Her lips moved as she read. She read slowly, Juno, the way she did everything that was not running.
"They sell that?" said Juno. Her voice was very quiet. "Being held?"
"They sell everything," said Lyra.
"Who buys it?"
Lyra did not answer. She did not know how to answer. People who never had it, she might have said. People who had it and burned it, and want it back. People who want their children to remember something they were too busy to give them. She did not say any of that. She turned the page.
The next section was not like the others.
It had no heading in gold. Only a line, printed small, in plain black ink, at the top of the page. As if it were not quite proper to mention it among the other things.
Bulk Lots. For the trade. Viewing by appointment, in the lower rooms.
And under it, a short list. No beautiful descriptions. Just numbers and words.
Lot 30. Summers, assorted. Lower Docks. By the crate (twelve). Mixed quality.
Lot 31. Childhoods, assorted. Lower Docks and Saltgate. By the crate (twelve). Mostly complete.
Lot 32. Skills, manual. Dockside. By the crate (twenty). Rope, net, lifting, etc.
Lot 33. Kindnesses, small. Various. By the crate (forty). Low tier. Suitable for blending.
Corin had come to stand by the bed. Lyra had not seen him move either. He was looking down at the page over her other shoulder, very still.
"By the crate," he said.
His voice was perfectly flat.
"The Lower Docks," said Juno. "That's where the sick children were. In the autumn. By the river. Corin, that's where the slag carts—"
"I know."
Nobody said anything.
Lyra looked up at him. His face had gone the way it went. Still. A stopped clock. But there was something under the stillness this time. Something she had not seen in it before. It was not fear, and it was not grief.
It was the face of a man who has found, at last, the thing he is going to spend the rest of his life fighting.
Kindnesses, small. By the crate (forty). Suitable for blending.
"Turn the page," said Corin quietly.
She turned it.
And the next page was black.
Not printed black. The paper itself was black. A thick, smooth, heavy black card, like the service card, edged in gold. And on it, in gold ink, in the same beautiful hand as the first page, but larger, so that the letters seemed to glow in the lamplight, there were three words.
The Fourth Tier.
And under them, much smaller:
Lots of the fourth tier are offered by private sale. Bids are sealed. They are not to be spoken of outside the Bourse. Their nature is known to the House, and warranted by it.
The House reminds its guests that a gem of the fourth tier is a whole thing. It does not keep. It does not copy. Once opened, it is gone.
Lyra sat very still with the catalogue on her knee.
She had heard of the fourth tier. Everyone had. It was a story, like the stories about the Glass Wastes, or the Emperor's lost children, or the old war. Something your governess told you to frighten you into going to sleep. Memories so big that they are not memories any more. A whole life. A whole love. A whole death. Gems you could hold in your hand that were heavier than a house. Nobody she knew had ever seen one. Nobody she knew had ever met anybody who had.
She turned the black page.
There were only three lots on the next one. Each one alone, in the middle of its own space, with a wide border of empty cream paper around it, the way a jeweler sets a very large stone.
Lyra read the first.
She read the second.
And then she read the third, and the cabin went away around her, and the stove and the lamp and the heat and the faces, and there was nothing in the world but the gold letters on the heavy cream paper, and the tapping, going on and on through the wall at her back.
Lot 41
Lot 39. A War.
Eleven years under the colors, from the inside. One soldier, from the day he took the shilling to the day he came home. Every march, every camp, every field. Warranted unbroken.
By sealed bid.
Lot 40. A Marriage.
Thirty-one years. Both hearts, from the first meeting to the last day. Complete. The House has never offered its like.
By sealed bid.
And then the third. Alone, in the middle of its own wide cream space, like a stone in a ring.
Lot 41. A Lord's Last Evening.
One evening of the old houses, entire. The last evening of its kind, from a lord of the first rank, taken whole before the fire. Every face, every word, every thought of it, to the final hour. Sealed in the presence of the House. Warranted genuine. Its like will not be offered again, for its like does not exist.
Offered by: C. T., by proxy.
By sealed bid.
Lyra read it three times.
The first time, the words went past her the way the countryside goes past the window of a coach. She saw them. They meant nothing. Lord. Evening. Fire. Words.
The second time, she read it slowly, one word at a time, with her finger under the line like a child. A lord of the first rank. There were not many lords of the first rank left in Veridia. Perhaps a dozen. The old houses had been falling for years, one by one, like trees in a wood that nobody tends. Marrow had bought one. The Ministry had swallowed two. Hers had been—
Taken whole before the fire.
The third time, she did not need her finger.
She sat very still on the edge of the cupboard bed with the catalogue open on her knee, and the lamp hissing on its hook, and Juno breathing beside her, and Corin standing over her, and she listened to the tapping through the wall at her back.
Tap tap tap tap.
Two years ago, at the end of the winter, her father had gone into his study on Crown Walk one evening after dinner and shut the door. She had been upstairs. She had heard the door shut. She had heard it because he never shut it. He always left it a little open, so that the dogs could come and go, and so that he could hear her playing in the music room across the hall.
That night he shut it.
And in the morning, Bertram had found him in his chair by the dead fire, with his eyes open and his hands on the arms of the chair, and nothing behind his eyes at all. Swept clean. Burned down to the walls. The doctors had said it was the worst they had ever seen. They had said there was nothing left in him to save. Not his name. Not his house. Not his wife, or his children, or the color of the sky. Nothing.
And she had believed them. For two years. She had believed that whatever he had done in that room, he had done it to himself, all of it, in one night. That he had sat down in his chair and set fire to everything he was, and that there was nothing left, anywhere, of that evening. Of the last evening. Of whatever he had been thinking when he shut the door.
Taken whole before the fire.
Before.
Somebody had been there. Somebody had come into that room before he burned, or while he was burning, and taken it. The last evening. The whole of it. Every face, every word, every thought. They had taken it out of him the way you take a jewel out of a box before you throw the box on the fire.
And they had kept it. For two years.
And now they were going to sell it. Under a glass dome. On Midwinter Night. To whoever wrote the biggest number on a piece of paper and sealed it with wax.
"Lyra," said Corin.
She did not look up.
"Lyra." Very quietly. "Is it him?"
She could not speak. She nodded.
She heard Juno draw in her breath. She heard Mrs. Hobb, by the stove, say oh, very softly, and put down the kettle. She heard Oren, at the foot of the ladder, not move at all.
And behind her, through the wall, through the hay, the tapping went on.
Tap tap.
Bourse. She had heard him say it. This afternoon. In the gold light. His fingers closing on her hand, and his eyes coming up the long dark stair to find her at the window, and the one word, dry as a turning page. Bourse.
After two years of nothing.
He had known. Down there in the dark, in whatever was left of him, in whatever corner the fire had not reached, he had known. That a piece of him was out there. That it was going to be sold. That it was at the Bourse.
Or—
She stopped.
Or he had known before. Two years before. Before he shut the study door. He had known that somebody would come for that evening. And he had tried to burn it first. And he had not been quick enough. And whatever was left of him had been tapping ever since, four, two, nine, three, day and night, on walls and blankets and the backs of hands, trying to tell somebody where it had gone.
"Who's C. T.?" said Juno.
Lyra looked down at the page.
Offered by: C. T., by proxy.
Two letters. Gold. Beautiful. Each one with a little curl at the end, like the tail of a cat.
And something in her mind went toward them, the way your hand goes toward a hot stove before you know it is hot, and touched them, and flinched back.
She did not let it go back. She did not let herself look at what it had touched. She folded it small. She had been taught to.
"I don't know," she said.
Her voice sounded strange to her. Flat and far away, like somebody else's voice in another room.
"There must be a hundred people in the city with those letters," she said. "A thousand. It could be anybody. A merchant. A banker. A clerk." She shut the catalogue. Not hard. Very gently, as if it were asleep. "By proxy means they sent someone else. It means they didn't want their name in the book. It means it could be anyone at all."
Juno looked at her.
Corin looked at her.
She did not look at either of them. She looked at the shut black leather on her knee, at the small blind mask pressed into it, and held it in both hands.
"It doesn't matter who," she said. "Not tonight. It doesn't matter who's selling it." She took a breath. "What matters is that it's there. It's real. Somebody kept it. And it's going to be under that dome on Midwinter Night, and somebody is going to buy it, and open it, and then it'll be gone." Her voice cracked. "Once opened, it is gone. It says so. It says so right there."
Nobody said anything.
"Whatever he was thinking," said Lyra. "Whatever he burned himself to hide. Whatever he's been trying to tell us for two years with his fingers." She looked up at last. At Corin. "It's in there. In Lot 41. And on Midwinter Night someone is going to buy it and open it and it will be gone, and we will never, ever know."
She stopped.
The tapping went on through the wall.
"When is Midwinter?" said Juno into the silence.
"Monday week," said Hessa, from the foot of the ladder. She had not moved all this time. She was sitting on the bottom step with her hands on her knees and her face very still. "Ten days from Friday. Monday, Midwinter Night."
"Ten days," said Lyra.
She looked down at the catalogue. Then at the small black card that still lay on the oilcloth beside her. ADMIT ONE. SERVICE. A kitchen door. A man in a black coat who did not look at faces.
Then she looked at Corin.
And she saw that he was already thinking it. Had been thinking it since before she finished reading. She could see it in his face. The stopped clock had started again, and it was going very fast.
"No," said Oren, from the ladder.
Nobody had said anything yet.
"No," said Oren again.
Taken Whole
"I haven't said anything," said Corin.
"You don't need to," said Oren. "I can see it on you. I can see it on her." He did not come any closer. He stayed at the foot of the ladder, bent under the beams, with his arms folded across his chest. "The Bourse is Vivienne's house. Vivienne's. The woman who sent four men over Pruett's wall with a club full of nails. The woman who wants your book, Vane, and has wanted it since the night you met. And you want to walk in through her kitchen door with a card you don't remember owning."
Corin did not answer.
He held out his hand to Lyra. She looked at it. Then she gave him the catalogue.
He did not sit down. He opened it standing, under the lamp, and turned the pages slowly. Not reading the way she had read. Reading the way he read jars, at the Archive, the way she had seen him read Ash. With his whole face. Every word weighed. Every line turned over and looked at from underneath.
He went past the Childhoods and the Talents and the Loves. He stopped at the Bulk Lots and stayed there a long time. Then he turned the black page, and the next, and stopped again.
Lyra watched his eyes go down the cream paper. Lot 39. Lot 40. Lot 41.
"Taken whole before the fire," said Corin.
He said it very quietly. Not to anybody.
"What does it mean?" said Juno. "Taken whole. How do you take a whole evening?"
Corin did not look up from the page.
"You can't," he said. "Not usually." He turned the catalogue a little toward the lamp. "When a person burns a memory, it goes up. All at once. Like paper. That's what burning is. The fire uses it, and what's left is Ash, and a harvester can come afterward and draw the Ash out of them, and seal it in a jar. That's my work. That's what I do." He paused. "Did."
"I know that," said Juno.
"But Ash isn't the memory. Ash is what's left of it. Bits. Pieces. Burned edges. A face here, a word there. You can see what it was, if you're good, the way you can see what a letter said from the bits that didn't catch." He looked at the page. "But you can't put it back together. Not whole. Never whole. The fire's had the middle of it."
"Then how—"
"You take it before." Corin's voice did not change. "Before the fire gets to it. While the person is still deciding. While they've got the match in their hand." He was quiet for a moment. "There are harvesters who can do it. Not many. You have to be very fast, and very close, and very sure. You have to be in the room. You have to be right there, with your hands on them, at the exact moment they start to burn. And you take it out of them, whole, the whole of it, before the fire can reach it." He looked up. "It's the hardest thing a harvester can do. I could never do it."
"And they did it to Father," said Lyra.
It was not a question.
Corin looked at her.
"Someone was in the study," he said. "That night. With him. When he shut the door."
The cabin was very quiet.
Lyra thought of the study door. Shut. For the first time in all her life, shut. She had been upstairs in the music room. She had heard it close. She had played on. She had played the whole of a sonata, and then another, and then she had gone to bed. And all that time, on the other side of that shut door—
"The next morning," she said slowly. "A carriage came. Bertram told Oren. A black carriage. From the Ministry." She looked at Oren. "Didn't he?"
Oren's face had gone very hard.
"A woman in white," he said. "And a man in gray. Bertram let them in. Bertram sent me away, to the stables, on some errand that did not need doing. When I came back, they were gone." His jaw moved. "I asked Bertram who they were. He said they were from the Ministry, to see to my lord. He said not to ask any more." A pause. "And then Bertram went to his sister in the country. And Doctor Aske went to his sister in the country. And nobody ever saw either of them again."
Nobody said anything for a long moment.
"The next morning," said Corin. "Not that night. They came the next morning."
"Yes."
"Then they weren't the ones in the room." Corin looked down at the page again. "Or they came back. Or—" He stopped. "I don't know."
He closed the catalogue.
Then he did something Lyra did not expect. He gave the catalogue back to her, and took out his Ledger instead, and sat down on the floor by the stove, cross-legged, next to Juno's basin of water, and opened it on his knee. And began to turn the pages. Back. Back. Further back. Past the doors, and the lists, and the lines in his own hand and the lines in other people's. Quickly at first. Then slower.
"What are you looking for?" said Lyra.
"The card," said Corin. He did not look up. "Moll says I gave it to him. I don't remember. So I'll have written it down." He turned a page. "I write everything down."
She watched him. Everybody watched him. He turned the pages one after another, his lips moving a little, his finger running down the margins. It took a long time. The Ledger was very thick now. Thicker than when she had first seen it, in the Inkwell, in the rain. Its spine had cracked in two places and been mended with thread. Its pages were full to the edges, and some of them had other bits of paper tucked in, and pinned, and pasted, so that it bulged.
And then his finger stopped.
He read something. Lyra saw his face as he read it. She saw it change. Not much. A small movement at the corner of his mouth. As if he had heard a joke, a long time ago, and had just understood it.
"What does it say?" said Juno.
Corin turned the Ledger round and held it up so they could see. There was a line on the page, at the bottom, in his own small sloping hand. Written fast. The ink had smudged.
Gave the black card (Bourse, SERVICE) to Sgt. Moll, for his locked drawer. If they search me, they will not find it. Do not ask for it back until you need it. You will know when.
And under it, smaller:
You will not remember this. That is the point.
Juno laughed. A short surprised bark of a laugh, quickly swallowed.
"You told yourself," she said. "You hid it from yourself."
"I hid it from anybody who might get it out of me," said Corin. He looked at the line for a moment longer. "Which includes me."
He closed the Ledger.
And he looked up at Oren, at the foot of the ladder. Steadily. The way Lyra had seen him look at Kross's men on the roof of the Archive, and at Vivienne's men in the Inkwell, and at the drowned dark of the sewer.
"Oren," he said. "Two years ago, somebody stood in a room with your lord and took the last evening of his mind out of him whole, before he could burn it. Somebody who knew how. Somebody who knew he was going to burn, and when, and came ready." He paused. "And then they kept it. Two years. And now they're selling it. Ten days from now. To the highest bid."
Oren said nothing.
"You know who goes to the Bourse," said Corin. "You know who's got that kind of money. The Bank. The Ministry. The old houses that are left. Marrow. Vivienne herself." His voice did not rise. "Whatever Lord Thorne knew that night, whatever he was willing to burn himself to the walls to keep, one of them is going to buy it. And open it. And know it."
He let that sit.
"And then it'll be gone," he said. "And so will he. Because it's the last of him, Oren. Whatever's in that gem is the last of the man you served for twenty-two years. The rest is ash. That's what's left. That's all that's left." He looked at the curtain of sacking, and the wall beyond it. "And he's been tapping for two years, trying to tell somebody where it is."
The tapping went on through the wall.
Tap tap tap tap.
Oren stood very still at the foot of the ladder. His arms were still folded. His face was still hard. But Lyra, who had known him all her life, who had seen him angry at men twice his size and at horses and at her, saw something move in it. Deep down. Like a stone turning over at the bottom of a pond.
Tap tap.
"He said a word," said Lyra.
Everybody looked at her.
She had not meant to say it. Not yet. She had meant to keep it, for a little while, just for herself. The gold light, and the fingers closing, and the eyes. But it came out.
"This afternoon," she said. "In the hay. After Mrs. Hobb went out. He held my hand. He looked at me. And he said one word, out loud." Her voice cracked. She made it stop. "Bourse."
Nobody moved.
"He said it, Oren," said Lyra. "After two years. He came all the way up to find me, and he said Bourse, and then he went back down." She looked at the old soldier at the foot of the ladder, the man who had carried her on his shoulders through the park when she was four. "He's asking us. He's been asking for two years. And today he finally found the word for it."
Oren looked at her for a long time.
Then he unfolded his arms.
He did not say yes. He did not say anything at all. He just unfolded his arms, slowly, and let them hang at his sides, and looked down at the floor of the cabin. And Lyra understood that that was all she was going to get, and that it was a great deal.
