Chapter 75: Grounding, Part 1 of 5: Sola's Hands
Sola's Hands
Lyra said his name, and he looked at her as if she were a bird making a noise on a wall.
She would have given anything not to have seen that look. She saw it. She was bent over him, upside down, with her face a hand's width from his, and she said his name for the last time, the hundredth time, the thousandth, with her throat torn raw from saying it, and his eyes looked up into hers and did not know what she was.
Not who. What.
Then he roared again.
And came up off the glass.
She had never seen anyone move like that. Not Oren in a fight. Not the Ashborn on the slope at Last Thorn. Not the Erasers. He had been lying flat on his back on the felt, with his bad leg out stiff and Oren gripping his left hand and the iron hoof hanging over him. And then he was not lying down. He was up. All at once. Off the glass in one long heave, as if something had lifted him by the chest.
His left hand was still in Oren's.
He did not pull it free. He did something else.
Lyra saw it, and she did not understand it until afterward. He turned. From the hips. One short hard turn, low, his whole body going round on his good knee like a door on a hinge. And his left arm, with Oren's hand still locked on it, went round with him. Across. Down. Not pulling. Turning. Using Oren's own grip, Oren's own weight, the way you use the weight of a man who is leaning on you.
And Oren went over.
Big Oren. Oren who was as heavy as two of Lyra. Who had stood in front of iron men and not moved. He went over Corin's hip like a sack of meal going over a wall, and came down flat on his back on the hot glass beside the felt with a crack that knocked the breath out of him in a great whoof.
No one had ever done that to Oren. Lyra had never seen it. Not in all the years.
And Corin did not know how to do it.
She knew that. She knew it as she watched. Corin was a clerk. A harvester. He could not fight. He had never thrown anyone in his life. He did not have the body for it, or the training, or the knowing in his hips.
But his body had just done it.
Like a soldier. Like an old soldier, who had done it a thousand times in a thousand fights and did not need to think. One turn of the hip. Short. Ugly. Perfect.
Not his throw.
Hers.
He was crouched on the felt now, on his good knee, with his stiff leg out to the side, his hands open in front of him. Low. Wide. Like a man in a ring, waiting. His face was turned to them. All of them. And it was not his face. It was the same face, the same bones and the same eyes and the same mouth. But there was something looking out of it that Lyra had never seen there. Something big and hot and furious and afraid. Like a bull in a pen. Like a fire in a room.
He was panting. His lips were drawn back off his teeth.
Vespera had fallen back, with her needles spilling off their cloth across the felt. Brin and Oda had dropped the shade. It lay crumpled on the glass and the white noon sun came down on all of them. The Ashborn in their ring had their spears up, all of them, pointing in. Pointing at him.
"Don't," said Lyra. She did not know who she was saying it to. "Don't. Don't. Don't—"
Corin's eyes went to the nearest spear. Oda's. And his body began to turn toward it. Low. The hips going round. The hands coming up.
And the silent man was there.
Lyra had not seen him come. No one had. He had been sitting on the dark glass beyond the ring, with Tansy holding his arm. And then he was inside the ring. Between Corin and Oda's spear. He had come through the ring of Ashborn the way water comes through the gaps between stones. Without touching anyone. Without hurry. Without a sound.
He did not stop.
He walked straight into Corin.
Not hard. Not fast. Calm as water. He walked into the crouched shape on the felt and took it, the way you take a child who is screaming in a fit. Both arms round. One across the chest, one across the arms, pinning them. And his whole long thin body behind it. Not hitting. Not throwing. Just weight. All of his weight. Leaning in. Pressing Corin down, down, slowly, back onto the felt, the way a wave presses a swimmer down. Steadily. Without stopping. Without any anger at all.
Corin fought.
Lyra saw him fight. Saw the hips try to turn, and the man's weight already there, already on the hip, so that the turn went nowhere. Saw the arms try to come up, and the man's arm already across them. Every move Corin's body tried, the man was there before it. As if he knew. As if he had known that throw for a long time, and every way out of it, and had been waiting for it.
Corin roared under him.
The man did not let go.
He lay on top of Corin on the felt in the white noon sun, under the iron hoof, with his arms locked round him and his bearded face pressed against the side of Corin's head. Holding. Not saying anything. He never said anything. Only holding.
And slowly, very slowly, the roaring stopped.
Corin's body went still under him. Not limp. Still. The way a frightened horse goes still, at last, when someone has held its head long enough. Panting. Shaking. His eyes wide open on the white sky.
The man did not move. He did not loosen his arms.
Oren was getting up. Slowly. On his hands and knees on the hot glass, with his face red and his breath coming in whoops, staring at the two of them.
"Saints," he said. When he could. "Saints. That was a—that was a hip throw. An old one. The old valley way, low, off the back knee." He stopped. Swallowed. "I saw an old sergeant do that once, when I was a boy recruit. Just once. He was older than the hills. Nobody does it like that anymore."
He looked at Corin, lying still under the silent man.
"He's never been taught that," said Oren. "Not in his life."
"No," said Lyra.
She was on her knees on the felt. She did not remember kneeling. Her hands were shaking so hard she put them flat on the hot felt to stop them.
"It was hers," she said.
No Words
The silent man let him up, in the end. Not all the way. Slowly. A little at a time. The way you take your hand off a frightened bird, finger by finger, ready to close it again.
Corin did not fight. He lay on the felt with the man's arm still across his chest, and breathed. In and out. Hard and fast at first. Then slower. His eyes went round the ring of faces above him. One after another.
Lyra watched them go.
They did not stop on anyone. That was the thing. They went from face to face, quick and wary, the way an animal's eyes go round a ring of hunters. Oren's. Vespera's. Kestrel's. Oda's. Each one for a breath, and then on. Not looking at the faces. Looking at what the faces might do. At hands. At spears. At the distance between each person and himself.
They did not know anyone.
They did not know they were supposed to.
"Put the spears down," said Lyra.
No one moved.
"Put them down." Her voice cracked. "He doesn't know what they are. He doesn't know what we are. Put them down, before he—"
Kestrel said something in the round speech. Short. And the spears came down, one by one, all round the ring. Points to the glass.
Corin's eyes followed them down. Then came back up.
Lyra knelt.
She did it slowly. She had seen people kneel by frightened dogs, in the alleys behind the docks. You did not go fast. You did not stand over them. You got down, low, to where they were. And you did not look them in the eyes, not straight, not at first.
She knelt on the hot felt, a little way from him. On his right. Where Vespera's needles had spilled. And put her hands on her knees, open, where he could see them.
And looked at him.
He looked back.
And his lips drew back off his teeth.
Not a smile. Nothing like a smile. A baring. The top lip lifting, the teeth showing, the breath hissing out between them. The way a dog shows its teeth when you come too close to its food. The way a cat does, backed into a corner. A warning. Not closer. Not one step.
Lyra stopped breathing.
She had faced Kross. In the city, in the snow, in rooms with locked doors and men in gray coats. She had faced the dome coming down, gold and roaring, and Corin holding it up, and his arms going gray. She had faced an iron hand coming down on three children at Last Thorn, and given away the thing she loved most in the world to stop it.
She had never been as afraid as she was now. Kneeling on a piece of felt in the white sun. Looking at a man she loved, who was showing her his teeth.
Because there was nobody there.
That was what she saw. Behind the eyes. Behind the bared teeth. Nobody. Not Corin. Not the clerk who went red to the ears and said shut up, old man at supper. Not the man who had written SHE SLEPT in a book because it was important. Not even the empty, frightened, forgetting Corin of the last weeks, who had looked at her every day trying to remember, and failing, and trying again.
Nobody.
Something else. Something with no name, because it had given its name away, at the edge of a lake, sixty years ago. Something that did not know what Lyra was, or what Corin was, or what words were. Something that knew only how to fight, and how to be afraid, and how to roar.
She did not move.
She made herself not move. She stayed on her knees on the felt with her hands open on them, and her face as still as she could make it, and she looked at him. Not at his teeth. At his eyes.
"Corin," she said. Very quietly.
The teeth stayed bared.
"It's all right," she said. "It's all right. I'm not going to hurt you. Nobody's going to hurt you. You're on the Glass. You're with us. It's all right."
She did not know if he heard any of it. If he heard, it was only noise. She knew that. But she said it anyway. Because it was what you said. Because she had nothing else.
And slowly, very slowly, the lip came down.
Not because he understood. She could see that he did not understand. But because she had not moved. Because her hands were open. Because her voice was low and went on and on and did not go up or get sharp.
The teeth went away.
He looked at her. Wary. Still panting.
And then his eyes went past her. Over her shoulder. To something behind her.
To Oren.
And he flinched.
Oren was standing a few steps back, where he had got up from the glass. Big. Gray-bearded. His face red and his chest still heaving. And his hand on his sword hilt. Not drawing. Just resting there, the way it always rested there. Out of habit.
Corin looked at the hand on the sword hilt. And his whole body tightened. The hips started to turn.
The silent man's arm tightened across his chest.
"Oren," said Lyra, without turning round. "Your hand."
Oren looked down. At his own hand on his own sword. And took it away, fast, as if the hilt had burned him. And held both his hands out, open, empty, the way Lyra was holding hers.
"Sorry," he said. Rough. "Sorry, lad. Sorry."
Corin's body loosened. A little.
Lyra let out her breath. It shook all the way out.
She did not know what to do. She did not know what anyone could do. He did not know words. You could not talk him back. You could not tell him who he was. He did not know what who was.
She looked round at the others. At Oren with his open hands. At Kestrel. At Vespera, kneeling in the spilled needles, gray-faced.
"What do we do?" she said. And her voice was a child's voice. "What do we do?"
Giving
It was Vespera who answered.
She had not moved from where she knelt, at Corin's right side, with her needles spilled round her on the felt. She was gathering them up. Slowly. One at a time. Picking each one up off the hot felt between her blue finger and thumb and laying it back on its cloth, in its place. Longest to finest. Her hands were not quite steady. But they did not stop.
"We give him words," she said.
Lyra looked at her.
"You can't take on the Glass," said Vespera. She did not look up. She laid another needle on the cloth. "Nobody can. Not him, not her, not us. That's the rule. Nothing comes off you here." Another needle. "But giving works. Kestrel says. I said. You saw it yourself, with the water." She glanced at Lyra, just once, sharp. "You think I didn't see you watching her give it to the boy? I see everything, girl. I'm a needlewoman."
Lyra did not answer.
"So we give," said Vespera. She laid the last needle down. The finest one. Thin as a hair. "He's lost his words. She took them. Or he gave them, with her, in there. It doesn't matter which. They've gone into that light, wherever it is, and the Glass won't give them back. You can't put a thing back that's been given away. The page says so." Her mouth went thin. "You can't put back what you don't give."
"Then what—"
"So we give him new ones," said Vespera. "Not his own. Not back. New. From outside. In ink." She looked up, at last, at Corin's face. At the wary eyes, going from her to Lyra to the silent man and back. "Ink is giving. I told him that. I told him a dozen times. It's the only magic I know that works the wrong way round. You don't burn anything to do it. You don't take anything. You put something on. Into the skin. And it stays."
"Will it work?" said Oren. Low. "Out here?"
"I don't know," said Vespera. "Nobody's ever tried it out here. Nobody's ever needed to." She opened the little gold-rimmed pot at her belt, and looked into it. Lyra could not see what was inside. Something that glinted. "But it's the only thing I've got. And the Glass doesn't mind giving. So I'll give him words. On his skin. As many as he'll hold." She looked at Lyra. "And maybe, if there's enough of them, and they're the right ones, he'll read them. Not with his eyes. With the rest of him. And find his way back along them. Like a man following a rope in the dark."
No one said anything for a moment.
Then Kestrel, who had been standing at the edge of the ring with her spear grounded, spoke. In the southern tongue.
"Then give him a lot," she said.
Vespera looked at her.
"A lot," said Kestrel. "Not a few. Not the important ones only. A lot. Everything you can." She touched the gray line on her face. "We carry the dead because one breath is not enough. One handprint is not enough. You need many. Many breaths, from many people. Many handprints on the tent. So that the one who is carried has many hands to hold them up." She looked at Corin, wary, panting, under the silent man's arm. "Give him a lot. From all of us. So he has many hands."
Vespera looked at her for a long moment.
Then she nodded. Once. Short.
"Shirt," she said. To Oren. "Get his shirt open. And hold his arms. You and—" She looked at the silent man. Hesitated. "—and him. If he'll do it."
The silent man did not look at her. But his arm, across Corin's chest, did not move away.
"He'll do it," said Tansy.
She had come into the ring. Lyra had not seen her. She was standing just behind the silent man, with her chalk stub in her fist and her face white and fierce under the knife-cut hair.
"He'll do it," she said again. "He's already doing it. Look at him."
Lyra looked. The man was lying half across Corin on the felt, his long thin body heavy and still, his bearded face turned away from all of them, toward the north. His arm across Corin's chest. And his other hand, Lyra saw now, had found Corin's right hand. The gray one. The one that had touched the helmet.
And was holding it. Loosely. The long thin fingers round the gray ones.
Not gripping. Holding.
The way you hold the hand of someone who is asleep.
The False Trail
But before Vespera could begin, Arran came back from the swell.
He came fast. Not running; nobody ran on the Glass at noon. But walking as fast as a man can walk without running, with his long legs eating the hot glass and his eye hide pushed up and his face, under the burned stubble of his eyebrows, set and pale.
He went straight to Kestrel. And spoke low, in the round speech. Fast. Kestrel listened. Lyra watched her face. It did not change. But her hand on her spear tightened.
Then Kestrel turned to the rest of them. In the southern tongue.
"They are through the Fold Hills," she said. "Most of the way. Slower than we were. They do not know the short way, and some places are too narrow for them, and they go round. Or over." She paused. "But they are coming. Straight. Toward him."
"How long?" said Oren.
"By dusk they will be out of the hills. On the open Glass. Here." Kestrel looked at the sun. "And then the cold will stop them. If we are lucky. If the sun goes down before they reach us."
No one said anything.
"There is more," said Kestrel.
She looked at Arran. And Arran, still breathing hard, said it himself. In the southern tongue. Slowly.
"There's a man with them."
Lyra felt her skin go cold under the hot sun.
"Behind the iron ones," said Arran. "Walking. On his own feet. Not iron. A man. I saw him from the top of the swell, on the far side of the Fold Hills, where they come out on a ridge. Thin. A dark coat, long. A hat." He swallowed. "He had something in his hand. Something small and bright. A stick, maybe. Silver. He kept holding it out in front of him, pointing it at the ground, and shaking it. The way you shake a clock that's stopped. And then putting it away. And then taking it out and doing it again."
"A rod," said Vespera. Very quietly. "A mage's rod."
"It does nothing here," said Kestrel. "Nothing works here. He shakes it because he does not believe it." She was quiet a moment. "But he has eyes. The iron ones do not need eyes. They go to him the way water goes downhill. But a man does. A man follows tracks. A man looks for where we went, and why, and who with." She looked at the silent man, lying across Corin on the felt. And at Tansy. "And a man can count."
"Then we give him something to look at," said Oren.
Kestrel looked at him.
"A trail," said Oren. "A false one. Going off somewhere else. Somewhere useless. The iron will come straight here anyway, we can't stop that. But the man won't know the iron's coming straight. He'll think it's following the trail. He'll follow the trail. Behind it." His jaw moved. "And when the iron stops at dusk, and the man looks round, he'll be a mile off the wrong way, in the dark, on his own, on the Glass. With his silver stick that doesn't work."
Kestrel was quiet for a moment.
Then she smiled. Very slightly. At one corner of her mouth. Lyra had never seen her smile like that. It was not a nice smile.
"Arran," she said. "Sef."
She gave them their orders in the round speech. Lyra did not understand the words. But she understood what she saw.
Arran and Sef took a goatskin. One of the empty water skins, flattened. And tied a thong to it, so it would drag. And went off northwest, away from the Road, away from the horse, away from the way to the Eye. Dragging the skin behind them on the hot glass so that it left a long faint smear. Not a clear track; the Glass did not take tracks. But a mark, in the dust that lay thinly here and there. A line. Something a man with eyes might see.
And Sef carried a crust of bread, and broke bits off it as he went, and dropped them. Here. And there. A crumb on the glass every so often, the way a careless walker drops things. The way a hungry man drops things, eating as he goes.
"Make it look like a man who limps," said Kestrel after them. In the southern tongue. So they would all hear.
And Arran turned round, a little way off, in the white glare. And grinned. The old grin, the first since the humming lump. With his burned eyebrows going up.
And limped.
He did it beautifully. Lyra almost laughed, even then, even with Corin lying on the felt not knowing his own name. Arran swung his left leg out stiff, the way Corin did. And leaned on an invisible crutch. And went off across the glass like that, pat, step-step, pat, step-step, with the goatskin dragging behind him and Sef beside him dropping crumbs.
"Too much," called Oren after him. "Less. He's not a dancing bear."
Arran made it less. Still limping. Still grinning. Getting smaller across the white glass, going northwest, toward nothing.
Kestrel watched them go.
"They will go far," she said. "Until the sun is low. Then they will turn, and come back to us by another way, on the hard glass where nothing marks." She touched the gray line on her face. "If they come back before dark, we will all be lucky."
"And if they don't?"
Kestrel did not answer.
She turned round, to the felt, to Corin lying under the silent man's arm with his wary animal eyes. And to Vespera, kneeling beside him with her needles laid out in a row and her little gold-rimmed pot open.
"Begin," said Kestrel.
