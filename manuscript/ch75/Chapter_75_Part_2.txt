Chapter 75: Grounding, Part 2 of 5: What First
What First
Vespera did not begin at once.
She sat back on her heels on the felt, with the needles in their row in front of her and the little gold-rimmed pot open beside them, and looked at Lyra.
"What goes first?" she said.
Lyra did not understand.
"Words," said Vespera. "You heard her. A lot of them. But one first. One at the top. The first thing he'll find, if he finds anything." Her blue fingers touched the edge of the cloth, very lightly. "I can ink anything. But I don't know what he needs. I knew him in Hook Lane. I knew the man who came in at the back door with a jar under his coat and sat on my stool and let me put marks on him. I don't know the man you know."
She held out her hand. Palm up. The way she had held it out to Tansy for her feet.
"You do," she said. "So you say. And I'll write."
Lyra looked at the hand.
She could not write. That was the first thing she thought, and it went through her like cold water. She could not write. She could not make a single letter. She could not even draw an L in the grit. Whatever went on him, she could not put it there herself. Not one stroke of it.
She could only say it.
And someone else would make it real.
She thought of Corin, at the grit, two days ago. Drawing a clean square L beside her wandering one. I can write it for you. Whenever you want. You only have to say it, and I'll put it down.
Now he could not. Now he did not know what an L was.
Now it was her turn to say it. And someone else's turn to put it down.
"The book," she said. Her voice was not steady. "Give me the book."
Oren found it. Where she had put it, on the felt, by Corin's head, when he went in. The green book. Ledger VIII. With the worn gold tree on the cover, and the cracked spine, and the stub of pencil pushed down into it.
She opened it.
Not to the last page. Not to IF I COME BACK WRONG, READ ME THIS, and the lines under it, that she could not look at yet without her throat closing. To the first page. The very first page Corin had written on, after the pages of her mother's people's names in faded brown ink.
She had seen him write it. In the crypt at Saint Bride's, the night her father died. By one candle, with his gray hands shaking. She had been sitting on the cold stone floor against the wall, not crying, not anything, and she had watched him write it, because watching him write was easier than thinking.
BEGUN AT SAINT BRIDE'S, THE NIGHT AURELIAN THORNE DIED.
And under it. In the same square black capitals. Pressed hard into the paper, so hard the pencil had nearly gone through.
MY NAME IS CORIN VANE.
Lyra read it out loud.
She read it slowly. Clearly. The way you read the first line of a letter to someone who cannot read. Every word separate.
"My. Name. Is. Corin. Vane."
On the felt, Corin's eyes moved to her. At the sound. Wary. Not knowing.
Vespera nodded. Once.
"Your name is Corin Vane," she said. Not my. Your. Turning it round. Giving it to him. "That's first."
She picked up the finest needle.
"Shirt," she said.
Oren knelt at Corin's left side and began, very carefully, to undo the buttons of his shirt. Corin's whole body went tight at the touch. The hips began to turn. And the silent man, lying half across him, shifted his weight, just a little, just enough, and the turn went nowhere. Corin's lips drew back off his teeth. He hissed.
"It's all right," said Oren. Low. Rough. His big fingers were not good at small buttons. "It's all right, lad. Nobody's going to hurt you. We're only giving you something."
He got the shirt open. And pulled it back off Corin's chest. And Lyra saw his chest in the white sun.
She had seen it before. In the city, once, when Vespera inked the old marks. At Last Thorn, when he was ill. But she had not looked. Not properly. Now she looked.
He was thin. Thinner than she had thought. The ribs stood out under the skin, every one, like the ribs of the glass soldiers in the Trench. The skin was pale, where the sun had never touched it. And on either side, the gray came up his arms and stopped. The line was where Vespera had measured it, a hand's width below the shoulder, less a finger. But there was a shadow above it. A faint grayness, under the skin, like a bruise that has not come up yet.
And there were marks.
Old ones. Vespera's, from the city. Small lines of ink here and there, faded to gray-blue. Lyra could not read them. They were in the old letters, the ones Vespera used, that looked like little bird tracks. And there were scars. Old thin white scars, along the ribs on the left side. Three of them. Long and straight and evenly spaced, like the marks of claws. Or the marks of a cane.
Lyra had never seen those before.
She did not ask. Corin could not have told her.
Vespera looked at the scars for a moment too. Her mouth went very thin. Then she looked away from them, and dipped the finest needle into the gold-rimmed pot.
It came out glittering.
"Hold his arms," she said. "Both of you. And don't let go, whatever he does."
Oren took the left arm. The silent man already had the right.
Vespera bent over Corin's chest. Just below the collarbone. On the left side. Over the heart.
And began.
Inking
Lyra had watched Vespera ink before. In the back room in Hook Lane, with the lamp lit and the shutters closed. At Last Thorn, in the tent of hands, the night before the iron came. She knew how it went.
She had never watched it like this.
Vespera bent over Corin's chest in the white noon sun with the needle held between her blue thumb and two fingers, the way you hold a pen. Close. Her face a hand's width from his skin. Her eyes narrowed. And the needle went in.
Not deep. Barely at all. Just under the skin. A tiny quick prick, and out, and in again a hair's width along. Prick, and out. Prick, and out. Fast. So fast Lyra could hardly follow it. Like a bird pecking seed. Like rain starting on a window.
And where it went in, it left the ink.
The ink was the strangest thing. Lyra had not seen it before. It was not the black ink, or the blue, or the deep forest green from the little bottles on Vespera's belt. It came out of the little gold-rimmed pot on the needle's tip glittering. Silver-white, like crushed frost. Like ground glass. And when it went under Corin's skin, it lit up. For one moment. A tiny bright point of blue, under the skin, like a spark seen through ice. Shining. And then, as Lyra watched, the blue faded. Sank. Went darker. Through blue, to deep blue, to black. And stayed black. A tiny black dot under the skin, where the needle had been.
And beside it, another. Blue, and then black.
And another.
A line of them. Growing. Prick, blue, black. Prick, blue, black. Becoming a stroke. Becoming a letter.
"What is it?" said Oren. Low. He was kneeling at Corin's left, with both his big hands round Corin's left arm, holding it flat to the felt. "The ink. What's in it?"
"Glass," said Vespera.
She did not look up. Prick, blue, black.
"Ground glass. From under the Edge, where it's thinnest. Old Hask gave it me, years ago. When I came north the first time." Prick. "Crushed fine as flour, and mixed with the black, and a drop of something I won't tell you." Prick. "I've kept it in that pot thirty years. Never used it. Never knew what it was for." Her mouth went thin. "Hask said I'd know when."
Corin's body jerked under them at every prick.
Not much. Not a fight. A flinch. The way a horse's skin flinches under a fly. Every time the needle went in, his chest twitched, and his hands closed, and his lips came back off his teeth. And every time, Oren's hands and the silent man's hands held him. Not hard. Steady. The way you hold a child still for a splinter.
He did not roar again.
He lay on his back on the felt under the iron hoof and stared up at the white sky, and breathed fast, and flinched, and did not roar.
Vespera did not look at his face.
Lyra noticed that after a while. Vespera never once looked at his face. She looked at his chest. At the skin. At the needle and the ink and the line growing under it. Only that. As if the face were not there at all. As if she were inking a piece of cloth, or a page.
And she was humming.
Lyra did not notice that at first either. It was so low. Under the scratch of the needle, under the hot still air, under the ticking that came now and then through the glass from very far off. A hum. Low and tuneless. Not tuneless. A tune. One Lyra did not know. It went up and down, slowly, without words, the way a mother hums to a baby when she has forgotten the words of the song and remembers only its shape.
Vespera hummed, and pricked, and wiped.
That was the other thing. Every few pricks, Vespera stopped, and licked her blue thumb, quick, the way a seamstress licks her thread. And wiped the line she had made. One quick firm stroke of the wet thumb across Corin's skin. Wiping away the beads of blood and the loose glitter of ink. And then went on. Prick, prick, prick. Lick. Wipe. Prick.
Lyra had seen her do it a hundred times in Hook Lane. She had never thought about it. She thought about it now. About Vespera's tongue on her own thumb, and her thumb on Corin's blood. About how close you had to be, to do that. How little you had to mind.
The first word was finished.
YOUR.
It was small. Smaller than Lyra had expected. Each letter no bigger than the nail of her little finger. Square capitals. Black under the pale skin, below his left collarbone, over the heart. With a faint pink rim round each stroke where the skin was angry.
The letters leaned.
Lyra saw it, and her throat closed. They leaned back. A little. Every one of them. The way Corin's own letters leaned, in the Ledger. As if Vespera had looked at the Ledger and copied his hand. Or as if her own hand, at the end, could not help it.
Vespera wiped the word once more. And looked at it. And dipped the needle.
"Next," she said. Without looking up. "Say it again, girl. The next word. Don't make me wait."
"Name," said Lyra.
Prick, blue, black.
Lyra Chooses
It took a long time to put four words on a man.
YOUR NAME IS CORIN VANE.
When it was done, it lay across the top of his chest, below the left collarbone, in two short lines. Black and small and leaning. With the pink rim round each stroke, and a few beads of blood that Vespera wiped away with her thumb. Lyra looked at it and could not look away.
It looked like a label. Like the labels on the jars in the Harvesting Section, that Corin used to read to her, in the city, laughing at the clerks' spelling. A name, written on the outside of a thing, so you would know what was inside.
"Next," said Vespera.
Lyra opened her mouth. And closed it.
She did not know.
She had the book. She could read it all. Every page. She could read him the whole of his life since Saint Bride's, the way he had written it down. But that was not what Vespera meant. Vespera meant: what goes on his skin. What is so important that it has to be written on his body, where he cannot lose it, where he will see it every morning when he takes off his shirt.
There was not room for everything.
There was not even room for much.
"How many?" said Lyra. "How much can you—"
"As much as he'll hold," said Vespera. "And as long as the light lasts. And as long as my hands do." She flexed her blue fingers. "Not the whole book, girl. Choose."
Lyra looked down at him.
At his face. At the wary eyes, staring at the white sky. At his chest, rising and falling, with the four words on it.
Choose.
She had chosen things for him before. Small things. Which road. Which inn. Which of the things she told him, each day, to give him first. One thing a day. She had been careful with those. Choosing the ones that would not hurt. The heron. The sword at his throat.
This was not that.
This was choosing what he was.
"You are twenty-eight," she said.
Her voice came out very low. She cleared her throat. Said it again.
"You are twenty-eight. Put that."
Vespera's needle went in.
YOU ARE TWENTY-EIGHT.
Because he had not known. For a long time. He had not known how old he was. He had asked her, once, on the road north, in the snow, and she had not known either, and he had looked so lost. And then Vespera's jar had shown him. The spring night, the rain, the cottage. Twenty-eight years ago. He had come out of it and written it in the book, very carefully, with his hand shaking. I WAS BORN 28 YEARS AGO. He had looked at the number for a long time. He could not feel it. But he could see it. And he had said, it's nice to know, isn't it. How old you are. Everyone else seems to know.
Prick, blue, black.
"You were born in Oakhaven," said Lyra.
Vespera's needle stopped. Just for a breath. Then went on.
YOU WERE BORN IN OAKHAVEN.
Lyra did not know if that was right. To put that on him. Oakhaven was a word that made people go quiet, and shut doors. It was the village that burned of fever. It was the name on a crate in a vault. It was everything nobody would tell them. But it was his. He had seen it, in the jar. The cottage and the river and the man at the door watching the road. It was where he was from. And a man should know where he is from.
She watched the word go on. OAKHAVEN. Black, under his skin.
"You used to fear the dark," she said.
Vespera looked up. For the first time. At Lyra. A quick sharp look.
Lyra did not explain. She did not know how she knew it. He had never told her. He had never said. But she had seen him, in the first weeks. In the city. In the crypt at Saint Bride's, and the hay loft at Thistle Ford, and the inns on the road north. How he always lit a candle before he lay down. How he slept with his face to the light. How, if the candle went out in the night, he woke. Every time. And lay very still, breathing too fast, until he found the flint.
She had never asked. He had never said.
But she had seen him on the Glass. At night. In the heap. With the stars above and below and no candle anywhere. Lying on his back in the black, with his hand flat on the Glass and a light under it. Not afraid. Not at all.
"Put it," said Lyra. "You used to fear the dark. And then under it: Not anymore."
Vespera looked at her a moment longer. Then bent to the skin.
YOU USED TO FEAR THE DARK.
NOT ANYMORE.
The sun had moved. The shade that Brin and Oda had picked up again, and held over them on its two poles, threw its gold square of light a little to the east now. The heat was terrible. Sweat ran down Vespera's face and dripped off the end of her sharp nose, and she wiped it on her shoulder without stopping. Oren's face was red and wet. The silent man's face was dry. He did not seem to feel the heat at all.
"Lyra Thorne," said Lyra.
She heard herself say it and her face went hot.
Vespera did not look up. "Just the name?"
"No." Lyra swallowed. "Lyra Thorne. Partner."
Prick, blue, black.
LYRA THORNE. PARTNER.
She had thought about other words. She had thought about them for a long time, while the needle went in and out on the line before. There were other words she could have said. She did not say them. They were not hers to put on him. Not like this. Not when he could not say yes or no.
Partner was true. Partner was what they had been, in the city, before. Partner was what the Ministry's own papers had called them, when they hunted them. Partner was safe.
"And under it," said Lyra. "No lies between you."
Vespera's needle hung over the skin.
"That's a promise," said Vespera. Quietly. "Not a fact."
"I know."
"You're putting a promise on a man who can't make one back."
"I know," said Lyra. "I'm making it. Not him. Put it."
Vespera looked at her for a long moment. Then something in her thin face changed. Softened, a little, round the eyes. And she bent to the skin.
NO LIES BETWEEN YOU.
Lyra watched it go on. And thought: there. Now I can't. Now I can never. And felt something settle in her, low down, cold and heavy and right, like a stone set into a wall.
The Heron Line
There was one more she wanted.
She knew it. She had known it since the start, since Vespera said choose. It had been sitting at the back of her mouth the whole time, behind all the others, while she chose the age and the village and the dark and her own name. Waiting.
And she did not want to say it.
Because it was foolish. Because it was small. Because it was not a fact, like his name, or a promise, like no lies. It was only a thing she had said to him once, on the road north, in the snow, when he fell over. A joke, almost. Not even a joke. A thing that had come out of her mouth before she had thought.
You walk like a heron. Even when you fall over.
He had written it in the book that night. She had watched him. In the loft at Thistle Ford, by the light of the small frosted window, with the cows shifting below. He had written it very carefully, in his square leaning capitals, and then looked at it for a long time, and said nothing. And she had pretended not to see.
And today, on the Glass, she had given him the heron again. The real ones, at the bottom of the garden on Crown Walk. Standing on one leg on the wall with her mother, in good shoes. He had written that down too. THAT'S WHERE THE DANCING STARTED.
And the bone heron was under her coat. Against her chest. Warm.
She did not know why it mattered so much. It did not seem like a thing that should go on a man's skin. Next to his name. Next to the place he was born. It seemed like a thing you would be ashamed to ask for.
"What?" said Vespera.
She had stopped. With the needle lifted. Looking at Lyra's face.
"Nothing," said Lyra.
"It's not nothing. You've got a face like a girl who's lost her shoe at a dance." Vespera wiped her forehead on her shoulder. "Say it. I haven't got all day. The light's going."
It was not. It was the middle of the afternoon, and the light was white and terrible. But Lyra did not say so.
She looked down at Corin. At the words on his chest. YOUR NAME IS CORIN VANE. YOU ARE TWENTY-EIGHT. YOU WERE BORN IN OAKHAVEN. YOU USED TO FEAR THE DARK. NOT ANYMORE. LYRA THORNE. PARTNER. NO LIES BETWEEN YOU.
All true. All important. All the right things.
And not one of them was him.
That was the thing she understood, looking at them. They were the facts of him. The bones. The label on the jar. But they were not him. Not the man who went red to the ears. Not the man who got angry for a dead scribe with a sword at his throat. Not the man who wrote SHE SLEPT because it was important.
If he came back, and read these, he would know his name and his age and where he was born and who she was.
He would not know what he was like.
"You walk like a heron," said Lyra.
She said it to the felt. Not looking at anyone. Very fast, the way you say a thing you are ashamed of.
There was a silence.
She made herself look up. At Vespera.
Vespera was looking at her. Not sharp. Not anything. Just looking. With the needle held still over Corin's skin and the sweat running down her face.
"Put it," said Lyra. "Please. Put you walk like a heron."
She waited for Vespera to say something. To say it was foolish. To say there was no room. To ask what it meant.
Vespera did not.
She looked at Lyra a moment longer. Then she looked down. At Corin's chest. And moved the needle. Not under the other words. Lower. And to the side. Off the chest, onto the ribs, on the left. Below the heart. In a place on its own.
And began.
YOU WALK LIKE A HERON.
Smaller than the others. Lyra saw that. Vespera made the letters smaller. Neater. More careful. As if they were the most important ones. Or as if they were a secret.
Oren, holding Corin's left arm, watched them go on. He did not say anything. But Lyra saw his mouth move, under his beard. Not quite a smile.
"He does, too," said Oren. Very quietly. To nobody. "Saints help him. All legs."
Vespera wiped the line with her thumb. And looked at it.
"There," she said. And then, very low, not to Lyra, not to anyone: "That one he'll read first. You watch."
