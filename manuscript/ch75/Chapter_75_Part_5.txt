Chapter 75: Grounding, Part 5 of 5: Water for the Man
Water for the Man
Before the heap, Tansy came with the water.
She came across the glass with a small goatskin held against her chest in both arms, the way a child carries a cat that does not want to be carried. The blue lamp was hooked by its loop of wire on one finger and swung as she walked, so that her shadow swung too, huge and thin, across the hides and the shade poles and the sleeping shape of Arran.
She went straight past Lyra. She did not even look at her. She went to the silent man.
"You didn't drink," she said to him. Crossly. "All day. I watched. You never drank once."
The man did not look up.
"You forget," said Tansy. She knelt down in front of him on the glass and put the lamp down carefully beside her knee, where it would not tip. "He forgets," she said to Lyra, over her shoulder, as if Lyra had asked. "Nobody forgets everything. He forgets to drink and he forgets to eat and he forgets to sleep. He'd just sit there till he was dead, if you let him. So I don't let him."
She pulled the stopper out of the goatskin with her teeth. Then she held it up to the man's mouth. Not to his hands. His hands were busy, holding Corin's wrist. She held it to his mouth, the way you hold a bottle for a lamb.
"Drink," she said.
And the man drank.
Lyra watched him do it. He did not let go of Corin. He did not even move his hands. He only bent his head forward a little, toward the goatskin, and opened his mouth, and Tansy tipped it, very carefully, and he swallowed. Once. Twice. Three times. Lyra watched his throat move, long and thin, in the blue light. Then Tansy took it away.
"That's enough," she said. "Kestrel says only that much. Because of the four nights." She pushed the stopper back in with the heel of her hand. "Now eat."
She took a strip of the dried goat out of her pocket. It was black and hard and curled up at the ends, and it had bits of fluff from her pocket stuck to it. She picked the fluff off, one bit at a time, with great care. Then she tore the strip in half, and held one half up to his mouth.
The man looked at it.
Then he looked at her. For the first time. His eyes in the blue light were strange. Lyra could not have said what color they were. Pale. Very pale. And very still, like water at the bottom of a deep well. They looked at Tansy for a long moment.
And then he took the meat in his teeth, very gently, the way a horse takes an apple off a flat hand. And chewed.
"Good," said Tansy. "Good. You're getting better at it."
She sat back on her heels, satisfied, and ate the other half herself.
Lyra did not know what to say. So she said the thing that came first.
"How long have you been looking after him?"
Tansy thought about it, chewing.
"Two winters," she said. "Since I got lost in the Ridges. I was crying. I was very thirsty, and I was crying, which you shouldn't do, because it wastes water. And he was just standing there. On his own. Not doing anything. So I followed him, all night, and he went to a shrine, and there was water there, and bread. And I've been with him since." She swallowed the meat. "He never said anything. Not once. So I called him Nobody. Because that's who was there, when I looked for somebody. Nobody." She shrugged. "He doesn't mind. I asked."
"He answered?"
"No." Tansy looked at her as if she was very slow. "That's how I know he doesn't mind."
Lyra almost laughed again. She did not. She looked at the man instead. He had gone back to looking down at Corin's wrist. As if Tansy had never been there. As if he had never looked up at all.
"He likes the Vane," said Tansy. Lower. "I can tell. He never held anybody before. Not ever. Not me. Not anybody. He just sat. But the Vane, he holds." She frowned at the man, thinking. "Maybe he knows him. From before. From wherever he was, before I found him."
"Maybe," said Lyra.
"I asked him that too," said Tansy. "He didn't answer that either." She picked up her lamp by its wire. Stood. Looked down at the two of them, the man and Corin, in the blue light, with the hides spread out all round and the stars coming down to the edges of the glass. "But I think that one," she said slowly, "I think that one he didn't answer different."
Then she went off with her lamp swinging, to help Kestrel make the heap.
Lyra sat and looked at the silent man for a long time after that. At his long, pale, careful hands. At his face, which did not move. At the way he held Corin's wrist, not tight, not loose, the way you hold something you have been given to keep and are afraid to drop.
She did not know who he was. None of them did.
But she thought, for the first time, that perhaps he knew who Corin was. That perhaps, all those years standing alone in the Ridges, he had been waiting for somebody. And somebody had come walking across the Glass at last, with a stick under one arm and gray hands, and the man had got up and walked beside him, on his bad side, as if it was the place he had always been meant to be.
Night
They did not walk that night.
It was the first night on the Glass that they did not walk. Kestrel said it, standing in the middle of the ring of shades with her arms folded, when the stars were all out. We stay. Arran could not walk. Corin could not walk. The iron ones were frozen on the open glass, far enough. And the thin man with the silver rod was out in the northwest somewhere, alone in the cold, following a goatskin and a trail of crumbs.
Nobody argued.
So they made the heap.
Lyra had made it every night on the Glass so far, and she still did not like it. Nobody liked it. It was not a thing you liked. It was a thing you did so as not to die. The hide shades came down off their poles and were spread flat on the glass, two thick, fur side up. Then everybody lay down on top of them close together. Very close. Closer than strangers ever lie. Back to front, knees in the backs of knees, faces in the backs of necks, arms over whoever was in front. And then the rest of the hides went over the top, and the felt blankets, and the coats, and anything else they had. And they lay there in the dark under it all like puppies in a basket, and breathed on each other, and kept each other alive.
The cold on the Glass at night was not like any cold Lyra had known. Not like winter in the city, wet and gray and creeping. It was dry. It was clean. It came up out of the ground the moment the sun went, as if the Glass had been holding its breath all day and now let it out. It did not smell of anything. It did not make any sound. It just went into you. Through the soles of your boots. Through the seat of your trousers. Through your coat and your shirt and your skin, until it reached the bone and settled there and began, very quietly, to stop your heart.
That was what Kestrel said. It stops your heart. Quietly. You don't feel it. You just get sleepy and warm, and then you are dead in the morning with a smile on your face.
So nobody slept alone on the Glass. Nobody.
Tonight they put Corin in the middle.
Arran was on one side of him, with his wrapped foot sticking out and Kestrel's knees curled into the back of it. And the silent man was on the other side, still holding the right wrist. He had not let go all day. He did not let go now. He lay down when Corin was laid down, and fitted himself along Corin's back, long and thin as a rake, and put his arm over, and held on.
Lyra was in front. Facing Corin. Their faces a hand apart.
She had not meant to be. She had meant to go further out, where she usually went. On the edge, by Oda, by Oren. But when the heap was made, there was a space there, in front of Corin, and Tansy was pointing at it, and Vespera was looking at her, and Oren was looking at her, and none of them said anything. So she lay down there.
The hides came down over them. The dark came down.
And it was very quiet.
She could hear everyone breathing. Oda on the far side, quick and light, already asleep. Brin beyond her, slow and heavy. Tansy somewhere near the bottom, talking to herself in a whisper about goats. Oren's breath, deep and rough, the breath of a big man, somewhere behind her. Marrit, muttering her ward-words, the way she did every night, very low, so that they were more like a hum than words. Sef. Kestrel. Vespera's thin old breath, like wind under a door.
And Corin's.
It was in her face. Warm. Steady. A little fast. Not a sleeper's breath. Not quite. She thought he was somewhere in between. Down in the well, as Vespera said. Hand over hand.
She could not see him. It was too dark under the hides. But she could feel him. His forehead was very near hers. His knees were against her knees. And under the coat that had been put over him, under his shirt, on his chest, were the words. She could not feel them. But she knew they were there. She had watched every letter go in.
YOUR NAME IS CORIN VANE.
LYRA THORNE. PARTNER.
She lay and looked at the dark where his face was.
Talk to him, Vespera had said. About little things. Stupid things. The things that are yours and not hers.
Lyra did not know how to do that. She had never been good at talking. That was his job. He talked. She hit people. That was how it had worked from the very first night, in the dark of Old Joss's press room, with the great iron presses standing round them like sleeping animals and the gray light from the high dirty windows lying along her blade. She had come down off a stool with a red book in her arms and put the point of her sword against his throat, just under the jaw, where the pulse was. And he had not run. He had stood there with the cold edge on his skin and told her, very calmly, about a dead scribe called Pell, who laughed like a donkey.
She almost laughed. In the dark. Under the hides.
It was not even a month ago. It felt like years.
"I nearly cut your throat," she whispered. "The first time. Do you remember? You didn't even blink."
He did not answer. Of course he did not. But his breath changed. Just a little.
So she kept going.
She did not tell him anything important. She did what Vespera said. She told him stupid things. Small things. Things that were theirs. She told him about Old Joss in his nightshirt and his leather apron, with his bald speckled head shining in the candlelight, shouting because he was deaf, and how they had both told him to hush at the same moment and then looked at each other. She told him about running through the snow in the city with her boots full of water. She told him about Silas, folding himself out through the arches of the crypt like a heron through reeds, with his crooked spectacles and his bag, and how Corin had once said Silas looked like a heron, and she had said, So do you.
She told him about the hair.
She had not meant to. She had never told him properly. It was in the Ledger, in his own capitals, because he had written it down when he still could: SHE CUT HER HAIR AND SOLD IT FOR MY LEG. But she had never said it out loud. She told him now. How she had sat on a crate in the dark in the arches with Silas's knife and cut it off, all of it, the long dark weight of it, in big ugly hanks, and tied it with string, and carried it to a wig-maker in a back street, and come back with the money for Silas and her head cold for the first time in her life. How the back of her neck had felt naked for days. How she kept putting her hand up to push hair out of her eyes that was not there.
And how she had stood in the doorway afterward, with his new boots swinging from her hand, and not turned round, and said it for both of them. It'll grow. Because hair grows. Because it was the one thing that came back.
She told him about the heron.
That was the stupidest thing of all. She almost did not tell it. It was a Friday. They had been walking somewhere. She did not even remember where now. Somewhere cold. And she had been watching him walk in front of her, with his long legs and his long coat and his head stuck forward and his elbows going, the way he did when he was thinking hard, and she had said, before she could stop herself, You walk like a heron.
And he had not even turned round. He had just said, And you talk like a kettle.
She had wanted to hit him. She had very nearly hit him. And then that night he had written it in the Ledger, in his square capitals, FRIDAY. SHE SAYS I WALK LIKE A HERON. I SAID SHE TALKS LIKE A KETTLE, and she had taken the pencil out of his hand and written under it, in her small quick hand, the hand she did not have anymore, A kettle is useful. A heron just stands about.
She could not write that now. She could not write anything now. It was gone. She had given it.
But she could still say it.
"A kettle is useful," she whispered. "A heron just stands about."
She would have given anything to hear him answer.
She stopped talking. Her throat was tight. She put her forehead against his, in the dark. Very gently. His skin was warm.
"Come back," she whispered. "Please. Come back. I don't care if she comes with you. Bring her. Bring all of them. Just come back."
Nothing.
The breath in her face. Steady. A little fast.
Above the hides, the stars went on turning. She could not see them. But she knew they were there, the way she knew the words were there. All of them. More stars than there were in the city, more than there were anywhere. She had lain awake on the edge of the heap on the second night and looked at them through a gap in the hides, and there had been so many that the sky looked white, as if somebody had spilled salt over black cloth.
She thought of that. Of the salt.
And somewhere in thinking of it, she fell asleep.
Kettle?
She woke because something had changed.
She did not know what, at first. She lay very still under the hides, in the warm dark, with the smell of goat and felt and old smoke all round her, and listened. Oda was snoring. Tansy was asleep, a small hot weight somewhere down by Lyra's feet. Oren had turned over; she could feel his big back against hers now instead of his chest. Marrit had stopped muttering. Outside, beyond the hides, the night was so still that she could hear the Glass. That faint, high, endless hum, like a finger going round and round the rim of a cup.
Nothing was wrong.
And then she knew what it was.
The breath in her face had changed.
It was slower. Deeper. Not the quick, shallow, in-between breath of a man down a well. The breath of a man who was awake. Who was lying very still in the dark, a hand's width from her face, and breathing like someone trying to work out where he was.
Lyra did not move.
She was afraid to. She was more afraid than she had been in the trench. More afraid than she had been at noon, when he came up off the glass with the wrong eyes and threw Oren like a sack. Because at noon there had been nothing to lose. He was already gone. Now there was something there. In the dark. Breathing. And she did not know what it was. Whether it was him. Or her. Or the howling thing from the trench. Or nothing, a body that breathed, with the words written on its chest and no one inside to read them.
She made herself do it.
She put her hand up under the hides, slowly, and found the edge, and lifted it. Just a little. Just a gap. And the starlight came in.
It was not much. It was only starlight. But on the Glass, at night, starlight was more than it was anywhere else. It came down out of that white salt-spilled sky and hit the glass all round them and bounced, and came in sideways under the lifted hide, cold and blue and faint, and lay across his face.
His eyes were open.
She had been ready for that. She had told herself, in the dark, his eyes will be open, and they'll be wrong, and you'll have to bear it. She had told herself to be ready for the flat, far-off, silent look. Sola's look. Or the wild, white-rimmed, roaring look of the trench. Or the empty look, which would be the worst.
They were none of those.
They were gray. Ordinary gray. Tired. Red at the rims. A little puffy underneath, the way they always were in the mornings when he had sat up too late with the Ledger. And they were looking at her.
At her. Not through her. Not past her at something on the far side of the world. At her face. And there was something in them that she had not seen since the trench. Since before the trench. Since the night before, on the march, when he had walked in front of her with his crutch under his arm and his head stuck forward.
There was somebody in them.
She could not breathe.
His lips moved. Nothing came out. They were cracked and dry and there was a little dried blood at one corner where he had bitten them at noon. He swallowed. She saw his throat move in the starlight. He tried again.
"Corin," he said.
It was a croak. A dry, broken, hoarse thing, hardly a word at all. Like a door that has not been opened for a long time. But it was a word. It was his name. And he said it the way a man says his own name when he has just found it again, in the dark, in a pocket he forgot he had. Carefully. As if checking it was still the right shape.
"Corin," he said again. Stronger.
Lyra's mouth opened. Nothing came out of hers either.
She felt the silent man move behind him. Felt it through the heap, through the hides, the long thin body lifting a little on one elbow. Felt him go still, and look. And then, very slowly, she felt him let go. For the first time since noon. She saw the long pale hands come away from Corin's right wrist, one finger at a time, and lie down on the hide. Open. Empty.
Corin did not seem to notice. He was looking at Lyra.
He was frowning a little.
The small frown. The one he did when he was reading something hard. When there was a word he did not know on a page, or a line that did not add up, and he was holding the page close to the lamp and going over it again with his eyes, slowly, letter by letter, trying to make it mean something. She had seen that frown a hundred times. Over the Ledger. Over the red book. Over the scraps and lists and torn pages and letters of the whole long cold winter.
He was doing it to her face.
As if her face was a page. As if there was something written on it, in a hand he almost knew, and he could nearly read it, and not quite.
His eyes went over her. Her forehead. Her eyes. Her mouth. Her cropped hair, sticking up on one side where she had slept on it. Back to her eyes.
And then the frown changed. Not went. Changed. The way a page changes when the lamp is moved and suddenly you can see what is under the writing.
His cracked lips moved again.
"Kettle?" he said.
Puzzled. Uncertain. Hoarse as a crow. As if he was not sure it was a word. As if he was not sure what it meant, or why it had come up out of the dark in him at the sight of her, or whether he had got it right. Like a man who opens a drawer in a house he does not remember and finds, lying in it, a thing he knows is his.
Lyra made a sound.
She did not know what it was. It was not a word. It came up out of her chest all at once, too big for her throat, and burst out of her mouth, and it was a laugh. It was a laugh. And then it was not. And then it was both. She was laughing and crying at the same time, under the hides, in the cold blue starlight, with her face a hand away from his, and she could not stop. She did not try to stop. She put both her hands on his face, on his cheeks, on the rough stubble and the dried salt and the cracked lips, and held on, and laughed, and cried, and the tears ran sideways across her nose and into the hide under her head, and she could not stop.
The heap woke up round her. She felt it. Oren lifting his head. Tansy's voice, small and cross and sleepy, What? What is it? Is it the iron? Kestrel's sharp breath. Arran saying something in the southern tongue. Vespera, very far away at the edge, saying nothing at all.
Lyra did not look at any of them.
She was looking at him.
And he was looking at her. Still puzzled. Still frowning that small frown. Watching her laugh and cry as if she was a hard page, and he was going to read it, if it took all night, letter by letter.
She could not stop.
She did not want to.
