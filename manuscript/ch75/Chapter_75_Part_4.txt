Chapter 75: Grounding, Part 4 of 5: Counting What Is Left
Counting What Is Left
When the sun was gone, Kestrel did what she did every evening. She counted.
Lyra had watched her do it on every night on the Glass, and she had come to like watching it, the way you like watching someone who is very good at a small, plain job. A woman mending a net. A man laying bricks. Kestrel did it the same way each time, in the same order, without hurrying, squatting on her heels in the middle of the ring of shades with everything they owned laid out in front of her on a spread hide.
First the water.
The water was in goatskins. Fat ones and thin ones, all tied at the neck with leather cord, all gone dark and shiny with handling. Kestrel lifted each one in turn and weighed it in her two hands, bouncing it a little, with her head on one side, listening to the slosh. She did not open them. She did not need to. She had been weighing water on the Glass since she was smaller than Tansy. She laid the full ones on her left and the half ones in the middle and the empty ones on her right.
There were a lot of empty ones on her right.
Then the food. Strips of dried goat meat, hard and black and salty, tied in bundles. A bag of the flat gray oatcakes that the Ashborn baked on hot stones and that tasted of nothing at all. Two small cheeses wrapped in leaves. The last of the dried apple rings from Haverstock, threaded on a string like a necklace, that Abbe had given them a whole life ago, before the Glass, when there were still trees and rain and grass. A twist of salt. A little pot of goat fat.
Then the felt. The rolls of it, for the feet. Arran's foot had used a great deal of it. Kestrel picked up the roll that was left and turned it over in her hands and her mouth went thin.
Then everything else. The shade poles. The hides. The ropes. The knives. Vespera's pot, which nobody but Vespera was allowed to touch. Marrit's string of carved bone charms. The Ledger, which Lyra had put down there herself, when Kestrel asked, and which Kestrel did not count, only looked at, and nodded, and moved a little way off from the water, carefully, so it would not get wet.
When she was done she sat back on her heels and looked at it all for a long time.
"Well?" said Oren.
He was sitting on the edge of the hide with his back to a shade pole and his legs out in front of him. He had been sitting very straight all evening, more straight than usual, and Lyra knew why. His back hurt. He had landed hard at noon when Corin threw him. He had not said a word about it. He would not. He was a soldier, and he had been a soldier for thirty years, and soldiers did not say my back hurts in front of other people. They just sat very straight.
"Water for four nights," said Kestrel. "If we are careful. Five, if we are very careful and some of us are thirsty."
"And the Eye?"
Kestrel looked north. Into the dark. There was nothing to see out there. Only the glass going away under the stars, faintly shining, flat and endless, until it met the sky.
"Three nights," she said. "Walking well. With nobody carried."
Nobody said anything.
Lyra did the sum in her head. She did not need to. They all did it. Three nights walking well. But they were not walking well. Arran could not walk at all. Corin could not walk at all. Not tonight. Perhaps not tomorrow. And when they did walk, they would be carrying, and carrying was slow, and slow on the Glass meant more nights, and more nights meant more water.
And behind them were the iron ones. Frozen now. But they would not be frozen at dawn.
"We will make a litter," said Kestrel. "Two. Out of the shade poles. One for the brother, one for the Vane." She spoke as if it were nothing. A thing you did, like weighing water. "Four carry each. We change every hour. The ones who do not carry, carry the hides."
"Then we have no shade," said Sef. Quietly.
"Then we have no shade while we walk," said Kestrel. "We walk at night. At night there is no sun. At dawn we put the poles back up. It is only a little more work."
She looked round at them all. At Oda, small and wiry and fierce, who had carried more than anyone since the Edge and never once complained. At Brin, who had a blister on each heel and pretended he did not. At Marrit, old and bent, who should not have been out here at all, and had come anyway, because her son was out here somewhere, in a cairn, and she wanted to go past it one more time. At Sef, gray with tiredness. At Vespera. At Oren. At Lyra.
At Tansy, who was sitting with her chin on her knees, watching her with very big eyes.
"I'll carry too," said Tansy.
Kestrel looked at her.
"No," she said.
"I'm strong."
"You are as big as a goat," said Kestrel. "A small goat. You will carry the lamp."
Tansy thought about this. Then she nodded, very seriously, as if she had been given the most important job in the world. Which, Lyra thought, perhaps she had. The little clay lamp with its blue flame. The only light on the Glass that was not the stars.
"And Nobody?" said Tansy.
She was looking at the silent man. He was still sitting in the edge of the shade, holding Corin's wrist. He did not look up.
Kestrel looked at him too, for a long moment.
"The man walks beside the Vane," she said at last. "As he does. He does not carry. He holds."
Tansy nodded again. Satisfied.
Arran, lying on his back on a hide with his wrapped foot up on a rolled coat, laughed. A small tired laugh, up at the stars.
"Four nights of water," he said in the southern tongue. Then he said it again, for the city folk, in theirs. "Four nights of water and three nights of walking and two men on poles and a silver-stick man out in the dark following crumbs. And the iron behind." He grinned. "My grandmother used to say the Glass likes a good story. She said it only kills you if you are boring."
"Your grandmother died on the Glass," said Kestrel.
"Yes," said Arran. "But not boring."
And Sef laughed, for the first time Lyra had ever heard him laugh. And then Brin laughed. And then, surprising everybody, Oren laughed too, a short hard bark, and put his hand to his back, and winced, and laughed again.
Lyra did not laugh. But she felt something in her chest loosen, a little, like a knot that has been pulled too tight and then, very slightly, given.
Kestrel did not laugh either. She sat on her heels and looked at the water, and the felt, and the oatcakes. And then she began, slowly, to put it all away. In the same order. Without hurrying. The full skins. The half skins. The empty ones, which she rolled up tight and tied, and put in the bottom of the pack, because on the Glass you never threw away a thing that could hold water. Not ever. Not even when it was empty.
What Stays
When Arran's foot was wrapped, Vespera came back to her pot.
She sat down on the glass beside Corin, slowly, the way old people sit, one hand on the ground and then the other. Her knees cracked. She did not seem to notice. She took the long needles out of the little roll of oiled cloth one by one and wiped them.
She wiped each one three times. On a clean rag. Then on the hem of her own coat. Then on the rag again. She held each one up to the last of the light and looked down the length of it, the way a man looks down an arrow to see if it is straight, and then she laid it back in its fold of cloth and took the next.
Lyra watched her hands. They were thin and brown and spotted, and the knuckles were big. But they did not shake. Not once. Not all afternoon, through all the long lines of letters, and not now.
Corin was asleep. Or something like asleep. His eyes were closed. His chest went up and down under the new black words. The skin around them was red and puffed, and a little blood had come up along the lines and dried in tiny beads, like dark seeds. The silent man was still sitting with him, holding his right arm. He had not moved. He had sat there all afternoon, in the edge of the shade, with the heat coming off the glass at his back, holding Corin's wrist in both his hands as if it was a rope over a cliff.
"Is it done?" said Lyra.
"The ink is done." Vespera did not look up. She laid down another needle.
"Will it work?"
"It is working." She nodded at Corin. "You heard him. Name. Dark. Water. Vance." Her mouth went thin on the last one. "He's coming back up. Slowly. Like a man coming up out of a deep well, one hand over the other. The words help. They're rungs. He reads them, even asleep. The skin reads them. Don't ask me how. I don't know how. Hask didn't know how. It just does."
She wiped the last needle. Held it up. Put it away. Then she rolled up the oiled cloth and tied it with its string, and put it in her coat, and put her hands on her knees.
"Listen to me, Thorne," she said.
Lyra listened.
"When he wakes up," said Vespera, "he'll be Corin. Mostly. The ink will see to that. He'll know his name. He'll know you. He'll know the heron." She looked at the small line on his ribs, below the heart. "But he won't only be Corin."
"What do you mean?"
"I mean what I said." The old woman turned her head and looked straight at Lyra. Her eyes were pale and very clear in the brown, lined face. "He took her. Sola. He took her whole, in that trench. Her hands, that threw your captain over like a sack of grain. Her silence. She gave every word she had, at the end, on that iron horse, and what was left of her after was quiet. Nothing but quiet. And now that quiet is in him. He has Sola in him now. Her hands. Her silence."
She tapped her own chest. Once.
"I can't ink her out," she said. "Nobody can. You'll have to live with both of them."
Lyra looked down at Corin.
At his hands. Lying on the glass. The gray ones, with the black tips. The right one in the silent man's grip, the left one open, palm up, by his hip.
She thought of how they had moved. At noon. When he came up off the ground and caught Oren by the coat and the belt and turned his hip and threw him. A big man. A soldier for thirty years. Thrown like a sack. And Corin had never in his life thrown anyone. Corin fought with his mind and his tongue and the shine behind his eyes. Not with his hips.
Those had been Sola's hands. An old valley throw, Oren had said, afterward, rubbing his back, sitting up slow. My grandfather knew it. The old women used to teach it to girls. For drunk men at harvest.
"Will he do it again?" said Lyra. "Fight like that? Like her?"
"Maybe." Vespera shrugged. "If he's frightened. If he forgets himself. If the Corin part goes thin for a moment, the Sola part might come up to fill it. Like water filling a hole in sand." She paused. "Or maybe it'll just be small things. He'll go quiet for too long. He'll sit with his hands folded a certain way. He'll know how to milk a goat and not know why. He'll wake in the night and not want to speak, and not know why that either."
"And her silence?"
"Is the worst part," said Vespera simply. "A silence like hers is a heavy thing. It has weight. It sits on the tongue. Some days, I think, he'll find the words don't want to come. Not because he's lost them. Because she gave hers away, and her Ash remembers giving." She looked away, out at the dark glass. "Be patient with him on those days. Don't make him talk. Hold his hand. That's what Hask used to do, when one of ours came in off the Glass carrying a quiet one and couldn't speak for days. He just sat with them."
Lyra had not known that. She looked at the old woman.
"You talk about her," she said, "as if you knew her."
"I've carried enough of the dead," said Vespera. "I know what they leave behind." And she did not say anything else. And her mouth was folded tight in a way that meant that was all, and that there was a great deal more behind it, and that nobody was going to get it out of her tonight.
They sat for a while.
The sun had gone. The sky over the Glass was going from red to purple to deep blue, and the first stars were coming out low in the east, and already Lyra could feel the heat going out of the ground under her. Draining away. Like water out of a cracked bowl.
"Is there anything I can do?" she said at last.
Vespera got up. Slowly. Hand, and then the other hand, and the cracking knees.
"Talk to him," she said. "Not about the war. Not about the Glass. Not about anything that matters. Talk to him about little things. Stupid things. The things you two used to laugh at. The things that are yours and not hers." She looked down at Lyra. "Give him something to hold on to that she never had. That's how you keep him Corin. Not with ink. With that."
She went away to her own blanket in the far shade. And lay down. And Lyra heard her, a little while later, saying something very quietly into the dark. It might have been a prayer to the Saints. It might have been a name. Lyra could not hear which.
She stayed where she was.
She looked at the silent man. He was looking at her. He had been listening, she thought. All of it. His face did not change. But after a moment he nodded. Once. Slow.
As if to say: She's right.
And then he looked back down at Corin's wrist, in his hands, and went on holding it.
Oren Asks
Oren came over when the stars were out.
Lyra heard him before she saw him. He walked carefully, the way a big man walks when his back hurts and he does not want anyone to know. Short steps. Very straight. His boots, wrapped in felt like everybody's, made a soft scuffing sound on the glass. Then his shape came between her and the stars, a great dark block of a man, and stood there.
"Can I sit?" he said.
"Yes."
He sat. It took a long time. He did it in stages, one hand on the shade pole, one knee down, then the other knee, and a long slow breath out through his nose at the end, like a horse. Then he was sitting beside her on the hide, with his knees up and his big arms across them, looking at Corin.
The scar on his face was silver in the starlight. Temple to mouth. It pulled the corner of his lip down a little, so that he always looked as if he was about to say something hard. His short gray beard had glass dust in it. Everybody had glass dust in everything.
For a long time he did not say anything. Lyra did not either. She had learned that about Oren a long time ago, when she was a girl in her father's yard. That he did not like to be helped to talk. You waited. If you waited long enough, it came. If you did not, it did not.
"He threw me," said Oren at last.
"I saw."
"Thirty years." He shook his head slowly. "Thirty years, I've been thrown by maybe four men. Big men. Men who knew what they were doing. And that boy threw me like I was a bag of washing. With one hip."
"It wasn't him."
"No." Oren looked at Corin's hands, lying on the glass. "No. It was her."
He was quiet again.
"My grandfather knew that throw," he said. "He showed me once. When I was small. In the yard behind our house, in the city, with the washing on the line. He said the old women where he came from used to teach it to the girls. So nobody could put a hand on them at a harvest dance that they didn't want there." He breathed out. "He never said where he came from. Not once. Not to my father, not to me. You asked, and he'd just swear Sola's teeth and go out to the yard. I thought it was only a thing old men said."
Lyra did not say anything.
"It wasn't a story," said Oren.
"No."
"She was his aunt." He said it slowly, as if he was setting each word down on the hide in front of him, one at a time, to look at. "Sola. My grandfather's father's sister. My blood. And that boy has her in him now. Her hands. The old woman says her quiet too." He rubbed his face with one big palm, hard, up and down, over the scar and the beard. "I'd have liked to meet her. That's a stupid thing to say. She's been dead sixty years. But I would. I'd have liked to see her. Just once. On that iron horse."
"He saw her," said Lyra. "In the trench. Inside her Ash. He saw all of it."
"I know." Oren nodded. "I know. When he's better, I'll ask him." A pause. "If he can tell me. If she lets him."
Then he did something that Lyra had not expected. He leaned forward, slowly, with a grunt, and looked at Corin's chest. At the black words on the red skin, under the open shirt.
He looked at them for a long time.
"What do they say?" he said.
Lyra had forgotten. For a moment she had forgotten that Oren could not read. That he had never been able to. That he had gone thirty years in the army and never once read his own orders, and always had someone read them to him, and been ashamed of it, and hidden the shame under that hard, scarred, about-to-say-something face.
She leaned over too. And she read them to him.
She went slowly. She put her finger beside each line, not touching, just near, the way you would show a child. "Your name is Corin Vane. That's the first one. The big one. You are twenty-eight. You were born in Oakhaven. Then this one. You used to fear the dark. And under it, Not anymore."
Oren grunted. "That's a lie."
"It's a hope," said Lyra.
"Same thing, in the army." But he almost smiled. "Go on."
"Lyra Thorne. Partner." She felt her face go hot in the dark. She did not stop. "No lies between you."
Oren looked at that one longest.
"The old woman said that one wasn't a fact," he said.
"No. She said it was a promise."
"Hm." He sat back. Slowly. With a wince. "Those are better."
"Better than what?"
"Than facts." He looked up at the stars. "Facts, anybody can take off you. A good harvester. A bad night. You lose your name, you lose the town you were born in, you lose how old you are. I've seen it. Men in the war who didn't know their own mothers by the end. But a promise." He shook his head. "You can lose a promise. But you have to choose to break it. Nobody can burn it out of you. Not really. If you made it with all of you, it's not in one place. It's everywhere."
Lyra looked at him.
She had never heard Oren say so many words together. Not since she had known him. He seemed to know it too. He cleared his throat and looked away and rubbed the back of his neck.
"And the little one," he said gruffly. "On his side. What's that?"
"You walk like a heron."
Oren stared at her.
Then he laughed. Not the short hard bark from before. A real laugh, low and rumbling, from deep down in his big chest, that he tried to keep quiet and could not, so that it came out in snorts through his nose and shook his shoulders and made him put a hand to his back again and say ow and laugh harder.
"He does," he said, when he could. "Saints help him. He does."
And then, because he had laughed, and because it was dark, and because nobody could see, Lyra laughed too. Very quietly. Into her knees.
They sat like that for a while. Not talking. Looking at Corin.
"And the old letters?" said Oren at last. "Down there. On the rib. The ones she wouldn't say."
"I don't know," said Lyra. "I can't read them. Nobody can, except her. She says it's for later."
"Later." Oren thought about that. "I don't like later."
"No."
"In the army, later meant a funeral."
Lyra did not answer that. There was nothing to say to it.
After a while Oren got up again. In stages. Knee, other knee, hand on the pole, the long breath like a horse. He stood over her, blocking the stars.
"You should sleep," he said.
"So should you."
"I will. I'll lie behind you tonight. Keep the cold off your back." He looked down at Corin one more time. Then at the silent man, who had not moved, and had not looked up, the whole time they talked. "You," he said to him. Not unkindly. "Don't let go."
The silent man did not answer. But his long fingers, around Corin's wrist, tightened. Just a little.
Oren nodded. As if that was an answer. As if, between two men who did not talk much, it was the only kind that counted.
Then he went away into the dark, short steps, very straight, toward the heap.
