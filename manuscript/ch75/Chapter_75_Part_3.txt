Chapter 75: Grounding, Part 3 of 5: For Later
For Later
Vespera did not stop when Lyra stopped.
Lyra had nothing more to say. She had said it all, everything that mattered, everything she could bear to choose. She sat back on her heels on the hot felt with the book shut on her knees and her hands flat on its cover, and watched the last line dry. YOU WALK LIKE A HERON. Small and neat on his ribs, below the heart.
She thought Vespera would wipe the needle then. And put it away.
Vespera did not.
She dipped it again. Into the little gold-rimmed pot. And it came out glittering, silver-white, like crushed frost.
And she bent lower. Lower than before. Down to his left side, where the ribs curved away under the arm toward the back. To the lowest rib. The last one, the short one, that sticks out a little at the bottom of the chest like the end of a barrel hoop.
And began to ink along it.
Lyra watched.
It was not like the other lines. That was the first thing she saw. The other lines were square capitals, leaning back, like Corin's hand in the Ledger. Letters she knew. Letters anyone could read.
These were not.
These were small, and curled, and joined. Hooks and loops and little crossed strokes, running one into the next along the curve of the rib in a single thin line, like a vine. Like the marks of a bird's feet in wet sand. Like the old marks Vespera had put on him years ago in the city, that Lyra had seen faded to gray-blue on his skin and never been able to read.
The old letters.
Lyra had seen them once or twice, in the back room in Hook Lane, on the labels of Vespera's ink pots. She had asked about them once. Vespera had said, they're older than you, girl, and older than me, and you've no business with them. And that had been the end of it.
The needle went along the rib. Prick, blue, black. Prick, blue, black. Faster now than before. Much faster. As if Vespera had done this line before, many times, and knew it by heart. As if her hand did not need her eyes at all.
And she was not humming anymore.
Lyra noticed the silence. Vespera's mouth was shut tight. A thin line, like a seam pulled too hard. And her face, bent close over the rib, was very still.
The line was not long. A hand's width. Less. When it was done, Vespera lifted the needle and looked at it. Wiped it with her thumb, once. And looked at it again, for a long time.
Then she laid the needle down on its cloth. At the end of the row. In its place.
"What does it say?" said Lyra.
Vespera did not answer.
"Vespera. What does it say?"
"It's for later," said Vespera.
She began to wipe her hands on a rag from her belt. Slowly. One finger at a time. The blue fingers, stained to the wrist, that the rag could not make any cleaner.
"For later when?"
"Later."
"For who?"
Vespera's hands stopped on the rag. Just for a moment. Then went on.
"For him," she said. "When he can read it."
"He can't read those. Nobody can. You said yourself—"
"I said you had no business with them," said Vespera. "I didn't say nobody could read them." She tucked the rag back into her belt. "He can't read them now. Maybe he never will. Maybe one day somebody will read them to him. Somebody who knows how." She looked at Lyra. Sharp. Old. Tired. "And if that day comes, it'll be there. On him. Where nobody can take it off. That's all. That's why it's for later."
Lyra looked at the line. The little curling vine of black along the lowest rib.
"Is it something you know?" she said. "About him? Something you haven't told us?"
Vespera stood up.
She did it stiffly, the way old people stand, a hand on her knee, a grunt. And stood there in the white sun at the edge of the felt, small and bent, looking down at Corin with his chest covered in her writing. At his face. For the first time all afternoon, Lyra saw her look at his face.
He was looking back at her. Wary still. But not showing his teeth.
"I know a great many things, girl," said Vespera. "About a great many people. That's what happens when you put needles in them for forty years." She pulled her eye hide down over her eyes. "Most of them I'll take to my grave. Some of them I won't. That's one I won't. When it's time."
"When is it time?"
"When I say," said Vespera.
And she went away across the glass to the shade on the far side, where Marrit sat, and lay down there with her back to them all, the way she had in the green bubble.
Lyra stayed where she was.
She looked at the line along the rib. She could not read a single mark of it. She did not even know where one word ended and the next began.
She thought: everybody in my whole life has known something I don't.
And now it was written on him. On the man she had just promised no lies.
She did not know whether to be angry or afraid.
She found, after a while, that she was neither. She was only very tired. And that the line along his rib, small and black and curling, looked, when you did not try to read it, very like the stitches on the dolls. The ones that leaned back.
Looking home.
Words Come Back
The first word came back while Lyra was not looking for it.
She had turned away. Only for a moment. To take the water skin from Oren, who had held it out to her without a word, and to drink her one mouthful, and to give it back. And when she turned round again, Corin was looking at his own chest.
Not at the sky. Not at the ring of faces. Down. At himself. With his chin pressed into his collarbone and his eyes squinting, the way a man looks at something written too close to read.
At the words.
Lyra went very still.
He could not read. She knew that. He did not know what letters were. He did not know what words were. There was nothing behind his eyes to read with.
But he was looking at them. At the small black leaning marks on his own pale skin. The way a baby looks at its own hand, the first time it finds it. Puzzled. Interested. As if it had not been there before, and he was trying to work out whose it was.
His right hand moved.
The silent man let it. Lyra saw his long fingers open, round Corin's gray ones, and let go. And Corin's hand came up, slowly, off the felt. Shaking a little. And the gray fingers, with their black tips, went to his chest. To the top line. YOUR NAME IS CORIN VANE.
And touched it.
The black fingertips, that could not feel. Laid on the new ink, on the pink angry skin round it. Pressing. Very gently.
And drew along it.
One word. From left to right. The way you draw your finger along a line in a book when you are learning to read.
His lips moved.
No sound. At first. Only the lips. Shaping something. And Lyra, kneeling a few feet off on the felt, with her heart going like a drum, watched them shape it, and could not breathe.
Then a sound came.
Cracked. Rough. From a throat torn raw by roaring. Hardly a sound at all. A breath with a shape in it.
"...name."
Lyra put her hand over her mouth.
Corin did not look at her. He was looking at his chest. At his finger, on the word. He said it again. Slower. As if he were tasting it. As if it were something he had found on the ground and picked up and was turning over in his hand.
"Name."
Oren made a sound. Low. Like a man who has been hit.
Corin's finger moved on. Along the line. Past the other words. And stopped. Not at the end. Somewhere in the middle. And lay there. And his lips moved again. And nothing came.
He frowned.
It was the first expression Lyra had seen on his face since he came back. Not the wary animal look. Not the bared teeth. A frown. The small tight pull between the eyebrows, the one she had looked at so many times when he was reading small writing. The one that had gone smooth when he slept.
His frown.
She would have known it anywhere.
He took his finger off the line. And moved it down. Off the first lines. Down over the ribs, slowly, the gray fingertips trailing over the skin, past YOU ARE TWENTY-EIGHT, past OAKHAVEN, to the lines under it.
YOU USED TO FEAR THE DARK.
His finger stopped on the last word. And pressed.
And he said, quite clearly, in that raw cracked voice:
"Dark."
And then he looked up.
Not at the sky. At the shade over him. At the gold light through the stretched hide. And past the edge of it, at the white blaze of the noon sky that was not quite noon anymore. And something went across his face. Quick. Like the shadow of a bird going over a field.
"Dark," he said again. Softer. As if he were asking.
"Not anymore," said Lyra.
She did not mean to say it. It came out of her. Shaking.
He looked at her.
At her. Not through her. Not at her hands, or the distance between them, or whether she might move. At her face. With the frown still there. Puzzled. Trying.
She did not know if he understood. She did not think he did. Not anymore was two words, and he had only just found one.
But he looked at her for a long moment. And the frown did not go away. And he did not show his teeth.
Then his eyes went back down to his chest. And his finger went back to the top line. And began again, from the beginning, slowly, along the first word.
"...your," he said. Barely a breath. "Name."
And stopped.
And tried again.
Lyra sat on her heels on the hot felt with her hand pressed over her mouth and the tears running down over her fingers, and did not wipe them, and did not care who saw.
Vance
The words came slowly after that. One at a time. Far apart. Like the first drops of rain on a roof, when you lie awake listening, and each one is so far from the last that you think it must be the only one.
But they came.
"Name." Again, and again, with his finger on his chest.
"Dark."
Then, much later, when Oren held the water skin to his mouth and tipped it, and he swallowed, and coughed, and some of it ran down his chin: "Water." Hoarse. Surprised. As if he had just remembered there was such a thing. And Oren made the hit sound again, and turned his face away, and pretended to be looking at the horse.
Then "Hot." Very clearly. Squinting up at the white sky past the edge of the shade. And Brin, holding the pole of the shade, laughed out loud, a big slow surprised laugh, and said something in the round speech that made Oda snort.
Each one came out of him like a stone out of a well. Heavy. Slow. Hauled up from somewhere very deep, a little at a time, on a rope that might break.
Lyra did not know where they were coming from. From the ink, Vespera said, when Lyra asked her. From the giving. Each word on his skin was a word given to him, from outside, and the Glass did not mind giving, and so it let them in. And once a word was in, it was his. And sometimes it brought another with it. The way one fish brings another, in a net. Name brings name. Dark brings dark. Water, on its own, because his mouth needed it.
"Will they all come back?" said Lyra. "All of them? All his words?"
"No," said Vespera. Flatly. From the far shade, without turning round. "Not his. His went into the light with hers. These are new ones. I gave them. He'll have as many as he can catch." A pause. "It'll be enough. Or it won't."
The afternoon went on.
And then, late, when the sun had begun at last to go down the sky toward the west and the white glare had softened a little to gold, it happened.
Lyra was not watching him. She was reading. She had opened the Ledger again, at the first page, and begun to read it aloud, from the start, slowly, the way you read to someone who is ill. She was on the third page. She was reading about the road out of the city, and the fens, and the punt, and the reeds. Her voice had gone flat and steady with reading, the way voices do.
And Corin, lying on the felt with his eyes shut, said a word.
Not to her. Not to anyone.
He did not open his eyes. He did not look at his chest. He did not touch the ink. He lay there with his face turned a little to the side, toward the iron horse standing over them in the gold light, and his lips moved, and the word came out. Clear. Quite clear. Clearer than any word he had said since he came back. Not cracked. Not hauled up out of a well. Just said. The way you say a word you have said all your life.
"Vance."
Lyra stopped reading.
She looked up. At Oren.
Oren was kneeling at Corin's left, where he had knelt all afternoon. Holding the left arm, still, though there was no need to hold it now. And he had gone very still.
Not the stillness of a man listening. The stillness of a man who has been struck so hard he does not yet know where.
He was looking at Corin's face.
Corin's eyes were still shut. His face was quiet. Not frowning. Not anything. The way a face is quiet in sleep.
The word was not on his skin. Lyra knew that. She had chosen every word that went on him, and that was not one of them. She had not said it. Vespera had not inked it. It was not in any line on his chest or his ribs. It was not in the part of the Ledger she had been reading. It had not come from outside.
It had come from somewhere else.
From in him.
From her.
"Oren," said Lyra. Very quietly.
Oren did not answer. He did not look up.
He was looking at Corin's mouth. At the lips that had said it. His own face was gray under the red of the sun.
"That's not him," said Oren. His voice was so low that Lyra could barely hear it. "That's not his voice. Not how he said it." He swallowed. "That's how they said it at Haverstock. The old men by the fire, at the Sun and Pike. When they said the General's name. Vance. Like that. Like it was the name of something heavy, that you'd carried a long way."
He let go of Corin's arm. Slowly. As if it had become very fragile.
"She's still in there," said Oren. "Isn't she. Some of her."
Lyra did not answer. She did not know.
Oren sat back on his heels. And took off his knitted cap. And held it in both his hands, in his lap, and looked at it. And did not say anything else for a long time.
Corin slept on, under the iron hoof, in the gold light. And did not say the word again.
Reading for Hours
Lyra read.
She read until her voice went, and then she drank a mouthful of water and read on in a whisper, and when the whisper went too she drank again and read on. She did not stop. She did not let herself stop. Kestrel came once and touched her shoulder and said something about resting, and Lyra did not hear what, and did not look up, and Kestrel went away again.
She read the whole of Ledger VIII.
Every page of it. Every line he had written since Saint Bride's. In his square leaning capitals, pressed hard into the paper so the dents would last if the pencil faded.
She read him the road out of the city. The fens. The reeds taller than a man, rattling. The punt. Wat, and the boy Robin asleep in his basket with the doll under his arm.
She read him Thistle Ford. Granny Abbe in her army coat with the sleeves rolled four times. The little girl with two fair plaits who touched his gray palm and said it was cold, like a stone in the river, and gave him a dried apple ring. EAT IT, NOT KEEP IT. Hild, and the goose fat, and the loft over the byre with the cows below.
She read him the night talk. WHAT DOES YOUR VOICE SOUND LIKE WHEN YOU'RE HAPPY. YOU'LL KNOW IT. YOU'RE VERY ANNOYING.
She had to stop, there, for a moment. Then she went on.
She read him Haverstock, and the cross. SHE READ OREN HIS FAMILY. TWENTY-THREE NAMES. HE SAID EVERY ONE AFTER HER. Oren, kneeling at Corin's other side, heard that, and looked down at the glass, and did not say anything.
She read him the Lip. The thorn slope. Last Thorn. Kestrel's tent of hands. Old Hask, with his blind white eyes and his little brass bell, touching Corin's chest. A ROOM IN ME WITH THE DOOR OFF ITS HINGES. She read him the putting-back page, all of it, with its backward R in JAR and its backward N in DONT. SHE FOUND MY WRONG LETTERS. She read him Vespera coming in a box of turnips.
She read him the fire.
TUESDAY NIGHT. THE CAMP BURNED. I HEARD HER HAPPY VOICE. SHE WAS DANCING. THAT'S WHAT IT SOUNDS LIKE. THEN SHE GAVE THE DANCING AWAY TO SAVE THREE CHILDREN.
She read that one twice. She did not know why. Her voice did not shake on it the first time. It did the second.
She read him the Glass. SHE SLEPT. The heron on the garden wall. The Trench, and the men of glass, and the drum boy with the doll in his pack. I TRIED TO TAKE ONE. IT WAS SHUT. LYRA SAYS IT IS NOT ALL I AM. The sun with its eyes shut. HE WAVED AT ME. I DON'T KNOW HIM. SOMETHING IN ME DOES.
And she read him the last page. The one he had written lying on his back under the iron hoof, before he went in.
IF I COME BACK WRONG, READ ME THIS.
MY NAME IS CORIN.
I LOVE THESE PEOPLE.
She read it to him three times. Then she shut the book. And opened it at the first page again. And began again.
She did not know if he heard. He lay on the felt with his eyes shut, mostly, and his chest with its black words rising and falling. Sometimes his lips moved. Sometimes a word came. Water. Hot. Name. Once, when she read about the apple ring, his gray hand closed, a little, on the felt. As if it were holding something small.
The silent man did not let go of his arm.
All afternoon, he did not move. He lay where he had lain since he pinned Corin down, half across him, with his long body heavy and still and his bearded face turned to the north. And his long thin fingers round Corin's right arm, above the wrist. Not gripping. Holding. The way you hold the rope of a boat when the boat is not going anywhere, but you are not taking any chances.
Tansy brought him water. He did not drink it. She put it to his lips and tipped it and he swallowed, without seeming to know. She brought him bread. She put it in his free hand. He did not eat it. He held it, and went on holding Corin's arm.
"He won't let go," said Tansy. To Lyra. In a whisper, between pages. "I've tried. I said, you can let go now, he's not fighting anymore. He won't."
"Leave him," said Lyra.
"I am leaving him. I'm only saying."
The light began to go.
Not to dark. To gold. The white glare softening, going yellow, going amber, as the sun went down the sky behind them toward the west. The shadow of the iron horse grew long across the felt. Long and red and strange, with its lifted hoof and its arched neck, lying over them like the shadow of a giant.
And as it went, Tansy got up. And went to her pack. And came back with something in her hands.
A little clay cup. A lamp. The kind of lamp the Ashborn used, a shallow dish with a lip for the wick. And in it, a twist of something dry, and a thorn twig.
She struck a spark off a stone with a bit of iron, the way Brin had done in the morning on the open Glass. It took her four tries. Then the twig caught.
Blue.
The small cool sky-colored flame stood up off the end of the twig in the clay lamp. No smoke. No heat. And not much light; Lyra knew that. Not enough to read by, out on the open Glass.
But here, close, under the shade, in the going gold, it was enough. Tansy held it up beside Lyra's head, close to the page, so close that the cool blue light lay right across the square black letters. And the letters stood out on the paper, sharp and clear.
"There," said Tansy. "Now you can see. Go on. Read him the next bit."
Lyra looked at her. At the small fierce face in the blue light. At the lamp, held up steady in two small grubby hands.
Then she looked down. And went on reading.
Back from the Trail
Arran and Sef came back at sunset.
Lyra did not see them come. She was reading. Tansy saw them first, and dropped the lamp, nearly, and shouted, and the blue flame wobbled on its twig and righted itself. And Lyra looked up, and there they were. Two dark shapes coming in across the glass from the east, not the northwest, with the red sun behind them throwing their shadows out long in front of them like poles.
They were walking slowly. Very slowly. Sef had his arm round Arran's waist.
Kestrel went out to meet them. Fast. Not running, but nearly. And Lyra saw her stop in front of them, and put her hands on Arran's shoulders, and look at his face. And then down.
At his feet.
Arran was limping.
Not the way he had limped going out. Not the beautiful foolish swinging limp, pat, step-step, that had made Lyra almost laugh at noon. A real one. He was putting no weight at all on his left foot. It hung. And the felt on it was dark. Darker than sweat.
They brought him into the ring of shades. Sef was gray with tiredness. He let go of Arran and sat down on the glass as if his legs had been cut, and put his head on his knees.
"Felt wore through," said Arran.
He said it cheerfully. He was grinning. The burned stubble of his eyebrows went up. But his face, under the grin, was the color of ash, and his lips were pressed white at the corners.
"Felt wore through," he said again. "On the hot glass. In the afternoon. Far out. I didn't notice. I was too busy limping." He laughed. It came out wrong. "Then I noticed."
Vespera was there. Lyra had not seen her get up from the far shade. She was kneeling at Arran's foot with her knife out, cutting the felt away. Not unwinding it. Cutting it. Because it was stuck.
Lyra looked. And looked away.
The sole of the foot was not skin anymore. The Glass had had it. In the afternoon, in the heat, through the worn felt, while Arran walked on it limping on purpose and grinning over his shoulder. A long patch of it, from the heel to the ball of the foot, gone white and blistered and split, and red under the split, and weeping.
Cooked like meat, Kestrel had said. The first noon. You lie down and go to sleep, and when you wake, the side you lay on is cooked like meat.
Arran had not lain down. He had walked on it. For hours.
"You stupid boy," said Vespera. Very low. Not sharp. "You stupid, stupid boy."
"It worked, though," said Arran.
Kestrel looked at him.
"The man," said Arran. "With the silver stick. He followed." He drew in a breath through his teeth as Vespera's salve went on. "We went northwest, far, three hours, more, dropping crumbs. Limping. And we went up on a swell to look back. And there he was. Coming. Out of the Fold Hills, behind the iron ones. And when the iron ones went straight, toward the Road, toward here, he didn't follow them. He stopped. And looked at the ground. And looked at the iron ones going. And looked at our trail going off the other way."
He grinned again. A real one, this time, under the pain.
"And he followed us," he said. "Our trail. Not them. He thought they'd got it wrong. He thought the iron was lost and he was cleverer. He went off northwest after a man who limps. On his own."
"Where is he now?" said Oren.
"Out there," said Sef. He lifted his head off his knees. It was the first thing Lyra had ever heard him say in the southern tongue. His voice was hoarse and quiet. "Far. Northwest. In the open. When we turned and came back on the hard glass, the sun was going down." He looked at the sky. At the last red light on the rim of the world. "Now it's gone."
"And the iron?"
"Stopped," said Arran. "We saw them stop. On the open glass. Between the Fold Hills and the Road." He jerked his head south and east. "Not far. Not as far as I'd like. But stopped. Frozen. The cold got them."
Kestrel let out a long breath.
She crouched down beside her brother. And put her hand on his head. On the short shaved scalp, with the plait tied with red thread hanging down behind. And left it there.
"Stupid," she said. In the southern tongue. Soft. "Stupid. Stupid."
"I know," said Arran.
"You will not walk tomorrow."
"I will."
"You will not walk tomorrow," said Kestrel again. And this time her voice cracked. Just a little. Just on the last word. "We will carry you."
Arran looked at her. And for a moment the grin went, and he looked very young, and very tired, and like a boy who has done something brave and foolish and wants his sister to tell him it was all right.
"It worked," he said. Quietly.
Kestrel touched the gray line on his face. Forehead to nose. Two fingers.
"It worked," she said.
