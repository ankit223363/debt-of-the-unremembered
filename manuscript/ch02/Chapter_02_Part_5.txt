Chapter 2: Ash in a Jar, Part 5 of 5: The Girl on the Roof
White Coats
They had not gone ten steps from the mouth of Gutter Lane when the crowd went quiet again.
Not the quiet of respect, like when the stretcher had come out. A different quiet. Sharper. The quiet of a flock of pigeons when a cat walks into the square. People stepped back. People looked at the ground. A woman pulled her child behind her skirts. The young constable with the thin blond mustache took his hand off the rope and stood up very straight.
Two people were walking down Needle Street toward them, through the crowd, which parted in front of them like water in front of a boat.
They wore white.
Long white coats, buttoned to the throat, falling to the tops of their boots. Clean. Spotless. So white, in the smoky gray of the Ink Quarter, that they almost seemed to glow. Each coat had a high collar of white stiffened cloth, and on the left breast, embroidered in silver thread, the sign of the Ministry. The open book. And above it, not a closed eye. An eye that was open. Wide open. Staring.
"Inquisitors," breathed Toby, and took a step back, behind Corin.
The first one was a man. Older, perhaps forty, heavy-set, with a shaved head and a flat, square, bored face, like a slab of cold meat. He had a short black rod hanging from his belt, of the kind the guards at the Ministry door carried, with a silver cap on the end.
The second one was a young woman.
She was perhaps twenty-two. Slim and straight-backed, with brown hair pulled back so tight from her face that it looked painted on, and a sharp, pale, careful face, with a long nose and a narrow chin and dark eyebrows drawn very straight across, like two strokes of a pen. Her white coat fit her perfectly. Her boots shone. She carried a small black notebook in one gloved hand and a silver pencil in the other, and she was writing in the notebook as she walked, without looking at it, while her eyes went everywhere. The crowd. The rope. The mouth of the lane. Sergeant Moll. Dr. Fane. Toby.
Corin.
Her eyes stopped on him, and stayed.
"Harvester Vane," she said. Her voice was clear and cool and polite, the voice of someone reading out the minutes of a meeting. "Third Basement. Senior Investigator. I'm told you've been in there." She nodded at Gutter Lane. "Inquisitor Halloway. This is Inquisitor Brack. We'll take the harvest now, please."
"The harvest?"
"The jar." She did not hold out her hand. She simply waited, pencil poised. "Scribe Dorran was Ministry staff. His death is a Ministry matter. The Inquisition will be handling it from here. The Watch report will be collected from the station." Her eyes flicked to Sergeant Moll, and back. "Any harvested residue becomes Inquisition property. I'm sure you know the rule."
Corin did not know the rule. He did not know if there was a rule. He looked at her for a long moment.
Then he reached into his coat and took out the small glass jar with the brass lid, and the label in his untidy handwriting, and held it out.
She took it. Her gloved fingers did not touch his. She held it up to the gray light and looked at it. At the thin pale thread of mist curled up in the bottom, turning slowly. Then at the label.
DORRAN, P. Last minutes. Stable. Do not open.
He hummed.
She read it twice. Corin saw her eyes go back to the start of the second line, and read it again.
"He hummed," she said.
"Yes."
"What did he hum?"
It was the first question she had asked that did not sound like it came from a form. Inquisitor Brack, beside her, looked at her with faint surprise.
Corin did not answer at once. The three notes were there, in his throat, waiting. He let them come. Softly. Through his nose. Up, and up, and up. And stopped there, in the cold damp air of Needle Street, asking.
Inquisitor Halloway went very still.
It lasted less than a heartbeat. Her face did not change. Her pencil did not move. But Corin, who had spent the morning looking at faces as if they were rooms he had to search, saw something go across the back of her eyes. Something quick and startled and lost, like a bird flying past a window.
Then it was gone.
"Thank you, Harvester," she said. Her voice was exactly as cool as before. She put the jar into a pocket of her white coat, and wrote something short in her black notebook, and closed it. "That will be all. You'll be sent a form."
She turned and walked away up Needle Street, with Inquisitor Brack lumbering beside her. The crowd parted for them again. Corin watched them go.
At the corner, under the painted sign of the ink mill, she stopped. Just for a moment. She opened her black notebook again and looked at something inside the front cover. Not at what she had written. At something tucked in there. Small and flat and brown.
Then she closed it, and turned the corner, and was gone.
"Do you know her?" said Corin.
Toby shook his head. His face was pale. "Only the name. Halloway. She's Kross's. Inquisitor Kross. Everyone knows Kross's people." He swallowed. "They say he picks them himself. From the orphan schools. And trains them. And that they'd walk into the river if he told them to."
"Kross," said Corin.
The name sat in his head like a stone. Like Valerius on the front of the fat man's newspaper. Hard and small and cold, and he did not know why.
He took out the Ledger and wrote: Inquisitor Halloway. Young. Brown hair. Kross's. Took the jar. Asked what he hummed. When I hummed it, she knew it. I think. And, after a moment: Something brown, pressed in the front of her notebook.
Then he put the Ledger away, and they walked to the tram.
The Boiler-Room Step
They went back to the Ministry on the tram. Neither of them said a word the whole way.
Corin stood at the back, holding the leather strap, with the green Treasury ledger and the red book and the woman in the white mask crowding the inside of his head. Every few minutes the three notes came back into his throat, up and up and up, and he had to swallow them down. Toby sat on the bench by the window with his hands folded in his lap over his coat pocket and stared out at the city going by without seeing any of it.
At the bottom of the great black stairs, under the huge stone eye, Corin said, "Not the Third Basement. Not yet. Show me the boiler room."
Toby looked at him. His eyes behind the thick lenses went wide.
"How do you know about the boiler room?"
"I've been there," said Corin. "Last Friday. After work. You'd gone home with a cold."
Toby's face did something complicated. Then he nodded, once, very small, and turned, and led the way.
The Copying Hall was full when they passed its door. Corin heard the pens through the wood, the slow tired scratching like hens pecking in a yard. He did not look in. He knew what he would see. A long dim room with a curved stone ceiling, and a barred window at the end, and a high desk on a platform with a man in a brown waistcoat behind it. And a row of scarred desks with one empty chair.
The short flight of stone stairs went down off the corridor to the left. At the bottom, the heavy iron door, and behind it the growl of the great boiler, like a dragon asleep. The wall beside it was warm. Corin put his hand flat against it and felt the heat soak into his palm.
He had never been here. He had been here a hundred times.
He knelt down on the bottom step. He ran his fingers along the crack between it and the step above. Dust. Grit. A dead beetle. A burned match. A little drift of gray ash, crushed flat by a boot.
And caught in the crack, half buried in the dust, so small he would never have seen it if he had not known exactly where to look, a chip of dark red wax.
He picked it out with the tip of his little finger. It lay on his gloved palm, no bigger than a fingernail. One edge was smooth and curved, where it had been pressed against the cap of a brass cylinder. The other edge was broken and jagged. And in the smooth part, pressed deep into the wax by a signet ring, there was a pattern. Half a pattern. A curving stem. And along the stem, sharp and clear, three long, curved thorns.
Toby leaned over his shoulder and looked at it.
"What is it?" he whispered.
"A seal," said Corin. "House Thorne."
Toby stared at it. Then at Corin.
"Thorne," he said slowly. "Lord Thorne? The one who—" He stopped. "There was something in the broadsheets. A year ago. Two. His house went bankrupt. Overnight. And he went mad, they said. Burned his own mind out for some reason nobody knows. His daughter sold the townhouse." He swallowed. "Why would Lord Thorne want to kill Pell?"
"He didn't," said Corin. He held the little chip of wax up toward the dim light from the corridor above, and turned it, and looked at the three thorns. "Someone wanted Pell to think he did. Someone copied his seal. And his handwriting. Carefully. Very nearly right." He closed his gloved hand around the chip. "Very nearly."
Form 12-B
Back in the Third Basement, under the single humming lamp, Toby sat down at his small neat desk with its row of straight pens and its dying plant, and took a sheet of paper from a drawer, and laid it in front of him, and squared its corners. Then he looked at it.
It was a printed form. Corin could read the top of it from where he stood.
MINISTRY OF RECORDS. HARVESTING SECTION. FORM 12-B. RECORD OF FIELD HARVEST. TO BE COMPLETED IN TRIPLICATE.
Under that, a great many boxes. Name of deceased. Place. Time. Grade of residue. Quantity harvested. Content of residue (summary). Disposal. Signature of Harvester. Signature of Clerk.
Toby dipped his pen. He wrote, in his small, neat, careful hand: DORRAN, PELL. Scribe, Grade Three. Then he stopped, with the nib hovering over the next box.
"Content of residue," he said. "Summary." He did not look up. "What do I write?"
Corin had sat down in the big creaking chair at his own desk, among the landslide of papers and the half-eaten apple and the pieces of broken teacup, which nobody had swept up. His hands were still shaking. He held them together between his knees to make them stop.
"Write that the residue was small," he said. "Write that it held the last minutes before death. Write that the deceased was attacked in Gutter Lane by persons unknown and drained with a siphon. Write that the residue was surrendered to the Inquisition on Needle Street."
Toby wrote it. Slowly. Then he looked up.
"That's all?"
"That's all."
"What about the woman? The mask? The lilies? You told the sergeant."
"The sergeant's report will say that. Let it say it there."
"What about the red book?"
Corin looked at him.
Toby's big ears were pink, but he did not look away. "You said red book in the alley," he said quietly. "When you were coming back. Before you knew where you were. You kept saying it. Red book, red book, the drawer, the Q. And you said Oakhaven." He swallowed. "Pell said that word once. When he'd had too much cider at the harvest feast last year. He said it and then he went white and laughed and said he'd made it up."
The lamp hummed. Water dripped somewhere in the walls.
"If I write it on the form," said Corin slowly, "where does the form go?"
Toby blinked. "One copy to the Section file. Here. One copy to the Records Office, upstairs. And one copy to—" He stopped. He looked down at the form. At the bottom, in small print, under the signature boxes. "To the Office of the Archon," he read. "For review."
"And who sent a note to Pell with a seal that was very nearly right?"
Toby did not answer.
"Someone who knew he'd found the book," said Corin. "Someone who knew within a day. Someone who knew he ate his lunch with a clerk who wears spectacles." He held his shaking hands tighter between his knees. "Pell didn't tell anyone. He was careful. He glued the cover back. He put the ledger back on the shelf. Nobody saw. So how did they know, Toby?"
Toby's face had gone very white.
"Somebody in this building," he whispered.
"Somebody in this building."
Toby looked at the form for a long time. At the three neat boxes he had filled. At the empty one, Content of residue, with its four bland sentences. At the small print at the bottom. To the Office of the Archon. For review.
Then he put the pen down. He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes. And he said, very quietly, without putting them back on:
"I've never lied on a form."
"I know."
"Not once. Five years. Not even about the paper allowance. Supervisor Brightwater said I was the most honest clerk in the Third Basement. He wrote it on my review." Toby put his spectacles back on. His eyes behind them were very bright. "It's the only thing anyone's ever written on my review."
"You haven't lied," said Corin. "Everything on that form is true."
"It's not all true."
"No," Corin agreed. "It's not all." He was quiet for a moment. "Pell burned it all, Toby. Right in front of her. So she couldn't have it. That was the last thing he ever chose to do." He looked at the form. "I'm not going to hand it to her now on a piece of paper in triplicate."
Toby looked at him for a long, long moment.
Then he picked up the pen again. He dipped it. And in the box marked Disposal, in his small, neat, careful hand, he wrote: Surrendered to Inquisitor Halloway, Needle Street, tenth bell. And signed it. And blotted it. And pushed it across the desk for Corin to sign.
Corin signed it.
Toby took it back. He copied it out twice more, exactly the same, in his neat hand. He rolled one copy into a brass cylinder and put it into the hatch in the pipe above his desk, and pulled the brass lever, and the pipe went whoosh and thump, and the cylinder was gone. He filed the second in a drawer. He put the third in a tray marked OUT.
Then he sat back in his chair and looked at the empty hatch in the pipe.
"I want to come," he said. "Tonight. Wherever you're going."
"No."
"I knew him. You said so yourself. Someone who knew him should be there."
"Not tonight." Corin stood up. His knees held, this time. "Tonight I need someone who'll be here tomorrow. Someone who'll read me the first page if I come back wrong. Someone who knows what I put in that box and what I didn't." He picked up the long wooden case from his desk and tucked it under his arm. "Someone with a red sock in his pocket that has to go to a girl called Nan."
Toby's hand went to his coat pocket.
He did not argue again.
The Ledger Entry
That night, in Room 9 at the top of No. 14 Tallow Street, with the little brass lamp burning on its crate and the door locked and Mrs. Marta's notes rustling on the frame outside in the draft from the stairs, Corin sat on the edge of his narrow bed and wrote.
He wrote for three hours. He filled eleven of the nineteen pages that were left. His hand cramped and he shook it out and went on. The three notes came into his throat and he hummed them without stopping himself, softly, alone in the little room, and wrote.
He wrote everything. The green ledger. S.F.R. Struck From Register. The list of villages. The last line: Oakhaven. Account closed. By order of the Inquisitor-General. The thick back cover. The hollow. The red book with the rose in thorns. The household accounts, and the oily sheen on the ink, and the faint grooved letters between the lines. Witness. K. For my children, if I forget. A.T. The three cold stones in black silk, marked U.W. The torn gray page. Oakhaven. Registration. Children under fourteen. The names. Dorran, Pell. 6. Clear. And the two lines near the bottom that he could not read. A B. And a longer word.
He sat looking at that for a long time. Then he wrote: Those two were different. Pressed harder. Pell burned that part. Why that part?
He wrote the note with the red seal, word for word. He wrote very nearly, and underlined it. He wrote the Inkwell, and Old Joss, deaf as a stone, speckled like an egg. Two quick. One slow. Two quick. The cabinet on the left wall. Top corner. Q. Gothic. 36 point.
He wrote the woman. Tall. White shell half-mask. Red mouth. Long dark coat, fur collar, silver-gray. Seal-skin gloves. A necklace of tiny glowing vials. Lilies. A silver siphon cup with a spiral inside. "Lord Thorne is not well. He sends his regrets." "The tide weighs it, and it comes up light." He wrote the big man. Huge hands. Beer and onions. He wrote Pell's flash, and Pell's run, four steps, and the cold cup, and what went into it, one thing after another, until there was nothing left but a song.
He wrote: He hummed. Three notes. Gran's song. The same one the busker plays on Lantern Bridge. The one that makes your chest hurt. Why do you know it? Why does it hurt?
He did not have an answer. He left a space under the question, in case one came.
Then he wrote, at the bottom of the last page, in letters bigger than the rest:
THE RED BOOK IS STILL IN THE DRAWER. SHE DOESN'T KNOW WHERE IT IS. NOBODY DOES. EXCEPT YOU.
Go tonight. Before you forget.
He looked at that for a long time.
Then he put the Ledger inside his coat, over his heart, and the rod in its narrow pocket on the right, and the jar marked Do not open on the shelf with the empty ones, so that he would see it in the morning and wonder.
He turned the little brass lamp down until the flame was only a tiny blue bead. He did not turn it out.
And he went down the narrow, crooked, hateful stairs in the dark, past the scratched words near the fourth-floor landing, past Della's door, past Old Terrence's empty chair by the round dirty window, past Mrs. Marta's purple door with its smell of onions and its ticking clocks, and out into Tallow Street, where the gemstone lamps were burning cold and blue on their tall black heron-necks, and a thin rain was beginning to fall.
The Girl on the Roof
Tallow Street at night was a different street.
The carts were gone. The chestnut seller was gone, and her brazier with her, leaving only a black ring of soot on the cobbles at the corner. The shouting was gone, and the clatter of hooves, and the thump of the steam engine. What was left was the rain, falling thin and steady and almost silent, and the hum of the gemstone lamps on their tall black necks, and the drip of water from the gutters, and, very far away, down by the river, the low mournful hoot of a barge feeling its way through the dark.
The lamps made long blue pools on the wet stones. Between them, the darkness was thick and soft. Corin walked from pool to pool. He walked fast, with his collar up and his hat pulled low and the long wooden case under his arm, and in the dark stretches between the lamps he found that he was humming. Three notes. Up, and up, and up. He did not try to stop.
He was halfway down the hill when someone above him said, "You're out late, Mister V."
He stopped dead in a pool of blue light and looked up.
The houses of Tallow Street leaned toward each other over the narrow street, the way all the houses of the Lower Docks did. High above him, four stories up, where the roofs of the two sides almost met, a washing line ran across the gap. And sitting on the edge of a roof, with her legs dangling over the street and one hand resting on the washing line as if it were a banister, there was a girl.
She was small and thin, and dressed like a boy, in a patched coat too big for her and a cap pulled down over her ears. He could not see her face clearly, only the pale shape of it, and two bright eyes catching the lamplight. She did not seem to mind the rain. She did not seem to mind the four stories of empty air under her boots, either. She was swinging them, back and forth, slowly.
Around her neck, on a string, something small and brass caught the light. It looked like a key.
"Gran says you should be in bed," said the girl. "She says you came in at nine and you've been writing ever since. She can see your lamp from the yard." She tilted her head. "Now you're going out again. At three in the morning. In the rain. With your jar-stick."
"Who's your gran?" said Corin.
The girl sighed. It was a long, theatrical, carrying sigh, the kind that is meant to be heard from the other end of a street, and Corin had heard it before. That morning. In a dark passage that smelled of onions.
"You forgot me again," said the girl. She did not sound hurt. She sounded as if she had expected it. "That's all right. Everybody does. I'm Juno. From the purple door." She swung her boots. "You want a message carried, Mister V? Anywhere in the Docks, anywhere in the Ink Quarter. Two coppers. One if it's raining, because nobody else will go."
"Not tonight."
"Suit yourself." She stood up on the very edge of the roof, easily, as if she were standing on a kitchen floor, with the rain blowing around her and nothing at all between her and the cobbles far below. "Gran'll want to know where you went."
"Then don't tell her."
The girl grinned. He could see that, even from four stories down. A quick white flash in the dark.
"I never tell her anything," said Juno. "That's why she trusts me."
And she was gone. Not down. Up. Over the ridge of the roof and away, across the slates, so quickly and so quietly that he heard nothing but the faint scrape of a boot and the patter of the rain.
Corin stood in the blue pool of lamplight for a moment longer, looking up at the empty roof edge and the empty washing line, swinging gently in the wind where her hand had been.
Then he took out the Ledger, and shielded it from the rain with his hat, and wrote very small at the bottom of the last page: Juno. Mrs. Marta's granddaughter. On the roofs. Two coppers a message, one in the rain. Brass key on a string.
He did not know why he wrote down the key.
He put the Ledger away and went on down the hill toward the river, and across Lantern Bridge, where the little house-shaped lamps glowed blue along the walls and the busker's place between them was empty and wet, and on into the dark tangled streets of the Ink Quarter.
The bells across the city were ringing the third hour after midnight as he turned into Needle Street.
The Inkwell printing house stood on its corner, black and squat in the rain, like a toad asleep. All its windows were dark.
The rope was gone from the mouth of Gutter Lane. The crowd was gone, and the young constable with his thin blond mustache, and the lantern on its barrel. The lane was only a black slot between two black walls again, with the rain falling into it and the thin trickle of waste ink running down its middle, shining faintly where the light from the nearest lamp reached in. Somewhere down there, around the second bend, there was a heap of rubbish with nobody lying on it anymore.
Corin did not look. He had been there twice today. Once as himself, and once as a young man who hummed his gran's song in the dark because he was afraid. He did not need to go there a third time.
He stood for a moment under the dripping eaves of the printing house, with the rain running off the brim of his hat, and listened. To the city. To the hum of the lamps. To his own heart, which was beating, steadily, the way hearts are meant to beat. He put his hand flat on the Ledger inside his coat. Then he went on, round to the back.
He lifted his hand to knock. Two quick. One slow. Two quick.
He did not knock.
The small back door, with its brass knocker shaped like a printer's ink ball, was already open. Just an inch. Just a dark crack. And from inside, from somewhere deep among the sleeping presses and the hanging ghosts of paper, came a sound.
The soft, careful, sliding sound of a drawer being pulled open.
And then the small, heavy, metal clink of letters being lifted out of it, one by one.
