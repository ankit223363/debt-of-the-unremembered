Chapter 2: Ash in a Jar, Part 4 of 5: Drowning
Drowning
Corin did not feel the club come down either.
He felt the heart stop.
It stopped the way a clock stops when nobody winds it. Not with a bang. Not with a jolt. It simply beat, once, slow and soft, and then waited for the next beat, and the next beat did not come. And the waiting went on. And on. And there was no end to it.
And Corin was inside it.
He was lying on his back on a heap of rubbish at the end of a black lane, looking up at a strip of sky that was turning gray at the edges, and he did not know his name. He did not know what a name was. He did not know what he was. There was nothing in him. Nothing at all. Only a cold, clean, swept, empty place, like a room after the furniture has been carried out. White walls. Bare floor. And in the middle of the bare floor, very small, a sound. Three notes. Humming. Going up, and up, and up.
And then not even that.
Scavenger.
The voice came from very far away. Further than the gray sky. Further than the city. Thin and dry and faint, like a voice calling down a well.
Scavenger. Wake up.
Corin did not wake up. He did not know what waking up was. He lay on the rubbish heap and looked up at the gray and felt the cold come up from the bottom of the pool around him, closing over his chest, his throat, his mouth.
It was peaceful.
That was what frightened him, later, when he could be frightened again. Not the cold. Not the dark. Not the stopped heart. It was how peaceful it was. How easy. Like sinking into a warm bath at the end of a long day. Like closing your eyes. There was nothing to remember, and nothing to be afraid of, and nothing to want. Nothing to carry. Nothing at all.
He could stay here, he thought. If he could have thought. He could stay here forever, in the clean white empty room, and it would be fine.
Scavenger. The voice was sharper. Closer. You fool. You absolute, thrice-blessed, curious fool. I told you. I told you, and you went to the bottom anyway, and now look at you. Lying there like a Slate on a landing. Smiling at the sky.
Corin was smiling. He had not noticed.
Do you know what happens if you stay? said Malakor. I'll tell you. Your heart will stop. Not his. Yours. Out there, on that rubbish heap, in front of a Watch sergeant and a surgeon and a deaf old rag-picker. You'll just stop. Like he did. And they'll find two of you on the heap instead of one. And your frightened clerk will turn round at the end of the alley and see.
The cold had reached Corin's eyes.
And I, said Malakor, with sudden, savage bitterness, will have to wait another forty years in this wretched rod for some other fool to pick me up.
The Wrong Name
Something moved in the white empty room.
Not much. A flicker. A word. It came up from somewhere very deep, from underneath the floor of the room, from a place where the cold silver cup had not reached, because it had never been Pell's to give.
A name.
Pell.
No. That was the wrong name. That was not the name. That was the dead man's name.
Pell Dorran. Grade Three. First Basement. Oakhaven.
No. No, that was wrong too, that was all his, that was the scribe's, none of that was—
Oakhaven.
The word stopped him. Stuck. Like a fishhook catching in the soft part of a thumb.
Oakhaven.
He had read that word somewhere. Not in a green Treasury ledger. Not in a list of names on a torn gray page. Somewhere else. Somewhere that was not here, not this lane, not this cold. In his own handwriting. On the inside of a leather cover. In the morning. In a room with a fish-shaped stain on the ceiling and a little brass lamp burning a tiny blue bead of flame.
You were born in Oakhaven.
Who was?
You were.
Who was you?
The white walls of the empty room rippled, as if a stone had been dropped into a pond behind them.
Read the list, said a voice.
Not Malakor's voice. A woman's voice. Low and calm and fierce. He did not know it. He had never heard it in his life. It came up from under the floor of the empty room, from the same deep place the name had come from, and it was gone as soon as it had spoken, like a fish turning in dark water.
Read the list. Whatever happens. Wherever they take you. If you forget, read the list.
Corin did not know what list. He did not know whose voice. But something in him that was older than the Ash, older than Malakor, older than the rod or the jar or the Ministry or the room with the fish-shaped stain, something very small and very stubborn, like a child holding on to a doorframe while someone tries to pull it away, said:
No. Not yet. Not his. Mine.
And reached up.
The Surface
He could not see. He could not breathe. He was at the bottom of the cold dark pool, with the weight of the dead man's last minutes pressing down on him like deep water, and he did not know which way was up.
But he could hear.
Far away. Faint. Up there, somewhere. Through the water. A voice. Not Malakor. Not the woman. A young man's voice, high and cracked and frightened, saying the same words over and over and over, like someone knocking on a door that will not open.
"—name is Corin. You were born in Oakhaven. You had a younger sister named Elara. You used to fear the dark. Your name is Corin. You were born in Oakhaven. You had a younger sister—"
Corin.
The word went through the cold water like a rope thrown down from a boat.
He reached for it.
His fingers closed on it.
And he pulled.
Coming Back
He came up gasping, like a man pulled out of a river.
The first thing he knew was that he was lying down. Not on a rubbish heap. On something hard and flat and wet. Stone, or mud. His cheek was pressed against it. It was cold and gritty and smelled of ink.
The second thing he knew was that someone was holding his head. Two hands, on either side of his face. Thin hands. Shaking. Very cold.
The third thing he knew was the voice. Right above him. Very close. Hoarse, as if it had been talking for a long time.
"—had a younger sister named Elara. You used to fear the dark. Your name is Corin. You were born in Oakhaven. You had a younger sister named—"
"Toby," said Corin.
The voice stopped.
Corin opened his eyes.
He was lying in the mud at the bottom of the rubbish heap, on his side, in the yellow light of the lantern on its barrel. His knees were drawn up. His hat had come off. One of his gloves had come off. The silver rod was lying in the mud a little way from his hand, its spiral tip shining. The open jar was lying beside it, on its side. Empty.
And Toby was kneeling over him.
Toby's face was gray. Gray as old porridge, gray as wet ash, with two bright red spots high on his cheekbones, like the cheeks of a painted doll. His thick spectacles had slid down to the end of his pointed nose. Behind them, his eyes were huge and wet and red-rimmed. He had the Ledger open in one hand, held up close to his face, and the other hand was pressed flat against the side of Corin's head, holding it still. His fingers were trembling so hard that Corin could feel them through his hair.
"Inspector?" Toby whispered.
"Yes."
"Is it you?"
Corin thought about that. It seemed a fair question.
He looked inside himself. At the soft gray quiet place behind his eyes. It was not empty. There were things in it. Too many things. A green Treasury ledger. A little knife. A red book with a rose in thorns. A deaf old printer speckled like an egg. A woman in a white shell mask, with a necklace of tiny lights. A cold silver cup. A strip of sky turning gray. They were crowded in there, shouldering each other, loud and bright and close. And they were not his.
But under them, very small, at the bottom, there was a fish-shaped stain on a ceiling. A little brass lamp with a blue bead of flame. A bowl of stew, burned on the bottom. A big old man with caterpillar eyebrows writing YOU HAVE INK on a chalkboard. A frightened young clerk saying I keep hoping.
Those were his.
"Yes," said Corin. "It's me."
Toby made a sound. It was not a word. It was the sound of a breath let go after being held for a very long time. He sat back on his heels in the mud, and the Ledger slid out of his fingers into his lap, and he put both hands over his face.
How Long
"How long?" said Corin.
It was not Toby who answered. It was Dr. Fane.
The tall gray surgeon was crouched on Corin's other side, with his black bag open beside him. He had two long fingers pressed against the inside of Corin's wrist. His long gray face was very still, and his lips were moving slightly. Counting.
"Your heart stopped," he said, in his deep slow funeral-bell voice. He did not take his fingers away. "Did you know that, Harvester? For eleven of my own heartbeats, it stopped. You fell off the heap. Straight down, like a sack of grain. You were smiling." He paused. "I have seen many men die. I have never seen one smile like that."
"And then?"
"And then your young clerk came running round that corner like a hare with a dog behind it," said Dr. Fane, "and pushed me out of the way, which nobody has done in thirty years, and took your book out of your coat, and started reading to you. Loudly. Over and over." He took his fingers off Corin's wrist at last. "Your heart started again on the fourth time through. You have been lying here with your eyes open, not seeing anything, for nearly a quarter of an hour. He did not stop once."
Corin turned his head, slowly. It felt very heavy.
Toby had taken his hands away from his face. He was sitting in the mud with his knees drawn up and the Ledger in his lap, wiping his spectacles on his shirt. His hands were still shaking too badly to do it properly.
"You said I could stand at the end of the alley," said Toby. His voice cracked. "With my back turned."
"I did."
"You promised."
"I know."
"I heard you fall." Toby put his spectacles back on. They were smeared. He did not seem to notice. "I heard Dr. Fane shout. And I turned round. And you were lying there, and you weren't breathing, and you were smiling, just like—just like—" He could not finish. He swallowed. "Just like Pell," he said at last, very quietly.
Corin looked at him for a long moment.
"Thank you, Toby," he said.
Toby looked away. His big ears had gone red. He picked up the Ledger from his lap and held it out to Corin, stiffly, without looking at him.
"Eleven times," he muttered. "Again. I counted."
Still Him
Corin sat up.
It took a long time. His body did not want to do it. It was heavy and cold and stiff, as if he had been lying in the mud for hours, not minutes. His head swam. The walls of Gutter Lane tilted and swung around him, and the lantern on its barrel left long yellow streaks across his eyes. He put one hand flat on the ground to steady himself, and it sank into the cold black mud up to the wrist.
He looked at his hand.
It was his hand. Bigger than Pell's. Broader. Scarred across the knuckles in places he did not remember being cut. No ink on the first two fingers. No hard lump on the side of the middle one.
But it was shaking. And when he tried to make it stop, it went on shaking. And he knew, without knowing how he knew, that it would go on shaking for the rest of the day.
And there was something else.
He was humming.
He did not know he was doing it until he heard it. Very soft. Through his nose. Three notes. Going up, and up, and up. And stopping there, in the air, asking a question that never got an answer.
Toby went white again.
"Stop that," he whispered. "Please. That's—that's Pell's song. He hummed it all the time. At his desk. On the steps. Grane used to shout at him for it." His voice shook. "Please stop."
Corin stopped. It took an effort. The three notes did not want to go. They sat in his throat like a word on the tip of the tongue.
"Sorry," he said.
He took the Ledger from Toby's hand. He held it for a moment, feeling the weight of it, the soft old leather, the corners worn pale. Then he opened it at the first page. At the inside of the front cover. At the five lines in his own big, untidy, black handwriting.
Your name is Corin.
He read them. Once. Slowly. The way you check a lock that you know is already locked.
Then he closed the book and put it back inside his coat, over his heart. And his hand stopped shaking. Just a little. Just enough.
Well, said a dry, thin, faraway voice behind his eyes. It sounded tired. It sounded, very faintly, almost relieved. That was closer than I like, scavenger.
Corin did not answer. He reached out and picked the silver rod up out of the mud, and wiped it on his coat, and put it back in its narrow pocket on the right, opposite the Ledger.
The voice went quiet.
What He Knows
Sergeant Moll had not moved from his place by the far wall. He was standing there with his pipe in his hand, cold and forgotten, and his boiled-ham face had gone the color of uncooked dough. Behind him, on his crate in the shadows, old Wick had his eyes squeezed shut and his hands pressed over his ears and was rocking back and forth, back and forth, muttering something over and over to himself.
"Harvester," said the sergeant. His voice was rough. "Did you get it? Whatever you went in for. Was it worth that?"
Corin looked at him. Then up at the heap of rubbish, where Pell Dorran was still lying on his back with his arms spread wide and his warm brown eyes open to the strip of sky. The gray light had come further down the lane while Corin was gone. It lay on the young scribe's face now, pale and soft. In that light, the empty, gentle smile looked almost peaceful.
There was no blue left in the brown eyes. Not even a thread. Nothing at all.
Corin had taken it. All of it. It was in him now. Crowded and loud and bright. And he would have it for perhaps a day, perhaps two, perhaps a week, before it faded the way all Ash faded, the way the morning's stew and Della's cat and Mrs. Marta's purple door would fade, and be gone.
Unless he wrote it down.
"Yes," he said. "I got it."
He stood up. His knees nearly gave way. Toby jumped up and caught his arm. Corin let him.
"Sergeant," he said. "Pell Dorran was not robbed. Pell Dorran was lured here by a note with a forged seal, and drained with a siphon cup by a tall woman in a white half-mask who smells of lilies and wears a necklace of memory-vials. A big man held him. The big man hit him afterward, to make it look like a robbery, because the woman told him to. Because she heard Wick tread on a bottle at the second bend."
On his crate, old Wick stopped rocking. He opened his eyes.
"And she didn't get what she came for," said Corin. "Pell burned it first. Right in front of her. All of it." He paused. "Pell Dorran was the bravest man in this lane last night. Write that in your report."
Sergeant Moll stared at him. Then, very slowly, he took the stub of a pencil from behind his ear, and a small black notebook from his coat pocket, and opened it, and began to write.
Afterward
The Jar
There was one more thing to do before they left the lane. Malakor told him what it was.
The dying, said the dry voice, very quietly, as Corin stood swaying beside the heap with Toby holding his arm. You took all of him. The knowing and the dying, both. Keep the knowing. You'll need it. But give the dying back. Put it in the jar. If you carry a man's death around inside you, scavenger, sooner or later it starts to think it's yours.
"How?"
The way you always do. You've done it a thousand times. Your hands know.
Corin bent down, slowly, and picked up the empty jar from the mud. He wiped the rim on his coat. He held it up in front of his mouth, close, the way you hold a cup to drink.
And he breathed out.
Not an ordinary breath. A long, slow, careful one, from the very bottom of himself. And as he breathed, he thought, very clearly, of the end. The cold silver cup. The draining. The white empty room. The heart, waiting for a beat that did not come. He thought of it, and held it, and let it go, out of his mouth, into the glass.
A thread of pale blue-white mist came out with his breath. Thin. Faint. It curled into the jar and settled at the bottom, and turned slowly there, round and round, like a cat settling to sleep.
Corin screwed the brass lid on, tight.
He felt lighter at once. As if a weight he had not known he was carrying had been lifted off his chest. The green ledger was still there, in his head. The red book. The Inkwell. The drawer marked Q. The woman in the white mask. But the cold was gone. The stopped heart was gone. The peaceful, smiling, terrible emptiness was gone.
He took the pencil from the Ledger's spine and wrote on the small paper label on the side of the jar, in his big untidy hand:
DORRAN, P. Last minutes. Stable. Do not open.
And under it, after a moment, smaller:
He hummed.
The Wagon
The Watch wagon came for Pell a little after the ninth bell.
It was a plain black box on four wheels, pulled by a tired brown horse with a white blaze on its nose. Two constables carried a stretcher down Gutter Lane, past the bends, and lifted the young scribe off the heap of rubbish as gently as they could, and laid him on it, and covered him with a gray blanket. Dr. Fane closed the warm brown eyes with two long fingers before the blanket went over his face. He said something under his breath as he did it. Corin did not hear what.
Toby walked beside the stretcher all the way back up the lane to the street. He did not say anything. He kept one hand on the edge of the gray blanket, near where Pell's shoulder would be. When they reached the rope at the mouth of the lane, the crowd was still there, thinner now, and when they saw the stretcher come out under the gray blanket they went quiet and moved back. A woman crossed herself. A man took off his cap.
The constables slid the stretcher into the back of the black wagon and shut the doors. The tired brown horse shook its head and stamped. And the wagon rolled away up Needle Street, slow and creaking, past the ink-shops and the tattoo parlors and the painted signs swinging in the smoky wind, and turned the corner, and was gone.
Toby stood on the cobbles and watched the corner where it had gone for a long time.
Then he put his hand on the inside pocket of his too-big gray coat, where a red sock was buttoned in, and kept it there.
Sergeant Moll came and stood beside Corin at the mouth of the lane. He had relit his pipe. He smoked it for a while without saying anything, looking up the street.
"They'll send their paper," he said at last. "Two weeks. Natural causes. Same as the others."
"Maybe," said Corin.
The sergeant looked at him sideways. "You going to stop them?"
"I don't know." Corin was quiet for a moment. "I don't know if I'll remember to."
Sergeant Moll grunted. He took the pipe out of his mouth and looked at it. Then he took the small black notebook out of his coat pocket again and tore a page out of it, carefully, and folded it in half, and held it out.
"Copy of what I wrote," he said. "What you told me. Woman in a mask. Lilies. The big man. The forged seal. All of it. Signed and dated." He pushed it into Corin's hand. "In case your Ministry loses mine."
Corin looked at the folded page. Then at the sergeant's square, red, tired face.
"Thank you," he said.
"Don't thank me." Sergeant Moll put the pipe back between his teeth. "Just put it in that book of yours. Somewhere you'll find it."
