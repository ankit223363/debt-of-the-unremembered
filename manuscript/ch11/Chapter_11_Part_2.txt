Chapter 11: Aunt Cressida, Part 2 of 5: The Drawing Room
The Drawing Room
The long room was white and gold, like the hall, but softer.
It ran the whole width of the house, from the windows on Crown Walk at the front to the windows over the white garden at the back, and both rows of windows had their curtains drawn. The curtains were heavy white silk, falling from gold poles in long smooth folds, and the light from the room lay on them so that they glowed like the inside of a shell. The walls were hung with pale yellow silk, the color of primroses, and on the silk, at even spaces, there were mirrors. Tall mirrors, in thin gold frames, each one facing another across the room, so that wherever Corin looked he saw the room going on and on, smaller and smaller, into a gold distance that never ended. A hundred long rooms. A hundred crowds of people. A hundred green coats standing by a hundred walls.
He did not like the mirrors. He did not know why. He kept his eyes off them.
At either end of the room there was a fireplace, white marble carved with leaves, and in each one a fire was burning, low and red, though the room was already too warm. Over each fireplace hung a painting. One was of a lake at sunset, all pink and gold. The other was of a young girl in a white dress, sitting on a swing in a garden with her hair down her back and a lily in her lap. The girl's hair was pale gold. Her mouth was red. She was laughing.
There was a harp in one corner, tall and gilded, with nobody playing it. There were little sofas and little chairs, covered in white and yellow silk, set about the room in small friendly groups. There were tables with lamps on them, and tables with silver dishes of sugared almonds and crystallized violets and tiny cakes no bigger than a thumbnail, iced in pink and white. And everywhere, of course, on every table and in every corner and along the mantelpieces in long white ranks, there were the lilies.
The room was full. Thirty people, perhaps. Forty. Corin did not count. He found his place.
The servants' place was along the wall by the door, behind a long sofa, where the light from the lamps did not quite reach. There were six or seven of them there already, standing in a row with their heels together and their hands behind their backs. A footman in yellow with silver buttons. A tall thin maid in black with a white cap, holding a lady's shawl over her arm. A man in deep plum, very young, with a pale clever face and dark hair cut short. Another footman in sky blue, round-faced and sleepy. They moved apart, a little, without looking at him, to make a space. He stepped into it. They closed up again on either side.
Nobody said anything. Nobody looked at him. But he felt the footman in yellow, on his left, go stiff for a moment as the green coat brushed his sleeve. And he heard the young man in plum, on his right, breathe out very softly through his nose, as if he were trying not to laugh, or not to sigh.
Corin found a spot on the opposite wall. Above the painting of the lake at sunset. A small gold leaf in the frame of the mirror beside it. He put his eyes there and let the rest of the room come to him from the edges.
Cressida had taken Lyra into the middle of the room. She was walking her round, from group to group, like a lady showing a new horse to her friends. Her red mouth was talking all the time. Her gloved hand stayed on Lyra's arm.
"Harriet, darling, here she is, you see, I told you—"
Harriet Langley was a small round woman in pink, with three tall pink feathers standing up in her hair like the tail of an angry bird and a great many diamonds on her round pink chest. She had a round pink face and small round blue eyes, and the eyes were wide with joy. She caught Lyra's free hand and squeezed it and did not let go.
"Oh, my dear," said Harriet Langley. "Oh, my dear. I saw you. From my very own window. At noon. And I said to Langley, I said, Langley, that is Isolde's girl, I'd know that walk anywhere—"
She stopped. Very suddenly. As if she had trodden on something.
All the little group around her had gone quiet. A gentleman with a gray beard looked at the ceiling. A thin lady in amber looked at her glass.
Cressida's red smile did not move at all.
"Harriet," she said gently. "We don't say her name, dear. You know that. It upsets Aurelian."
"Oh. Oh, of course. Of course, how silly of me, I forgot." Harriet Langley had gone redder than her gown. "I'm so sorry, my dear. Old habits. We were girls together, you see, your mother and I. Well. Not girls. She came to us when she was—well. I always called her by her name." She patted Lyra's hand, fast and flustered. "Forgive a silly old woman."
"There's nothing to forgive," said Lyra.
Her voice was steady. But Corin, from the wall, saw her hand close on Harriet Langley's round pink fingers. Just for a moment. Hard.
Isolde, he thought.
He did not know if it was the real name. Madame Hesper's pattern book had said only Lady Thorne, and the marks on the back of the silver brooch had been, Lady Thorne said, my real name, the one I was given on the water. So this was the name she had been called on the Hill. Isolde. A Hill name. Perhaps not her own.
He wrote it on the inside of his eyes, to put in the Ledger later. ISOLDE. Harriet Langley said it. "We don't say her name." Cressida: "It upsets Aurelian."
Aurelian, who did not know his own name anymore. Who sat at a window over a stable and smiled at swallows.
It upsets Aurelian, said Malakor dryly. Yes. I am sure it upsets him terribly.
Cressida had already moved Lyra on.
To an old gentleman in black, short and stooped, with a face like a dried apple and a row of shining jet buttons all down his waistcoat. Lord Pembrook, mourning, will not take them off, Madame Hesper's pattern book had said. He bowed over Lyra's hand and said something Corin could not hear, in a voice like rustling paper, and Lyra bowed her head.
To a pair of young ladies in sea-green, sisters by the look of them, who giggled behind their fans and asked Lyra if it was true she rode a horse called Patience with no saddle, and Lyra said yes, and they giggled harder.
To a tall thin gentleman with silver hair and a Councillor's chain of office round his neck, heavy gold links with a small gold book hanging from them, and on the book, very small, a closed eye. The Ministry's sign. He did not smile at Lyra at all. He looked at her the way a man looks at a column of figures that will not add up, and then he looked over her head, straight across the room, at Corin in his green coat by the wall.
Corin did not look back. He looked at the gold leaf on the mirror frame.
The gentleman looked at him for a long time. Then he turned away.
Councillor, said Malakor. One of her colleagues. He does not like the green any more than she does. But he likes it for different reasons.
And then Cressida brought Lyra to the fire.
There were two people standing by the fireplace under the painting of the girl on the swing. They were apart from the rest. Not by much. A few paces. But the few paces were empty, and the other guests did not step into them, the way people do not step onto the ice of a pond in spring.
One of them was a woman.
She was not young, and she was not old. She was dressed in black. Not mourning black, like Lord Pembrook's. A black so deep and so rich that it was almost blue where the firelight touched it, a black velvet gown cut low at the shoulders, with no jewels at her throat at all. Her hair was black too, smooth and shining, parted in the middle and drawn back into a heavy knot at her neck. Her skin was dark gold, like honey, and her face was broad and calm, with heavy-lidded eyes and a small wide mouth, and she was holding a fan. A black fan of lace, folded shut. She tapped it, slowly, against her palm, the way Madame Hesper tapped her spectacles.
The only bright thing about her was her hands. She had taken off one glove. Her bare hand, holding the fan, was covered in rings. Every finger. Two or three on some. Gold and silver and black iron, and every ring had a stone in it, and every stone was the same: clear and pale and faintly glowing, like a drop of water with a light inside.
Gemstones. Small ones. The kind of stones, Corin knew without knowing how he knew, that the Bank made at its long bright counters out of people's afternoons.
Beside her, half a step behind, stood a boy. A young man. Seventeen, perhaps, or eighteen. Slight and neat, in a gray coat so well cut that it made Corin's own Ministry coat, folded away in a trunk on Market Row, look like a sack. He had brown hair and a pale serious face and very dark eyes, and he was not looking at Lyra. He was looking at the room. All of it. Slowly. The way Corin looked at it.
And then, for a moment, the dark eyes came to the wall by the door, and found the green coat, and stopped.
The young man in gray looked at Corin. Corin looked at the gold leaf on the mirror.
The dark eyes stayed on him for a long, long time.
"Lady Vivienne," Cressida was saying, in a voice that had become a little higher and a little brighter than before. "May I present my niece? Miss Lyra Thorne. Lyra, darling, this is Lady Vivienne, who is very generous to all of us, and her ward, Mr. Castellan."
Lady Vivienne did not offer her hand. She looked at Lyra with her heavy-lidded eyes for a long, calm moment. Then she smiled. It was a slow smile, and a warm one, and it did not reach her eyes at all.
"Thorne green," she said. Her voice was low and soft and a little husky, like velvet rubbed the wrong way. "On a footman. In this house." She tapped the black fan against her ringed palm. "I did not think there was anyone left on the Hill with that much nerve. I like it."
"Thank you," said Lyra.
"Don't thank me. It will get you killed, very probably. Nerve usually does." Lady Vivienne's smile did not change. "But it will be interesting while it lasts." She turned the fan in her fingers. "I give a little evening, Miss Thorne. On Thursdays. Quite small. Quite private. Cressida knows. People come to look at beautiful things, and sometimes to buy them, and sometimes to sell." The heavy eyes went over Lyra, slowly, from her short black hair to her mother's gown. "You should come. Bring your green man. I think you would find a great many things worth looking at."
"Vivienne," said Cressida lightly. "The child has only just come out of mourning."
"Has she?" said Lady Vivienne. "How lucky for her. Some of us never do."
She inclined her head, very slightly, to Lyra. And to Cressida. And then she turned back to the fire and looked at the painting above it, the girl on the swing with the lily in her lap, as if nobody else were in the room.
The young man, Mr. Castellan, bowed to Lyra. A short, neat, polite bow. As he straightened, his dark eyes went, once more, across the room to Corin by the wall. And then he said something to Lyra, very quietly, under the noise of the room, so quietly that Corin almost missed it.
But Corin had been trained by a tall woman with pins in her mouth to see everything and show nothing. And he saw the young man's lips move. And he read them.
Your footman stands like a Harvester.
Lyra did not change her face at all. She only smiled, the way a lady smiles at a compliment she does not understand, and let Cressida draw her away.
Corin stood very still by the wall. He did not move his feet. He did not move his hands. He made his weight sit on both feet, evenly, and he made his right hand loosen behind his back, and he made his shoulders drop. The way the tall woman had taught him. A footman stands as if he has already given everything away.
The young man in gray was not looking at him anymore. He had gone back to looking at the room.
But Corin knew. The way you know when a dog in the street has noticed you, even after it has looked away.
That one, said Malakor, very low, keeps a list. I would put money on it. Look at the way he looks at people. He is not looking at their faces. He is counting them.
At the far end of the room, a bell rang. A small silver bell, in Bramwell's long thin hand, by the tall white doors.
"Tea," called Cressida Thorne, in her light high happy voice. "Tea, everyone! Bram, the cups. And send the servants down, would you, poor things, they've been standing there for an hour, they'll be dropping."
And the line of servants along the wall, as if pulled by one string, turned to the door.
Below Stairs
The stairs down to the servants' hall were behind a door in the paneling of the hall, a door so cleverly made that it had no handle and no frame and looked like part of the wall. A white footman pressed on it with the flat of his hand, and it swung open, and behind it there was a different house.
Corin went down with the others. Single file. The footman in yellow first, then the tall maid in black, then the round sleepy footman in sky blue, then Corin, then the young man in plum, close behind him. The stairs were narrow and steep and made of plain gray stone, and lit by small gas lamps in wire cages on the wall. After the gold and white above, the light here seemed very dim and very yellow, and the walls were bare plaster, painted a dull brown up to the height of a man's shoulder and a dirty cream above it. The smell of lilies grew weaker with every step. By the bottom, it was only a thread. Something else had taken its place.
Food. Hot fat and roasting meat and boiling cabbage and new bread. And under it, strong and sour and warm, beer.
Corin breathed it in, and felt his whole body loosen, the way it had loosened in the mews. The part that smells like home, Lyra had said.
The servants' hall was a long low room under the front of the house, with small barred windows high up in one wall at the level of the pavement outside. Corin could see the feet of the carriage horses through them, standing in the road, and the wheels of the carriages, and the legs of the coachmen walking up and down to keep warm. In the middle of the room there was a long table, plain scrubbed wood, with benches down both sides. At the far end there was a fire, a big one, in an open grate, with a kettle on a hook over it and a great black pot beside it, steaming. There were dressers along the walls with rows of plain white plates and plain white cups, very different from the cups upstairs. There was a clock on the wall, a plain round clock with a loud tick. There was a board on the wall by the door with a row of bells on springs, each with a label under it, exactly like the board in the kitchen of Number 3.
Corin looked at the labels without meaning to. Drawing Room. Dining Room. Her Ladyship's Room. Study. Glasshouse. No children's names. No painted-over label. Cressida Thorne had no children.
The room was already half full. There were servants from other houses here before them, coachmen in their heavy caped coats steaming by the fire, maids sitting together at one end of the table with their heads close, a boy of twelve or so in a too-big livery of brown and gold, asleep with his head on his arms. Two of Cressida's own servants in plain white aprons, not the white silk of upstairs, were going up and down the table with jugs and plates. One of them, a big red-armed woman with a face like a ham, thumped down a plate in front of each new arrival as they sat: two slices of bread and dripping, a wedge of yellow cheese, a pickled onion, and a mug.
"Tea or ale," said the red-armed woman, to nobody in particular. "There's no wine for you lot, so don't ask."
"Tea," said Corin.
She looked at him. At the green coat. Her face did something complicated.
"Tea," she said, and filled his mug from a great brown pot, and moved on.
He sat down on the bench. The young man in plum sat beside him. The footman in yellow and the round one in sky blue sat across the table. Nobody said anything for a while. They ate. Corin ate too. He found he was very hungry. The bread was soft and the dripping was salty and good, and he remembered, from somewhere, that a tall woman with pins in her mouth had told him to eat something.
Around them, the room talked. It was a different kind of talk from upstairs. Louder, and rougher, and quicker, and a great deal more interesting.
"—and she says to him, she says, If you think I'm wearing that hat to the Archon's, you've another think coming, and he says—"
"—three months' wages, and she's let him go without a character, after eleven years—"
"—seen the new Councillor's carriage? Gold wheels. Gold. On a Ministry salary—"
"—the Bank took her mother's whole wedding, you know, every bit of it, she can't remember who she married—"
And under all of it, coming back again and again, like the beat of a drum under a tune:
"—green. Thorne green. Down here. In her house."
They were talking about him. The maids at the end of the table. The coachmen by the fire. Not loudly. Not to his face. But their eyes kept coming to him and going away again, the way eyes go to a fire in a room.
The young man in plum leaned a little toward him, without turning his head.
"You've got a nerve," he said, very low. "I'll say that for you."
He had a quick soft voice with an accent Corin did not know. Not the Docks and not the Hill. Something from further off, with the ends of the words clipped short. He was very young, Corin saw now that he was close. Nineteen. Twenty at most. He had a pale narrow face, with a long jaw and quick brown eyes and dark hair cut very short, so short that his ears stuck out a little. There was a small white scar through his left eyebrow, like a tick on a page.
"I just walk where I'm told," said Corin.
"Course you do. Course you do." The young man broke his bread in half and looked at it. "Name's Tam. Tam Hollin. I'm Lady Vivienne's. Second footman." He did not offer his hand. Servants did not, below stairs, it seemed. He just ate his bread. "You're Thorne's."
"Vane."
"Vane." Tam chewed. "Never heard of a Vane on the Hill."
"I'm new."
"You must be." Tam swallowed. His quick brown eyes slid sideways, very briefly, to Corin's hands, where they held the mug. The white cotton gloves, and under them, at the wrist, where the cotton had pulled back a little, a sliver of gray. "Most of us take our gloves off below stairs," he said. "Saves them. The white goes gray in a week otherwise, and then you're paying for new ones."
"I'll remember."
"Will you?" Tam smiled a little, into his bread. "Good."
He did not say anything else. He ate his cheese. But Corin felt him there, close on the bench, the way you feel a cat beside you in the dark.
Then the door at the far end of the room, by the fire, opened, and a man came in from the area steps, out of the cold, and the whole room went a little quieter.
He was big.
He had to stoop to come under the lintel. When he straightened up on the other side, his head nearly brushed the beams of the low ceiling. He was wide too. Wide in the shoulders, wide in the chest, wide in the neck, so that his head seemed to sit straight on his shoulders without any neck at all. He wore a coachman's coat, the heavy kind with three short capes over the shoulders, in dark gray wool spotted with rain, and under it a waistcoat of white, which was Cressida's color, gone a little gray at the edges. His face was broad and flat and red, with a nose that had been broken more than once, and small pale eyes set deep under a heavy brow, and a jaw like a block of wood. His hair was cropped close to his skull, brown going gray.
And his hands. Corin looked at his hands, and felt his heart stop, and start again.
They were enormous. Each one as big as a dinner plate. Thick and red and scarred across the knuckles, with fingers as fat as sausages. He was pulling off a pair of leather driving gloves as he came in, and slapping them against his thigh to get the rain off, and the sound of them was like somebody beating a carpet.
The big man. Huge hands.
He came down the room toward the fire, and as he passed behind Corin's bench, the smell of him came too. Rain and horse and leather. And under it, strong and stale and sour, so close that Corin could almost taste it:
Beer. And onions.
Corin did not move. He held his mug of tea in both gloved hands and looked into it. He made his shoulders soft. He made his breathing slow. He thought about the slag room, and the clouded glass, and putting things in jars, and closing the lid, and not letting his face move.
Steady, said Malakor. Very quiet. Very close. He does not know you, boy. He has never seen your face. You were a gray coat in a dark lane, behind a lamp. You are a green coat now, in a warm room, eating bread. He does not know you.
The big man had reached the fire. He dropped onto the end of the bench there, which creaked loudly under him, and the coachmen sitting nearest shuffled along to make room without being asked. The red-armed woman brought him a plate without being asked either. Not bread and dripping. A thick slice of cold beef, and a heap of pickled onions, and a whole loaf, and a mug so big it was almost a jug, full to the brim with dark brown ale.
"Gorse," said the red-armed woman. It was a greeting.
"Mags," said the big man. His voice was the voice from the long gallery. Deep and thick and slow, like something rolling at the bottom of a barrel.
He drank half the ale in one long swallow. Then he put the mug down, and wiped his mouth with the back of one huge hand, and looked up the long table.
His small pale eyes went along the faces. The maids at the end. The sleeping boy. The coachmen. The footman in yellow, the footman in sky blue. Tam, eating his cheese.
And Corin.
The eyes stopped.
They stopped on the green coat, the way everyone's eyes had stopped. They stayed there a moment. Then they came up to Corin's face, slowly, and stayed there too.
Corin looked into his tea.
"You," said Gorse.
The room had gone quiet. Not all of it. The maids at the end were still whispering. But the table nearest the fire had stopped talking, and the coachmen had turned their heads.
"Green coat," said Gorse. "You. Thorne's man."
"Yes," said Corin.
"Where'd you serve before?"
It was such a simple question. Corin had not thought of it. He had thought of everything else. How to stand. How to walk. Yes, my lady. Where to put his hat. But he had not thought of this, and nobody had told him the answer, and the Ledger did not say.
"Before," said Corin.
"Before you were hers. Where?" The small pale eyes did not blink. "Nobody's worn that coat in eight years. Not since the old lady took her footman to the seaside and the pair of them went in the water. So where'd she dig you up?"
Went in the water.
Corin did not let his face move.
He opened his mouth. He did not know what was going to come out of it.
And Tam Hollin, beside him, put down his cheese, and laughed.
"Leave off, Gorse," said Tam. Easily. Cheerfully. In his quick clipped voice. "He's from the Langleys' country place. Isn't that right, Vane? Down in the Fens. I heard the girl got him off Harriet Langley's cousin for a song when the cousin's man died. Harriet was telling the whole room upstairs." He picked up a pickled onion and looked at it. "You know what Harriet's like. Can't keep a thing in her mouth but cake."
Gorse looked at Tam. Then at Corin. Then at Tam again.
"The Fens," said Gorse slowly.
"Down past Saltmarsh," said Tam. "Isn't it, Vane?"
"Past Saltmarsh," said Corin.
The big man looked at him for a long time. His small pale eyes were not stupid. Corin saw that. They were slow, the way a bull is slow. But a bull is not stupid. It only takes a while to decide which way to charge.
Then Gorse grunted, and picked up his beef in his fingers, and bit off half of it, and turned back to the fire.
The room started talking again. Slowly, then all at once. The coachmen turned their heads away. The maids at the end of the table went back to their whispering.
Corin sat very still on the bench with his mug of tea.
"Thank you," he said, very low, without looking at Tam.
Tam ate his pickled onion. He did not look at Corin either.
"Don't," he said, as quietly. "Don't thank me. I didn't do it for you."
"Then who?"
Tam did not answer that. He chewed. He swallowed. He took a long drink of his ale.
"The Langleys don't have a country place in the Fens," he said at last. "They've never been further east than the river. And Harriet Langley hasn't got a cousin. She's an only child, and her mother was an only child, and she tells everyone that too." He put down his mug. "Gorse doesn't know that. Gorse doesn't know anything that isn't a horse or a door or a head. But the Councillor knows. So I'd keep out of the Councillor's way, if I were you, Vane-from-the-Fens. And I'd take those gray gloves off before you go upstairs again."
He stood up. He brushed the crumbs off his plum coat. He looked down at Corin, finally, with his quick brown eyes, and there was something in them that Corin could not read at all. Not friendly. Not unfriendly. Just very tired, and very young, and very careful.
"My mother had a pair like that," said Tam Hollin. "Gray gloves. Ministry gloves. She cleaned the Third Basement. She wore them for nine years so the glass wouldn't take her hands." He pulled his own white gloves back on, one finger at a time. "She wore them right up until the day the Bank came for her. And then she didn't need them anymore."
He turned and walked away down the long table, toward the stairs, to wait at the bottom for the bell.
Corin sat and looked at his tea.
Then, under the table, where nobody could see, he slid off the white cotton gloves, and then the gray ones under them, and folded the gray ones small, very small, and pushed them deep into the outside pocket of Abel's green coat. He put the white ones back on over his bare hands. They felt thin and strange.
At the end of the table, by the fire, Gorse was eating pickled onions one after another out of the heap on his plate, and drinking his ale, and staring into the flames.
He was humming.
It took Corin a moment to hear it, under the crackle of the fire and the noise of the room. Low. Tuneless. A big man's careless hum, the kind men make without noticing, while they eat. It went up. And up. And up.
And stopped.
Three notes.
Corin's hand went still on the table.
He heard it, said Malakor, very soft and very cold. In the lane. In the Ink Quarter. The last thing Pell Dorran did with his mouth was hum. And that brute stood over him and listened. A pause. And now it is in his head too. And he does not even know where he got it from.
Gorse hummed the three notes again. Up, up, up. Then he took another onion, and stopped humming, and ate it.
Above them, through the ceiling, through the floor of the long white and gold room, a small silver bell rang, very faint and very clear.
The servants stood up.
