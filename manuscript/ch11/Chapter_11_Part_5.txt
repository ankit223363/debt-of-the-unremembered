Chapter 11: Aunt Cressida, Part 5 of 5: The Glass Cabinet
The Glass Cabinet
Corin had the cape. He had held it over his arm all evening, folded, the dark gray wool and the little silver clasps. He shook it out now, softly, and held it open, the way Abel must have held a thousand capes in a thousand doorways, and Lyra came to him across the white carpet and turned her back and let him lay it over her shoulders.
Her shoulders were shaking. Very slightly. So slightly that nobody but the person standing right behind her could have felt it through the wool.
He fastened the clasp at her throat. His bare fingers, under the thin white cotton, touched the side of her neck for a moment, by accident, and she was cold. Cold as the stone girl in the pond.
"Thank you, Vane," said Lyra. In her lady's voice. Perfectly steady.
"My lady."
"Oh—wait." Cressida had been standing by the desk, watching. Now she lifted one gloved hand. "Wait, darling. Before you go. I nearly forgot. I have something for you." She turned her head, and her gray eyes found Corin. "Vane. The cabinet. Behind you, by the wall. The second shelf from the top, on the left. There's a little box. Blue leather, with a silver catch. Bring it here, would you?"
Corin turned.
He had not looked at the cabinet. All evening, in this room, he had not let himself look at it. He had felt it there, at the edge of his eyes, tall and glittering against the white quilted silk of the far wall, and he had kept his eyes on the little painting of the boat on the water and let the cabinet stay where it was. Now he was being sent to it. By name.
She wants you to look, said Malakor. Be careful, boy. She does not do anything by accident. Remember what she said about Lyra's mother. She never did anything without a reason. They are sisters, those two, in that way, if in no other.
Corin crossed the room. Not fast. Not slow.
The cabinet was taller than he was. It was made of pale wood, painted white and gilded at the edges, with two tall doors of glass, and behind the glass there were shelves. Seven of them. Lined with blue velvet, the deep dark blue of the sky just after the sun has gone. And on the velvet, in rows, with space around each one, the way jewelers lay out their best rings, there were things.
He saw them all at once, and then one at a time.
A fan of carved ivory, open, so fine that the light came through it in a lacework of little gold holes. A small knife with a handle of green stone. A child's rattle of silver, shaped like a fish. A bracelet of black pearls. A tiny silver bird in a tiny silver cage, so small it would have fitted in the palm of his hand. A lock of hair, pale gold, tied with white ribbon, under a little glass dome. Three gemstones in a row, each one as big as a hen's egg, glowing faintly, one gold, one green, one a deep and angry red. A pair of child's shoes, white kid, very small, with silver buckles.
And on the second shelf from the top, on the right, alone on its own square of blue velvet, a mask.
Not a little one. Not a brooch. A real mask. The kind you wear on your face. A half-mask, covering the forehead and the eyes and the nose, and leaving the mouth bare. It was made of shell. Thin curved white shell, polished until it shone like the inside of a cup, with a faint pink glow deep in it where the light came through. It had two long slanting holes for the eyes. And around the edges of the eyeholes, and along the top, and down over the cheekbones, there were tiny white pearls sewn in a pattern like the curl of a wave.
A white shell half-mask.
Corin stopped in front of the cabinet with his white-gloved hand lifted toward the little catch on the glass door, and could not move.
The woman. Tall. White shell half-mask. Red mouth.
It was lying there on blue velvet behind a glass door in a white room on Crown Walk, as calm and pretty as a seashell on a beach. As if it had never been anywhere else. As if it had never stood in a dark lane in the Ink Quarter, over a young man running four steps.
And beside it, on the same shelf, on its own square of velvet, there was a cup.
Not a Marrowmere cup. Not like the little glowing eggshells on the tea tray. This one was silver. Plain silver, heavy and dull, about the size of a big egg cup, with a short thick stem and a round foot. Its bowl was wide and shallow. And the inside of the bowl was not smooth. It was carved. Lined, all the way round and down into the bottom, with fine grooves. A spiral. Winding round and round toward the center, like the inside of a seashell, or the thread of a screw, or the tip of the rod in the long pocket at his ribs.
A silver siphon cup with a spiral inside.
Corin made himself look at it. Properly. The way he had made himself look at the spiral under the jar on Elara's glass desk. He made himself follow the groove with his eyes, from the rim of the cup round and down toward the middle. Round. And round. And round.
Which way did it turn?
Left.
Against the sun. Against the clock. Like water going down a drain on the other side of the world.
In the long pocket at his ribs, Malakor made a sound. Corin had never heard the rod make a sound like it before. It was not a word. It was the sound a man makes when he puts his hand on something in the dark that he thought was a rope and it moves.
Malakor.
No answer.
Malakor. You said you only ever knew one other harvester who cut his spiral to the left.
The rod was silent. So silent that Corin, for a moment, was afraid it had gone. That whatever lived in it had simply gone out, like a candle, the way the vial at Cressida's wrist went dim.
Then it spoke. Very low. So low that it was hardly there at all.
Not now.
Who was it?
Not now, boy. Not here. Not in this room, with that woman watching your back. A pause. And the old dry voice shook. Corin had never heard it shake. Get the box. Get her out. Get out of this house. And then ask me again. When we are somewhere with no glass.
"Vane?" said Cressida behind him. Light. Pleasant. "The left, darling. The second shelf, on the left. You're looking at the right."
Corin opened the glass door.
He did not touch the mask. He did not touch the silver cup. He put his hand past them, to the left side of the second shelf, where a small box sat on the blue velvet. It was the size of a man's palm. Covered in blue leather, a little faded, with a silver catch shaped like a wave. He took it out. He closed the glass door. It clicked.
He turned round.
Cressida was watching him. She stood by the white desk in her white gown with her hands folded in front of her, and her red mouth was smiling, and her gray eyes were on his face. Not on the box. On his face. As if she had been reading it, from behind, through the back of his head, the whole time he stood at the cabinet. As if she had known exactly what he would see, and had wanted to watch him see it.
She knows, Corin thought. She knows who I am.
And then, more slowly, colder: Or she knows what I am. And she wanted to find out if I would know the mask.
He did not let anything show. He did not let his hands shake. He walked across the white carpet with the little blue box held flat on his white-gloved palm, the way a footman carries a thing that belongs to his betters, and he held it out to Cressida Thorne.
She did not take it.
"Give it to your mistress," she said.
He gave it to Lyra.
Lyra held it in both hands. She looked at her aunt.
"Open it, darling."
Lyra opened the silver catch.
Inside, on a bed of faded blue velvet, there was a cup. One of the little Marrowmere cups. White and glowing, thin as an eggshell, with its silver rim and its three little silver feet like curling waves. And in the bottom of it, faint under the white, the shape of a little silver mask, with its two empty eyes.
"One of the twelve," said Cressida softly. "There were twelve, and I have eleven on the tray downstairs and in the cupboard. And this one I've kept in that box, by itself, since the day I took them from the long gallery." She smiled. "It seemed wrong, somehow, for me to have them all. You should have one of your mother's cups, darling. Something to drink your tea from, over your stable. Something to remember her by, besides a handkerchief."
Lyra looked down at the little glowing cup in its box.
"Thank you," she said.
"Turn it over," said Cressida. "When you get home. Look underneath." Her voice was very gentle. "There's something scratched there. I could never make it out. I think it must be Marrowmere writing. Perhaps it's a name." She smiled. "Perhaps it's yours."
Lyra closed the box. The silver wave of the catch clicked shut.
Corin understood then, standing by the door with his heels together and his hands behind his back.
It was not a gift. It was a hook. A little silver hook, with a little bright bait on it. Perhaps it's a name. Perhaps it's yours. Cressida had looked at the marks under that cup a hundred times, in two years, and not been able to read them. And now she was giving it to the only person in the city who might be able to. Or who might find somebody who could. And then she would only have to wait, and watch, and see where the girl went with it, and who she asked.
She is fishing, said Malakor, very quietly. His voice was steadier now. Not much. A little. And she has just put her line in the water. Do not let your lady take that cup to the tall woman on Market Row, boy. Not tomorrow. Not for a long time. That is exactly where she wants it to go.
"Goodnight, darling," said Cressida Thorne.
She came forward and kissed Lyra on the cheek. Once on each side. Lightly. The way ladies kiss, without their lips quite touching. Her pale gold head bent, and the white stones in her comb flashed blue, and the thick sweet wave of lilies came off her skin and her hair and her white silk and wrapped round Lyra like a second cape.
Lyra did not move. She stood and let herself be kissed. She held the little blue box against her chest.
Then Cressida stepped back, and looked past her, at Corin, one last time.
"Goodnight, Vane," she said.
And this time Corin bowed, and said, in the light quick empty voice the tall woman had taught him, the voice like a door closing quietly:
"Goodnight, my lady."
Cressida's red mouth curved, very slightly, at one corner.
"Oh, very good," she said softly. "Very good indeed. Hesper always did know how to dress a man."

Bramwell was waiting for them in the passage outside, as if he had been standing there the whole time with his long horse face turned to the wall. Perhaps he had. He bowed to Lyra, the small exact bow, and led them back along the quiet gray-carpeted passage and down the narrow stair and down the great curving white one, between the ropes of lilies, into the hall.
The hall was emptier now. Most of the guests had gone in to supper. Through a pair of tall doors on the right Corin could hear the clink of silver on china and the steady rising and falling murmur of talk, and once, high and shrill, Harriet Langley's laugh, like a gull. In the hall itself there were only servants. A few footmen by the front door, holding capes and hats. The two white footmen at the foot of the stairs, still and blank as statues. And by the wall near the door, alone, with a black velvet cloak folded over his arm, Tam Hollin.
Tam did not look at them. He was looking at the spot above the front door, where the great fan-shaped window showed the night outside, black, with the white glow of the gemstone lamps in it. His face was empty.
But as Corin passed him, two steps behind Lyra, a little to the left, Tam's lips moved. Very slightly. Without a sound.
Outside pocket.
Corin did not change his face. He walked on.
At the door, Bramwell bowed one last time.
"Goodnight, Miss Thorne," he said, in his grave dry voice. And then, lower, as Lyra stepped past him onto the top step, so low that it was hardly a voice at all, so low that only she and the man two steps behind her could possibly have heard it: "Your mother took her tea without sugar, miss. Always. She used to say it was a waste to sweeten a thing that was already good."
Lyra stopped. She half turned.
But Bramwell had already stepped back into the hall, and the white footman had already begun to close the great white door, and the light and the heat and the lilies were already shrinking behind it to a thin gold line. And then the line was gone, and the door was shut, and they were standing on the top step of Number 11 Crown Walk in the cold, and it was night.
The air hit Corin like a bucket of water.
It was cold and clean and smelled of rain coming, and wet stone, and horses, and coal smoke drifting up from the city far below. Not one breath of lilies. He stood on the white step with the tall black hat in his hand and breathed it in, and in, and in, as if he had been under the river for an hour and had only just come up.
Put your hat on, said Malakor. You are outside. A footman with a bare head on the Hill at night looks like a man who has been thrown out.
He put his hat on.
Lyra went down the white steps. Not fast. Not slow. She crossed the cobbles of Crown Walk in the cold white light of the gemstone lamps, past the waiting carriages with their patient horses and their coachmen stamping their feet, toward the dark garden in the middle of the crescent. She did not go to the gate. She went to the railings, to the place under the old lime tree where its branches came out over the black iron spears, and she stopped there, in the shadow, with her back to the lit houses.
Corin stopped two steps behind her.
She stood for a long time without moving. The little blue box was in her hands. She was holding it against her chest.
Then she put out one hand and took hold of one of the iron railings, hard, and leaned her forehead against the cold black bar beside it, and her shoulders began to shake.
She did not make a sound. Not one. That was the worst of it. She stood in the dark under the tree that her brother had fallen out of when he was six, and held on to the railing, and cried without any sound at all, the way you cry when you have learned that nobody is coming if you make a noise.
Corin did not touch her. A footman did not touch his lady. And he did not know if Corin was allowed to either. He stood two steps behind her with his hands at his sides and his eyes on the dark branches of the lime tree, and he waited. He thought that was what he could do. Wait. And be there when she turned round.
It was a long time before she turned round.
When she did, her face was wet and her eyes were red, but her chin was up.
"There was a quarrel," she said.
Corin waited.
"The night before." Her voice was low and rough. "Rowan's right. He heard us. In the study. Father had been sitting at his desk all evening with the door locked, and I knocked and knocked until he let me in, and there were papers everywhere, Corin, all over the floor, and he was burning them in the grate. One at a time. And I asked him what they were and he wouldn't say." She swallowed. "And I said, the house is going, isn't it? And he said yes. And I said, then let me help. Let me burn something. I'm good at it. I'm better than you. I'll burn anything you want, the piano, the dancing, my letters, anything, just let me fight for it. And he stood up, and he took me by the shoulders, and he shouted at me." She shut her eyes. "He never shouted. Never. In my whole life. And he shouted, You will never burn anything for me. Never. Not one thing. Do you hear me? Not for me, not for this house, not for this name. You will keep every single thing you are."
She opened her eyes.
"And then he sent me to bed," she said. "And in the morning he was at the window, smiling at nothing. And there was a carriage in the yard. And Bertram had sent Oren away." She looked at Corin. "So Rowan's right. Isn't he. I did make him do it. If I hadn't gone in. If I hadn't said it. If I hadn't offered—"
"No," said Corin.
He said it before he knew he was going to. Lyra looked at him.
"He was already burning the papers," said Corin slowly. "Before you went in. You said so. He'd locked the door. He was already doing something he didn't want anyone to see. And there was already a carriage coming in the morning, with a Harvester in it, and a guest. Bertram had already been told to send Oren away. Doctor Aske had already been found under the bridge nine days before." He shook his head. "You didn't make anything happen, Lyra. You walked into the middle of it. That's all."
She looked at him for a long time.
"How do you know?" she said.
"I don't," said Corin. "I can't remember anything past this morning." He touched the left side of Abel's coat, over his heart, where the Ledger was. "But I wrote it all down. Every bit. It's all in here, in the right order. And when I read it in the right order, that's what it says."
Lyra stood under the lime tree in her mother's gray gown and looked at the place on his coat where the book was.
Then she laughed. Wetly. Shakily. It was not much of a laugh.
"You're a very strange footman, Vane," she said.
"Yes, my lady."
She wiped her face with the back of her hand. She looked down at the little blue box.
"She wants me to take this to Hesper," she said. "Doesn't she. The cup. To have the marks read. And then she'll know Hesper has the books."
"Yes," said Corin. "Malakor thinks so."
"Then I won't." Lyra put the box inside her cape. "Not yet. I'll put it on the shelf over the stable next to Father's cup, and I'll drink my tea out of it every morning, and I'll let her wait." Her mouth set. "She's good at waiting. So am I."
"She'll come for Hesper anyway," said Corin. "She said so. I'd love to look through her pattern books."
"Then we'd better tell her," said Lyra. "Tonight."
They went down the Hill steps in the dark. All the way down. The gemstone lamps on their brass-topped posts gave way to plain iron lamps, and then to old oil lamps hanging from brackets, and then to no lamps at all, only the light from windows. The clean cold air of the Hill gave way to the smell of the city, coal and fish and wet streets. Corin had never been so glad to smell anything in his life. Or that he could remember.
Market Row was dark and shut. The stalls were packed away. A cat ran across the cobbles in front of them and was gone. They went round into the narrow alley behind it, past the baker's and the butcher's and the candle-maker's, and stopped in front of the plum-colored back door with the knocker in the shape of a hand holding a ball.
Lyra lifted the knocker. Twice. And then once.
As if they were sure of being let in, but too polite to say so.
The door opened at once. Madame Hesper stood there in her black dress, with a candle in one hand and her spectacles on, and pins in her mouth, as if it were the middle of the afternoon. She looked at Lyra's face. Then at Corin's. Then at the little blue box under Lyra's cape.
She took the pins out of her mouth.
"She gave you something," she said.
"One of Mother's cups," said Lyra. "Marrowmere. With marks underneath."
Madame Hesper's face did not change. But her hand, holding the candle, went very still.
"And she asked about you," said Corin. "About your shop. About your pattern books. She said she'd like to come and look through them. For old times' sake."
The candle flame bent in the draft from the alley, and straightened.
"Did she," said Madame Hesper.
"Yes."
Madame Hesper looked at them both for a long moment. Then she stood aside.
"Come in," she said. "Both of you. Quickly. The gray coat is upstairs in the trunk, Mr. Vane, and you will fetch it yourself, because my knees have had enough of those stairs for one day." She shut the door behind them and slid the bolt. "And while you are up there, you will help me carry down a box. A heavy one. From under the bed."
"What's in it?" said Lyra.
"Nine pattern books," said Madame Hesper. "Which are going on a cart to my sister's in the morning." She set the candle on the long scarred table. "I do have a sister, child. She lives in Saltmarsh and she keeps geese and she has not spoken to me for thirty years. She will be delighted to have something to complain about." She took off her spectacles and let them fall on their chain. "Let Miss Cressida come. Let her look through every book in my shop. She will find a great many measurements for a great many dresses, and not one single thing worth her trouble."
She looked at Corin.
"Keep sewing pockets, Mr. Vane," she said. "Somebody always needs one."
It was very late when Corin got home to Tallow Street.
He was wearing his gray coat again. Abel's green coat was folded over his arm, wrapped in brown paper, with the tall black hat on top. Madame Hesper had made him change in the back room. You will not walk through the Docks at midnight in Thorne green, she had said. You will be robbed for the buttons before you reach the river. He had moved everything back. The rod into the long right pocket. The Ledger over his heart. The black square with its three warm stones, cut free from the green coat and sewn back into the gray, in eleven small stitches, by Madame Hesper, without a word.
And the list. And the little brass bell. And the folded square of paper with the rubbing of a single letter on it. Out of the secret pocket in the green coat. Into the secret pocket in the gray, the one Madame Hesper had sewn there today, under the left arm, close to his heart, while the gray coat lay in her trunk upstairs waiting for him. He had found it tonight with his fingers. Nobody had told him it was there.
At the bottom of the narrow crooked stairs at Number 14, in the dark, he stopped. He remembered something. Not remembered. He had written it on the inside of his eyes, hours ago, and it was still there, faint, the way writing stays on a slate after it has been wiped.
Outside pocket.
He unwrapped the brown paper from the green coat, there in the dark hall, and put his hand into the outside pocket on the right side. The pocket where he had pushed his gray gloves, folded small, under the table in the servants' hall of Number 11.
The gloves were there.
And folded inside them, so that he would only have found it if he had taken them out, there was a slip of paper.
He carried it up the stairs. All the way to the top. He lit the little brass lamp on the crate, and turned it down to a small blue bead, and sat on the edge of the narrow iron bed, and unfolded the paper under the light.
It was a small square of plain cheap paper, torn from the corner of something larger. A menu, perhaps, or a list of wines. The writing on it was quick and small and very neat, leaning a little to the right.
The Langleys have no cousin. You stand like a Harvester, and so does she, and she knows it.
Thursday. The Bourse. Ask for Tam at the kitchen door, not the front.
Bring your gloves. You'll need them.
There was no name. Only, at the bottom, a small drawing. Very small. Very quick. A few lines, the way a man draws a thing he has drawn many times before, so often that his hand does it without him.
An open book. And above it, an eye.
Open.
Corin sat and looked at it for a long time, with the little blue lamp hissing beside him and the rain beginning, at last, to tick on the slates over his head.
The Ministry's sign was an open book and an eye. He knew that. It was carved in stone over the great black stairs at the front of the Ministry. It was stamped on every form he had ever signed. And on the Ministry's sign, the eye was shut. Closed. Asleep. The eye that forgets. Only the Inquisitors wore it open, the Ledger said, staring, stitched in silver thread on their white collars. But this was not an Inquisitor's eye. It was drawn small and quick, in pencil, by a hand that had drawn it many times. The way, a line in the Ledger said, somebody had once drawn it in chalk, very small, in the bottom corner of a stationer's board on Quill Row.
This eye was open.
He took out the Ledger. He opened it at the last page. And he wrote, small and careful, everything. The hall of lilies. Bramwell, who was the Bram of the good cups and not the Bram of the chalkboard. Cressida. Her red mouth. The little glowing vial at her wrist, and the lily that came back to life, and the name of a pony she could not remember. Never burn anything you'd miss. Harriet Langley. Isolde. Lord Pembrook's jet buttons. The Councillor with the gold chain who looked at the green coat and did not like it. Lady Vivienne, and her rings, and her Thursdays. Mr. Castellan, who counted people. Gorse. Huge hands. Beer and onions. Three notes, hummed into a fire. Tam Hollin, whose mother wore gray gloves for nine years. The cups. The masks that came up blinking through the tea. Every girl in Marrowmere is given a mask on the day she's named. Rowan. The Academy. Kross. Tell her I know what she did. The quarrel. The papers. WHEREAS. The brooch. The portrait. The Ministry has houses in the country. The first snow. The white shell mask on blue velvet.
And the silver cup beside it, with its spiral turning left.
When he had written that, he stopped. He sat with the pencil in his hand.
"Malakor," he said. Out loud this time. Quietly. To the dark room. "There's no glass here. You said to ask you again when there was no glass. Who was it? The other harvester who cut his spiral to the left?"
The rod was in the long pocket of the gray coat, hanging on its hook behind the door. For a long time it said nothing.
Then, very softly, from across the room, in the dark:
Ask me in the morning, boy.
"In the morning I won't know to ask."
No, said Malakor. You will not.
And after that, no matter how long Corin sat there with the pencil in his hand and the blue lamp hissing and the rain ticking on the roof, the rod would not say another word.
So he wrote it. At the bottom of the page. In letters bigger than the rest, so big that the pencil nearly went through the paper.
ASK MALAKOR: WHO ELSE CUT HIS SPIRAL TO THE LEFT? HE KNOWS. HE WON'T SAY. MAKE HIM.
And under it, smaller, because he was so tired now that his hand would hardly hold the pencil:
She wears lilies. She burns only little things. She has the mask. She has the cup.
She is not afraid of anything.
Find out what she's afraid of.
Then he lay down on the narrow bed in his clothes, with the book on his chest and his hand flat on its stiff new cover, and the little blue box he did not have, and the little silver cup he had not touched, and the white shell mask lying on its blue velvet in a white room on the Hill, all going round and round in his head like the grooves of a spiral, turning, turning, the wrong way.
And somewhere in the middle of it, he slept.
