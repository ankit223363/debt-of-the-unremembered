Chapter 11: Aunt Cressida, Part 4 of 5: Rowan
Rowan
Cressida put her cup down. She sat for a while without speaking, looking at the fire, and the light of it moved on her pale face and on the white stones in her hair. When she spoke again her voice had changed. It had gone lower, and slower, and very gentle. It was the voice you use to tell somebody that a person they love has died.
"Lyra," she said. "Darling. There's something I have to tell you. Something I ought to have told you a long time ago. And I've been putting it off all evening, chattering about cups and cakes and soap, because I'm a coward, and I didn't want to see your face when I said it." She folded her gloved hands together in her lap. "It's about Rowan."
Lyra had been sitting very straight in the white velvet chair, as straight as she had sat all evening. Now she sat straighter. Corin saw her shoulders go back. He saw her hands, in her lap, close on each other.
"What about Rowan?" she said.
"Do you know where he is?"
Lyra did not answer at once.
"No," she said at last. It came out very quietly. "He went away. A year ago. Last autumn. He said he was going to find work. He said he couldn't stand the smell of the horses anymore, and he couldn't stand Father smiling at nothing, and he couldn't stand—" She stopped.
"Couldn't stand what, darling?"
"Me," said Lyra.
The fire crackled.
"He took his good coat and his father's old penknife and three silver out of the tin on the shelf, and he went out of the door one morning before it was light, and he didn't come back." Lyra's voice was flat and careful. "I asked everywhere. Every shop. Every boarding house. The docks. I asked Captain Oren to ask the soldiers. I put a notice in the window of every chandler's on the river. Nobody had seen him." She looked at her aunt. "Do you know where he is?"
"Yes," said Cressida.
Lyra did not move.
"He came to me," said Cressida gently. "The same morning. Before it was light. He came round to the mews and knocked on the kitchen door, and Mags let him in, and gave him tea, and woke me. He was sitting at the servants' table in his good coat with his knees up to his chin, and he'd been crying all the way across the Hill, poor lamb, and he wouldn't say why. He just said, Aunt Cressida, you're a Councillor. Tell me how to be something. Tell me how to be anything that isn't this."
Lyra's mouth opened, very slightly. Nothing came out.
"So I did," said Cressida. "I told him. I gave him a bed, and a bath, and a good breakfast, and I wrote three letters, and called in three favors, and at the end of the week he went up the Hill in a hired carriage with a new trunk and a letter of introduction in my own hand." She smiled. It was a sad little smile, and a proud one. "To the Academy."
"What Academy?" said Lyra.
But she knew. Corin saw that she knew. He saw it in the way the color went out of her face all at once, as if a lamp had been turned down inside her.
"The Inquisitors' Academy," said Cressida. "At the top of the Hill. Behind the Archon's temple." She paused. "He's a cadet, darling. A second-year cadet now. And he's doing so well. You'd be so proud of him. They say he's the quickest in his year with a blade. Quicker than some of the masters. And Inquisitor Kross has taken a very special interest in him. Silas Kross. You've heard of Silas Kross?"
Corin had.
He did not remember hearing of him. But the name went through him like a cold wind through an open door. Silas Kross. It was in the Ledger. On page after page. Wren Halloway's master. The name that had made Toby go white to the lips in the Third Basement, and look over his shoulder at the stairs.
Kross, said Malakor, very low and very bitter. Of course. Of course it would be Kross. He collects them, boy. Clever angry children with nowhere to go. He takes them in and he feeds them and he teaches them to cut, and they love him for it. They would die for him. Some of them do.
Lyra had gone completely still.
"Kross," she said.
"He treats Rowan like a son," said Cressida warmly. "Truly. He has him to dine. He takes him riding. He lends him books. Rowan adores him. You should hear him talk about Silas. Silas says this. Silas says that." She laughed softly. "It's quite charming. He brings me barley sugar from Silas's own tin when he visits. Silas always has a pocket full of sweets, did you know? He's famous for it."
"When he visits," said Lyra.
"Oh, yes. Every month or so. On his free Sunday. He was here yesterday, as it happens." Cressida tilted her head. "In his grays. His cadet grays, with the silver collar. He looked so handsome. So tall. He's grown, Lyra, he's so much taller than you'd think. He sat in that chair," she nodded at the white velvet chair that Lyra was sitting in, "and ate four of Mags's lily cakes and told me all about his examinations."
Lyra looked down at the arm of the white chair she was sitting in. At the velvet, under her hand.
She took her hand away.
"Yesterday," she said.
"Yes, darling."
"He was here. Yesterday. In this house. On the same crescent as—" She stopped herself. "And nobody told me. For a whole year. You knew where he was for a whole year, and you never once—"
"I wanted to," said Cressida. Quickly. Softly. Leaning forward in her chair. "Oh, darling. Believe me. I wanted to, a hundred times. I'd sit down to write to you and I'd get as far as Dear Lyra and then I'd stop. Because he asked me not to." She reached into a little silk bag that hung from the arm of her chair, white silk, embroidered with white lilies, and drew out a folded paper. "He made me promise. He made me swear, on Father's grave. And I've kept my promise for a year, and I'm breaking it now, tonight, because I think you have a right to know." She held out the paper. "He left this with me. The first week. In case you ever came. He said if you ever came, I was to give it to you, and then you'd understand."
Lyra looked at the paper in her aunt's white-gloved hand.
She did not take it at once. Corin saw her not take it. He saw her look at it the way you look at a letter from a doctor, before you open it. Then she put out her hand, and took it, and unfolded it.
Corin could not see the words from the door. He could see the paper. Plain, cheap, ruled with faint blue lines, the kind of paper a schoolboy uses. He could see that the writing on it was large and quick and spiky, sloping hard to the left, and that it did not fill even half of the page. And he could see that Lyra read it once, fast, and then again, slowly. And then she did not read it any more. She just sat holding it, and looking at it, with no expression on her face at all.
After a long while, she read it out.
She read it in a flat voice, with no feeling in it at all, the way you read a list of things you have to buy.
"Aunt C. If Lyra ever comes looking, tell her not to. Tell her I don't want to see her. Tell her I know what she did. She knows what I mean. I'm going to be somebody now and I'm going to get our name back, and I'm going to do it without her. Tell her not to come up the Hill. Tell her not to write. R."
She stopped.
The fire crackled. In the window, the glasshouse in the white garden glowed like a lantern, misted with warmth.
"I know what she did," said Lyra. Very quietly. To the paper. "What does he think I did?"
Cressida's gray eyes were wet. She looked at her niece with an expression of great sorrow and great tenderness.
"He thinks you made Aurelian do it," she said gently. "The burning. He thinks there was a quarrel. The night before. You and your father, shouting, in the study. He heard you through the floor. And then in the morning, your father was—the way he is. And Rowan has always believed that whatever you said to him that night was the reason." She shook her head slowly. "I told him it wasn't. Of course I did. I told him his father was a sick man, and a sad man, and a man with many troubles, and that nothing a girl of eighteen could say would make a man burn his whole mind away. But he's sixteen, darling. He's sixteen and he's angry, and he needs somebody to be angry with. And you were there." She sighed. "And I wasn't. And Silas wasn't. So it's you."
Lyra folded the paper. Small. Very small. In half, and in half, and in half again, until it was a tight little square.
She did not say there was no quarrel. She did not say anything at all.
Corin, by the door, did not know whether there had been a quarrel. The Ledger did not say. Lyra had never told him. He looked at her face, white and still in the firelight, and he did not know if the thing he saw in it was that's a lie or how did she know.
Either way, said Malakor, very softly, she has just been cut. Look at her. That one went in deep.
"May I keep this?" said Lyra.
"Of course, darling. It's yours. It was always meant to be yours."
Lyra put the folded square inside her collar. With the iron key, and Bertram's note, and the handkerchief that said Fold it small.
"Does he know," she said, "that Father's still alive? That he sits at the window? That he says bright sometimes, when the swallows come home?"
"He knows," said Cressida.
"And he's never once come to see him."
"No." Cressida's voice was very gentle. "No, darling. He says it isn't his father anymore. He says his father died two years ago, in the study, and the thing at the window is only something that wears his face." She looked at Lyra. "Silas told him that, I think. Silas has very firm ideas about people who've been emptied. He says there's no use visiting a house when nobody lives in it anymore."
Lyra stood up.
She did it suddenly, and too fast, so that the little white table with the tray on it rocked, and one of the Marrowmere cups tipped on its three silver feet and rolled, and fell off the edge of the tray onto the thick white carpet. It did not break. The carpet was too thick. It lay on its side in the white wool, empty, with the little silver mask in the bottom of it looking sideways at the fire.
"Excuse me, Aunt," said Lyra. Her voice was perfectly polite and perfectly steady and perfectly, terribly calm. "I think I need some air."
She walked to the window. Not to the door. To the window. She stood in front of it with her back to the room, with her hands folded in front of her, looking out at the white garden and the glowing glasshouse and the dark curve of the backs of the houses of Crown Walk, going away along the crescent toward a house with one round window at the top like an eye.
She did not cry. Corin could see her face, faint, in the dark glass of the window, laid over the garden like a ghost. Her face was white and set and her eyes were open and dry.
Cressida did not get up. She sat in her white velvet chair and watched her niece's back, and her red mouth was very sad, and her gray eyes were very bright.
Then she looked at Corin.
She looked at him for a long time. Longer than before. And this time she did not look at the coat. She looked at his face.
And she smiled. Just a little. Just at one corner of the red mouth. The smile of a woman who has made a move on a board, and is waiting to see what the other player will do.
"Pick up the cup, would you, Vane?" she said softly. "There's a good man."
The Papers
Corin crossed the room.
It was only six steps from the door to the little white table, over the thick white carpet, but it seemed much further. He walked the way a footman walks, not fast, not slow, with his eyes down and his hands at his sides. He felt Cressida's eyes on him all the way. He felt them on his face, and his shoulders, and his hands in their thin white gloves with the bare skin underneath, and on the right side of the green coat, where the long pocket ran from his ribs to his hip.
He knelt and picked up the cup.
It weighed almost nothing. It was like holding an eggshell, or a dry leaf. It was still warm from the tea, and a last gold drop of tea ran round the inside of it as he lifted it, and over the little silver mask in the bottom, and the mask's two empty eyes seemed to blink at him.
He turned it over in his fingers, to set it back on its three silver feet.
And on the underside, in the white, between the feet, there were marks.
Tiny. Scratched. So fine you could hardly see them, unless the firelight caught them at exactly the right angle, the way it caught them now. Little lines and little curves. Not letters he knew. Not letters at all, perhaps. But he had seen them before.
He had traced them, two days ago, on thin paper, from a drawing in a pattern book in a shop on Market Row. From the back of a little silver mask. Marks on the back, Madame Hesper had written. Makers' marks? Ask. And under it: They are not makers' marks, Madame. They are my name. My real one.
They were not the same marks. He could see that, even without the tracing in front of him. The shapes were different. But they were the same kind of marks. The same hand, perhaps. The same way of cutting a line into something hard and white and small, so that it would last.
Someone's name, said Malakor. Very quiet. On every cup, I would wager. Twelve cups. Twelve names. A pause. For special guests.
Corin set the cup down on the tray on its three silver feet. Gently. Exactly. The way a footman sets things down. He stood up.
"Thank you, Vane," said Cressida.
He bowed and went back to the door. Six steps. He did not look at her. He did not look at Lyra's back at the window. He put his heels together and his hands behind him and found the little painting above the fire again. The boat with the lantern on the wide flat water. The tall thin houses on stilts in the distance.
Cressida got up.
She crossed the room to the white writing desk, her white gown whispering over the carpet, and sat down at it, and opened a drawer. Then another, underneath. She took out a folder. It was large and flat and bound in dark green leather, very old, rubbed pale at the corners, with brass clasps. On the front of it, in faded gold, there was the rose with the thorn wound round it.
She laid it on the white leather of the desk and undid the clasps.
"Lyra," she said. "Come here, darling. Come and look at this."
Lyra turned from the window.
She came slowly. Her face, when it came into the lamplight again, was very pale, but it was calm. Whatever had happened to her at the window, she had folded it small and put it away somewhere. She stood at her aunt's shoulder and looked down at the green folder.
Cressida opened it.
Inside there were papers. A thick sheaf of them. The top one was large and stiff and cream-colored, with a border of tiny painted roses and thorns all the way round it, and at the bottom a great round seal of red wax with a ribbon through it. The writing on it was large and black and old-fashioned, with long tails on the letters, and it began with a word in red, much larger than the rest, so large that Corin could read it even from the door.
WHEREAS.
"The letters patent of the house of Thorne," said Cressida softly. "The original. Signed by the first Archon, three hundred years ago, when the Hill was a hill and the city was a village on a river." She touched the seal with one gloved fingertip. "It's what makes us Thornes, darling. Not our name. Not our blood. This. This piece of paper says that the Thornes are a house of Veridia, and may hold land, and sit on the Council, and bear arms." She lifted the top sheet. Under it were others. Smaller. Newer. With Ministry seals on them, the open book and the eye. "And these are what took it all away again."
She spread them out, one beside the other, on the white leather, like a hand of cards.
"The Bank's notice of seizure. For your father's debts. The Council's order removing the Thorne seat. The Ministry's ruling on your father's condition. Emptied. Not fit to hold property or office. Heirs not of age." She tapped each one. "The house. The seat. The name. All gone. All signed and sealed and stamped in triplicate. All perfectly legal." She looked up at Lyra. "And every one of them can be undone."
Lyra did not move.
"How?" she said.
"By the Archon." Cressida said it very simply. "A ruling of the Ministry can only be undone by the Ministry. And the Ministry is the Archon. One line in his hand. One seal. And the seizure is lifted, and the seat is returned, and the ruling is set aside, and the house of Thorne is a house again." She smiled. "With a Thorne at its head. A young one. Of age, or nearly. In her mother's gown."
"And the Archon would do that," said Lyra slowly. "For us."
"Not for us, darling. For me." Cressida closed her gloved hand gently over the edge of the papers. "I've been on the Council for two years. I've sat at his table. I've done him favors, small ones and large ones, and he has not forgotten any of them. The Archon never forgets a debt." She laughed softly. "It's his one great virtue. He owes me. And I could ask him. And he would say yes."
She took the Thorne seat, Corin thought. Two years ago. When her brother burned. She took his seat on the Council, and now she offers to give it back.
Not give, said Malakor. Sell. Listen.
"Why would you?" said Lyra.
Cressida looked at her for a long moment.
"Because I'm a Thorne too," she said. "Whatever you think of me. Whatever Aurelian told you. Whatever old Hesper has been saying behind her pins." Her gray eyes held Lyra's. "Because it is a terrible thing to sit at a table on the Council in a chair that used to be your brother's, and hear the other Councillors call you Councillor Thorne, and know that there is no house of Thorne behind the name. Only a woman with nobody. A brother at a window. A nephew who won't speak to his sister. And a niece over a stable who sews handkerchiefs to pay the rent." Her voice was very soft. "I want it back, Lyra. I want it all back. Not for me. For us. For the name."
The fire crackled.
"And what do you want from me?" said Lyra.
Cressida smiled.
"So like her," she said. "Straight to the point. Always." She let go of the papers. "Not very much, darling. Hardly anything. The Archon is a collector, you know. He likes old things. Rare things. Things from far away. And there are certain things that belonged to your mother that he would like very much to have." She began to gather the papers together again, slowly, one by one, and slide them back into the green folder. "Her brooch, for one. The little silver mask. Marrowmere work. Very rare. There aren't more than a handful in the whole of Veridia, and none of them as fine as hers." The WHEREAS went back in, on top. "If it should ever turn up. If you should ever find it, among her things. Or remember who she might have given it to."
"I told you," said Lyra. "She didn't give me anything."
"I know you told me, darling. And I believe you. But people forget things." Cressida closed the folder. "It's a city of forgetting. You'd be amazed what turns up when you go looking. In old trunks. In old houses. In old seamstresses' cupboards." She fastened the first brass clasp. "And the portrait, of course. When you're ready to turn it round. The Archon has a great fondness for good portraits."
She fastened the second clasp.
"And that," she said, "is all."
Lyra looked down at the closed green folder under her aunt's white gloved hand. At the rose and the thorn in faded gold.
Corin, by the door, watched her look. He saw what she was seeing. He saw it as clearly as if it had been written in the Ledger. A house on Crown Walk with its shutters thrown open and its windows full of light. The rose garden cut back and the stone girl's jug pouring water. Rowan in the front hall in his good coat, coming home. Her father at the head of a long table. Her mother's portrait back in its place in the long gallery, over Lot 41, facing out.
And all she had to do was give away the one thing her mother had hidden from all of them.
"And if I can't find it?" said Lyra. "The brooch?"
Cressida put the green folder back in the drawer. She closed the drawer, and locked it with a little silver key from the chain at her wrist, the same thin gold chain that held the tiny glowing vial. The vial swung and flickered as the key turned.
"Then there's nothing to be done," she said. "And we go on as we are. You over your stable. Rowan at his Academy. And poor Aurelian at his window." She stood up. She smoothed the front of her white gown. "Though I do worry about him, darling. I worry about him terribly. A man like that, in a room like that, with only a nurse to look after him. It's not right. It's not safe." She sighed. "The Ministry has houses, you know. In the country. For the emptied. Lovely places, by all accounts, very quiet, with gardens, and nurses day and night. I've been thinking that he might be happier in one. I've been thinking I might put his name forward." She smiled at Lyra. "As his closest relation of age. Since you're not quite twenty-one."
Lyra went white. Not pale. White, all at once, as if every drop of blood in her body had run out through her feet.
A sister in the country, thought Corin. Bertram went to a sister in the country. And Doctor Aske went to a sister in the country, a week before he was found under the Lantern Bridge steps.
In the country, said Malakor softly, is where people go in this city when they are not coming back.
"He's happy where he is," said Lyra. Her voice was very low. "He has the swallows."
"Of course. Of course he does. And I wouldn't dream of moving him without your blessing, darling." Cressida came round the desk and took Lyra's cold hands in her own gloved ones again, the way she had in the hall, warm and soft and kind. "Not without talking to you first. Not unless there were no other way." She pressed the hands. "Think about it. That's all I ask. Think about the brooch. Think about the house. Think about Rowan coming home." Her red mouth smiled. "The first snow, shall we say? You can tell me your answer before the first snow. There's no hurry. Snow is weeks away."
She let go.
And she turned, still smiling, and looked at Corin by the door.
"Your mistress is tired, Vane," she said. "She's had a long day. She's been walking all over the Hill, and in and out of old houses, and up and down stairs." The smile did not change. "Fetch her cape. Bram will see you out. And Vane—"
He bowed.
"Mind the stairs," said Cressida Thorne softly. "Old houses have such steep ones. And they're very dark. Especially the back ones."
