Chapter 11: Aunt Cressida, Part 3 of 5: The Marrowmere Cups
The Marrowmere Cups
The bell was not for all of them. It was for him.
Corin understood this at the foot of the servants' stairs, when the others had gathered there in their line, and the door at the top had swung open, and Bramwell had come halfway down the stone steps and stopped. The old butler stood looking down at them with his long horse face in the yellow gaslight, and his pale winter eyes went along the line, and came to the green coat, and stayed.
"Vane," said Bramwell.
"Sir."
"Miss Thorne has asked for you. Her ladyship is taking tea with her in her own sitting room, and Miss Thorne wishes her man to attend." A pause, very slight. "Her ladyship has been good enough to allow it."
The line of servants did not move. But Corin felt them all, in the narrow stairwell, go very still. A footman was not asked for. A footman waited in the hall until he was sent for at the end of the evening, and then he fetched the cape and the umbrella and walked behind. A footman was not taken up to a lady's private rooms to stand at the door while she drank tea with a Councillor.
Unless the lady wanted him there. Unless the lady was afraid.
Or unless the Councillor wanted him there. For reasons of her own.
Corin went up the stairs. As he passed the young man in plum, Tam Hollin did not look at him. But Tam's eyes went, very quickly, to Corin's hands. To the white gloves, which were thin now, with bare skin under them.
Tam's mouth moved. Not a word. Just a small, tight, satisfied line.
Bramwell led him up through the hidden door in the paneling, and across the white marble of the hall, where the guests had thinned now, some gone into the dining room for supper, some leaving, some standing about the foot of the staircase in little bright knots, laughing. Nobody looked at Corin this time. A green coat behind a butler was only a servant going somewhere, after all. They went up the curving white staircase, between the twisted ropes of lilies, to the landing where its two arms met, and then on up a narrower stair that turned back on itself, carpeted in thick pale gray, so that their feet made no sound. And then along a passage, quiet and dim and warm, lined with doors.
At the end of it, there was a door that stood a little open, with lamplight coming through the gap.
Bramwell knocked once, softly, and pushed it wide.
"Miss Thorne's man, my lady."
"Bring him in, Bram. Bring him in."
It was not a large room. After the long white and gold drawing room downstairs, it seemed almost small. But it was the most beautiful room Corin had ever seen, or that he could remember seeing, and it frightened him more than the hall and the drawing room together.
It was white. Of course it was white. The walls were hung with white silk, quilted in little diamonds, so that they looked soft, like the inside of a jewel box. The carpet was white, and so thick that his boots sank into it. The ceiling was painted with a sky, pale blue with soft white clouds, and white birds flying across it, and in the middle, where the lamp hung on a silver chain, a pale gold sun. There was a fire in a small white fireplace, burning low and clear, with no smoke. There were two chairs by the fire, low and deep and covered in white velvet, and a little white sofa under the window, and a white writing desk with a white leather top and a silver inkstand on it. And against the far wall, tall, almost to the ceiling, there was a cabinet with glass doors, full of things that glittered.
Corin did not look at the cabinet. Not yet. He looked at a spot above the fireplace, where a small painting hung in a silver frame, and he let the rest of the room come to him.
The window behind the white sofa had its curtains open. Through it he could see the dark, and in the dark, below, the white garden of Number 11 lying pale under the gemstone lamps, with its white gravel paths and white urns, and at the end of it, against the back wall, the long glasshouse, glowing faintly from inside like a lantern, misted with warmth. Lilies, crowded behind the glass.
And beyond the glasshouse, beyond the back wall, beyond the mews, a little way along to the left, he could see the backs of the other houses of Crown Walk going away in a curve. Dark windows. Dark gardens.
And one dark house, at the end, with all its windows shuttered. Except one, at the very top, under the slates. A little round window, like an eye.
You could see Number 3 from Cressida Thorne's sitting room. You could watch it.
"Stand by the door, Vane, there's a good man," said Cressida. "You make the room look crowded."
Corin stood by the door. Bramwell bowed, and went out, and closed the door behind him with a click so soft it was hardly a sound.
Cressida sat in one of the white velvet chairs by the fire. Lyra sat in the other. Between them there was a low round table, white, with a white cloth on it edged in white lace, and on the cloth there was a tray, and on the tray there was a silver teapot, and a silver jug, and a silver bowl of sugar lumps, and a plate of small iced cakes, and two cups.
The cups.
Corin had read about them in the Ledger, in his own hand, an hour ago. Two lines, written in the dark of a back staircase. She said: tell Bram to put out the good cups. The Marrowmere ones. He had not known what they would look like. He had not thought about it.
They were not like any cups he had ever seen.
They were small. Smaller than a hen's egg, almost. Round and deep, like the bowl of a spoon, with no handles. They were made of something so thin and so white that the firelight came straight through them, and they glowed, warm and pale, the color of the inside of a shell. The rims were silver. A thin line of silver, like a thread, all the way round. And they stood on little silver feet, three each, shaped like tiny waves curling over.
Cressida lifted the silver teapot and poured. The tea was pale gold, and it smelled of flowers. Not lilies. Something else. Something light and green and bitter-sweet, like a field after rain.
And as the tea filled the first cup, Corin saw something come up out of the bottom of it.
A face.
Not a real face. A painted one. At the very bottom of the cup, inside, under the gold of the tea, somebody had painted a mask. A little half-mask, covering the eyes and the nose. Painted in silver, very fine, with two empty holes where the eyes would be. When the cup was empty you could not see it, because the white of the cup was too bright. But when the tea poured in, and the gold covered it, the mask came up through the gold like a face coming up through water. And the light of the fire, moving on the surface of the tea, made the two empty eyeholes seem to blink.
Corin's hand, behind his back, closed on his other wrist.
He knew that mask. He had traced it on thin paper in a shop on Market Row two days ago, from a dressmaker's pattern book. He had knelt in front of it this afternoon, on the dusty floor of a long gallery, where it had been cut into the wood of a little square door at the height of his knee, lined with silver.
Lyra had seen it too. Corin saw her see it. He saw her hand stop, just for a breath, on its way to take the cup her aunt was holding out to her.
Then she took the cup.
"How beautiful," said Lyra. Her voice was quiet and polite. "I've never seen anything like them."
"No?" Cressida filled the second cup, and the second mask came up blinking through the gold, and she set down the teapot. "How strange. They were your mother's."
The fire crackled.
"My mother's," said Lyra.
"Oh, yes. Part of her wedding portion." Cressida lifted her own cup in her white-gloved fingers and looked down into it, at the little silver mask looking up at her through the tea. "Twelve of them, there were, in a box lined with blue velvet. She brought them with her when she came to marry Aurelian. All the way from the water." She drank. A small neat sip. "She never used them. Not once. She kept them in the box at the back of a cupboard in the long gallery and never took them out. I asked her why, once. She said they were for special guests." Cressida smiled. "I took them, after the house went. I didn't think you'd mind, darling. They'd only have gone in the sale with everything else, and some dealer on Gilt Street would have bought them for a song and sold them to a merchant from Aurum as curiosities. I couldn't bear that. They're the last of her, really. The last thing of hers in this city." She tilted the cup, so that the mask in the bottom caught the light. "I use them for special guests."
Lyra held her own cup in both hands, the way she had held the thin white cup in Madame Hesper's shop. She looked down into it. At the mask.
"All the way from the water," she said. "You mean Marrowmere."
Cressida's cup stopped halfway to her red mouth.
Just for a moment. Just a pause, no longer than a breath. Then it went on, and she drank, and set the cup down in its little silver feet on the tray.
"So you do know," she said softly. "I wondered. Aurelian always said you didn't. He said she never told you, and he never told you, and you'd grown up thinking she came from somewhere in the south, from a family of sheep farmers with a great deal of money and no manners." She laughed. "Which was what we all told everyone, at the time. It seemed kinder."
"Kinder to who?"
"To her, darling. To all of us." Cressida settled back in her white chair and folded her hands in her white lap. "You don't know what it was like. A Thorne. The heir to the Thorne seat. Bringing home a girl from Marrowmere. From the canals. A girl with no family anybody had ever heard of, and no name that anybody could say properly, and a mask on her collar." She shook her head slowly. "Your grandmother took to her bed for a week. Old Lady Agatha. She said the Thornes had been on the Hill for three hundred years and never once married water."
"Married water," said Lyra.
"It's what they say. Up here. About Marrowmere people." Cressida waved one gloved hand. "Not kindly. They say Marrowmere people are like the water in their canals. Always moving. Never still. You can't hold them. You can't trust them. They slip through your fingers. And the masks—" She stopped.
"What about the masks?"
Cressida looked at her niece for a long moment over the rim of the little glowing cup.
"Every girl in Marrowmere," she said slowly, "is given a mask on the day she's named. A little silver one. Like hers. Like the ones in these cups. And on the back of the mask, they scratch a name. Not the name the girl is called by. Another name. A true name. A name that only the girl and the mask-maker know." She turned her cup slowly on the tray with one gloved fingertip, round and round, so that the little mask inside it turned too, round and round, like a face looking for something. "They say that if you have someone's mask, Lyra, you have their true name. And if you have their true name, you can open anything they have ever closed."
The fire crackled.
In the long right pocket of Abel's coat, against Corin's ribs, Malakor said nothing at all. But Corin felt the rod go cold. Colder than it had ever been. So cold that it burned.
Anything they have ever closed.
A little square door at knee height, in a long gallery, behind the place where a clock had stood. With a silver keyhole in the shape of a mask.
"That's a fairy story," said Lyra.
"Of course it is." Cressida smiled. "Of course. All the best things are." She picked up the silver plate of iced cakes and held it out. "Have a cake, darling. The pink ones are rose, and the white ones are lily. Mags makes them herself, downstairs. She's a treasure."
Lyra took a pink one.
Cressida took a white one, and bit it, and chewed, and looked at the fire.
"Your mother," she said, after a while, "was the cleverest woman I ever knew. Did you know that? The cleverest. Cleverer than Aurelian. Cleverer than me, and I'm very clever, darling, I promise you, or I wouldn't be sitting where I'm sitting." She licked a crumb of white icing from the tip of her glove. "And she never did anything without a reason. Not one thing. Not marrying Aurelian. Not keeping those cups at the back of a cupboard for twenty years. Not going to the sea." Her gray eyes came round from the fire and settled on Lyra's face, gently, the way a moth settles on a sleeve. "So when she went, I've always wondered. What did she leave behind? And who did she leave it with?"
Lyra drank her tea. She drank it slowly. All of it. Down to the bottom, until the little silver mask lay bare and white at the bottom of the empty cup, with its two empty eyes looking up at the ceiling, at the painted sky and the white birds and the pale gold sun.
Then she put the cup down on its three silver feet.
"I don't know, Aunt Cressida," she said. "I was twelve."
Cake and Questions
"Twelve," said Cressida softly. "Yes. Of course. Twelve."
She said it as if it were a sad thing, a pitiful thing, a thing to be sorry about. And perhaps it was. But Corin, standing by the door with his heels together and his eyes on the little painting in its silver frame above the fire, heard something else in it too. A note. Very small. The note you hear in a voice when a door it had hoped was open turns out to be shut, and the voice is already thinking about the window.
Cressida poured more tea. Into Lyra's cup first. The little silver mask disappeared under the gold and came up again, blinking. Then into her own.
"Twelve is a terrible age to lose a mother," she said. "Old enough to remember, and too young to understand. I was twenty-two when my father went. Edmund. Your grandfather. The same year you were born. Did you know that? He went down to his workshop one night after supper to finish a clock, and in the morning they found him at his bench with his head on his arms and a little brass wheel in his hand, and he'd been dead for hours." She stirred her tea with a tiny silver spoon, though she had put nothing in it. "For weeks afterward I used to go and stand outside his workshop door. A grown woman. Waiting for him to come out. I was sure he would. I was sure it was a mistake." She laid the spoon down on the tray. "So you see, darling, I do understand. More than you think."
Lyra held her cup and said nothing.
She is very good, said Malakor. Listen to her. Every word a little gift. Every gift a little hook.
"Did she write to you?" said Cressida. "From the coast?"
"No."
"Not once? Not one little letter? To her own daughter?"
"Father said she was too ill to write."
"Ah." Cressida nodded slowly. "Yes. Of course. Too ill." She looked into her cup. "And before she went? In the last days? Did she give you anything? A keepsake? Something to remember her by? Mothers do, you know, when they're going away. A locket. A ring. A little box of something." Her voice was gentle and wondering and very, very kind. "A brooch, perhaps."
The fire crackled.
Corin did not look at Lyra. He did not look at anything but the painting above the fire. It was a very small painting. A boat on a wide flat stretch of water, at evening, with a lantern hanging at its bow, and in the distance, very faint, the shapes of tall thin houses standing up out of the water on stilts. Marrowmere, he thought. Or somewhere like it. Hanging in the room of a woman who said the Thornes had never married water.
"She gave me a handkerchief," said Lyra.
It was such a small, quiet, ordinary answer that for a moment Cressida did not seem to know what to do with it.
"A handkerchief," she said.
"She embroidered it herself. The week before she went." Lyra reached into the collar of her gray gown, slowly, with two fingers, and drew out a small square of white linen, folded, worn very soft. Not Bertram's note. Not the iron key. The other thing she always carried there. She held it out across the little white table so her aunt could see, without letting it go. In one corner, in tiny stitches the color of roses, there were three words.
Fold it small.
Cressida looked at the handkerchief for a long time.
"How sweet," she said at last. "How very like her." Her red mouth smiled. "Fold it small. What does it mean, darling?"
"I don't know," said Lyra. "I think it was something she used to say." She folded the handkerchief again, along its old soft creases, and put it back inside her collar. "It's all I have of her. That and the portrait."
And she looked straight at her aunt, with her mother's gray eyes in her mother's face, and did not blink.
Corin felt something go through him, standing there by the door, that he did not have a word for. It was something like the feeling of watching Juno jump the gap over the Cut, in the Ledger. It was something like the feeling of watching a lily come back to life under a white glove, only the other way round.
The handkerchief was true. Lyra had made it. The Ledger said so, in his own hand, from the house over the stable. I made this. The last one I didn't sell. I don't remember making it. It was not her mother's at all. But it was true that it was all she had. And it was true that her mother had said the words. And so Lyra had told her aunt a lie made entirely out of true things, sewn together so close and so neat that you could not see the stitches.
In, through, pull, he thought.
Cressida set down her cup.
"Well," she said lightly. "It's a lovely thing to have. You must keep it safe." She brushed a crumb of white icing from her lap. "And the portrait. You have the portrait. The big one, from the long gallery, with the blue dress. I heard you bought it back from the auction men. That was very brave of you. And very expensive, I'm sure. On what you earn." She smiled. "Do you still have it?"
"Yes."
"Where is it?"
"In my room."
"Over the stable."
"Yes."
"How sad. A Thorne portrait over a stable." Cressida shook her head. "It ought to be here. Somewhere safe. Somewhere it can be looked after properly. I have a man who cleans paintings, on Gilt Street, a very good man. He could take it. Have it cleaned. Have the frame mended. I'd pay, of course." She leaned forward a little in her white velvet chair, and the white stones in her comb flashed blue. "You could bring it to me. Or I could send Gorse round with the carriage. He's very strong. He could carry it down those terrible stairs of yours in no time at all."
No, thought Corin. No. Not Gorse. Not up those stairs. Not into that room with Lord Thorne at the window.
Lyra put down her own cup on its three silver feet.
"Thank you, Aunt," she said. "But it's facing the wall. It's been facing the wall for a week. I'm not ready to turn it round yet."
Cressida's gray eyes rested on her for a moment.
"Facing the wall," she said. "Well. Whenever you're ready. The offer is always open." She sat back. "There's no hurry, darling. Paintings don't go anywhere."
She wants the portrait, said Malakor. Why? It is only paint.
The brooch, Corin thought. The mask on the collar. In the painting. The marks on the back of the silver. Madame Hesper copied them from the brooch itself. But if you didn't have Madame Hesper's book—
Then you would want the next best thing, said the rod slowly. A good painting, by a careful painter. Who might have painted the back of the brooch, if the lady had asked him to. For reasons of her own.
Corin did not know if the painter had. He had not seen the portrait's face, in the Ledger or out of it. Only the back of the canvas, gray and dusty, with Lot 41 chalked on the frame.
He wrote it on the inside of his eyes. She wants the portrait. Ask Lyra: is there anything painted on the back of the brooch in the picture?
Cressida had turned her attention to the iced cakes again. She chose a pink one this time. She held it in her gloved fingertips and looked at it.
"And how is my poor brother?" she said.
"The same," said Lyra.
"Still at the window?"
"Yes."
"Still tapping?"
The word dropped into the warm white room like a stone into a well.
Lyra's hands, in her lap, went still. Corin, by the door, did not let his face change. But he heard, in the long pocket at his ribs, Malakor draw in his breath, though the rod did not breathe.
"Sometimes," said Lyra.
"Mrs. Hobb tells me he taps," said Cressida. She bit the pink cake. Chewed. Swallowed. "Four, and then two, and then nine, and then three. Over and over. On the wall by the window. She says it's the only thing he does all day, apart from smile at the birds. She finds it very trying. She says she hears it in her sleep." She smiled at Lyra. "I pay Mrs. Hobb, you know. Out of my own purse. Every month. Since the Fund stopped. I couldn't bear to think of him with nobody to look after him, after the Council cut him off. So unfair. So cruel. But that's the Council for you." She brushed pink crumbs from her gloves. "I send her a little something extra too. Soap. Good soap, with lilies in it, from my own glasshouse. That house of yours must smell dreadful, with the horses underneath."
The fire crackled.
Lyra did not move at all.
Corin, by the door, felt the moment go tight again, the way it had gone tight in the mews. Tighter. Tight as a bowstring. He could see Lyra's hands, in her lap. The knuckles had gone white. He could see her mouth, closed hard. He could see, in her gray eyes, something that was going to come out.
Since the Fund stopped. With Lord Thorne's money in a little brown packet with green string, poured out into her gloved hand in the dark of the long gallery an hour ago, clinking. He won't miss it. He doesn't know what money is anymore.
I pay Mrs. Hobb. Out of my own purse.
With a little of his own money. And a bar of soap.
Lyra opened her mouth.
Corin moved.
He did not decide to. His body did it, the way his body had done so many things that day. He shifted the folded gray cape from his left arm to his right. That was all. A small movement. A footman's movement. The kind a footman makes a hundred times an evening without anybody noticing. The heavy wool rustled, softly, and one of the small silver clasps at its neck clinked against a button on his coat, very lightly, like a spoon against a cup.
It was the only sound in the room.
Lyra's eyes came to him. Just for a moment. To the door. To the green coat. To the cape on his arm.
And then back to her aunt.
Whatever had been going to come out of her mouth went back down again. He watched it go. He watched her swallow it, the way you swallow a mouthful of something too hot. Her hands loosened in her lap. Her chin came up.
"That's very generous of you, Aunt Cressida," said Lyra. Quietly. Steadily. "Mrs. Hobb is very fond of the soap."
Cressida looked at her. Then, for the first time since Corin had come into the room, she looked past Lyra. At the door. At him.
It was only a glance. Light and quick. Like a bird's.
But it stayed on him a moment longer than it needed to. On the cape over his arm. On his white-gloved hands. On his face.
Then she smiled, and looked back at Lyra, and reached for the teapot again.
"More tea, darling? It's gone a little cold. I'll ring for Bram." She did not ring. She poured anyway. "Oh, and before I forget. Somebody told me you were on Market Row on Saturday. In the rain. Going into that funny old shop at the end, the one with the plum door. The Needle and Thimble." The gold tea rose in Lyra's cup. The little silver mask came up through it, blinking. "Is old Hesper still alive? Goodness. She must be a hundred. She used to make all my dresses when I was a girl, you know. And all your mother's. She was very fond of your mother. Very fond." Cressida set down the pot. "What were you doing there, darling?"
Lyra did not look at Corin this time. She did not look at anything but her aunt.
"I had a cuff that needed mending," she said. "On my brown. I'd done it myself, with the wrong color thread."
"How sensible," said Cressida. "I always say a girl in reduced circumstances should keep a good seamstress. It's the one thing that makes a gown look new." She drank. "I must go and see the old thing myself one of these days. For old times' sake. I'd love to look through her pattern books. She kept such lovely pattern books."
She smiled over the rim of her cup.
And Corin, standing by the door in the coat that Madame Hesper had sat up all night to finish, felt the three stones at his hip grow so cold, so suddenly, that it was like a hand of ice laid flat against his side.
She knows about the books, said Malakor. Or she guesses. Either way, boy, that old woman is in danger. And she does not know it.
