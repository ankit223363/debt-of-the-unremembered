Chapter 11: Aunt Cressida, Part 1 of 5: Number 11
Debt of the Unremembered · Arc 1
Number 11
The light hit him first, and then the heat, and then the lilies.
They came at him all together through the open white door, like a wave breaking over a sea wall, and for a moment Corin could not see anything at all. He could only stand on the top step with his eyes stinging and his throat closing, two steps behind Lyra and a little to the left, and wait for the world to come back.
It came back in pieces.
A hall. A great tall hall, so tall that the ceiling was lost in a haze of golden light, like the inside of a church at Midwinter. A floor of white marble, polished so bright that he could see the lamps upside down in it, and his own boots, and the green hem of Abel's coat. Walls of white plaster, carved all over with leaves and garlands and ribbons, every leaf and every ribbon picked out in thin lines of gold. A staircase that went up from the far end of the hall in two white curving arms, like a pair of open hands, and met at a landing high above, and went up again.
And lilies. Everywhere, lilies.
They stood in great white urns on either side of the door, taller than a man, so many in each urn that they leaned out in every direction like the spray of a fountain. They stood on little white tables against the walls, in tall glass vases, five and six and ten to a vase. They wound up the white banisters of the staircase in long twisted ropes, with ribbon tying them, so that the whole staircase seemed to be made of flowers. They hung from the gold lamp in the middle of the ceiling in a great round ball of white and green. Every one of them was open. Every one of them had its six long curling petals spread wide and its six long yellow tongues stuck out, dusting the air with orange powder, so that the gold light in the hall was full of tiny floating specks, like the dust in a sunbeam.
And the smell. Corin had smelled it on the street, and in the garden of Number 3, and through the crack under a little door in the dark. But that had been a breath. This was the whole sky. It was so thick and so sweet that it seemed to have a weight, a warm wet weight like a blanket soaked in honey, and it lay on his face and his tongue and the inside of his nose, and it would not let him breathe anything else.
Steady, said Malakor, from the long pocket at his ribs. His voice was very small and very far away, as if it were coming through a wall. Breathe through your mouth, boy. Slowly. It is only a flower. It cannot hurt you.
It feels like it's trying.
Yes, said the rod. I expect it is meant to.
The footman in white had stepped back from the door to let them in. Another one was waiting in the hall. Two more stood at the foot of the double staircase, one on each side, like statues on plinths. All of them were in white, from their powdered hair to their stockings. All of them had the same flat, empty, polite faces, with eyes fixed on a spot just above the heads of the guests. Corin looked at them from the edges of his own eyes and thought: That is what I look like. That is what the tall woman taught me to look like.
It was not a comfortable thought.
The hall was full of people. Ladies in silk gowns of every color, rose and amber and plum and sea-green, with bare shoulders and long gloves and jewels that flashed like water in the lamplight. Gentlemen in black, very tall and very straight, with white gloves and white shirt-fronts and little glittering pins in their neckcloths. They stood in small groups on the white marble, talking. Not loudly. Never loudly. The sound of their talking was a soft, steady, rising and falling murmur, like the sound of the river under the Lantern Bridge, and through it, now and then, like a fish jumping, came a laugh.
And behind them, along the walls, in a long patient line, stood their servants.
Corin saw them all at once, with a strange jolt, as if somebody had turned a lamp up in a dark corner. Footmen. Maids. A coachman or two, still in their heavy caped coats, waiting to be sent back to the horses. They stood with their heels together and their hands behind their backs and their faces empty, in liveries of every color he had seen on Crown Walk outside: yellow with silver buttons, sky blue, deep plum, bottle-green, a gray so pale it was nearly white. They did not talk. They did not move. They watched the room, and the room did not watch them.
The guests were the river. The servants were the banks.
And as Lyra stepped onto the white marble in her mother's dove-gray gown, with her short black hair brushed smooth under its little lace cap and her chin up, the river stopped.
Not all at once. It went from the door outward, the way silence goes through a room when somebody has dropped a glass. The group nearest the door stopped talking first. A lady in rose silk turned her head, and her mouth stayed a little open, as if she had forgotten what she had been going to say. A gentleman beside her lowered his glass of something pale and sparkling. Then the next group turned. And the next. A whisper went across the hall, quick and soft, like wind through dry grass.
Thorne.
Then, under it, a second whisper, quicker:
Green. Look. Thorne green.
Corin did not look at anybody. He looked at a spot just above Lyra's head, where one dark lock of hair curled against the lace of her cap. He stood two steps behind her and a little to the left, with the tall black hat now held under his arm, the way a footman must hold it indoors, and the white cotton gloves on his hands over the gray ones, stiff and hot. He did not move.
But he saw.
He saw a lady in plum silk lift her fan, very slowly, and open it in front of her face, and look at him over the top of it. He saw a gentleman with a gray beard frown, and say something short and sharp to the man beside him. He saw, along the walls, the line of servants. Not their faces. Their faces did not change. But their eyes. Every pair of servant eyes in that hall, for one moment, came off the spot above the guests' heads and went to the green coat.
And went away again.
They knew it. They had all heard of it. Thorne green. The old family. The house that had fallen. And here it was, walking into Number 11 Crown Walk two steps behind a girl who lived over a stable.
She wanted them to see, said Malakor quietly. Well. They have seen.
A man came toward them across the white marble.
He was not a guest. He was dressed in black, not in white, but it was not the black of the gentlemen. It was a plainer black, a working black, a long black coat buttoned high to the throat over a black waistcoat and a white neckcloth tied very small and very tight. He was old. Seventy, perhaps. Tall and thin and very straight, like a walking stick, with a long narrow head as bald as an egg and a fringe of white hair around the back of it, and a long, thin, deeply lined face, like a horse that has worked hard all its life and expects to work hard for the rest of it. He walked very slowly, and his shoes made no sound at all on the marble.
A butler. Corin knew it the way he knew a gray coat. You did not have to be told.
The butler stopped in front of Lyra. He bowed. It was a small bow, very exact, as if he had measured it beforehand with a ruler.
"Miss Thorne," he said. His voice was low and dry and grave, the voice of a man who announces deaths and dinners in exactly the same tone. "Welcome. Her ladyship is expecting you. She asked to be told the moment you arrived."
"Thank you," said Lyra.
"May I take your cape, miss?"
Lyra unfastened the dark gray cape at her throat. She did it without hurry, as if she did it every evening of her life. Corin stepped forward, at exactly the right moment, the way the tall woman had taught him, and took it from her shoulders before the butler could reach for it, and folded it over his arm.
The butler looked at him.
It was the first time any servant in that house had looked straight at Corin's face. It was a long look, and a careful one. The old man's eyes were a very pale, watery blue, like the sky in winter, and they were not empty at all. They went over Corin slowly, from the top of his head down to his boots. They stopped at the dark band on the left sleeve of the green coat. They stayed there for a moment.
Then they came back up to Corin's face, and the long horse face did something very slight. A twitch. At one corner of the thin mouth. Corin could not tell if it was a smile or the opposite of one.
"You will be Miss Thorne's man," said the butler.
"Yes, sir."
"Name?"
"Vane, sir."
"Vane." The butler said it as if he were writing it down in a ledger of his own, somewhere behind his eyes. "I am Bramwell. I keep this house. When Miss Thorne goes in to her ladyship, you will wait in the hall, by the wall, with the others. When the tea is served, you will go below with the others, and you will be given something in the servants' hall, and you will come up again when you are rung for. You will not wander. You will not speak to the guests. You will not speak to her ladyship unless her ladyship speaks to you first, which she will not." A pause. "Is that clear, Vane?"
"Yes, sir."
"Good." Bramwell turned back to Lyra and bowed again, the same small exact bow. "If you will follow me, miss."
He turned. And as he turned, from somewhere up the curving white staircase, a woman's voice called out, light and high and pleased:
"Bram! Is that her? Bram, don't you dare keep her standing in the hall like a parcel!"
Bram, thought Corin.
He felt something let go in his chest, a knot he had not known was tied. Tell Bram to put out the good cups. Not the stationer on Quill Row. Not the big square man with the chalkboard and the strongbox under the floor, whom he had written down in the Ledger, in his own hand, as somebody to trust. Just an old butler with a horse's face, called Bramwell, whom his mistress called Bram.
It was such a small thing. But he found that his hands, under the white gloves, were not quite steady.
Do not relax, said Malakor. A name is a name. It does not tell you whose side a man is on. It only tells you what to shout when you want your tea.
Bramwell had stopped at the foot of the staircase, beside the two still white footmen, and turned his long face upward. He did not shout. He did not need to. His dry grave voice simply went up the stairs, the way smoke goes up a chimney.
"Miss Thorne has arrived, my lady."
And above them, on the landing where the two white arms of the staircase met, among the ropes of lilies and the gold light and the floating orange dust, somebody came to the banister and looked down.
Cressida
She came down the stairs the way water comes down a slope. Without hurry, and without stopping, and as if there had never been any question of where she was going.
She was tall. As tall as Madame Hesper, Corin thought, and nearly as slender, but there the likeness ended, because Madame Hesper was a heron standing in a reed bed and this woman was a swan on a lake. Everything about her was smooth. Her gown was white silk, the white of fresh snow, cut close to her body from the shoulders to the hips and then falling away in a long soft rush to the floor, so that as she came down the stairs it poured over each step behind her like cream. Her arms were bare to above the elbow, and then covered by long white gloves that went up past the elbow and were fastened at the wrist with tiny pearl buttons. Her shoulders were bare. Her neck was long and pale and very straight, and round it, close to the throat, she wore a single row of pearls, small and perfectly round and perfectly matched, glowing softly in the lamplight like a row of little moons.
Her hair was the color of pale gold. Not yellow. The gold of old coins, or of honey held up to a window. It was piled high on the back of her head in a great soft shining crown, and pinned there with a long comb set with white stones that flashed blue and white when she turned her head. Something white and shining in her hair, Corin thought. He had seen it from the round window at the top of Number 3, an hour ago, in the white garden. He had seen it bend over the white bench while a big man in a dark coat nodded beside her.
She reached the bottom of the stairs. The two white footmen did not move. The guests in the hall had gone very quiet again, watching. She did not look at them. She walked straight across the white marble toward Lyra, holding out both her gloved hands.
And as she came close, Corin saw her face.
It was a beautiful face. He did not want it to be, but it was. It was long and fine-boned and pale, with high cheekbones and a narrow, straight nose and a small pointed chin, and there were almost no lines in it at all, though she must have been past forty. She had the Thorne eyes. That was what struck him first, and hardest. Gray eyes. Clear gray, like rain on a window. The same color as Lyra's. The same shape, a little long, a little slanting at the corners. Lyra's eyes, in an older face, looking down at Lyra.
And her mouth was red.
Not pink. Red. A deep dark red, like the inside of a rose, or like wine in a glass. It was the only color on her. Everything else, the gown and the gloves and the pearls and the pale gold hair and the pale skin, was white or nearly white. And in the middle of all that white, the mouth. Red. Smiling.
Tall. White shell half-mask. Red mouth.
Corin kept his eyes on the spot above Lyra's head. He kept his face as still as the footmen's. He did not let anything show at all.
But inside his white gloves his fingers had gone as cold as the rod.
"Lyra," said Cressida Thorne.
She said it softly. Warmly. The way you say the name of somebody you have been missing for a very long time. And she took both of Lyra's hands in her own gloved ones and held them, and looked down into Lyra's face, and her gray eyes went bright and wet, as if she were going to cry.
"Oh, darling," she said. "Oh, look at you. Look at you. You were five. The last time I saw you, you were five years old, in a little blue dress with no sash, sitting at the end of the table with a face like thunder because there were no more plums." She laughed, a soft little shaken laugh. "And now look. A woman. A grown woman." Her eyes went down over Lyra, quickly, from the lace cap to the hem of the gray gown, and came back up again. "And so like her. So terribly like her."
"Aunt Cressida," said Lyra.
Her voice was perfectly steady. Corin was proud of it, in a way he did not understand. She stood in her mother's gown with her hands held by the woman who had been counting her father's silver in the dark an hour ago, and her voice did not shake at all.
"Thank you for asking me," Lyra said.
"Asking you! Darling, I should have asked you years ago. I should have come to you. I should have carried you here." Cressida pressed Lyra's hands. "I have been so ashamed. Do you know that? So ashamed. After your father—after everything—I didn't know how to begin. I kept writing letters and tearing them up. And then I'd think, she won't want to hear from me, why should she, after all these years, and I'd put down the pen." She shook her head. The white stones in her comb flashed. "And then your little letter came. So stiff and so brave. And I sat down and cried, Lyra. I truly did. Bram will tell you. Won't you, Bram?"
"Her ladyship was much affected, miss," said Bramwell, from a little way off, in his grave dry voice. His face did not change at all.
Lyra said nothing. She let her hands be held.
Then Cressida looked past her. At Corin.
She did not look at his face. Not at first. She looked at the coat. Her gray eyes went over the deep green wool, the double row of brass buttons, the high collar, the dark band on the left sleeve. They went over it slowly, the way Madame Hesper's had, but not like Madame Hesper's at all. Madame Hesper had looked at the coat like a doctor looking at a wound. Cressida looked at it like a cat looking at a bird on a branch just out of reach.
"Thorne green," she said softly.
She let go of one of Lyra's hands. She reached out, with one white-gloved finger, and touched the brass button at the top of the double row, on Corin's chest. Just touched it. The rose and the thorn. He felt the touch through the wool, very light, like a moth landing.
Under her glove, at her wrist, where the little pearl buttons fastened, there was something else. A thin chain. Very fine, gold, like a thread. And hanging from it, so small that he almost did not see it, a glass vial no bigger than a grain of rice. It glowed. Faint. Blue. Like a tiny lamp, deep inside the white kid of the glove.
A necklace of tiny glowing vials.
Not a necklace. Not tonight. Just one. Just a little one. At the wrist, where nobody would ever look.
"How brave," said Cressida Thorne. Her red mouth smiled up at him. Her finger stayed on the button. "How very brave of you, darling, to bring him." She did not take her eyes off the coat. "Your mother would have done just the same. She always did love to make an entrance."
"He's my man," said Lyra. "He walks with me."
"Of course he does." The finger lifted away from the button. Cressida turned back to Lyra and smiled, and the smile was warm and sad and very kind. "Of course. Everyone ought to have someone to walk with them. I have Bram, and Bram has been walking with me since I was a girl in plaits, haven't you, Bram, and I don't know what I'd do without him." She tucked Lyra's hand into the crook of her arm. "What is his name?"
"Vane," said Lyra.
Cressida's eyes came back to Corin's face. This time they did look at his face. Just for a moment. A quick light glance, like a stone skipping across water.
"Vane," she repeated.
Nothing changed in her face. Nothing at all. But Corin, standing very still with his eyes on the spot above her golden head, saw the little blue vial at her wrist flicker. Just once. The way a candle flickers when a door opens somewhere else in the house.
"What a plain, good name," said Cressida. "Good evening, Vane."
Corin bowed. The bow Madame Hesper had taught him. Short and exact. He did not speak. She will not speak to you, Bramwell had said. Unless her ladyship speaks to you first, which she will not. She had. He did not know what that meant. He did not answer.
Cressida laughed.
"Oh, he's very well-trained," she said to Lyra. "Where did you find him?"
"He found me," said Lyra.
"How romantic." Cressida turned, drawing Lyra with her. "Come. Come in, come in. Everyone is dying to meet you. Harriet Langley has been at the window since five o'clock, she's quite wild, she's told the whole Hill you walked past my door at noon in broad daylight with a man in green, and they've all come to see if it's true." She laughed again, lightly. "I told her you'd been to look at the old house. Had you, darling? I quite understand if you had. I'd want to, in your place. It must be so strange for you, after all this time."
It was so smooth. It was so easy. Corin felt Lyra go stiff, just for a breath, against the white silk arm.
"I walked past it," said Lyra. "That's all."
"Of course." Cressida patted her hand. "Of course you did."
She did not ask anything else. She did not need to. She had said it. I know you were there. And Lyra had said: I was not. And now they both knew where they stood.
They walked away across the white marble together, the tall woman in white and the young woman in gray, arm in arm, like an aunt and a niece who had loved each other all their lives. The guests parted for them. The murmur of talk started up again behind them, faster and louder than before. And Corin followed two steps behind, a little to the left, with the dark gray cape folded over his arm and the tall black hat under the other.
At the end of the hall, by a pair of tall white doors standing open on a long room full of light, there was a table. A little round table, with a white cloth, and on it a vase of lilies, like every other table in the house. One of the lilies was dying. Its petals had gone soft and brown at the tips, and it hung its head over the side of the vase like a tired child.
Cressida stopped beside it.
"Oh, look," she said, in a little cross voice. "Bram. Look at this one. I won't have dead things in the hall."
And before Bramwell could move, she reached out with one white-gloved hand and touched the drooping flower. Just with her fingertips. Under the stem, where the head joined it.
The vial at her wrist flared. Bright blue. For a single moment, so bright that the white kid of her glove glowed from inside like a lantern.
And the lily lifted its head.
It straightened on its stem, slowly, the way a sleeper sits up. Its petals uncurled. The brown at their tips faded, and went cream, and went white. Its long yellow tongues stood out again, stiff and dusty and alive. In the space of three breaths, it was as fresh as all the others. Fresher. As if it had been cut that morning.
Cressida took her hand away. The vial at her wrist went dim again. Faint and blue.
She was frowning a little. Not at the lily. At nothing. Her gray eyes had gone far off, the way eyes go when somebody is trying to remember a word that is on the tip of the tongue.
Then she laughed.
"There," she said. "Gone. The name of my first pony." She shook her head. "A fat brown thing with a white nose, I used to ride him on the lawn at Number 3 when I was six, and I can't for the life of me think what he was called now. Never mind. I never liked him. He bit." She took Lyra's arm again. "That's the way to do it, darling. Little things. Only ever little things. Never burn anything you'd miss."
Lyra did not say anything.
But Corin, two steps behind her, thought about Lyra's hands on a blue cushion in a room full of gold light, sewing with a needle that was not there. He thought about Captain Oren, who had burned lilies. He thought about Lord Thorne at his window, tapping, tapping, tapping on the wall.
Never burn anything you'd miss.
She is the only one of them, said Malakor, very softly, who has never once burned anything for anybody else. A pause. I have known people like that, boy. They are very hard to kill. Because there is nothing they love enough to die for.
The tall white doors were open in front of them. The long room beyond was full of light, and color, and the sound of a great many people talking at once.
Cressida Thorne walked into it with her brother's daughter on her arm, smiling her red smile, and the room turned to look at her the way flowers turn to look at the sun.
