Chapter 57: Ashes on Lantern Quay, Part 2 of 5: The Light
The Light
Gage had picked her up.
She did not remember him doing it. One moment she was standing at the Lady's left hand by the open brass box, with the first lamp bursting white over the gallery and the cold blue cloud of it pouring out into the hot air. And the next she was off her feet. Lifted, the way you lift a child out of the way of a cart. One great arm under her knees and one behind her back, and her face pressed against the rough gray cloth of his coat, and the green ledger crushed between them.
She had not struggled. She had held on to the ledger.
He had carried her through the fire.
She remembered pieces of it. Afterward. The way you remember a fever. The heat on the side of her face, like an open oven. The cloud of strangers' memories going over them, and through her, and the faces in it. A woman with flour on her hands. A boy on a wall with an apple. She had shut her eyes and pressed her face into Gage's coat and they had gone through her and out the other side and left nothing. She was not a harvester. She had no door in her for them to stay in.
The white flash. She remembered that. The whole dome going white, from the floor to the glass, so bright that through her shut eyelids and Gage's coat she had seen the red of her own blood. And the heat after it, like a blow. And Gage not stopping. Not even slowing. Walking through it with his great soft boots, step after step, the way a wall walks, if a wall could walk.
And she remembered the Lady.
That was the last thing she saw before the flash. Over Gage's shoulder, as he turned. The Lady of the Memory Bourse, standing alone by the open brass box with the two cards for Lot 41 still in her long pale fingers, looking up at the burning gallery. Not screaming. Not running. Just looking up. With her painted face lit blue by the bursting lamps. And then a boy in gray livery, coming from nowhere, out of the smoke, taking her by the arm. A thin sharp-faced boy. Pulling her away, toward the great stair.
The Auditor had seen that boy before. On the kitchen stair, two weeks ago, lighting her way down with a candle. With a fresh cut on his thumb.
Then the flash, and she had seen nothing.
When she opened her eyes again, they were in a passage.
Low and stone-flagged and full of smoke, with an open door at the far end and a red glow beyond it. The kitchen passage. She knew it. She had come down it on the Thursday before last, with Gant and the lantern, to weigh the case. And at the end of it there was a stair, and at the foot of the stair there was a door, and beyond the door was the kitchen yard and the gate and Mews Road.
Gage had stopped.
He was standing in the passage with her in his arms, very still, with his back against the wall. Breathing. She could feel his great chest going in and out under her cheek. And he was looking back. Back the way they had come. Through the door at the near end of the passage, the door that let out under the gallery onto the floor of the dome.
She turned her head and looked too.
And saw the dome stop.
She did not understand it at first. She saw the fire. The whole inside of the dome alight, every curtain, every rail. The floor a field of flame, with the glass tables cracking and the lots bursting on them, one after another, in small white flashes. And above it all, the great black shell of glass and iron, coming down. She saw it coming down. The iron ring at the top, red at its edges, dropping slowly out of the smoke toward the hole in the floor. The ribs bending in toward it like fingers. The panes of glass breaking loose and falling, turning over and over in the firelight like leaves.
And then it stopped.
In the air. Twenty feet above the brass box. As if it had been caught.
And under it, on the floor, on his knees, there was a man.
She could see him through the smoke, through the door, across the burning floor. Not clearly. A dark shape. Kneeling, with his back to her, with his arms lifted straight up over his head, palms up, fingers spread. And his hands were burning. Not with fire. With light. A pale cold blue, at the fingertips, like the flame in the Bank's Ash lamps. And from his lifted hands, going up into the smoke, something she could not see. Only feel. The way you feel heat from a stove across a room. A great invisible pressure, going up, up, out of the kneeling man's hands, into the falling sky. Holding it.
She did not know who he was.
She could not see his face. She could see only the coat. A heavy dark coat, the kind servants wore. A cellarman's coat.
She went very still in Gage's arms.
His hands were wrong, she thought. At the eleventh bell. Gray at the wrists.
And past him, beyond him, a girl.
A thin girl in a gray gown, running across the burning floor under the held dome. Pulling a girl up off the floor by the arm and pushing her toward the wall. Kneeling at the edge of the hole and reaching down into the smoke and pulling up a man in a plum apron by the wrists. Running. Pulling. Pushing. Getting them out. The Auditor watched her drag a porter to the foot of the great stair and leave him there and run back for another, and she understood, all at once, what she was looking at.
The kneeling man was holding up the sky. And the girl was spending it.
And neither of them was coming this way.
"Gage," she said.
He did not answer. He could not. But his arms tightened round her.
"Gage, we have to—"
She did not know what she was going to say. We have to help. But what could they do? He could not hold up the sky. She could not. She could weigh it, perhaps. She could write down what it weighed. Midwinter. The Bourse. The dome. Held by one man. For a time. That was all she could do. That was all she had ever been able to do.
Gage looked down at her.
And then, very carefully, he set her on her feet. On the stone floor of the passage, with her back against the wall. And held her there with one hand on her shoulder, so that she would not fall. And with the other hand, he took the slate from round his neck, and the chalk on its string, and wrote. Fast. Faster than she had ever seen him write.
He held it in front of her face.
Stay.
And he went.
Back through the door, onto the burning floor of the dome. Under the held sky. Into the fire. A great gray shape in the smoke, going fast, faster than she had known he could go. She saw him reach the foot of the great stair, where the thin girl had left the porter, and bend, and lift the porter over his shoulder like a sack of meal. And turn. And come back, through the fire, through the smoke, toward the passage door.
He put the porter down in the passage beside her. A young man, with his face black and his plum apron burned. Coughing. Alive.
And Gage went back.
Three times. She counted. Three times he went out under the held dome and came back with someone over his shoulder or in his arms. The porter. A woman in a white cap. A young lord in a black mask with his coat burned off his back, who screamed when Gage touched him and then stopped screaming. Three times. And each time he went out, the Auditor stood against the wall of the passage with her green ledger pressed to her chest and watched the kneeling man with his burning hands, holding, holding, holding, and thought, Not yet. Not yet. Hold on. Not yet.
She did not know who she was saying it to.
The fourth time, Gage did not go back.
He came in through the door with the young lord in his arms and put him down and turned, and stood in the doorway, looking out. And she saw his shoulders go still.
She looked past him.
The girl in the gray gown had stopped running. She was kneeling beside the man on the floor. With her arms round him. Pulling. Dragging him. Away from the hole, a pace at a time, toward the wall, toward the overhang of the gallery, where the stair came down. And the man was letting himself be dragged. He was still on his knees. His arms were still up. His hands were still burning blue. But he was moving. A pace at a time. With the girl's arms round him.
And then they were under the gallery. At the wall.
And he put his hands down.
And the sky came down.
Gage turned in the doorway and threw himself back into the passage, over her, against the wall, the whole huge gray bulk of him, so that she was in the dark between him and the stone and could see nothing. And she heard it. The sound of it. She would hear it for the rest of her life, in the place where she kept the things she could not afford to burn. A thousand panes of glass and twenty-four ribs of iron and a great iron ring, coming down all at once into the hole in the floor of the Memory Bourse.
And then the quiet.
One breath.
And then the light.
She saw it round the edge of Gage's shoulder. White. Coming up. A great white pillar of it, out of the hole, out of the cold room, out of the jars, going straight up past the burning gallery into the open sky where the dome had been. So bright that the passage, the stone, Gage's gray coat, her own white gloves, all went white with it. So bright that for a moment she could not see anything at all.
And it went into her.
Not like the strangers' memories from the lamps. Those had gone through her and out the other side and left nothing, because she had no door for them. This was different. This did not go through her. This went in. Into the places in her that were empty. The two thousand four hundred and nine empty days. The six years. The long gray nothing before the morning of the ninth of the Thaw month. It went into all of it, the way water goes into dry ground.
And for a hundred breaths, she remembered.
Not much. Not her name, or her mother's face, or the place she had come from. Not anything she could write down. Small things. Pieces. The way a broken window, in the sun, throws small pieces of light on a wall.
Grass. Long grass, up to her chin, wet, and the smell of it.
A hand. A boy's hand. Bigger than hers. Holding hers, very tight, too tight, pulling her along through the long wet grass. Fast. And a voice. A boy's voice. Out of breath. Come on. Come on, Ellie. Keep up. Don't let go.
And herself. Laughing.
That was the thing. That was the thing she would keep. Not the grass, not the hand, not even the voice. Herself. A little girl, running through long wet grass with her hand in a bigger boy's hand, being pulled along too fast, and laughing. Laughing and laughing. The way a child laughs when she is being chased in a game and does not want to be caught and does not want it to end. High and bright and quick, going up and up, like a bird.
She had not known she could laugh like that.
She had not laughed in six years. She knew that, standing there in the white light in the kitchen passage. She knew it the way you know you have not eaten. She had smiled. She had a gold tooth that showed when she smiled, over a gap, and people looked at it. But she had not laughed. Not once. Not since the morning of the ninth of the Thaw month. She had never even wondered why.
And now, for a hundred breaths, in the white light, she could hear it. Her own laugh. From before. From the long wet grass.
And then the light began to fade.
And the laugh went with it.
She felt it go. Out of her. Not burned, the way the Sight burned a day. Just gone. Drawn back out, the way the sea draws back from the sand. The grass went. The hand went. The boy's voice went. Come on, Ellie. And last of all, the laugh. Going up and up, high and bright, like a bird. And then not there.
She was standing in the kitchen passage of the burning Memory Bourse with her back against the wall and Mister Gage's great gray shoulder in front of her face and her green ledger pressed to her chest. And she was crying.
She had not cried in six years either.
Gage moved back. Away from the wall. Away from her. He looked down at her.
And the Auditor looked up at him. With the tears running down her face, through the soot, onto her collar. And said, in a voice she did not know:
"Ellie. Somebody used to call me Ellie."
Gage did not move.
Then he lifted the slate. And wiped it, slowly, with the side of his hand. And wrote. And held it down for her to see, in the fading white light.
Yes.
She looked at it for a long time.
She wanted to ask him. Who? Who called me that? Do you know? Were you there? She had a hundred questions. She had had a hundred questions for six years, and every time she had looked at Mister Gage's plain wooden face and his blank slate, she had not asked them. Because she was afraid of what he would write.
She was afraid now.
She did not ask.
Gage wiped the slate. And put it back round his neck. And bent, and picked her up again, one arm under her knees and one behind her back, the way you lift a child. And carried her down the kitchen passage, past the porter and the woman in the white cap and the young lord, who were sitting against the wall with their faces turned up to the fading light, as if they were listening to something. Through the kitchen. Out into the yard.
At the kitchen gate, there were men in gray.
Two of them. With truncheons. Standing by the brass Ash lamp, which had burst, and was lying in pieces on the cobbles. They were looking at every face that came out of the gate. Every one. Holding each man and woman by the arm, and looking, and letting them go.
Gage stopped in front of them.
One of them looked up at Gage. And then at the Auditor, in his arms. At her white gloves, gray with soot. At her green ledger.
And stepped back.
"The Auditor," he said to the other one. "Let her through." He did not look at her face. He looked at a place just over her shoulder. "The Inquisitor's orders. She's not to be held."
The Auditor turned her head.
"Whose orders?"
The man in gray did not answer.
Gage carried her out of the gate, into Mews Road, into the screaming horses and the falling snow. And she lay in his arms with her face turned back toward the gate, toward the two men in gray, toward the burning house behind them, and thought:
The Inquisitor's orders. She's not to be held.
She had met Silas Kross four times in six years. At Council. In a corridor at the Ministry. Once on the steps of the Bank. He had always bowed. He had never said more than Good morning, Auditor. She did not think he knew her name.
She's not to be held.
She took out her black book, there, in Gage's arms, in the snow, and wrote it down on the inside of the back cover, where she would find it later. Not under Found something. She had not written that yet. She wrote it with a pencil that would not stop shaking.
Then she put the book away, and did not look at it again until dawn.
Weighed and Agreed
Mister Harrow came in at the first bell, as he always did, to the minute.
She heard him on the stair. A small dry cough on every landing. The tap of his umbrella on the steps. He had carried that umbrella up the stairs of the Oblivion Bank every morning for twenty-two years, rain or shine or snow, and he had never once opened it inside. Then the door. And the small dry cough again, as he saw Gage on his bench and gave him his good morning, which Gage never answered. And then his umbrella going into the stand, and his hat onto the peg, and his coat onto the peg beside it, and his gray woolen gloves into the pocket of the coat. And the creak of his chair at the middle desk. And the small sharp click of his inkwell lid.
She did not turn round from the window.
"Good morning, Miss Elara," said Mister Harrow.
"Good morning, Mister Harrow."
"A terrible night."
"Yes."
He did not say anything more. He was a small gray neat man, with small gray neat whiskers and a small gray neat voice, and he did not say anything more than he had to. That was why she liked him. She heard him open the green ledger, and dip his pen, and begin the day's first line, the way he began it every day: the date, and the weather, and the hour.
She wondered what he would write for the weather.
Ash, perhaps.
She turned round at last.
The Fourth Desk looked as it always looked at the first bell. Long and narrow and low, with the gray morning light coming in through the tall river windows, and the black cupboards with their brass numbers down the other side. Gage on his bench by the door. Mister Harrow at his desk in the middle, with his head bent over the green ledger and his pen moving. And her own desk at the end, by her window, with its brass lamp and its inkstand and the Bank's brass scale in its leather case, and the green ledger from last night lying shut on the blotter where she had put it down at the third bell.
She had not opened it since.
She went to the desk and sat down. And took off her other glove, the left one, so that both her hands were bare. And laid them flat on the green leather cover of last night's ledger. And did not open it.
She was still sitting like that when the knock came.
Not at the door of the Fourth Desk. Nobody knocked at that door. People came up the stairs, and Gage looked at them, and either they came in or they went back down. The knock was on the frame of the door. Three small knocks, with a knuckle, very light and very exact. Spaced out. One. Two. Three.
The Auditor knew that knock.
She looked up.
Lucian was standing in the doorway.
He looked exactly as he always looked. That was the first thing she thought. After a night like last night, after the fire and the screaming and the white light, he looked exactly as he had looked every time she had ever seen him, which was perhaps a dozen times in six years, always in a doorway, always at the first bell or the last. A small neat man in a plain dark coat, with his back very straight and his head a little bent. A gray woolen scarf. Black gloves. His face was quite smooth, quite pale, quite calm. A face like a clean page.
In his arms, held against his chest, very carefully, he had a black wooden case.
Silver corners. A silver clasp, shaped like a closed hand. And under the silver fingers, a little square of folded paper.
Gage had stood up.
He did not do anything else. He stood beside his bench, between Lucian and the room, and looked down at him. And Lucian looked up at Gage, a long way up, and inclined his head, very slightly, the way you incline your head to a wall you have to walk round.
"Good morning, Mister Gage," he said. His voice was soft and dry and exact. "I have a lot for the Auditor. Under clause eleven. I am asked to wait for it."
Gage looked at him for a moment longer.
Then he sat down.
Lucian came down the long room. Between the black cupboards and the tall windows. Past Mister Harrow's desk, where Mister Harrow had stopped writing and sat with his pen in the air and his small gray eyes very wide. And he stopped in front of the Auditor's desk, and set the black case down on her blotter, beside the green ledger. Squarely. In the middle. The way a porter sets out a lot on velvet.
"The Archon's compliments," he said. "He asks that the Auditor of the Fourth Desk will weigh Lot 41, as bought last night by the Council, and enter it as held by the Council under clause eleven of the Charter. And sign the entry."
The Auditor looked at the case.
"Now?"
"Now, if you please." Lucian folded his black-gloved hands in front of him. "The Archon wishes it kept cold. In the Bank's own vault. It is to go down at the second bell." He paused. "He has said that he will not open it until the spring."
He will not open it until the spring.
The Auditor did not say anything.
She opened the leather case on the corner of her desk, and took out the Bank's brass scale.
It was a beautiful thing. She had always thought so. A small brass beam on a brass pillar, with two shallow brass pans hanging from it on fine brass chains, and a needle at the top of the pillar that swung against a little curved scale of ivory, marked in figures so small you needed a glass to read them. It had come with her desk. Or she had come with it. She did not know which. She had used it every day for six years, and kept it in its leather case, and cleaned it every evening with a soft cloth, and she knew its voice the way you know the voice of somebody you live with. The small click of the beam when it settled. The tiny chime of the chains.
She set it on the blotter. She opened the drawer under the desk, and took out the box of brass weights, and opened it. Little brass weights, round, with handles on top, from one the size of a cherry stone to one the size of a fist, each in its own velvet hollow. She chose the weights, without counting, without looking at the green ledger from the Thursday before last. She did not need to look. She remembered the figure. She would remember it until it was burned.
She put the weights in the right-hand pan.
Then she stood up, and lifted the black case off the blotter in both bare hands, and put it in the left-hand pan.
The chains chimed. The beam rocked. Up, down, up. The needle swung across the little ivory scale and back, and back again, slower, slower, and settled.
She looked at it.
It was light.
Not by much. She had known it would not be by much. Whoever had made it had known his trade. He had used the same wood. The same silver. He had made it the same size to a hair. A man holding it in his arms would never know. A lord lifting it off a velvet tray would never know. But the Bank's brass scale knew. The needle had settled one mark short. One small black line on the ivory, so fine that she had to bend close to see it.
One mark. The weight of a few drops of water. The weight of a folded square of paper.
Or the weight of a man's last evening.
The Auditor straightened up.
She did not look at Lucian. She looked at the needle. It did not move. It was a needle. It could not lie. That was what it was for.
And then, still not looking at Lucian, she sat down. And opened the green ledger from last night. To the page where, at the twelfth bell, standing at the Lady's left hand by the open brass box, she had written: Lot 41. The Council, by the hand of the Archon. No sum named. Winner.
She dipped her pen.
And under that line, in her small neat hand, the hand whose record would stand, she wrote:
Tuesday after Midwinter, first bell. Lot 41 brought to the Fourth Desk by the Archon's secretary. Weighed against the Thursday figure. Weighed and agreed. Held by the Council under clause eleven. E., Auditor.
Weighed and agreed.
She put the pen down.
The ink shone on the page, black and wet, and began to dry.
It was the first lie she had ever written in a Bank ledger.
She knew that. She knew it the way you know the first time you have cut yourself. She had found ten thousand lies in six years. She had laid her bare hand on page after page and felt them under the writing, like stones under a carpet. She had burned two thousand four hundred and nine days of her own life finding them. And she had never once written one.
She had done it now. For a man she did not know. Who had knelt on the floor of a burning dome and held up the sky with his bare hands. Whose hands were gray at the wrists. Whose name was scratched on a wall by a bed in an empty room on Tallow Street, beside her own.
She did not know why.
She only knew that if she had written the truth, weighed and found light, then by the second bell the Archon would know that the case in his vault was not the case he had bought. And he would want to know where the other one was. And who had it. And he would look. The Archon did not look for things often. Lucian had said once, in a doorway, that the Archon never read anything twice. But when he looked for a thing, he found it.
And whoever had it would not live to open it.
She blotted the line. Carefully. And closed the ledger.
And only then did she look up at Lucian.
He was standing quite still in front of her desk, with his black-gloved hands folded. He had not moved the whole time. He had not looked at the scale. She was almost sure of that. He had looked at her. And his face was still a clean page. Calm and pale and smooth, with nothing written on it.
But his eyes had gone to the green ledger, as she closed it.
And she understood, quite suddenly, that he had read the line upside down. As she wrote it. Every word.
She waited for him to say something.
He did not.
He bent, instead, and lifted the black case out of the left-hand pan of the scale, in both arms. Carefully. Against his chest. And the beam of the scale swung up, and the brass weights in the other pan went down with a small click, and the needle went all the way across the ivory to the end and stopped there, and trembled.
"Thank you, Auditor," said Lucian.
He turned to go.
And then turned back. Just his head. Over his shoulder.
"The Archon asked me," he said, "to ask you a question. One. When it was done."
The Auditor waited.
"He asked me to ask you whether it weighed what it ought."
The room was very quiet. Mister Harrow at his desk had not moved. Gage on his bench had not moved. Outside the tall windows, the ash was still falling, very fine now, very light, past the glass, and the river went by under it, gray.
"It is written in the ledger," said the Auditor. "Weighed and agreed."
Lucian looked at her for a moment.
"Then I will tell him so," he said. "I will only have told him what the record says."
He inclined his head. The small exact bow of a man who does not like to be thanked, to a woman who had not thanked him.
And went away down the long room, between the cupboards and the windows, past Mister Harrow, past Gage, with the black case held against his chest, and out of the door, and down the stairs. She heard his steps go down. Soft and exact and even. Every one the same. Until she could not hear them any more.
The Auditor sat at her desk with her bare hands on the green ledger.
"Miss Elara," said Mister Harrow, after a long time. Very quietly.
"Yes, Mister Harrow."
He did not say anything. She looked up. He was sitting at his desk in the middle of the room with his pen in his fingers and his small gray eyes on her. He had worked at the Fourth Desk for twenty-two years. He had seen four Auditors come and go. He had never once in all those years, so far as she knew, asked any of them a question.
He did not ask one now.
He looked down at his own green ledger, and dipped his pen, and wrote a line. And then he blotted it, and looked up again.
"The weather, Miss Elara," he said. "For the day book. I have put ash. Will that do?"
The Auditor looked at him.
And then she looked out of the window, at the gray sky and the gray river and the gray fine ash still falling, falling, on Lantern Quay.
"Yes," she said. "That will do."
