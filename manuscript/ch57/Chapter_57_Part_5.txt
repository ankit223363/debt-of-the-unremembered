Chapter 57: Ashes on Lantern Quay, Part 5 of 5: Ferrule Street
Ferrule Street
There were three names on the Archon's list. She had seen it on the Friday. She had not been meant to see it.
It had been lying on Lucian's tray, in the corridor outside the Council room, while Lucian stood at the door and waited to be called in. A half-sheet of the Archon's own gray paper, with three lines on it, in the Archon's hand, which was small and upright and very plain, like printing. She had walked past the tray on her way to the Fourth Desk with Gage behind her, and she had glanced down, the way you glance at any paper on any tray, and she had seen it. Three lines. The names of the three people who had held the black case with the silver clasp.
Hale, L. Harvester. Retired.
Thorne, C. Councillor's widow. By proxy.
E. Auditor. The Fourth Desk. Weighed and held, under clause eleven.
And beside the first name, in the margin, drawn in the same black ink, very small and very neat, a circle.
She had walked on. She had not stopped. She had gone up the stairs to the Fourth Desk, and sat down at her desk, and opened her ledger, and she had not thought about it again for the rest of the day. But that night, in her chair by the window, she had taken out her black book and written it down. The Archon's list. Three names. Mine is the third. A circle by the first.
She had read it again at dawn this morning.
She did not know what the circle meant. She had seen it once before, she thought. On a form. Long ago, or not so long, she could not tell. A small circle, in the margin, beside a name. And then the name had been on another form, a week later. A different kind of form. The kind that went to the Intake Hall.
She went to Ferrule Street.
Gage came behind her.
It was the fourth bell when they came out of the Bank, onto Lantern Quay. The ash had almost stopped falling. It lay on everything, gray and soft, an inch deep on the cobbles and the steps and the roofs of the barges, like a snow that had forgotten how to be white. The lamps of the quay were still lit, though it was day. Nobody had come to put them out. They burned on, blue and steady, through the gray, and the ash lay on the glass of them, so that their light came out dim and strange, like light through a dirty window.
The Crescent Gas. The lamps of the Hill. One tenth to the Treasurer. The balance to the lamps.
She did not look at them.
Ferrule Street was in the Old Town, behind the Ministry of Records, up the hill from the river. A narrow street of tall thin old houses, leaning together over the cobbles like old men telling secrets, with shops on the ground floors and rooms above. A chandler. A cobbler. A shop that sold nothing but string. And halfway up, on the left, a house a little wider than the others, with a lamp over the door. Not an Ash lamp. An old oil lamp, in an iron cage, very old, with a little iron ladder fixed to the wall beside it, the kind lamplighters used to climb when there were lamplighters. And a sign, painted on a board over the door, so faded you could hardly read it.
THE LAMPLIGHTER'S REST. Rooms for retired men of the Guild.
There was no Guild now. There had been no lamplighters in Veridia for thirty years, since the Crescent Gas came, and the lamps learned to light themselves. But the house was still there. And some of the old men were still in it. She had looked it up in the Bank's books, on the Friday night. Six rooms. Six old men. Paid for out of a fund that nobody had remembered to close. One of the six was a harvester, not a lamplighter. Hale, L. Room six. At the top.
The door was open.
Not standing wide. Just not shut. Off the latch, a finger's width, with the ash blown in over the step in a small gray drift.
She stood on the step and looked at it.
Gage stood behind her and looked at it too.
Then she pushed the door open and went in.
It was a narrow hall, with a narrow stair going up, and a smell of old men and old soup and lamp oil. There was a door on the left, standing open, and through it a small hot kitchen with a range, and an old woman sitting by the range in a rocking chair with her hands in her lap. A very old woman, in a black dress and a white cap, with a face like a walnut. She looked up as the Auditor came to the door.
"He's upstairs," she said.
She did not ask who the Auditor was. She did not ask what she wanted. She looked at her, and then at Gage behind her, filling the hall, and then back at the range.
"Mister Hale," said the Auditor.
"Mister Hale." The old woman nodded. "At the top. Room six." She rocked a little. "I took him up his breakfast at the first bell. Porridge, he has, and a bit of bread. Every day for nine years. And he didn't answer when I knocked. So I went in." She rocked. "And he's sitting there. At his table. And he looks at me. And he doesn't know me." She was quiet for a moment. "Nine years. Porridge and a bit of bread. And he doesn't know me."
"Has anybody else been?"
"Last night." The old woman did not look up from the range. "Late. After the fire started. I could see the glow from my window, over the roofs, from the Exchange. And there was a knock at the door. Very soft. And I went down, and I opened it." She stopped rocking. "And there was nobody there."
"Nobody."
"Nobody that I saw." The old woman's hands moved in her lap, very slightly. "I stood on the step and looked up the street and down. And there was nobody. Only the glow, over the roofs. And the snow. And I came back in, and I thought, that was strange, and I went to bed." She looked at the Auditor at last. She had very pale eyes. "But I didn't shut the door. I'd have sworn I shut the door. And in the morning it was open."
The Auditor said nothing.
She went up the stairs.
Five flights. Narrow and steep and dark, with a window on each landing looking out at the backs of other houses, and doors on each landing with numbers painted on them in white. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. And at the top, under the roof, one more door. Smaller than the others. With a six on it.
It was not locked.
She knocked anyway. Three times. And waited. And then opened it.
It was a small room. Very small. Smaller than the Fourth Desk's cupboard eleven, almost. A room under the slope of the roof, so that you could only stand up straight in the middle of it. A narrow iron bed against the low wall, very neat, with a gray blanket folded square at the foot. A washstand with a jug and basin. A row of hooks with an old coat on one. A shelf, with books on it. Old books, thick, in faded covers, twenty or thirty of them, in a row. And a window. A small square window in the slope of the roof, a skylight, with the gray sky beyond it, and the ash lying on the glass.
And under the window, a table.
And at the table, a man.
He was very old. Older than anybody she had ever seen. That was the first thing she thought. He sat in a hard wooden chair at the small table under the skylight, bent forward over the table, the way very old men bend. He was tiny. Thin as a stick. His round gold spectacles had slipped down his nose. With thin white hair, very fine, lying flat on his skull like frost on a stone. He wore a dark coat, old but very clean, buttoned to the neck. And his hands lay on the table in front of him.
His hands.
They were gray. All the way. She could see where the sleeves of his coat ended at the wrists, and the skin there was gray, gray like slate, gray like the hands in the pens of the Intake Hall. And it went on down, over the backs of his hands, over the knuckles, over the fingers. Gray. Darker and darker. And at the very ends of the fingers, it was not gray any more.
It was blue.
A deep, cold, dark blue, like the sky an hour after sunset. All ten fingertips. As if he had dipped them in ink, very carefully, to the first joint, and let it dry.
He had a pen in his right hand. An old pen, steel-nibbed, with a wooden handle worn smooth. He was holding it the way you hold a pen to write. The nib was resting on a sheet of paper on the table in front of him.
The paper was blank.
Quite blank. White. Clean. Not a mark on it. Not a word. Not a blot. The nib of the pen was resting on it, at the top, at the left, where you begin, and there was ink on the nib, dried now, black. But the paper was blank.
He was looking at it.
Not at her. He had not looked up when she opened the door. He sat at his table under the gray skylight with the pen in his blue-tipped fingers and the nib on the blank page, and looked at the page, very calmly. The way you look at a page when you are just about to begin, and know exactly what you are going to write.
He was breathing. She could see his chest move under the buttoned coat. Slow. Even.
"Mister Hale," said the Auditor.
He did not look up.
She came into the room. Slowly. Under the slope of the roof, bending her head. Gage stayed in the doorway behind her. He could not have got in. He filled the doorway from side to side and top to bottom, and stood there, and did not move.
She came to the table.
"Mister Hale," she said again. "I am the Auditor of the Fourth Desk. I held the case. The black case. After you."
Nothing.
She looked at his face.
It was a fine face. Long and narrow and lined, with a high thin nose and deep eyes under white brows. A clever face. A face that had seen a great deal, for a very long time, and remembered all of it.
It did not remember anything now.
She could see that. The way you can see that a house is empty, from the street, even with the curtains drawn. It was in his eyes. They were open, and clear, and pale, and looking at the blank page. And there was nobody behind them. Nobody at all.
She had seen eyes like that this morning. In the Intake Hall. In pen two. Like windows, Briggs had said.
This was what the circle meant.
She stood beside the table for a long time, and looked at the old man, and did not say anything. Gage did not move in the doorway. The gray light came down through the skylight onto the table and the blank page and the blue-tipped fingers on the pen.
And then she saw the mark.
It was on the page. In the top right-hand corner, far from the nib of the pen, where you would never begin writing. So small that she had not seen it. So small that she had to bend down close, with her face almost on the table, to be sure it was there at all.
A circle.
A small, plain, perfect circle. Drawn in black ink. Very neatly. In a hand that was not shaking at all.
The old man had not drawn it. She knew that. Not with that pen. Not with those fingers. Not with that nib resting where it rested, at the top left, where you begin.
Somebody else had drawn it. With his own pen, perhaps. Taken it out of his blue fingers, and drawn the small neat circle in the corner of his blank page. And put the pen back in his hand. And arranged his fingers round it. Carefully. The way you would arrange the hands of the dead.
And gone.
And left the door open.
The Auditor straightened up.
She looked at the blank page with the circle in its corner.
And she began, slowly, to take off her right glove.
The Blank Page
Gage made a sound in the doorway.
Not a word. He had no words. A sound, low, in his chest. The sound a big animal makes when it sees a hand go toward a fire.
The Auditor did not look round.
"I know," she said. "One day."
She laid the glove on the table, beside the old man's gray hand.
She had done this two thousand four hundred and nine times. She knew how it went. She knew every part of it the way you know the stairs of your own house in the dark. You laid your bare hand flat on the page. You shut your eyes. You let yourself go down. Not into the paper. Into what had happened to the paper. Into the hand that had held the pen, and the moment the pen had touched, and everything that had been in that moment and was written there, under the writing, in a place where ink does not go. And you looked. And while you looked, somewhere behind you, in the dark, a day of your own life caught fire and burned, and you never knew which one until afterward, when you tried to remember it and found the place where it had been.
She laid her bare right hand flat on the blank page.
Beside the nib of the old man's pen. Beside the small neat circle in the corner.
She shut her eyes.
And went down.
Candlelight.
That was first. Always the light first. A candle in a tin holder, on the table, at the left. Burning low. And the skylight over the table black, with snow on it, and a red glow at the edge of the black, very faint, from the north and west. The glow over the Exchange.
And the old man. At the table. Writing.
She saw him the way you see a man in a dream. Not from outside. Not from inside. From the page. From under his hand. She saw the pen come down, and the nib touch, and the ink go into the paper, and she felt it, every stroke, the way the paper felt it. And she saw the words.
They were not blank.
They were there. In a small, slanting, old-fashioned hand, very neat, a little shaky. A whole page of them. He had been writing for a long time. Since the fire began, perhaps. Since he saw the glow from his window.
To Corin Vane. If he lives.
The Auditor's breath stopped.
She did not open her eyes. She could not. She was down too far. She held on, and read, as the nib moved.
To Corin Vane. If he lives.
I gave you my book on Sunday and told you not to open it until after Monday. It is after Monday now, or nearly. I can see the glow from here. I do not know if you are in it. I am writing this in case you are not, so that somebody will have it. And in case you are, so that you will.
The key I gave you, with the oak leaf on it, opens a door on a level that does not exist. I told you that. I did not tell you where. It is under the Ministry. Under the old Harvesting rooms, where Master Corvell first put a rod in my hand, where the boy Toby sits now. There is a stair behind the last cupboard, on the left, that the clerks do not use, because the clerks do not see it. You will see it. You have the eyes for it. I gave you those too, a long time ago, before you can remember.
At the bottom of the stair there is a door. The key opens it. What is behind it, I will not write. I was afraid to write it in the book, and I am afraid to write it here. But you will know it when you see it. You will know it the way you knew the tune on the Saltmarsh.
If you cannot go yourself, ask the giant. The silent one. The one who stands by the Auditor's door.
He was there.
The Auditor's bare hand pressed down on the page so hard that, far away, in another room, in the gray daylight, she felt the paper crumple under her palm.
She held on.
He was there, at the end, in the valley. He carried her out. The little one. He has never told her. He promised somebody he would not. I do not know who. I think it may have been her.
I made you kind instead, Corin. I told you that on Sunday. I did not tell you the rest. I made you kind because it was the only thing I could give you that they would not think to take. Nobody steals kindness. Nobody thinks it is worth anything.
Forgive me. I did not know how else to keep you.
And then the nib stopped.
Because there was a knock at the door.
The Auditor felt it. Through the page. Through the old man's hand. The knock, soft, three times, on the door of room six. And the old man's hand going still on the pen. And the nib resting where it was, at the end of the last line. Forgive me. I did not know how else to keep you.
He did not get up.
He did not say come in.
The door opened anyway.
She could not see who it was. She was in the page. She could only see what the page saw, and the page was looking up, at the candle, at the old man's face, at the skylight with the snow on it. But she saw the candle flame bend. All at once. Away from the door. Bend over almost flat, and shiver, and stand up again. As if a great cold breath had come into the room.
And the old man's face.
It did not change. Not at first. It stayed quite calm. Quite still. Looking at the door. And then, very slowly, it began to smile.
It was not a happy smile. It was the smile of a man who has been waiting a long time for a carriage, in the rain, at night, and sees its lamps at last coming round the corner. And knows where it is going to take him.
"You," said Linus Hale.
And somebody answered.
The Auditor heard the voice through the page. Through the paper, through the old man's hand, through the wood of the table. It was a soft voice. Very soft, and warm, and kind. A voice like warm milk. A voice you would trust in the dark. A voice that made you want to shut your eyes and put your head down and let somebody else carry you for a while.
She could not hear the words. Not all of them. The page did not hear words. It heard only the shape of them, the way you hear voices through a wall.
But she heard the last three.
Because the old man said them back. Very quietly. As if he had heard them before, a long time ago, from somebody else, and was only now understanding what they meant.
"Gently," said Linus Hale. "If you would."
And a hand came down over the page.
She saw it. From below. From the paper. A hand, coming down out of the candlelight over the written page, and laying itself flat on the words, the way her own hand lay flat on them now. A long hand. Thin. Very white. With long thin white fingers, spread. No glove. No ring. Nothing on it at all. Only white, and long, and very still.
And under it, the words began to go.
Not burn. Not blur. Go. Lift. The ink came up off the paper, under the long white hand, the way frost comes up off a window when you breathe on it. Line by line. From the top. To Corin Vane. If he lives. Gone. I gave you my book on Sunday. Gone. The key I gave you, with the oak leaf on it. Gone. Ask the giant. Gone. He was there. Gone. I made you kind instead. Gone. Lifting off the page into the white hand, and into somewhere beyond it, like smoke going up a chimney. Until the page was white. Clean. Not a mark on it.
And the old man's eyes, over the page. Looking up at whoever stood beside him. Still smiling. And going out. Slowly. Like the candle when you pinch it. Not all at once. One thing at a time. She saw them go. Room after room in him, going dark. A boy in a field. A woman with red hair. A war. A valley. A rod with a spiral turning left. A name. A name. A name.
And last of all, very faint, very far back, a boy. Ten years old, and small for it. Sitting on the floor of a cold room, with a gray blanket round him, looking up. And a voice, the old man's own voice, much younger, saying: There. There, now. I can't give you back what they took. But I'll give you this instead.
And then that went too.
And the long white hand lifted off the page.
And took the old man's pen out of his blue-tipped fingers, very gently. And dipped it in the ink. And bent, and drew, in the top right-hand corner of the blank white page, far from where you begin, a small plain perfect circle.
And put the pen back in the old man's fingers. And folded the fingers round it. Carefully. The way you arrange the hands of the dead. With the nib resting at the top left of the page, where you begin.
And the candle flame bent, all at once, toward the door. And stood up again.
And the page was alone with the old man and the candle. And the candle burned down. And went out.
The Auditor opened her eyes.
She was standing in room six of the Lamplighter's Rest, under the slope of the roof, in the gray daylight from the skylight, with her bare right hand flat on a crumpled sheet of blank white paper. And the old man was sitting in his chair beside her, quite still, with the pen in his blue fingers, looking at the page. Calm. Empty. Like a window.
She took her hand away.
It was shaking. She looked at it. She could not make it stop.
Somewhere behind her, in the dark, a day was gone. She did not know which. She would find out later. She would reach for something, a Tuesday, a Thursday, a face, a conversation, and find the place where it had been, burned clean, like the page.
She did not care.
She took out her black book, with the hand that would not stop shaking, and opened it to the last page, and wrote. Fast. Before it went. Before the shape of it went the way the words had gone off the page under the long white hand.
Ferrule Street. Room six. Hale, L. Emptied in the night, after the fire began. Used the Sight on his last page. One day.
Running total: 2,410 days.
He wrote a letter. To Corin Vane. It was taken off the page. I read it before it went. I will write down what I can.
The oak-leaf key. A door under the Ministry, under the old Harvesting rooms. A stair behind the last cupboard, on the left, that the clerks do not see.
Ask the giant.
He was there. At the end, in the valley. He carried her out. The little one.
She stopped.
She looked at the last line for a long time.
Then she turned round.
Gage was standing in the doorway. Filling it. Very big, and very still, in the gray light from the landing window behind him. He had not moved. He had watched her the whole time, with her hand on the page and her eyes shut. She could see that in his face. In the plain wooden face that never showed anything.
It was showing something now.
She did not know what. She had never seen it before. She could not have put a name to it, or written it in a ledger, or weighed it on a scale.
She held up the black book. Open. At the last page. So that he could see what she had written.
He looked at it.
At the last line.
He was there. At the end, in the valley. He carried her out. The little one.
He did not lift his slate.
He did not move at all.
And the Auditor of the Fourth Desk, standing under the slope of the roof in room six of the Lamplighter's Rest, beside an old man with blue fingers and a blank page, with her own bare hand shaking and a day of her life gone that she would never get back, looked up at Mister Gage in the doorway, and said, for the first time in six years, out loud, the name that had been scratched into a wall beside her own.
"Corin Vane," she said.
The name hung in the cold little room.
Gage's hand went to his slate. Slowly. As if it were very heavy. He lifted it. And the chalk on its string. And wrote, a long time, slowly, with the chalk squeaking on the slate in the silence. And held it out to her across the little room.
Not yet.
And under it, smaller, with the chalk pressed so hard it had broken at the end:
But soon. I promise. Soon.
The Auditor looked at it.
Then she nodded, once.
She put her glove back on. She folded the blank page from the table, very carefully, in half, and in half again, and again, and again, until it was a small tight square no bigger than her thumbnail, with the circle folded away inside it where nobody could see. And she put it in her black book, between the last two pages, and shut the book, and put it back in the inside pocket of her dark green coat, against her ribs. The way some people carry a letter.
She did not know why she had folded it so small.
She only knew that her hands had wanted to.
Then she bent and laid her gloved hand, very lightly, for a moment, on the old man's thin white hair. The way Gage had laid his on hers.
"Thank you, Mister Hale," she said.
He did not look up from the page.
She went out past Gage, and down the five narrow flights of the Lamplighter's Rest, past the doors with the white numbers on them, and the windows on the landings, and the old woman in the kitchen in her rocking chair, who did not look up either. And out onto Ferrule Street, into the gray noon. And Gage came behind her, the way he always did.
And on Lantern Quay, the lamps were still burning, blue, under the ash, in the middle of the day. Because nobody had remembered to put them out.
