Chapter 57: Ashes on Lantern Quay, Part 3 of 5: The Intake Hall
The Intake Hall
At the second bell she went down to the Intake Hall.
She did not have to. Nobody had sent for her. The Fourth Desk audited the Intake Hall once a month, on the last Thursday, by the book, and this was not the last Thursday and nobody had asked. But she could not sit at her desk with the green ledger shut under her hands and the needle of the brass scale still trembling at the end of the ivory in her mind. She had to move. She had to do something with her hands that was not writing.
So she put on clean gloves at last, and took her pencil and her black book and a fresh green ledger, and went down.
Gage came behind her. He always did.
The Intake Hall was at the bottom of the Bank. Below the ground. On the river side, where the old stone of the foundations went down into the black water of the river and the walls were always wet. You went down to it by a stair from the back of the great front hall, behind the counters where the Bank's clerks took in deposits and paid out loans, behind a door with no number on it and a porter on a stool. Down, and down, and down. Six flights of stone. The light getting grayer and the air getting colder and the smell of the river getting stronger at every turn, until at the bottom there was no daylight at all, only lamps, and the smell was the smell of the river and of wet stone and of something else, faint and sweet and sour at once, like milk left out in a warm room.
She knew that smell. She had smelled it in the kitchen passage of the Bourse last night, under the burnt sugar of the Ash. And once before, in the autumn, in the cold room, with Gant and his lantern.
Grayroot.
The Intake Hall was a long low vault, like the inside of a great stone barrel lying on its side. Pillars down the middle. Lamps on the pillars. Ordinary oil lamps, yellow, not Ash lamps. The Bank did not use Ash lamps below the ground. Too dear, they said. Along one side, under the curve of the roof, a row of tall wooden pens, like the pens in a cattle market, each with a gate of iron bars and a number painted over it in white. Twelve pens. Along the other side, a long counter of black wood, with ledgers on it, and inkstands, and a brass bell. And at the far end, a pair of great iron doors, double, with a bar across them, that let out onto the river. The Intake doors. Where the boats came in.
There were people in the pens.
Not many. Fewer than she had seen on the last Thursday. Two pens were full, and one was half full, and the rest were empty, with their gates standing open and fresh straw on the floor. In the full pens, the people were sitting on the straw. Or standing. Or lying. Men, mostly. Some women. Some children. All of them quite still. All of them with their hands lying open in their laps, or hanging at their sides. Gray hands. Gray to the wrists, gray to the elbows. Slate-gray, like stone.
Nobody was crying. Nobody was talking. Nobody was looking at anything.
She had audited this hall sixty-odd times. She had never got used to that. The quiet.
At the far end of the counter, by the iron river doors, there was a small window. It was the only window in the Intake Hall, a narrow slot of thick old glass high up in the wall, where the wall came up above the waterline, so that it looked out at the level of the river. Gray light came in through it, and the shadow of the water moving.
And under the window, at a little table of his own, with his back to the room, a man was sitting.
He was a big man. Not as big as Gage. Nobody was as big as Gage. But broad and heavy and slow, with a thick neck and thick red hands, in a brown coat that was too tight across the back. His hair was gray and cut very short, and the back of his neck under it was red. There was a decanter on the table in front of him. Cut glass, with a silver label on a chain round its neck. And a glass beside it. Small. The kind of glass you drink brandy from.
He was pouring.
She stopped where she was, at the end of the counter, and watched him.
He poured very slowly. The way a man pours when he is doing a thing he has done many times and wants to do properly. He lifted the decanter in his thick red hand, and tipped it, and the brandy came out of it in a thin bright stream, brown and gold in the gray light from the window. Into the glass. Until the glass was full. Right to the brim. And then he set the decanter down. And put the stopper back in it. And sat with his two thick red hands flat on the table, one on each side of the full glass, and looked at it.
He did not pick it up.
She waited.
He did not pick it up.
"Mister Briggs," said the Auditor.
He did not turn round. He did not start. He had known she was there. She could see that in the set of his heavy shoulders.
"Auditor," he said. His voice was surprisingly soft, for so big a man. Soft and slow, like a voice coming up out of a well. "It's not Thursday."
"No."
"Then you've come to look at my book."
"Yes."
He was quiet for a moment, looking at the glass.
"It's on the counter," he said. "The black one. The last page." And then, after a moment: "There's nothing on the last page. There won't be. Not this week."
She went to the counter.
The intake book was there, as he said. Black, very big, very heavy, bound in black leather gone brown at the corners from a hundred hands. She did not take off her glove. She did not lay her bare hand on it. She did not need the Sight for this. She opened it at the ribbon and turned the pages over with her gloved fingers, and read.
She had read these pages before. Sixty-odd Thursdays. The same columns. The same hand. Briggs's hand, large and round and careful, like a schoolboy's.
Friday. By the cart, from the Bourse chute (the Lady's house). Eleven. Treated. Received. Pen four.
Saturday. Pen four to the docks. Nine. To the mills, by the north road. Two.
Friday. By the cart, from the Bourse chute. Fourteen. Treated. Received. Pen six.
Saturday. Pen six to the docks. Ten. To the Choir, at the Spire, by request. Four.
By request.
She had read that before. She had written it in her own audit, on sixty-odd Thursdays. By request. Four. She had never once asked whose request. It was not her work. Her work was the figures, and the figures had always agreed.
She turned to the last page with writing on it.
Friday. By the cart, from the Bourse chute. Nine. Treated. Received. Pen two.
That was last Friday. Two days before the children had gone missing from the House of Rest. And under it, nothing. Clean ruled paper. The columns waiting.
"The cart won't come," said Briggs.
She looked up.
He had turned round on his chair at last. He was looking at her, across the length of the counter. He had a broad red face, heavy, with small pale eyes in it, and a great deal of loose skin under the eyes, gray, like a bloodhound's. He looked very tired.
"Not this Friday," he said. "Not next Friday. Not any Friday. The chute's gone. The Lady's gone. The cold room's gone, they say. The whole floor of the dome went down into it and there's nothing left but a hole full of fire." He spread his thick red hands. "And the House of Rest is empty. Did you know that? Since Sunday night. Somebody came in the night and took them all. Forty-seven. Out of Saint Bride's. And the gentleman from the Health—" He stopped.
"Is dead," said the Auditor.
Briggs looked at her.
"Is he."
"I think so. He was in the Fourth Tier rooms when the gallery burned. He did not come out."
Briggs was quiet for a long time.
Then he turned back round on his chair, to the table and the window and the full glass of brandy.
"I've been Keeper of Intake for nineteen years," he said. With his back to her. "Nineteen years this spring. Before that I was a porter here. Before that I was a boy on the boats. I've seen a lot come in through those doors." He nodded at the iron river doors, barred, beside him. "A lot. You'd not believe what. Lords' sons, some of them, when I was young. Soldiers, after the war. Thousands of soldiers. They brought them in by the boat-load after the war, and they sat in those pens just like that lot there, and we sent them out to the docks and the mills, and they worked till they dropped, and they never once complained." He touched the glass with one thick finger. Not lifting it. Just touching the rim. "Then the soldiers ran out. And then it was debtors. And then it was the Lower Docks. And then it was the House of Rest."
He was quiet.
"Children," he said.
The Auditor did not say anything.
"I didn't mind the soldiers," said Briggs. "Saints forgive me. I didn't. They'd been to the war. They'd seen things. They'd done things. Some of them were glad of it, I think, the quiet. Some of them asked for it." He shook his heavy head. "But the little ones. Off the Friday cart. With their hands gone gray and their eyes like that. Like windows." He looked at the pens. "They don't cry. That's the worst of it. A child ought to cry."
The Auditor looked at the pens too.
There were three children in pen two. She had not seen them at first. They were sitting together in a corner, on the straw, close, the way puppies sit. Two boys and a girl. The girl was perhaps eight. She was sitting with her gray hands open in her lap, looking at nothing, and her lips were moving. Very slightly. Over and over. As if she were saying something to herself. Or singing.
"That one hums," said Briggs, without looking round. "The girl. All day. The same four notes. I don't know where she got them."
The Auditor stood very still.
"I pour a glass every Friday," said Briggs. "When the cart comes. Before I go down and sign them in. I pour it out, full, like that. And I don't drink it."
"Why?"
"My wife." He almost smiled. "She asked me to stop. Fourteen years ago. She said, Tom, you come home from that place and you drink till you can't stand, and I won't have it. I won't have the children see it. So I stopped." He touched the rim of the glass again. "But I still pour it. Every Friday. I pour it, and I sit here, and I look at it. And I think, if I drink that, I won't care what comes in through those doors. Not for an hour. And then I don't drink it. And I go down. And I care." He was quiet. "That's the bargain. I've kept it fourteen years."
"It isn't Friday," said the Auditor.
"No," said Briggs. "It isn't."
He sat and looked at the full glass.
"There was a man here at the first bell," he said. "Before you. From upstairs. From the Treasurer's office. With a paper. He took the House of Rest book. The other one. The one I keep for the cart. Every page since the autumn." He did not turn round. "He said the Treasurer was very sorry for my trouble, and would I hand it over, it was wanted for an inquiry. And I handed it over. And he put it under his arm and went away up the stair, whistling." Briggs's thick red hands lay flat on the table on each side of the glass. "I've never been asked for that book in nineteen years. Not once. Not by anybody."
The Auditor wrote it down.
In her black book, in pencil, standing at the counter of the Intake Hall with Gage behind her and the gray light from the river window on the page. Tuesday after Midwinter. First bell. The Treasurer's office took the Intake Hall cart book (House of Rest), autumn to date. Briggs gave it. No receipt.
Then she looked up.
"Mister Briggs," she said. "The girl. In pen two. Who sent her?"
Briggs turned his heavy head and looked at the pen.
"She came in last Friday," he said. "On the cart. With the nine. From the chute. From the House of Rest before that, I suppose." He frowned. "No. Not the House of Rest. She wasn't on their list. She was a bulk lot. From the Bourse. Lot thirty-one. Saltgate, by the crate." He looked at the Auditor. "Why?"
The Auditor did not answer.
She did not know why. She only knew that the girl in the corner of pen two, with her gray hands open in her lap and her lips moving, was humming four notes. And that somewhere, very far back, in the long gray nothing before the morning of the ninth of the Thaw month, in a place she could not reach, something had turned over at the sound of them. The way a sleeper turns over at a sound in the night, and does not wake.
"Put her down in your book," she said. "As held. By the Fourth Desk. Under audit."
Briggs looked at her for a long moment.
"Is there a clause for that?"
"There is now."
He looked at her a moment longer. And then something moved in the heavy red bloodhound face. Not a smile. Something older than a smile.
He reached out and picked up the full glass of brandy.
The Auditor watched him. So did Gage.
He held it up. In his thick red fingers, in the gray light from the river window, full to the brim. The brandy shone in it, brown and gold. He looked at it for a long time.
Then he stood up, and turned, and walked to the iron river doors, and lifted the bar, and pulled one of the doors open a hand's width. And the cold came in, and the gray light, and the sound of the river. And Tom Briggs, Keeper of Intake of the Oblivion Bank, held his glass out through the gap in the iron door, over the black water, and tipped it, and poured it out. Slowly. All of it. In a thin bright stream, brown and gold, into the river. Where it lay for a moment on the gray surface, among the floating ash, and then was gone.
He pulled the door shut, and dropped the bar.
"Held by the Fourth Desk," he said. "Under audit." He went back to the counter, and pulled the black intake book toward him, and took up his pen. "What's her name?"
"I don't know," said the Auditor.
Briggs dipped his pen.
"Then I'll put the girl who hums," he said. "Till we find out."
The Treasurer
The Treasurer sent for her at the third bell.
A page brought the message. A boy of twelve in the Bank's dark blue, with brass buttons and a face scrubbed pink, who came up the six flights from the great hall at a run and stood in the doorway of the Fourth Desk with his chest going in and out and did not dare to come past Gage. The Treasurer's compliments, and would the Auditor step down to his rooms, at her convenience.
At her convenience meant now. Everybody knew that.
She went down.
The Treasurer's rooms were on the second floor, at the front of the Bank, overlooking Lantern Quay. You went to them along a wide carpeted corridor with tall windows on one side and portraits on the other. Portraits of Treasurers. Twenty or thirty of them, going back two hundred years, in heavy gilt frames, each one painted standing beside the same small table with the same small gold scale on it. Old men, mostly. Some young ones. All of them with the same look. As if they had just been told the price of something, and found it fair.
The last portrait in the row was empty. The frame was there, gilt and heavy, on the wall. But there was no painting in it. Only a square of dark red silk, stretched across the back of the frame, where the canvas should be.
She had asked Mister Harrow about that once. In her first year. He had coughed, and said that the Treasurer did not care to be painted. That was all.
There was a footman at the door. He opened it for her. Gage stopped outside, the way he always did at this door. He would stand in the corridor until she came out. He would not sit. There was no bench here for him, and he would not sit on the carpet.
She went in.
The room was warm. That was the first thing, always. After the Fourth Desk, which was never warm, and the Intake Hall, which was never anything but cold, it was like walking into a bath. A great fire burned in a white marble fireplace, so big you could have stood up in it, with a basket of sea-coal beside it and a brass fender polished like a mirror. The carpet was thick and soft and the color of red wine. There were books on the walls, from the floor to the ceiling, behind glass. There was a great desk of pale wood by the window, with nothing on it at all, not a pen, not a paper. And by the fire, two chairs.
The Treasurer was sitting in one of them.
"Elara," said Dorian Sterling. "Come and be warm."
He did not get up. He never got up. He smiled at her across the room, from his chair by the fire, and lifted one hand, and turned it over, palm up, toward the other chair. The way you show a guest to a seat.
She went and sat in it.
He looked as he always looked. As he had looked last night, in the dome, in his gray silk with the silver thread. He was in a dark coat this morning, very plain, very good. His silver hair was brushed back from his forehead, smooth as water. His eyes were very dark, almost black, and there were lines round them, fine lines, the kind that come from laughing. He had a gold watch chain across his waistcoat, with a small gold key hanging from it, and as he sat, his long fingers rested on the key, lightly, the way you rest your fingers on a thing you like to know is there.
He looked at her for a moment without saying anything.
Then he said, "You're filthy."
She looked down at herself.
She had not thought about it. She had put on clean gloves at the second bell, and that was all. Her dark green coat was gray with ash at the shoulders and black with soot at the hem. Her collar was stained where the tears had run down onto it. Her hair, which she had pinned flat for the masquerade at the eighth bell yesterday, had come down at one side, and she had not pinned it back. She had not looked in a glass since.
"I beg your pardon," she said.
"Don't. You look like a woman who has been in a fire. It suits you. It gives you color." He smiled. "You've never had any color, Elara. In six years. I've often wondered why."
She did not say anything.
The Treasurer leaned forward a little, and took a poker from the stand by the fender, and moved a piece of coal in the great fire, so that it fell and broke and flared up. He watched it. The firelight moved on his face.
"You were at the box," he said. "At the end."
"Yes."
"And you wrote it all down."
"Yes."
"Of course you did." He put the poker back. "And this morning, at the first bell, the Archon's secretary brought you a black case, and asked you to weigh it."
The Auditor sat very still.
She had not told anybody. Mister Harrow had not left the Fourth Desk. Gage did not speak. Lucian did not speak to anybody but the Archon.
"Yes," she said.
"And?"
"It is written in the ledger."
"I know it is." The Treasurer's voice was soft and pleasant and quite patient. "I'm asking you what you wrote."
She looked at the fire.
"Weighed and agreed," she said.
And Dorian Sterling smiled.
She had seen him smile many times. He smiled a great deal. He had a pleasant, easy, handsome smile, that went up into the fine lines round his eyes and made him look younger than he was, and kind. He smiled at Councillors and clerks and footmen and pages, all the same. He had smiled at her last night, in the dome, when he dropped his card into the box. A long night for you, Miss Elara.
This was not that smile.
This one was slower. It came up from somewhere further down. It did not go up into his eyes at all. It stayed in his mouth, and spread there, slowly, the way a stain spreads in cloth. And his long fingers closed, very gently, round the small gold key on his watch chain. And held it.
"Weighed and agreed," he said. "Well. Well, well."
He leaned back in his chair.
"Then the Archon has what he paid for," said the Treasurer. "Doesn't he. It says so. In the record of the Fourth Desk. Under clause eleven. Whose record shall stand." He looked at the fire. "Ten thousand crowns I offered for that case, Elara. Ten thousand. Out of my own purse. Do you know why?"
"No."
"So that he would have to put down a circle." He was still smiling. "He never names a sum. He doesn't need to. But he would not put down a circle for a thing nobody else wanted. That would be vulgar. He would only do it if somebody else had made it dear." His fingers turned the little gold key. "And now he has put down his circle, in front of every lord on the Hill. And his secretary has carried the case to the Fourth Desk and had it weighed. And the Auditor has written that it weighed what it ought. And he will put it in his cold vault, and not open it till the spring."
He turned his head and looked at her.
"And all the time," he said softly, "it will be sitting there. Being exactly what you wrote it was."
The Auditor felt the warmth of the fire on her face. It did not reach the rest of her.
He knew.
She did not know how. But he knew. He knew it was not the case. He had known, perhaps, before she did. Before Lucian did. Before the Lady opened the box.
And he was pleased.
"Sir," she said.
"Mm?"
"If the record is wrong—"
"The record is never wrong, Elara." He said it gently. As if he were telling a child that the sun comes up in the morning. "That is what the record is for. You know that better than anybody. The record is the one thing in this city that is never wrong. Because if it is wrong, then nothing is right, and the whole thing comes down. Like a dome." He smiled again, the first smile, the easy one. "So it isn't wrong. It says weighed and agreed. And so it was weighed, and so it agreed. And that is the end of it."
He let go of the gold key.
"Now," he said. "Something else. Two things. Then you can go and have a bath."
She waited.
"Poor Ambrose," said the Treasurer.
She looked at him.
"Pallister. You'll have heard. In the Fourth Tier rooms, they tell me. Locked in. Some careless fellow turned the key on him and went off with it in his pocket." He shook his silver head. "A terrible thing. A terrible way to go. I shall miss him at Council. He always brought sweets." He looked at the fire. "He did so like to be thanked, Ambrose. For the sweets. For the quiet. For the lamps. He used to wait, after he'd given you one. Just for a moment. To be thanked." The smile came and went. "I never once thanked him. I don't think he ever forgave me."
The Auditor said nothing.
She was thinking of the brass key that some careless fellow had turned, and gone away with in his pocket. And of a man in gray at the kitchen gate saying The Inquisitor's orders. And of a page from the Treasurer's office, at the first bell, going up the stair from the Intake Hall whistling, with the House of Rest book under his arm.
Some careless fellow.
"The office of Public Health will want a new Under-Secretary," said the Treasurer. "And the Health's books will want auditing, before he comes. All of them. Every page, since the autumn. I'd like the Fourth Desk to do it. Carefully. I'd like you to find—" He paused. "I'd like you to find that poor Ambrose was a little careless with his figures. A little greedy. That he kept back more than the tenth. That the Dispensary receipts did not go where they should. And that nobody else knew."
"Nobody else."
"Nobody else." He smiled. "Not the Treasurer's office, certainly. We sign what we are given. Seen and agreed. In green." His long fingers moved on the arm of his chair, very lightly, as if he were writing it. "You'll find it all in poor Ambrose's hand. I'm quite sure."
The Auditor looked at the fire for a long moment.
"And the second thing?" she said.
The Treasurer did not answer at once.
He got up.
She had never seen him do that. In six years. He got up out of his chair by the fire, easily, the way a younger man gets up, and went to the tall window behind his empty desk, and stood there with his hands behind his back, looking out at Lantern Quay. At the gray river. At the gray ash still falling, very fine now, over the lamps and the barges and the water.
"The Lady kept books," he said. With his back to her.
"Yes, sir."
"Not the Bourse books. The Bourse books are dull. The Bourse books say what was sold and for how much and to whom, and you have audited them every Midwinter for six years, and they have always agreed." He did not turn round. "I mean her own. Her own books. In her own hand, or somebody's. What she took. From whom. When. And for whom. Forty years of it. She never threw anything away, Vivienne. Not a jar. Not a name. Not a face." His voice was quite pleasant. "She'll have kept a book."
The Auditor did not say anything.
"Her rooms burned last night," said the Treasurer. "The old wing. The stone. It went last of all, they tell me. Toward the second bell. Long after the dome." He was quiet for a moment. "My people went through what was left at dawn. Very carefully. There was a bed. A chair. A great deal of ash. A stand with her hair on it, of all things, quite unburned." He almost laughed. "No books."
She waited.
"And no boy," said the Treasurer.
He turned round.
"She had a boy," he said. "A servant. A clever, quick, sharp little thing in gray, with ink on his cuff. Remy, they called him. He slept in her room. He knew all her doors. He knew all her keys." He looked at her. "He was seen on the great stair last night, at the first lamp, taking the Lady by the arm. And then not again."
A thin sharp-faced boy. Pulling her away, toward the great stair.
"I'd like him found," said Dorian Sterling. "Gently. Kindly. He's had a terrible night, poor child. He's lost his mistress, and his home. He'll be frightened. He'll be hungry. He'll be looking for somewhere to go." The smile came back. The first one. The easy, kind one. "And when he's found, I'd like the Fourth Desk to look after whatever he happens to be carrying. Under clause eleven. Until I can come and see it for myself."
He came back to the fire, and stood beside her chair, and looked down at her.
"You'll do that for me, Elara," he said. "Won't you."
It was not a question.
She looked up at him. At the dark eyes. At the fine lines round them. At the long fingers resting, again, lightly, on the small gold key on his chain.
She had worked for him for six years. He had chosen her for the Fourth Desk, Mister Harrow said, in the week after the ninth of the Thaw month, when she could not yet remember where the stairs went. She had never once asked herself what that meant. She asked herself now.
"Yes, sir," said the Auditor.
"Good girl," said the Treasurer.
And smiled.
