Chapter 92: The Daily List, Part 2 of 5: The Register
The Register
The register was very old in some places and very new in others.
It was not one book. Elara had always thought of it as one book, because it sat on the shelf in cupboard four as one, with one brown cover and one spine. But it was not. It had been taken apart and sewn back together so many times, with new pages put in and old ones taken out, that it was really a great many books, bound up inside one skin. Some of the pages were thick and creamy and soft at the edges, and written in faded brown ink in a hand full of loops. Some were thin and white and new, in a clerk's black. Some had been written on, and scraped clean with a knife, and written on again, so that if you held them up to the lamp you could see the old words underneath the new, like faces under water.
She had used it every Thursday for six years. She had never read it.
She read it now.
She started with the Corn Steps, because that was where Abel Fitch had his stall. The register had a page for the Corn Steps, though nobody lived on them; it listed the stalls, and who rented them, and what they paid to the Bank's own steward for the right to sit there. Stall seven. A. Fitch. Letter-writer. One penny the week. Paid. And then, beside it, in another hand, Paid. Paid. Paid, down the margin, all the way back for thirty years.
Then Tallow Row. A room above the rope-maker's.
She found Tallow Row. She found the rope-maker's. No. 9. Ropewalk and house. J. Tanner, rope-maker. Holdings: the ropewalk, the house, the stock. And then a line through all of it, in red. And under the line, very new, in a clerk's black, dated the autumn just gone:
Burned. House and walk a loss. J. Tanner removed to his daughter's, Pump Court. No lodgers listed.
Elara sat back.
She looked at the list. A room above the rope-maker's, Tallow Row.
There was no room above the rope-maker's. There was no rope-maker's. It had burned in the autumn, and the man who owned it had gone to live with his daughter, and the register said no lodgers.
So Abel Fitch did not live where the list said he lived.
She thought about it. Slowly. Carefully. The way she would have thought about a sum that did not add up. Abel Fitch might live somewhere else now. He probably did. He might have lived over the rope-maker's once, before the fire, and the Ministry's books had never been brought up to date. The Ministry's books were very bad. Everyone at the Bank knew that. It was a thing the clerks downstairs joked about. Ministry figures, they said, about anything that was wrong.
She could look for him. She could turn the pages of the register, all of them, every street, and look for Fitch until she found him. It might take an hour. It might take all night.
Or she could write what was true.
She picked up the pen. She held it over the long empty column next to One. Abel Fitch. For a long time.
Then she wrote:
No such room at the address given. The house burned in the autumn. No lodgers on the Bank's books. Returned to the Office for a true address.
She read it over. Every word of it was true. She had not looked for Abel Fitch anywhere else. That was true too. But it was not in the line.
She found her hand was shaking a little. She put the pen down and waited for it to stop.
"Is that all right?" she said. Out loud. Not to anybody.
Behind her, on his bench, Gage did not answer. But she heard the chalk. A small dry squeak on the slate. She turned round.
He had wiped the slate. For the first time in two days. He knows now was gone. And in its place, in big square white letters, there was one word.
TRUE.
Elara looked at it for a long time. Then she turned back to the register.
She went through the names one by one.
She did not look for mistakes. That was the thing she told herself, all night, and it was true. She did not look for mistakes. She only read. She read what the list said, and then she read what the register said, very carefully, every word, the way Mister Harrow had read his weather every morning for twenty years. And sometimes they were the same. And sometimes they were not.
Five. Oswin Pyle. Bookbinder. The Sign of the Bell, Paternoster Lane.
The register said: The Sign of the Bell. O. Pyle and Son, binders. Holdings: the shop and the stock. Lease from the Bank, held till Midsummer. And there was nothing wrong with it. It was all just as the list said. And the old books Oswin Pyle kept were in the shop, and the shop was the Bank's, held on a lease, so the Bank would take the books.
She looked at that for a long time.
Then she wrote, Found at the address given. Holdings: the stock is on the Bank's lease and is the Bank's. Because it was true.
She did not like it. She wrote it anyway.
Four. Name not known. Called Dutch. Harvester, not of the Ministry. Sells by the jar at the Fish Stairs. No fixed address.
She did not need the register for that one.
She sat and looked at it. She thought about the Charter, in its black box, in the cupboard behind her, with a boy asleep behind it. She did not need to get it out. She knew the words. She had read them so many times that she could see them when she shut her eyes.
For every soul that comes before the Bank, the Auditor shall write one line. And the line shall be set against the soul's name, in the book, so that the line and the name are one.
Against the soul's name.
She wrote: No name given. The Charter requires a line be set against a name. The Auditor cannot set a line against a name not known. Returned to the Office.
She read it. It was true. It was the truest thing she had written all day.
She went on.
At the eleventh bell she found that a man listed as a scribe of Thimble Lane was a woman, Margery, not Martin, according to the register and also according to the Bank's own Thursday list from two winters ago, when her husband had died owing. The name given does not match the register. Returned for correction. At the twelfth bell she found that the old priest of Saint Clement's was listed at the Clergy House, and the register said the Clergy House had been sold to the Duke of Marrow's steward the year before, and the priest now lodged in the bell tower. Not at the address given. Which was true. He was in the bell tower. She did not say so. The line did not ask.
She found that three of the children were listed with the wrong ages. One was listed twice.
She found that two of the street harvesters had died. In the register, in the black ink of the Bank's own clerks, in the last month. Taken by the river, said one. Found on the Fish Stairs, burned out, said the other. She wrote what the register said. Dead. The Bank's books, this month. And she sat for a while with her hands in her lap.
And then there was number forty-one.
Name not known. A woman, old, small. Gray hair cut short. Hands stained blue to the wrist. Writing upon both arms. Ink-Binder. Believed lately returned to the city. Sought at the Archon's own request.
She did not open the register. There was no name to look for.
She sat with the pen in her hand and looked at it for a long time.
She knew what she would write. She had known since she wrote the line for Dutch. It was the same line. It had to be the same line. If it was true for a man called Dutch who sold jars on the Fish Stairs, it was true for an old woman with blue hands. The Charter did not say except when the Archon asks. The Charter did not know who the Archon was. It was three hundred years older than him.
But she sat there with the pen for a long time anyway.
She thought about the long table in the Council room, under the painted ceiling like broken ice. The man at the head of it, in plain gray, with his gloved hands folded on the dark wood. His ordinary dry voice. We will call it a Cleansing. People like a clean house. And the way he had looked at her, down the length of the table, when she wrote a line about a fire. A long look. Not angry. Interested. The way you would look at a clock that had begun, for no reason you could see, to keep a different time.
He knows now.
She wrote it.
No name given. The Charter requires a line be set against a name. The Auditor cannot set a line against a name not known. Returned to the Office.
She blotted it, very carefully. She sat and looked at it while the ink went from wet and shining to dry and black.
Then she put the pen down and pressed both her hands flat on the green leather and sat there, quite still, in the lamplight, with the snow going past the windows and the blue lamps on the quay, until her hands had stopped shaking.
It took a long time.
She finished the list at the third bell of the morning. Forty-four lines. She counted them, twice. Then she tied the bundle up again in its gray tape, the way it had come, and set it in the middle of her desk, square, for the sergeant.
Then she went to cupboard eleven and opened the left-hand door very quietly and looked in.
Remy was asleep. On his blanket, behind the Charter box, curled up on his side like a dog, with his black book under his cheek and his gloved hands tucked in under his chin. His mouth was a little open. He looked about eleven.
She shut the door.
She went up the little stair at the back of the Fourth Desk to her rooms under the roof, and lay down on her bed in her clothes, and did not sleep.
A Line That Is True
Sergeant Marsh came at the first bell on Sunday, as he had said he would. Tull came with him, carrying the new case.
The Bank was closed on Sundays. It had always been closed on Sundays. The great doors on the square were shut and barred, and the porters were at home, and the long halls on the ground floor were dark and smelled of floor wax and cold. Only the night man was there, with his lamp and his keys, and he had to come all the way up the back stair to the third floor to let them in, puffing, with his nightcap still on under his hat, because nobody had told him.
Elara was at her desk. She had not slept. She had lain on her bed for two hours with her eyes open, watching the snow-light on the sloping ceiling over her head, and then she had got up and washed her face in cold water from the jug and put on a clean collar and come down.
Gage was on his bench. Gage was always on his bench. She did not know when he slept, or if.
The sergeant came down the room. He stopped at her desk. He looked at the bundle tied up square in its gray tape, and then at her.
"Done, Auditor?"
"Done."
"All forty-four."
"All forty-four."
He picked up the bundle. He did not put it in the case at once. He untied the tape, there and then, standing at her desk, and opened it, and began to read.
She had not expected that. She sat with her hands flat on the green leather and watched him do it.
He read slowly. He was not a quick reader. His lips moved, a little, under the short gray beard. He went down the first page with one thick finger, name by name, line by line. Found at the address given. Nothing. Dead. The Bank's books, this month. Nothing. No such room at the address given. Returned to the Office for a true address.
His finger stopped.
He read it again. Then he looked at her. Then he went on.
He went down the whole first page. Then the second. She watched his finger stop, and go on, and stop. Eleven times. She had counted them herself in the night. Eleven lines that ended with the word Returned.
And then his finger came to number forty-one.
It stopped there for a long time.
Elara watched him read it. She watched his red face go, not white, but a kind of mottled color, red and pale in patches, like a man who has been out too long in the cold. She watched his lips move. No name given. The Charter requires.
He looked up at her.
"Auditor," he said. His voice had changed. It had gone lower.
"Sergeant."
"This one." He put his finger on it. "This is the Archon's own."
"Yes. It says so."
"You can't send back the Archon's own."
"I haven't sent back the Archon's own," said Elara. "I've sent back a line without a name. The Charter doesn't allow it." She kept her voice very even. The voice of the Auditor. The one that was not hers, or was the only one that was. "If the Office sends me a name, I'll write a line against it. The same day. I'll write it within the hour."
"There is no name. That's the whole—" He stopped. He looked at the line again. "Nobody knows her name. That's why it's name not known."
"Then nobody can write a line for her," said Elara. "Not me. Not anyone. Not under the Charter."
The sergeant stared at her.
Behind him, Tull had gone very still, with the new case held in front of his chest. His eyes were very round.
"The Order of Need," said the sergeant slowly, "overrides—"
"The Order of Need overrides a great many things," said Elara. "I read it last night. Mister Harrow lent me his copy. Clause four gives me one day instead of a week. Clause nine says the Bank shall hold what is found. Clause twelve says nothing shall stand in the way of the work." She paused. "There's nothing in it about the Charter. Not one word. I looked."
That was true. She had looked. She had read the Order of Need in the night, between the names, by lamplight, in Mister Harrow's copy, which he had left on the corner of his desk, open, when he went home. Which he never did. Mister Harrow never left anything on his desk.
"Then that's a hole in it," said the sergeant.
"Then that's a hole in it," said Elara.
They looked at each other.
She saw him think about it. She saw him think about going back down the back stair with the bundle, and across the square in the snow to the Ministry, and up to the Cleansing Office, and standing in front of whoever stood over him, and saying: The Auditor sent back the Archon's own. She says there's a hole in the Order. She saw him think about what that man would say. And the man over that man.
She saw him decide that it was not his hole.
He folded the bundle up again. He tied the gray tape. He put it in the case, and Tull locked it, with fingers that did not quite work.
Then Tull opened the new case. And took out a new bundle, thicker than the first. And laid it on her desk. And the receipt on top.
Elara unfolded the receipt.
Fifty-one.
"Fifty-one," said the sergeant, "and the eleven back, corrected, tomorrow." He paused. "Or the next day. The Office is short of clerks."
"I'm sure it is."
He looked at her for a moment. Something in his red face had changed. She did not know what. It was not friendly. But it was not what it had been yesterday either.
"My mother kept a book," he said suddenly.
Elara did not answer. She did not know what to say.
"Old book," said the sergeant. "Births and deaths. All her family, going back. Every one, in her own hand, and her mother's before her." He was not looking at her now. He was looking at the bundle on the desk. "She'd have been on one of these. If she was still alive." He breathed in, through his nose. "Keeps old books not on the list."
He stood there a moment longer.
Then he said, "Good morning, Auditor," and turned, and went.
Tull went after him. At the way in, the young man looked back at her, once, quickly. As if he wanted to see what she looked like. As if he had never really looked before.
Then they were gone.
Elara sat with her hands on the new bundle.
She found that she was not shaking this time. She found that she felt very strange. Very light. Light the way you feel when you have been carrying something heavy up a long stair and you put it down at the top and stand up straight, and your arms want to float.
This is what he does.
She had thought that, a week ago, about a tired man in a gray coat. A man she had met four times, on the great stair and in the Council room and once at the door of the Intake Hall, and who had always said Good morning, Auditor, in exactly the same way, and walked on. She had thought it the day she wrote a line for Cressida Thorne's house that was true, and was not the whole of the truth, and kept a boy's family papers in his own pocket where they belonged.
She thought it again now.
The line had to be true. That was the law. The oldest law in the Bank, older than the Archon, older than the Ministry. And the Bank had to do what the line said. And everybody had always thought, for three hundred years, that this meant the Auditor had no choice. That the line was a thing you found, like a stone in the road. Not a thing you chose.
But there was more than one true thing. There was always more than one. There were a great many true things about every soul who came before the Bank, more than you could write in one line if you wrote for a year. And the Auditor could only write one.
So the Auditor chose which.
She had always chosen. Every Thursday. For six years. She had just never known it, because she had always chosen the thing that the Bank wanted, the first thing, the thing that was easiest to find. And now she was choosing something else.
It was still true. It was all still true. Every line.
She sat at her desk and looked at the snow and felt it go all the way through her, cold and clean and frightening, the way the river looks on a winter morning when you are standing on a bridge over it.
Then she untied the gray tape on the new bundle, and pulled the register toward her, and began again.
The Treasurer's Rose
The Treasurer came on Monday, at the fourth bell of the afternoon, which was the hour when the light began to go.
Nobody told her he was coming. Nobody ever told her when he was coming. In six years Elara had seen Dorian Sterling in the Fourth Desk perhaps ten times, and every time he had simply been there, standing in the way in, as if he had grown there, with the lamplight on his silver hair.
He was standing there now.
She did not know how long he had been there. She had been reading. The Monday list had come at the first bell, fifty-eight names, and the eleven from Saturday had come back with it, corrected, in a different clerk's hand. Abel Fitch had a new address. A room behind the Corn Exchange, at the sign of the Wheatsheaf. Margery the scribe had her right name. The priest was found in his bell tower. And Dutch was still name not known, with a new line under it in heavy black capitals, as if somebody had pressed very hard: CALLED DUTCH. THIS IS THE NAME HE IS KNOWN BY.
She had been sitting with that for some time. Thinking.
And number forty-one was not there at all. Not with a name. Not without one. It had simply gone from the list, as if it had never been.
She looked up, and he was in the way in.
Penn had seen him first. Penn had gone white and then red and then stood up so fast that her chair went over backward with a crash, and she had stood there beside it with her hands at her sides like a soldier. Mister Harrow had stood up too, slowly, and put down his pencil, and cleared his throat.
Gage had not stood up.
Gage sat on his bench by the way in with his big hands on his knees and his slate on its cord against his chest, and the Treasurer stood not three feet away from him, and Gage did not move at all. He did not even turn his head.
Sterling looked down at the slate.
TRUE.
He looked at it for quite a long time. Then he smiled. A small smile, very pleasant, that did not go anywhere near his eyes.
"Good afternoon, Gage," he said.
Gage did not answer. Gage never answered anybody. But Elara saw his hands, on his knees. They had closed. Only a little. Into the beginnings of fists.
Sterling came down the room.
He was tall, and he walked well, the way men walk who have been taught to walk by somebody who was paid for it. He wore a dark coat, very plain, very good, and a waistcoat of dove-gray silk with a fine silver thread in it, and a white rose in his buttonhole. He always wore a white rose. In winter too. Elara had wondered, once, where he got them in winter, and then she had stopped wondering, because she had understood that a man like Sterling could get anything he liked in any month of the year, and that this was the point of the rose.
His hair was silver. All of it. Thick and soft and silver, brushed back from a long smooth face. He had told someone, once, in her hearing, that it had gone silver before he was forty, and that he did not mind, because it saved a great deal of time with the barber. People had laughed.
He stopped at her desk.
"Auditor."
"Treasurer." She stood up. She had to. "I didn't know you were coming."
"No one ever does." He smiled. "It is one of the few pleasures left to a man in my position." He looked down at her desk. At the bundle. At the register open beside it. "Please. Sit. I won't keep you long. I know how busy you are." He paused. "Everyone knows how busy you are."
She sat.
He did not. He stood by the desk, with one gloved hand resting lightly on the corner of the green leather, and looked down at her, and at the list, and at the snow beyond the window. He seemed in no hurry.
"I had a visit this morning," he said, "from the Cleansing Office. A very worried man. One of the under-secretaries. He had your Saturday lines with him. He had come to ask me, as your Treasurer, whether the Auditor was quite well."
"I'm very well."
"So I told him." Sterling smiled again. "I said the Auditor of the Fourth Desk has not been ill a single day in six years. I said I would be very surprised to hear that she had begun now." He turned his head and looked at her. His eyes were a pale clear brown, almost gold. Like honey held up to a lamp. "He showed me the lines. All of them. I read them. I read them twice, in fact. And do you know what I found?"
Elara did not answer.
"I found that every one of them was true," said Sterling. "Every single line. I sent a boy down to the register room to check them. Eleven of them. The rope-maker's house is burned. The priest has moved. The scribe is a woman. The Charter does require a name." He smiled. "Every word. True as a plumb line. I told the under-secretary so. He was not comforted."
"I'm sorry to hear it."
"Are you?"
She met his eyes.
"No," she said.
Sterling laughed.
It was a real laugh. Short, and surprised, and quite warm. It went down the long room and Penn jumped at it. He put his hand over his mouth, lightly, with his glove, as if he had been caught out.
"No," he said. "No, of course not. Forgive me." He took his hand away. "I should tell you, Auditor, that I was not the only one who read them. The under-secretary had already been upstairs. To the Archon's own rooms. And the Archon read them too."
Elara felt her hands go cold on the green leather.
"He read number forty-one," said Sterling. "Out loud. To his secretary. The jumpy one, with the hair. No name given. The Charter requires a line be set against a name. And then he laughed." Sterling paused. "Not like me. Quite softly. To himself. And he said, She is learning."
He let that sit.
"He seemed pleased," said Sterling. "I thought you would want to know."
Elara did not say anything at all.
She is learning.
She thought about the pale gray gloves, folded on the dark wood. She thought about the long look down the table. Interested. The way you look at a clock that has begun to keep a different time. And she understood, all at once, sitting at her own desk in her own room with the snow outside, that he had not been angry. Not at the Council. Not now. He had not been angry at all.
He had been watching to see what she would do.
"Number forty-one is gone from today's list," she said. Her voice came out quite steady. That surprised her.
"Yes. I noticed that."
"Why?"
"Because it is no longer the Office's business," said Sterling. "The Archon has given it to someone else. Someone who does not need a name to find a person. Or a line to hold her." He took his hand off the desk. "A man you know. A man who says good morning."
Elara went very still.
"He came back this morning," said Sterling. "From the north. Very tired, I'm told. Very cold. With a girl. And the Archon received him in the garden room, before breakfast, and gave him tea, and gave him the Cleansing." He smiled. "All of it. To run himself. Beginning, I believe, with the old woman with blue hands."
He watched her face while he said it. He watched it the way she watched the register. Looking for what did not add up.
She gave him nothing. She did not know how. It was simply that there was nothing in her face to give. It had all gone somewhere deep, and cold, and still.
Sterling seemed satisfied, or disappointed. She could not tell which.
"Well," he said. "I won't keep you."
He reached up to his buttonhole and took out the white rose. He looked at it for a moment, turning it in his gloved fingers. Then he laid it on her desk. On the green leather. Beside the list.
"Something for the desk," he said. "It's so very gray up here."
Then he turned and walked away down the long room. Past Mister Harrow, standing at his desk. Past Penn, standing by her fallen chair. Past Gage on his bench, whose hands were fists now, closed and still on his knees.
At the way in, he stopped and looked back.
"Do go on learning, Auditor," he said. "I find I'm curious to see where it ends."
And he went.
Nobody moved for a long time.
Then Penn picked up her chair, very carefully, and set it on its legs, and sat down on it, and burst into tears. Quite quietly. With her face in her hands.
Mister Harrow sat down too. He cleared his throat. He picked up his pencil. He looked at it.
Elara looked at the rose.
It lay on the green leather beside the list of fifty-eight names. White. Perfect. Not a single brown edge on any petal. In the middle of winter. She could smell it. A cold sweet smell, like snow on a garden.
She did not touch it.
She sat and looked at it until the lamps on the quay came on, blue, and the room went dark around the circle of her own lamp. And then she took a sheet of blotting paper, and folded it over the rose, without touching it, and picked it up in the paper, and got up and walked to the window.
She did not throw it out. She wanted to. She stood at the window with the rose in the folded paper in both hands and looked down at the river, black under the snow, and wanted to.
She put it in the drawer of her desk instead. At the back. Under the spare nibs.
Then she sat down and went on with the list.
