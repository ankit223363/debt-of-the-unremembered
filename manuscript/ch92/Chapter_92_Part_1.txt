Chapter 92: The Daily List, Part 1 of 5: The Case at First Bell
The Case at First Bell
The list came on Saturday.
It had never come on a Saturday before. In six years it had come on a Thursday, every Thursday, at the third bell of the afternoon, carried up the back stair of the Bank by a thin boy from the Ministry in a gray coat with a gray leather case under his arm. Elara had known the sound of his feet on the stair. She had known the way he knocked, twice, quick, like a woodpecker. She had known the smell of the case when he opened it: old leather and cold air and the faint sour smell of Ministry ink, which was made with oak galls and something else, something that made it go brown in a year.
On Saturday, at the first bell of the morning, before it was even properly light, the knock came again.
It was not the boy.
Elara was at her desk already. She was always at her desk before the first bell. She liked the Fourth Desk best at that hour, when it was still dark outside the three tall windows and the lamps were lit, and the long narrow room was quiet, and nobody else had come in yet but Gage. She liked to sit with her hands flat on the green leather top of her desk and look out at the river. At the blue Ash lamps along the quay. At the snow coming down.
It was coming down now. It had been coming down for two days, since the Council. Big soft flakes, slow, so many of them that the far side of the river had gone gray, and the lamps on Lantern Bridge were only blurs of blue light hanging in the white, one behind the other, like a string of beads.
She heard the feet on the back stair and knew at once that they were wrong.
Too heavy. Too many. Two people, not one, and neither of them was a boy. And when the knock came, it was not quick. It was three slow knocks, evenly spaced, with the flat of a hand.
Behind her, on his bench by the way in, Gage stood up.
She heard him do it. She heard the bench creak, and his big feet on the boards. She did not turn round. She said, "Come in," in her ordinary voice, the voice of the Auditor of the Fourth Desk, and the door opened.
Two men in gray came in. Ministry gray. Not the soft gray of the Bank, which was almost blue, but the hard flat gray of the coats on Lantern Bridge. One of them was older, with a square red face and a short gray beard cut very close. The other was young, and thin, and carried the case. It was not the old leather case. It was new. Black. With brass corners, and a brass lock, and on the lid, stamped in silver into the leather, two words.
THE CLEANSING.
The older man stopped in the middle of the room. He looked round at it. At the black cupboards along the inner wall, each with its brass number. At the three desks, two empty. At the lamps. At the windows and the snow. At Gage, standing by the way in with his slate on its cord against his chest, and the three words still on it in chalk, where he had written them two nights ago and never wiped them off.
He knows now.
The older man looked at the slate for a moment. Then he looked away from it, as if it were nothing. A dumb man's scribble.
He came to Elara's desk and stopped in front of it.
"Auditor," he said.
"Good morning," said Elara.
"Sergeant Marsh, of the Cleansing Office. Ministry of Memory." He did not bow. He did not take his cap off. "From today, the lists come daily. Every morning, first bell, by my hand or by Tull's." He nodded at the young man. "Sundays included. The law of need does not keep Sundays."
"I see," said Elara.
"The Archon's own order."
"I see."
The young man, Tull, put the black case on her desk. He took a key out of his waistcoat on a chain and unlocked it. It opened with a click. Inside there was a bundle of papers tied with gray tape. Much thicker than the Thursday bundles had been. Much thicker.
He took it out and laid it on the green leather in front of her. Then he took out a second paper, single, folded, and laid it on top.
"Receipt," he said. His voice was high and nervous. "For the Auditor's hand. Please."
Elara unfolded it.
It was a printed form. Received of the Cleansing Office, this day, one list of names, being the persons sought under the Order of Need, for the Bank's audit and holding. And a line for the date. And a line for the number of names. And a line for her signature. Someone had already written the date, in Ministry brown. And the number.
Forty-four.
She looked at the number for a while.
The Thursday lists had been thirty names, or near it. Twenty-eight. Thirty-one. Once, in a bad week in the autumn, thirty-six. A week's worth of the city's dead and debtors and burned-out harvesters and people who had sold their last good memory to the Bank and then died owing. A week's worth.
This was one day.
"Forty-four," she said.
"Yes, Auditor."
"In one day."
"The first day, Auditor." Sergeant Marsh's red face did not change. "The Office expects more. As the work goes on. There are a great many on the old books who were never brought in. The Order of Need is a chance to tidy up."
Tidy up.
Elara picked up her pen. She dipped it in the black ink, the Bank's ink, which did not go brown. She signed the receipt. E., and the long flat line after it that was all she ever wrote, because it was all the name she had. She blotted it. She folded it and held it out.
Tull took it and put it in the case and locked the case.
"The Office will want the lines back," said the sergeant, "by the first bell tomorrow. With the list."
Elara looked up at him.
"The Thursday lists were a week," she said. "I had a week for each line. The Charter gives the Auditor a week."
"The Order of Need gives the Auditor a day." He said it without any feeling at all. As if he had been told to say it and had learned it on the stair. "Clause four. You'll find it in the Order. The Treasurer has a copy."
"I'm sure he does."
Something moved in the sergeant's face. Very small. Then it was gone.
"Good morning, Auditor," he said.
He turned and went to the way in. Tull went after him, with the case held against his chest in both arms like a baby. As they went past Gage, the sergeant looked at the slate again. Only for a moment. And Elara, watching him, saw him read it. She saw his lips move. He knows now.
And she saw him not understand it.
Then they were gone. Their feet went away down the back stair, heavy and even, and a door banged somewhere below, and it was quiet again in the Fourth Desk. Only the lamps hissing. Only the snow, which made no sound at all, going past the windows.
Elara sat and looked at the bundle on her desk.
After a while, Gage came and stood beside her. She felt him there. Big and quiet, at her shoulder. Like a wall at her back.
"Forty-four," she said, to him. To the room. "In a day."
Gage did not move.
She untied the gray tape. It came apart under her fingers. The bundle opened like a book.
The first sheet was a printed heading. THE CLEANSING. Persons sought under the Order of Need. First day. And under it the date, and a seal in gray wax: an open eye. The Ministry's mark.
The second sheet was names.
She began to read.
Forty-Four Names
The names were written in a clerk's hand, in Ministry brown, in two columns down the page. A number. A name. A trade. An address. And then, in a third column, a space. A long empty space, ruled off with a thin line, waiting.
That was where the line went.
The Auditor's line. Her line.
It was the oldest thing about the Fourth Desk, older than the cupboards or the desks or the Bank itself. Elara had read it in the Charter, in the black box with the silver corners in cupboard eleven, on her first morning, in the handwriting of somebody who had been dead for three hundred years. For every soul that comes before the Bank, the Auditor shall write one line, and the line shall be true, and the Bank shall do as the line says.
One line. True. And the Bank would do as it said.
If the line said Debt: nil, the Bank let you go. If it said Holdings: the house and its contents, the Bank took the house. If it said Held, the Bank held you. In the Intake Hall, six floors down, in a pen, for as long as the line stood. If it said Weighed and emptied, that was what happened. There was no other way for it to go. The line was not a wish. It was not a judgment. It was a fact, written down, and the Bank was built on facts the way a house is built on stone.
And it had to be true.
Elara had never written a line that was not true. Not once, in six years, in all those Thursdays. She did not know what would happen if she did. Nobody did. The Charter did not say. It only said the line shall be true, the way you would say water is wet. As if there were no other kind of line that could be written.
She had begun to wonder, lately, whether that was because there wasn't.
She read.
One. Abel Fitch. Letter-writer. Unlisted. A stall under the Corn Steps, and a room above the rope-maker's, Tallow Row.
She knew the Corn Steps. She had seen the letter-writers there from a Bank carriage, a row of old men at little folding tables with ink pots and quills, writing letters for people who could not write their own. Love letters. Letters home. Letters to the Ministry. A penny a page.
Two. Marjory Kell. Needlewoman. Suspected of ink upon the skin. The Dye-Works, the third arch from the river.
Three. Thomas Kell. Her son. Aged fourteen. Same address. Suspected of the same.
Four. Name not known. Called Dutch. Harvester, not of the Ministry. Sells by the jar at the Fish Stairs. No fixed address.
Five. Oswin Pyle. Bookbinder. Keeps old books not on the list. The Sign of the Bell, Paternoster Lane.
She read them all. Slowly. Every one.
There were scribes. Six of them. Men and women who wrote things down for money without a Ministry license, which she had not known you needed. There were Ink-Binders, or people the Ministry thought might be: needlewomen, sailors with tattoos, a man who drew pictures on the skin of dock porters for a shilling. Eleven of those. There were harvesters. Street harvesters, the kind who bought and sold memory by the jar in back rooms and on stairs, and had never been inside the Ministry except to be fined. Nine. There were people who kept old books. A schoolteacher. Two booksellers. A retired clerk of the Long Office. An old priest of Saint Clement's, who kept the parish books of births and deaths going back two hundred years, and would not give them up.
There were children. Five of them. Listed after their mothers and fathers, like Thomas Kell. Her son. Aged fourteen.
And at the bottom of the second page, number forty-one, there was a line without a name.
Forty-one. Name not known. A woman, old, small. Gray hair cut short. Hands stained blue to the wrist. Writing upon both arms. Ink-Binder. Believed lately returned to the city. Believed to have kept a shop under the Dye-Works arches, at the old indigo vats. Sought at the Archon's own request.
Elara read it twice.
She did not know who it was. She had never heard of a woman with blue hands. But she sat and looked at those words for a long time. Sought at the Archon's own request. None of the others had that. Not one, in forty-four. They had been sought by the Cleansing Office, or under the Order of Need, or on information. Only this one had his name on it.
She found she was holding her pen very tightly. She put it down.
The door at the end of the room opened, and Penn came in.
Penn was seventeen, and pink, and always a little out of breath, as if she had run up the stairs, which she usually had. She had a round face and fair hair that would not stay in its pins, and a dark gray Bank coat that was too big for her in the shoulders. She sat at the desk nearest the way in. She copied things. She copied them very well, in a small round careful hand, and she was frightened of everything. Of the cupboards. Of the clerks downstairs. Of Gage. Of Elara, a little, though she tried not to show it.
She stopped just inside the way in and looked at the bundle on Elara's desk.
"Is that the list?" she said. "On a Saturday?"
"It's the list," said Elara. "It will come every day now. Sundays too."
Penn's pink face went pinker. "Every day?"
"Every day."
"But how will we—" Penn stopped. She looked at the thickness of the bundle. "How many?"
"Forty-four."
Penn did not say anything. She went to her desk and sat down at it and took her gloves off, and put them on again, and took them off. Then she took out her pens and laid them in a row, very straight, the way she did every morning, and looked at them.
Mister Harrow came in a little after the second bell.
He came in the way he always came in. Slowly. Without a sound. A small neat gray man in a small neat gray coat, with a black umbrella rolled very tight under his arm, which he hung on its own peg by the way in, as he had every morning for more than twenty years. He never opened it indoors. Elara had never seen him open it anywhere. She had once asked Penn whether he opened it outdoors, in the rain, and Penn had looked frightened and said she didn't know, she had never seen Mister Harrow outdoors.
He took off his hat. He hung it on the peg over the umbrella. He came down the room to his desk, in the middle, between Penn's and Elara's. And before he sat down, he went to the window nearest his desk and looked out at the river for a long moment, the way he did every morning. Then he took a small card out of his waistcoat pocket and a pencil, and wrote one word on it, and put it on the corner of his desk, face up.
Elara did not need to look. He had written the same word for three days.
Snow.
Then he sat down and looked across at her desk. At the bundle.
"Ah," he said quietly.
"Forty-four," said Elara. "Daily. The lines are wanted back by the first bell tomorrow."
Mister Harrow cleared his throat. It was a small dry sound. He did it when he was going to say something, and also when he had decided not to.
He did not say anything.
He got up again. He went to cupboard four, behind him, and took out his key and unlocked it, and took down a big book with a brown cover. He brought it to Elara's desk and put it down beside the bundle without a word. Then he went back to his own desk and sat down and began to sharpen a pencil.
Elara looked at the book.
It was the street register. The Bank's own. Every house in the city, every street, every lodging, with the names of the people the Bank knew to be living there, and what they owed, and what they owned, if anything. Kept up by the clerks on the first floor and copied up to the Fourth Desk once a quarter. She used it for the lines. Everybody used it for the lines. You could not write a true line about a person without knowing what the Bank knew.
She had not asked for it.
She looked across at Mister Harrow. He was sharpening his pencil with great care, turning it against the little knife, watching the curl of wood come off. He did not look up.
"Thank you, Mister Harrow," she said.
He cleared his throat.
Elara opened the register and turned to the C's. Corn Steps. And began.
Cupboard Eleven
She did not finish that day.
She did twenty-two. Half. She did them slowly, one at a time, with the register open beside her and the list in front of her, reading each name and then turning to its street and its house and reading what the Bank knew. And then sitting for a while, with the pen in her hand, before she wrote anything at all.
Penn copied the ones she finished into the fair book. Mister Harrow did whatever Mister Harrow did, which was mostly sit very still at his desk and turn the pages of something and clear his throat. The light outside the windows went from gray to white to gray again. The snow did not stop. The lamps on the quay came on blue at the fourth bell of the afternoon, though it was nowhere near dark, because the sky was so heavy.
At the sixth bell Penn went home. At the seventh, Mister Harrow stood up and put on his hat and took his umbrella off its peg and tucked it under his arm, and stopped by Elara's desk.
"Goodnight, Auditor," he said.
"Goodnight, Mister Harrow."
He stood there a moment longer. Then he said, looking at the window and not at her, "It is very hard to read a register by lamplight. The figures run together. One finds mistakes in it. In the register. That one would not find by day."
Elara looked up at him.
He cleared his throat, and went out.
She sat and thought about that for a long time.
Then, when the building had gone quiet, and she had heard the last of the clerks go down the great stair, and the porters bolt the front doors, and the night man start his first round of the ground floor with his lamp and his keys, she got up and went to cupboard eleven.
It was the tallest of the cupboards, at the far end of the inner wall, near the windows. It had two doors instead of one, black, with brass handles, and a lock that was heavier than the others, and the number 11 on a brass plate at the top. It was where the Charter was kept. And the old Treasurers' letters, in bundles, and the old seals of the Bank laid out on red velvet in a flat drawer.
She unlocked it. She opened the left-hand door. Only the left.
Inside, it was dark, and smelled of old paper and velvet and dust. The black box with the silver corners sat on the middle shelf, where it always sat. And behind it, in the deep part of the cupboard, where the shelves stopped and there was a space as wide as a man's shoulders and as tall as a man sitting down, there was a folded blanket. And on the blanket, sitting with his back against the wall of the cupboard and his knees drawn up, there was a boy.
Remy Castellan looked up at her and grinned.
"Evening, Auditor."
"Good evening, Remy."
"Is it? Good?" He unfolded himself, carefully, and came out past the Charter box on his hands and knees, and stood up in the lamplight and stretched until his back cracked. He was twenty, and thin, with a quick sharp face and black hair that needed cutting and eyes that were always moving. He had been living in the cupboard for nineteen days. Since the night of the Bourse, when the Bourse burned and he ran. He came out only at night, when the building was empty. He walked up and down the Fourth Desk in his stockings to get the stiffness out of his legs. He used the porters' privy on the back stair. He washed at the pump in the yard, in the dark, in the snow, and came back blue and shaking and laughing about it.
He wore gloves all the time, even in the cupboard. He took the left one off now to scratch his ear, and Elara saw the mark on the back of his hand. A V. Burned in, old and white and raised, the size of a thumbnail.
Vivienne's mark. She had put it on him when he was four, the year she bought him, and then never put it on anybody again, because she said it was common.
Vivienne was dead.
"I heard them come," said Remy. "This morning. Through the door." He pulled the glove back on. "Two of them. Heavy. That wasn't the boy."
"No."
"So what was it?"
Elara went back to her desk. She sat down. She put her hand flat on the bundle of names.
"The list," she said. "It comes every day now. Forty-four today. More tomorrow, they said."
Remy did not say anything for a moment. Then he came and stood at the corner of her desk and looked down at it. At the brown writing. At the long empty column where the lines went, half filled now in her black.
"Can I see?"
She hesitated. Then she turned the pages round toward him.
He read them standing up, with his gloved hands flat on the desk on either side of the paper and his head bent. He read quickly. He was a quick reader. He had learned in the back rooms of Vivienne's house, from her ledgers, before he could do much else. Elara watched his face while he read. It did not move much. But at some of the names his mouth went tight at one corner.
"Dutch," he said. "Number four. I know Dutch. He sold me my first jar. When I was nine. It was a sailor's, a storm at sea, I didn't even like it, I was sick for a day." He turned a page. "Pyle. The Bell, in Paternoster Lane. He bound Vivienne's books. The seven. The black ones." He turned another. "The priest at Saint Clement's. He gave me soup once. Him and his soup." He was quiet. "Kell. Marjory Kell. And her boy."
"You know them too?"
"Everybody under the arches knows Marjory. She does sailors' arms. Anchors and mermaids. And other things, under the sleeve, if you know to ask." He looked up at her. "Tom's fourteen. He's simple, a bit. He holds the lamp for her while she works."
Elara did not say anything.
Remy turned the last page. And stopped.
She watched him read number forty-one. She watched him read it twice, the way she had.
"Saints," he said softly.
"You know who that is."
"Everybody who's ever been anybody in this city knows who that is." He straightened up. "Or knows of her. Blue hands. Vespera. That's Vespera." He said the name the way you would say the name of a storm. "The best needle in the city. Vivienne sent people to her. The ones who needed something put away so deep that no harvester would ever find it. Vivienne used to say she was the only person in Veridia she was afraid of." He shook his head. "She went away. Years ago. Nobody knew where."
"It says she's come back."
"It says believed."
"It says sought at the Archon's own request."
Remy did not answer that. He looked at the words for a long time.
Then he went back to the cupboard, and crawled in behind the Charter box, and came out again with something under his arm. A book. A plain black book, a cheap one, the kind that clerks bought by the dozen, with a cracked spine and the corners worn soft. His own. Not one of Vivienne's seven. Those were somewhere else, in the cupboard, wrapped in oilcloth; he had never shown her where. This was his.
He sat down on the floor by her desk with his back against the leg of it and opened the book on his knees. Elara knew what was in it. Names. Pages and pages of names, in his quick sloping hand, of everybody he had ever known who was taken, or emptied, or gone. And beside the ones who were dead, a little flower. Five petals, drawn with a single stroke each. Because nobody else will give them one.
Two nights ago he had started a new page. She had seen him do it. At the top, in capitals, he had written THE CLEANSING. And nothing underneath.
Now he took a stub of pencil out of his sleeve and began to copy the names off her list onto it. One by one. Abel Fitch. Marjory Kell. Thomas Kell. Dutch.
Elara watched him.
"What are you going to do with them?" she said.
He did not look up. "Write them down."
"And then?"
"And then they're written down."
"Remy."
He stopped writing.
"I could warn them," he said. Very quietly. "Some of them. Dutch. Marjory. I know where they sleep. I could go out tonight, over the yard wall, the way I go to the pump, and I could be at the arches by the ninth bell, and back before light, and nobody would ever—"
"No."
He looked up at her. His face had gone hard.
"No?"
"You're on a list too," said Elara. "You know you are. You were on the Thursday list before you ever came here. Castellan, Remy. Lately of Madame Vivienne's household. Sought." She looked at him. "If they take you, they take your book. And Vivienne's seven. And then they know about this cupboard. And then they know about me."
"So we do nothing."
"I didn't say that."
"Then what?" He shut the book on his finger. "You sit up here at your desk, with your green leather and your nice pen, and you write Weighed and emptied forty-four times a day, in your nice true black ink, and the Bank does what it says. And I sit in a cupboard and draw flowers." His voice had gone tight and fast. "What are you for, Auditor? If not that? What are you for?"
Elara did not answer at once.
She looked down at the list. At the twenty-two lines she had written. At the twenty-two she had not.
"I'm for the lines," she said at last.
"The lines are his. They're the Bank's. They go where the Bank tells them."
"The lines go where the truth tells them." She put her finger on the empty column. "That's what the Charter says. The line shall be true, and the Bank shall do as the line says. Not the line shall be what the Ministry wants. Not what the Archon wants. True." She looked at him. "And there's a great deal that's true, Remy. More than they think. More than they've ever bothered to look for."
Remy stared at her.
"I don't understand," he said.
"No," said Elara. "Neither do I. Not yet." She pulled the register toward her and opened it again. "Mister Harrow says one finds mistakes in the register by lamplight. That one wouldn't find by day."
"Mister Harrow said that?"
"Mister Harrow said that."
Remy looked at her for a long moment. Then something in his face loosened, a little. Not much.
"Mister Harrow," he said, "has never said anything to anybody in his life."
"I know."
She dipped her pen.
Remy sat on the floor with his book on his knees and watched her. And after a while, he opened the book again, and went on copying the names. Abel Fitch. Marjory Kell. Thomas Kell. Dutch. Oswin Pyle. All forty-four of them. And at the bottom, after forty-three names, in the last space, he wrote: Vespera. Blue hands.
He did not draw any flowers.
