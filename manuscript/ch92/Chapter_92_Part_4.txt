Chapter 92: The Daily List, Part 4 of 5: Miss Hollin's Pen
Miss Hollin's Pen
When she came back up the back stair, Penn was alone in the Fourth Desk.
Mister Harrow had gone down to the register room on the first floor, Penn said, to look something up, and had said he would be some time. Penn said it very fast, standing up at her desk as Elara came in, with her pink face gone blotchy and her hands knotted together in front of her apron. As if she had been waiting to say it. As if she had been waiting to say something else, and this was only the start of it.
Elara went to her own desk and sat down. She did not take her gloves off. She sat with her hands in her lap and looked at the list, and did not see it. She saw a boy's empty cupped hands.
Penn did not sit down.
"Auditor," she said.
Elara looked up.
Penn came down the room. She came slowly, past Mister Harrow's empty desk, and stopped a little way off from Elara's, the way she always stopped, as if there were a line on the floor that she was not allowed to cross. She was holding something in her knotted hands. Elara could not see what.
"Yesterday," said Penn. "What Mister Harrow said. About the other Auditor. Miss—" She swallowed. "Miss Hollin."
"You heard."
"I wasn't listening. I mean, I was going down the stair, and I'd forgotten my scarf, and I came back up for it, and the way in was open, and I heard him say my father. Mister Penn's father. That's my grandfather. I mean, it was my father, he sat at my desk, he's Mister Penn, and his father was Mister Penn too, before, but it was my father Mister Harrow meant, it must have been, because of the years—" She stopped. She went red to the roots of her hair. "I'm sorry. I'm talking too fast."
"It's all right."
"I heard him say my father said who. When Mister Harrow asked where she'd gone." Penn's voice shook. "And I stood on the stair with my scarf, and I couldn't go home. I stood there till the night man came by with his lamp and asked me if I was poorly."
Elara waited.
"My father never talked about the Fourth Desk," said Penn. "Not ever. Not at home. He sat at that desk for thirty years and he never said one word about it at the dinner table. Mother used to tease him. She used to say, Henry, you might as well work in a tomb. And he'd smile and say, It's very like." She looked down at her hands. "He died two winters ago. In his sleep. He was very peaceful."
"I'm sorry."
"I got his place. Here. Because the Penns have always had that desk. The Treasurer said so." She swallowed again. "And I got his things. From his desk at home. His pens, and his blotter, and his good penknife. And a little box. A wooden box, with a sliding lid. He kept it at the back of his drawer, under his papers. I'd never seen it before."
She held out her hands, and opened them.
In them there was a pen.
Not a Bank pen. The Bank's pens were black, all the same, with a steel nib and a brass band, and you got a new one from the stores whenever you asked. This was an old one. A slim pen made of some dark red wood, polished smooth with handling, with a nib of gold, very worn, and a little silver band at the top. It was a lady's pen. A pen somebody had chosen, and paid for, and cared about.
And on the silver band, scratched very small, as if with the point of a penknife, there were two letters.
M. H.
Elara did not take it. She sat and looked at it in Penn's open hands.
"It was in the box," said Penn. "On its own. Wrapped in a handkerchief. I didn't know whose it was. I thought perhaps it was Mother's. But Mother's name is Ada." Her voice had gone very small. "I kept it because it was pretty. I've been using it. At my desk. For the fair book. It writes so beautifully. Better than any of the Bank's." She looked up at Elara. Her eyes were wet. "And then yesterday Mister Harrow said Miss H. And I went home and I couldn't sleep, and I got up in the night and lit the candle and looked at it. M. H."
"Miss Hollin," said Elara.
"I think so. I think it must be." Penn's hands were shaking. "I think my father had it. I think he picked it up off her desk. That Monday. When it was empty. When he'd forgotten her." She swallowed hard. "I think he didn't know why he picked it up. I think he didn't know why he kept it. I think he just knew he had to."
They looked at each other.
Outside the windows, the light was going. The lamps on the quay came on, blue, one after another, all the way along the river.
"He never said who," said Penn suddenly, fiercely. "Not in his heart. He didn't. Somebody made him say it. Somebody took her out of him. But they couldn't take whatever made him keep the pen." Her chin was trembling. "Could they? They couldn't. Because he kept it."
Elara did not answer at once.
She thought about Mister Harrow's card. Miss H. is frightened. She asked me to remember her. In his own hand, that he did not remember writing. She thought about the blue tin on her shelf upstairs, with nothing in it, that she had never thrown away. She thought about a column of black ink in a register. Paid. Paid. Paid.
She thought that there were a great many things that people kept without knowing why.
"No," she said. "They couldn't."
Penn let out a breath that was nearly a sob.
She held out the pen again. Further. Across the line on the floor that she was not allowed to cross.
"I think you should have it," she said. "Auditor. I think it's yours. I think it was always meant for the desk by the window."
Elara looked at it.
Then she took off her right glove, slowly, finger by finger, and laid it on the green leather. She did not usually do that in front of anyone. Her bare hand looked very white in the lamplight, and very thin.
She did not touch Penn. She held out her bare palm, flat, and Penn laid the pen on it without touching her either. As if she knew.
It was warm from Penn's hands. It was lighter than it looked. The wood was smooth as a pebble from a stream. And there was something about the weight of it, the way it lay across her palm, that made Elara's breath catch in her throat for no reason she could find. As if her hand knew it. As if her hand had been waiting for it.
She did not use the Sight. She did not dare. It cost a day each time, and she did not know whose day it would be.
She closed her fingers on it.
"Thank you, Miss Penn," she said.
Penn nodded. She did not seem able to speak. She turned and went back to her desk and sat down and picked up one of the Bank's black pens, and looked at it as if she had never seen one before. Then she bent her head over the fair book.
She did not copy fast this time. She copied very slowly. Very carefully. Every letter.
And Elara saw, a little later, when Penn brought the day's pages to her desk to be checked, that she had copied every line that ended in Returned in her very best hand. Rounder than the others. Larger. Very clear. As if she wanted whoever read them, at the other end, in the Cleansing Office, to be quite sure they had read them right.
Elara said nothing about it.
She dipped Miss Hollin's pen in the Bank's black ink, and signed the pages, E., and the long flat line. The gold nib went over the paper like a skate over ice. It made the finest line she had ever made in her life.
Hale, L.
She found it that afternoon, near the bottom of the third page of the Tuesday list. Number fifty-nine.
She almost did not see it. She had been reading for five hours. Her eyes had begun to do the thing they did when she was very tired, where the brown letters on the page swam a little and then came back, like fish under ice. She read fifty-nine and the name next to it and went on to sixty before something in her caught on it, the way a sleeve catches on a nail, and pulled her back.
Fifty-nine. Hale, L. Harvester of the Ministry, retired. The Lamplighter's Rest, Cinder Lane, the room at the top. To be brought in. For the clearing of the old books.
She sat and looked at it.
She knew the name. She had known it for years. L. Hale had been signed at the bottom of a great many Ministry papers that came up to the Fourth Desk, back in her first years: transfer papers, certificates of harvest, a hand-written note now and then in a careful old-fashioned script, very upright, with a long flourish on the H. A senior harvester. One of the old ones. He had stopped signing things some years ago, and she had supposed he had retired, and had not thought about him again.
And then, this winter, she had heard the name spoken. More than once. In the Bourse, the night before it burned. On the lips of a woman with gray at her wrists. Linus Hale. As if it meant something. As if he were somebody who had been in the middle of something.
And she had heard, the way you hear things in the Bank, in the way clerks stop talking when you come past, that Linus Hale had been found in his room at the top of a lodging house in Cinder Lane, sitting at his table with a pen in his hand, emptied. Quite empty. Not a thing left in him. In the week before Midwinter.
He had been emptied for more than a month.
And he was on the list.
To be brought in. For the clearing of the old books.
She read the line again, and again. And on the third reading she saw what she had not seen on the first two.
There was a mark beside the name.
Not in the third column, where her lines went. In the margin. On the left. Very small, very faint, so faint she had to tip the page to the lamp to be sure of it. Not in Ministry brown. In pencil. A soft gray pencil, pressed so lightly that it was hardly a mark at all.
A circle.
A plain small circle, no bigger than her little fingernail. Drawn in one stroke, without lifting the pencil, so that the end of the line met the beginning exactly.
Elara sat back in her chair.
She did not have to think where she had seen it before. She knew. She had seen it on a page of violet leather in Cressida Thorne's day book, on the day of Aurelian Thorne's funeral, where there should have been words and there was only that. A circle. And she had seen it beside a line six years old, in the same book, in pale purple ink, beside the ninth day of the Thaw month. Bank. The Fourth Desk. The girl. Done.
And she had heard about it. A plain circle, pressed into red wax, on a cream card, put into a woman's glove the morning of a funeral. And a blank card on the floor, the morning after, by the woman's feet.
She looked at the other names on the page. All sixty-three of them. She tipped every page to the lamp, one after another, slowly, looking at the left margin.
There were no other circles.
Only this one. Beside a man who was already empty.
"Remy," she said.
It was the middle of the afternoon. It was broad day. Penn was at her desk by the way in, copying. Mister Harrow was at his. Elara had never once, in nineteen days, spoken to the cupboard by daylight.
Penn looked up. Mister Harrow did not.
Elara did not say it again. She got up instead, with the page in her hand, and walked to cupboard eleven, and stood beside it, with her back to the room, as if she were looking for something on the shelf above it. And she held the page flat against the black wood of the door, at the height of the crack between the two doors, where a pair of eyes behind it might see.
She stood there for some time.
Then she heard, from inside, very faint, a breath. Let out slowly.
She went back to her desk.
That night, when the building was empty, Remy came out from behind the Charter box and sat on the floor by her desk, the way he had on Saturday, with his back against the desk leg and his black book on his knees. He did not open it. He sat with his gloved hands flat on the cover and looked at the far wall.
"Linus Hale," he said.
"You knew him."
"Knew of him. Everybody did. The old man." Remy's voice was very quiet. "He was the one who trained Corin. Corin Vane. Took him in off the street when he was nobody, when he was a boy with nothing in his head. Taught him the old ways. The slow ways." He was quiet. "Corin used to pay his rent. Every month. Out of his own wage. Even when he had nothing. Even after."
Elara did not move.
She had looked, that afternoon. She had opened the register to Cinder Lane, to the Lamplighter's Rest, to the room at the top. And she had found the rent, set down in a column, month after month, in the steady black hand of the first-floor clerks. Paid. Paid. Paid. For eleven years. And beside each one, the name of the person who paid it. Not L. Hale.
C. Vane.
Until Midwinter. And then nothing. Two months, unpaid. And the landlady's mark beside them in pencil, which meant carried. Not pressed. She had let it ride. She was very old, the register said. She had kept the Rest for forty years.
Elara had sat and looked at that name for a long time. C. Vane. Paid. In a column. In black. Eleven years of it.
She had not said anything to anybody about it. She did not say anything now.
"Why would they want him?" she said instead. "He's empty. There's nothing in him. Nothing to weigh. Nothing to take."
"No," said Remy. "There isn't."
"Then why?"
Remy did not answer for a long time.
"I've seen that circle before," he said at last. "In Vivienne's books. Not the seven. Her old ones, from years back. She used to draw it, beside a name, sometimes. When she'd heard something. She'd draw it and then she wouldn't say that name again. Not ever." He turned his book over in his hands. "I asked her once what it was for. I was little. She said, It means somebody else has the pen." He was quiet. "I didn't know what she meant."
"And now?"
"Now I think it means somebody's tidying up." He looked up at her. "Somebody's going back over the old circles. The ones they've already done. Making sure. Clearing them off the books." His voice had gone flat. "Bringing them in for the clearing of the old books. So there's nobody left. Not even an empty old man in a room with a pen. Nobody who might have something written down somewhere that somebody might read."
Elara looked at the page in front of her.
To be brought in.
She thought about what happened to people who were brought in. She thought about pen seven, and a boy with his hands cupped round nothing. She thought about an empty old man, who had once taken in a boy with nothing in his head, sitting on the stone floor of the Intake Hall in the blue light, not knowing where he was, or why, or who.
She had already done what she could. That afternoon.
She had not waited for the night, or for Remy. As soon as she had shut the register on C. Vane. Paid, she had taken a fresh sheet of the Bank's paper out of the drawer and written a note on it, in her own hand, in black. To the first-floor clerk of the street register. Please enter against the Lamplighter's Rest, Cinder Lane, the room at the top: two months' lodging, paid this day in full, by the Fourth Desk. And signed it. E. With the long flat line.
Then she had opened the drawer again, at the back, behind the spare nibs, where the rose was, wrapped in blotting paper. And under that there was a small leather purse. Her wages. The Bank paid her monthly, in silver, and she had nothing to spend it on. She had counted out what two months at the Lamplighter's Rest would be, from the register, exactly, to the penny, and folded it into the note, and sent it down by a porter. And she had stood at the head of the back stair until the porter came up again and said the clerk had entered it.
And then she had written her line against number fifty-nine, there and then, at the fourth bell of the afternoon, and sent that page on its own straight across the square to the Cleansing Office by the same porter. Before any gray coat could set out for Cinder Lane with a cart.
She had kept a copy. She showed it to Remy now, on the floor by her desk, in the lamplight.
Found at the address given. Debt: nil, the lodging being paid. Holdings: none. Emptied before Midwinter; nothing left to weigh. The Bank has nothing to hold. Not to be brought in.
Every word of it was true. It had been true since the fourth bell.
Remy read it twice and gave it back.
"Will that work?" said Remy.
"I don't know." She looked at the little gray circle in the margin. "I don't know who has the pen."
Remy opened his book on his knees. He turned to his page headed THE CLEANSING. He ran his finger down the names until he came to the end. And then he wrote, under Vespera. Blue hands, in his quick sloping hand:
Linus Hale. The old man. Who took Corin in.
He sat looking at it. Then, very small, beside it, he drew a circle. Not a flower. A circle. In pencil. And then, after a moment, he drew a line through it. Straight through the middle. Hard.
"There," he said. "That's mine."
