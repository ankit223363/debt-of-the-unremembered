Chapter 92: The Daily List, Part 3 of 5: Mister Harrow's Weather
Mister Harrow's Weather
At the sixth bell Mister Harrow stood up and went to Penn's desk.
Penn had stopped crying a long time ago. She had wiped her face on her sleeve and blown her nose and gone back to copying, very fast, with her head bent so low over the fair book that her nose nearly touched the page. Her hand was shaking. Elara could see the lines going crooked from across the room.
Mister Harrow stood beside her desk and looked down at the page.
"Miss Penn," he said.
Penn jumped so hard that her pen went skidding across the page and left a long black tail.
"Oh," she said. "Oh, no. Oh, I'm sorry, Mister Harrow, I'll do it again, I'll do the whole page again—"
"You will go home," said Mister Harrow.
Penn stared up at him.
"It is the sixth bell," said Mister Harrow. "The fair book will keep until tomorrow. It has kept for three hundred years. It will keep for one night." He cleared his throat. "Go home, Miss Penn. Have something hot. Go to bed."
Penn's mouth opened and shut.
Then she got up, and put her pens away in a row, very straight, and put on her coat, which was too big for her in the shoulders, and her hat. And she went to the way in. And at the way in, beside Gage's bench, she stopped and turned round and looked at Mister Harrow.
"Thank you, Mister Harrow," she said, in a very small voice.
Mister Harrow cleared his throat.
Penn went.
Mister Harrow did not go after her. He went back to his own desk, in the middle of the room, and stood behind it. He did not sit. He did not put on his hat. He stood looking down at the little card on the corner of his desk with his one word on it.
Snow.
Then he picked it up and came down the room to Elara's desk, and put it down on the green leather in front of her.
She looked at it. Then at him.
"Do you know why I write the weather, Auditor?" he said.
It was the most words she had ever heard him say in one go. She found she was holding her breath.
"No," she said.
"I have served three Auditors," said Mister Harrow. "At this desk. In twenty-two years. You are the third." He was not looking at her. He was looking at the card. "The first was a Mister Quill. An old man. Very kind. He had been at the Fourth Desk forty years when I came. He wrote every line the first way. The way the register gave it. He never once looked for another." He paused. "One morning he came in and sat down at this desk, and he could not remember how to hold his pen. And they took him away. Downstairs. And he did not come back."
Elara did not say anything.
"The second," said Mister Harrow, "was a Miss Hollin. She was young. Younger than you are now. She was here four years." He cleared his throat. It was a different sound this time. "She was clever. She read the Charter. She began to read the register, the way you are reading it. She wrote a line one Thursday that was true, and was not the line the Bank expected."
He was quiet.
"What happened to her?" said Elara.
"I don't know," said Mister Harrow.
She looked at him.
"That is the truth, Auditor. I don't know." He looked at the card. "I came in one Monday, and it was snowing, and I wrote snow on my card. As I always do. And Miss Hollin was not at her desk. And I asked Mister Penn's father, who sat where Miss Penn sits now, where she was, and he looked at me, and he said, Who?"
The snow went past the windows. The lamps hissed.
"He did not know her," said Mister Harrow. "Nobody did. Nobody in the building. Not the porters, not the clerks downstairs, not the Treasurer, who was old Lord Fennimore then. Her name was not in the staff book. Her desk was empty. Her pens were gone. It was as if she had never been." He breathed out. "And I would have thought it, too. I would have thought I had dreamed her. Except that I had a card in my pocket, from the Friday before, that said Rain. In my hand. And on the back of it, in my hand, I had written, Miss H. is frightened. She asked me to remember her."
Elara sat very still.
"I did not remember writing it," said Mister Harrow. "Not a word. I had no memory of the Friday at all. Of her asking. Of anything. Someone had taken it. Taken all of it, out of me, while I slept, I suppose. All of her." He looked down at the card on her desk. "But they did not know about the card."
"And six weeks later," he said, "they sent you."
He was quiet for a long time.
"So I write the weather," he said at last. "Every morning. One word. And I keep the cards. Every one. Twenty-two years of them, in boxes, in my rooms. So that I know what day it is. And if I ever come in to work and look out of that window and see snow, and find in my pocket a card in my own hand from yesterday that says sun—" He stopped. "Then I will know that something has been done to me. And I will know to look for what is missing."
Elara looked at him. At his small neat gray face. At his small neat gray coat. At his hands, which were very clean, with the nails cut short and square.
"Why are you telling me this?" she said.
"Because he said she is learning," said Mister Harrow. "And because the last time someone at this desk was learning, I did not tell her anything. I sat at my desk and sharpened my pencil and wrote the weather. And she asked me to remember her, and I could not." He cleared his throat. "I am telling you so that someone will know. That is all. So that if one morning I come in and say who?, there will be someone who knows that I was told."
He picked up the card off her desk. He turned it over. On the back, in his small square hand, he had already written something. He laid it down again, face down.
E. is learning. She asked nothing. Remember her anyway.
Elara read it twice.
She found she could not speak. She sat with her hands flat on the green leather and looked at it, and her throat had gone tight and hot, and there was nothing she could say that would come out of it.
Mister Harrow picked the card up again, and put it in his waistcoat pocket, and buttoned the pocket.
"There is a ruling," he said, in his ordinary voice, as if nothing had happened, "in the margin of the Charter. On the fourth leaf. In red. Auditor Penhallow, some hundred and sixty years ago. On the matter of what a name is." He nodded at the list on her desk, at number four, at the heavy black capitals. CALLED DUTCH. "I believe you will find it of use. A calling is not a name."
He went back to his desk. He took his hat off the peg and put it on. He took his umbrella down and tucked it under his arm.
At the way in, he stopped beside Gage.
The two of them looked at each other. Mister Harrow, small and gray and neat, with his umbrella. And Gage, huge on his bench, with his wooden face and his slate on its cord, and his hands on his knees.
Then Mister Harrow did something that Elara had never seen him do, in six years.
He put out his hand. And laid it, very briefly, on Gage's shoulder.
And Gage turned his head and looked at it. At the small clean gray-gloved hand on his shoulder. As if it were something he had not seen in a very long time.
Then Mister Harrow took his hand away, and cleared his throat, and went down the back stair, and was gone.
Elara sat for a long time.
Then she got up and went to cupboard eleven. She opened both doors this time. Remy was sitting up behind the Charter box with his book on his knees, wide awake, very still. He had heard everything. She could see it in his face.
She did not say anything to him. She reached past him to the black box with the silver corners and lifted the lid and took out the Charter.
It was not one sheet. It was a little book, a very old one, made of leaves of vellum sewn together with a cord, and the leaves were brown and soft and smelled of age. She turned them very carefully. One. Two. Three. Four.
On the fourth leaf, in the margin, beside the clause about names, there was a note. In red ink, faded almost to pink. In a cramped old-fashioned hand with long tails on the letters.
Ruled this day, that a calling is not a name. A name is that which a soul is given and answers to before the Saints and the Bank. A calling is that which others put upon it. The Auditor shall set no line against a calling. — Penhallow, Auditor.
Elara read it three times.
Then she put the Charter back in its box, and shut the lid, and went back to her desk, and wrote against number four, CALLED DUTCH, in her own black ink:
A calling is not a name. Ruling of Auditor Penhallow, the Charter, the fourth leaf. Returned to the Office.
Behind her, on his bench, Gage lifted one big finger and tapped the slate on his chest, once, on the word that was already there. TRUE.
The Pens Fill
On Tuesday morning, a little after the second bell, Elara went down to the Intake Hall.
She did not have to. Nobody had asked her. The Auditor's place was at the Fourth Desk, with the lists, and the lines went down the building by themselves, in the fair book, carried by porters. She had been down to the Intake Hall perhaps a dozen times in six years. Each time because somebody had sent for her.
Nobody had sent for her this time.
She went because of Marjory Kell. And her son, Thomas, aged fourteen, who held the lamp. Because the Tuesday list had come at the first bell, sixty-three names, and pinned to the top of it there had been a second sheet, a short one, headed Brought in. Held at the Bank's Intake pending weighing. And there had been names on it from Saturday. Twelve of them. Found at the address given. Brought in.
Marjory Kell was the third.
Elara had written her line on Saturday night. She remembered it. She remembered sitting with the register open at the Dye-Works, the third arch from the river, and finding Marjory Kell there, just as the list said, with the rent paid to the Bank every quarter for eleven years. And she had written what was true. Found at the address given. Debt: nil. Holdings: needles, inks, one lamp. And she had felt nothing much. A needlewoman. No debt. Nothing for the Bank to take but some needles.
She had not thought about what happened after that. It was not the Bank's business, what happened after that. The Bank held what it owned. The Ministry weighed. That was the Order.
She went down the back stair. Gage came with her. He always came with her, when she went down. He walked one step behind, with his big feet very quiet on the stone.
The back stair went down and down. Past the third floor and the second and the first and the ground floor, with its smell of floor wax. Past the first cellars, where the clerks kept the old ledgers in iron presses. Past the second, where it began to be cold, and the walls were bare stone, sweating. Past the third and the fourth. The lamps on the walls got further apart. The steps got older. By the fifth floor down they were worn into hollows in the middle, like the steps of a very old church, by more feet than she could think about.
The sixth floor down was the Intake Hall.
She heard it before she saw it. A sound she had never heard there before. Not loud. A low sound, like wind in a chimney, or the sea a long way off. Voices. A great many voices, all talking quietly at once, and under them, coughing. And somewhere, a child crying.
The stair came out through a low arch into the Hall.
It was a long room, very long, with a low curved ceiling of old brick held up on squat stone pillars. It was lit with Ash lamps, blue, set in iron cages high on the pillars, so that the light came down cold and even and made everyone's face look like the face of a drowned person. Down one side of it, behind a black counter as long as a street, there were clerks at high stools with ledgers. Down the other side there were the pens.
The pens were iron. Iron bars from floor to ceiling, set into the stone, with iron gates. Each one was about the size of a small bedroom. There were twelve of them, in a row, going away down the Hall into the blue. And at the far end, past them all, the great iron doors to the river, shut and barred, with frost on them, on the inside, in white feathers.
She had seen the pens before. She had seen them nearly empty. One person in one pen, sitting on the bench along the back wall, waiting. Two, sometimes. Once, in the bad autumn, five.
They were full.
Every one of them. Pen one, and pen three, and pen four, and all the way down. People standing, because there was no room on the benches. People sitting on the stone floor with their backs against the bars and their knees drawn up. Old men in overcoats. Women with shawls over their heads. A man in a sailor's jersey with tattooed arms, sitting very still with his hands over his face. A boy of about ten, asleep across his mother's lap. A very old woman in a lace cap, sitting straight-backed on the end of a bench, with a book held in both hands against her chest, as if somebody might take it, which somebody would.
They looked up when Elara came in. Some of them. They looked at her, at her dark green coat and her white kid gloves and her Bank badge, and then they looked away again, quickly, the way people look away from a carriage going past in the street.
She stood at the bottom of the stair and could not move.
"Auditor."
Tom Briggs had come out from behind the black counter. He was a big man, broad and slow and gray, in a leather apron, with a ring of keys at his belt as big as a dinner plate. He had kept the Intake for twenty years. He had a face like a kind potato. He looked very tired.
"Mister Briggs," said Elara.
"I didn't know you were coming down."
"Nobody sent for me." She looked at the pens. "How many?"
"Seventy-one," said Briggs. "Since Saturday." He rubbed his face with one big hand. "The pens were built for twenty. Twenty-four at a push. I've asked for blankets. I've asked for bread. I've had the blankets." He paused. "The bread they say is coming."
"Where do they go? After?"
"Across the square. To the Ministry. To be weighed." Briggs looked at her. "Not till the weighing day. Sunday coming. The Order doesn't keep Sundays, they say. The Ministry hasn't the rooms ready before. So they bring them here, and the Bank holds them, under the Order, and I keep them." His voice was flat. "I keep them."
Elara walked down the Hall.
She walked slowly, past the pens, with Gage one step behind her. She did not know what she was looking for. She told herself she was not looking for anything. But she looked into every pen as she went past it, at every face, and she knew she was looking for a woman with a lamp-boy.
She found them in pen seven.
Marjory Kell was a short, strong, square woman of about fifty, with gray hair in a bun and a face like a closed fist. Her sleeves were pushed up. Her forearms were covered with pictures. Anchors and roses and a ship in full sail, and a woman's face, and a bird. In blue, and red, and black. They were very fine. Even in the cold blue light, Elara could see that they were very fine. And at her wrists, where the sleeves stopped, the pictures went on, under the cloth. Other pictures. Other things.
She was sitting on the floor against the bars, because the bench was full, with a boy's head in her lap.
The boy was tall, and thin, and very pale, with fair hair that stuck up at the back. He was lying on his side on the stone with his knees drawn up and his head in his mother's lap, and his eyes were open. He had his hands held up in front of his face, together, cupped. As if he were holding something. As if he were holding a lamp.
There was nothing in them.
Elara stood outside the bars and looked at them.
Marjory Kell looked up. She looked at Elara's coat, and her gloves, and her badge. And her face did not change at all. It did not go angry. It did not go frightened. It did not do anything.
"You're from upstairs," she said. Her voice was low and rough.
"Yes."
"You're the one writes the lines."
Elara could not answer for a moment. Then she said, "Yes."
Marjory Kell looked at her a while longer.
"Needles, inks, one lamp," she said. "That's what the clerk read out. When they brought us in. That's what we've got, he said. Needles, inks, one lamp." She looked down at the boy in her lap. At his empty cupped hands. "They took the lamp. On the stair. One of the gray ones. Tom wouldn't let go of it." She stroked his hair. "He's been holding it ever since."
Elara stood very still.
"It was a true line," said Marjory Kell. "I'll give you that. That's all we had."
The boy, Tom, turned his head in his mother's lap and looked up at Elara through the bars. He had very pale blue eyes, and they were not quite looking at her. They were looking at something a little to one side of her, a little above. The way a baby looks.
"Is it morning?" he said.
Elara opened her mouth.
"It's morning, love," said his mother. "It's always morning down here."
The boy seemed satisfied. He turned his head back and looked at his empty hands again.
Elara walked on. She did not know how she did it. Her feet did it.
She came to pen two.
Pen two was the only one that was not full. There was one person in it. The same person who had been in it for weeks, since before Midwinter, since before the Bourse burned, since before anything. A girl. Small and thin, of about twelve, with short dark hair and a gray smock and bare feet tucked up under her on the bench. She sat at the very end of the bench, against the wall, with her hands in her lap. She did not look up.
And she was humming.
Very softly. Four notes. Going up, and then down, and then up again, a little higher. And then a gap. A long gap, the length of three more notes, as if she were waiting for somebody to sing them. And then the four notes again.
The brass plate on the gate of pen two said: As held. By the Fourth Desk. Under audit.
Elara's line. From weeks ago. The only line in the Bank that held a person in a pen by itself.
Briggs had followed her down the Hall. He stood beside her now and looked in through the bars at the girl.
"They've asked for her again," he said quietly. "Twice since Saturday. The Spire. A man with a cart. The open hand." He looked at Elara. "I told him what I told the last one. As held. By the Fourth Desk. Under audit. As long as the Auditor's line stands." He paused. "He didn't like it."
"No."
"He said it won't stand long."
Elara looked at the girl. At her bare feet. At her hands, lying in her lap. At her mouth, humming. Four notes, and a gap.
Behind her, Gage made a sound.
Very low. Very soft. One note, from somewhere deep in his chest. The same note he had made in this Hall a week ago, the one and only time she had ever heard him make any sound at all. He made it into the gap. Into the silence after the girl's four notes. One low note, where the fifth should be.
The girl stopped humming.
She sat very still on her bench. She did not look up. But her head turned. A very little. Toward the bars. Toward Gage.
And then, after a long time, she began again. Four notes. Up, and down, and up. And then the gap.
And this time she left it longer. Much longer. Waiting.
Gage did not make the sound again. He stood behind Elara with his big hands at his sides and his slate on its cord, and his wooden face did not move at all. But Elara, turning to look at him, saw that his eyes were wet.
She turned back to the pen.
"It will stand," she said to Briggs. Very quietly. "The line will stand."
Briggs looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, and went back down the Hall to his counter, and his keys, and his seventy-one.
