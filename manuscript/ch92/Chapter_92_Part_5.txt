Chapter 92: The Daily List, Part 5 of 5: The Summons
The Summons
On Wednesday, at the first bell, Sergeant Marsh came alone.
He had the black case under his own arm. Tull was not with him. Elara heard him on the back stair, slower than before, one heavy step at a time, and she heard him stop on the landing below the way in, for longer than it would take a man to get his breath. Then he came on up.
He came down the room and stopped at her desk and put the case down on the green leather without a word. He unlocked it himself. He took out the new bundle. Thicker again. And the receipt. And then he took out something else, and held it for a moment in his thick red hand before he put it down.
A letter. Folded once, and sealed. The paper was heavy and very white. All round the edge of it there was a band of black, as wide as her finger.
The seal was gray wax. An open eye.
"For the Auditor," said the sergeant. "From the Cleansing Office. By order."
Elara looked at it. She did not pick it up.
"Thank you, Sergeant."
He did not go. He stood at her desk with his cap on and his hands at his sides, and looked down at the bundle, and then at the window, and then at her.
"The old man," he said. "Number fifty-nine. Yesterday."
"Yes."
"The line was taken." He said it carefully, as if each word had been counted out to him and he had to give them all back. "The Office read it. The Office sent a clerk to the first floor here, to the register room, at the sixth bell last night. To see. And the clerk came back and said the rent was paid. Two months. In full. By the Fourth Desk." He paused. "So the line stands. The old man stays where he is."
Elara let out a breath that she had not known she was holding.
"Thank you," she said.
"I've not done anything."
"Thank you for telling me."
The sergeant looked at her. His red face worked.
"There was a man in the Office last night," he said, "when the clerk came back. Not one of ours. Not Ministry. I've never seen him before. Tall. Thin. Very quiet. Gray coat, good one. He'd been sitting in the under-secretary's room all evening, by the fire, not saying anything. When the clerk said paid by the Fourth Desk, he looked up." The sergeant stopped. "And he asked who paid it before."
Elara did not move.
"The clerk didn't know," said Marsh. "He hadn't looked. He said he'd go back and look. And the man said, Don't trouble yourself. I know. And he got up and put his hat on and went out." He breathed in through his nose. "That's all. I don't know who he was. I thought you should know he asked."
She made herself nod. "Thank you."
"I've not done anything," said the sergeant again. More quietly.
He looked down at the black-bordered letter on her desk.
"You'll want to read that," he said. "I'll wait. I'm to take your answer back. It needs your mark."
Elara picked it up.
She broke the gray seal with her thumb. The wax was hard and cold and came away in two pieces, an eye cut in half. She unfolded the paper.
It was printed, mostly. With spaces filled in by hand, in a clerk's brown.
THE MINISTRY OF MEMORY. THE CLEANSING OFFICE. Under the Order of Need, Clause Eleven.
To the Auditor of the Fourth Desk of the Bank of Veridia.
The Auditor is summoned to attend the Weighing Rooms of the Ministry, the Long Gallery, the second floor, on the first Day of Weighing under the Order, being Sunday next, from the first bell until the work of the day is done; to witness, under the old law of the Charter, the weighing of all persons held at the Bank's Intake and brought before the Ministry; and to set down, in the Bank's record, the debt or holding found in each, so that the Bank may do as the line says.
The Auditor shall bring the record.
The Auditor shall sit at the witness's table and shall not leave it until the work is done.
The Auditor shall not speak to those being weighed.
And at the bottom, in a different hand. Not brown. Black. A small, neat, upright hand, very careful, every letter separate, as if the man who wrote it had checked each one before he went on to the next.
It is the Archon's particular wish that the Auditor attend in person. L.
Elara read it twice.
She thought about the Long Gallery. She had never seen it. She had heard of it. The Ministry's weighing room, on the second floor, at the back, where the windows looked out over the yards. A long room with a row of chairs down one side and a row of tables down the other, and in the middle, they said, a great brass scale, very old, as tall as a man, that was not used to weigh anything at all anymore, but had been left there because it had always been there.
She thought about pen seven. About Marjory Kell, with her pictures, sitting against the bars. About a boy with his hands cupped round a lamp that was not there. About seventy-one people, more now, sitting in the blue light six floors under her feet, waiting for Sunday.
To set down the debt or holding found in each.
The Auditor shall not speak to those being weighed.
She looked up.
"What happens," she said, "if the Auditor does not attend?"
The sergeant looked at her for a long time.
"I don't know, Auditor," he said. "I've never known anybody not."
She nodded slowly.
She dipped her pen. She wrote on the bottom of the summons, under the small neat L., in her own black: The Auditor will attend. With the record. And her mark. E., and the long flat line.
She blotted it, and folded it, and held it out.
The sergeant took it. He put it in the case and locked the case and stood there with it under his arm.
"Auditor," he said.
"Sergeant?"
He seemed about to say something. His mouth opened under the short gray beard. Then it closed again.
"Mind how you go," he said at last. And turned and went down the room, past Gage, who watched him go, and out, and down the stair.
Elara sat with her hands flat on the desk.
She found she was thinking, not about the Long Gallery, or the scale, or the Archon's particular wish. She was thinking about a tall thin quiet man in a good gray coat, sitting by the under-secretary's fire all evening, saying nothing. And looking up. And asking who paid the rent before.
Don't trouble yourself. I know.
She did not know who it was. She thought she knew who it was. She did not know which of those was worse.
Behind her, the chalk squeaked on the slate. She turned round.
Gage had wiped TRUE away. In its place there were four new words, in big square letters, pressed so hard that the chalk had crumbled at the ends.
I WILL BE THERE.
Elara looked at them for a long time.
"The summons is for the Auditor," she said. "Not for you."
Gage did not wipe the slate.
He sat on his bench with his hands on his knees, and the four words on his chest, and looked at her. And she understood that it did not matter what the summons said. It had never mattered, in six years, what any piece of paper said. Where she went, he went. One step behind. It was the oldest thing about him. Older, she thought sometimes, than her.
I drove the blue one. The girl was in it.
She turned back to the window, so that he would not see her face.
She told Remy that night, when the building was empty.
She did not mean to. She meant to keep it to herself, the way she kept most things. But when she opened the left-hand door of cupboard eleven he was sitting up behind the Charter box with his knees drawn up, waiting, and he looked at her face in the lamplight and said, "What's happened?" before she had said anything at all. And she found that she had taken the summons out of her pocket and was holding it out to him.
The sergeant had taken her answer away with him. But there had been a second copy of the summons inside the first, folded small, marked For the Auditor to keep, and she had kept it.
Remy read it on his knees, by the light of her lamp, with his lips pressed tight together.
When he came to the small neat L. at the bottom, and the line above it, he stopped. He read that line three times. She watched his eyes go along it and back, along and back.
It is the Archon's particular wish that the Auditor attend in person.
"Lucian," he said. "That's Lucian's hand. The secretary. He checks everything three times. Vivienne used to say you could tell a paper Lucian had touched because the ink was always dry." He looked up. "You can't go."
"I've said I will."
"Then unsay it."
"I can't. It's written. With my mark." She took the summons back from him and folded it along its creases. "And if I don't go, somebody else sits at the witness table. Somebody from the Treasurer's office. And they write the first line, every time, the easy one. Seventy-one times. More." She put the summons in her pocket. "If I go, at least it's me."
Remy stared at her.
"He wants you there," he said. "Don't you see? Particular wish. He wants you to sit at that table and watch. He wants to see your face when they weigh them. He wants to see what you write." His voice had gone low and fast. "It's not a summons, Auditor. It's a test."
"I know."
"Then—"
"I know it's a test," said Elara. "I've known since the Council. He's been testing me since the line about the fire. Perhaps before." She looked at the window, at the dark, at the snow. "I don't know what he wants to find out. But I know that if I don't go, he'll find it out anyway. He'll find out that I was afraid."
Remy did not say anything for a long time.
Then he pulled his black book out from under his blanket and opened it, and turned to the page headed THE CLEANSING. And at the bottom, under Linus Hale, under his crossed-out circle, he wrote, in his quick sloping hand:
Sunday. The Long Gallery. The Auditor. By his particular wish.
He did not draw anything beside it. He sat looking at it with the pencil in his gloved fingers.
"I'm writing you down," he said. "So you're in the book. So somebody's got you." He did not look up. "Same as Mister Harrow's card."
Elara stood in the lamplight and looked down at the top of his untidy black head.
"Thank you, Remy," she said.
"Don't thank me." He shut the book. "Just come back up the stair on Sunday night and tell me I wasted the ink."
The Book of Days
That night she wrote in the book of days.
She wrote in it every night. She had written in it every night for six years, since the first morning, because the Bank said she must. It was a plain book, bound in black cloth with a red spine, and the pages were numbered at the top corner in a clerk's hand, all the way to the end, so that no page could be torn out without somebody knowing. Once a quarter a man came up from the Treasurer's office and turned the pages and counted them and initialed the last one in pencil. He never read what was on them. He only counted.
She had used to write very little. Thursday. The list. Thirty names. The lines done by the sixth bell. The weather, sometimes, because Mister Harrow did. A word about the river. In the first year she had not known what else there was to write. She had not known that there was anything else.
She wrote more now.
She sat at the little table in her rooms under the roof, by the round window that looked out over the river. Her rooms were three small ones, one opening into the next, with sloping ceilings, so that she could only stand up straight in the middle of each. A bed. A chair. A washstand with a blue jug. A shelf with nothing on it but the book of days and a tin.
The tin was blue. Small and blue and dented, with the paint worn off the corners to bright metal. There was nothing in it. There had been nothing in it as long as she could remember. She did not know where it came from. It had been on the shelf on the first morning, when she woke in this room and did not know her own name, and she had never thrown it away.
Only one, Ellie. Only one. They have to last.
She had heard that a week ago. A boy's voice. In her head, out of nowhere, holding the tin. It had gone through her like cold water and left her shaking on the floor. She had not heard it again. She had tried, every night, holding the tin. Nothing came.
She took it down now anyway and set it on the table beside the book. Then she opened the book and dipped her pen.
Wednesday. The fifth day of the daily list.
Saturday, forty-four. Sunday, fifty-one. Monday, fifty-eight. Tuesday, sixty-three. Today, sixty-seven.
She looked at the numbers for a while. Then she added them up, the way she added up everything, in her head, without meaning to. Two hundred and eighty-three. In five days. More than nine Thursdays.
Of these, I have returned to the Office thirty-nine. Every line true.
Of these, the Office has corrected and sent back twenty-two. Seventeen are still out.
Of the rest, I have written what the register says. Found. Dead. Holdings. Debt. The Bank does as the lines say. The pens are full. Seventy-one on Tuesday. I did not go down today. I could not.
She stopped. She sat with the pen over the page. Then she wrote:
A woman in pen seven said, It was a true line. I'll give you that. That's all we had.
Her son held a lamp that was not there.
I wrote that line.
She put the pen down for a while.
The round window had frost on it, in the corners. Beyond it, the river was black. The snow had stopped. For the first time in five days, it had stopped, some time in the evening, and the sky over the city had cleared, and there were stars. Not many, over the city. The Ash lamps put them out. But a few, very high, very small.
She picked up the pen again.
Mister Harrow writes the weather so that he will know if something has been taken. He says the Auditor before me was Miss Hollin, and that one Monday she was gone, and nobody knew her name. Not even him. He knew only because of a card.
He has a card for me now. In his pocket. It says, E. is learning. Remember her anyway.
The Treasurer says the Archon read my line for number forty-one and laughed. He said, She is learning.
They are both right. I am.
I don't know what it costs. Mister Harrow does not know either. Miss Hollin knew, perhaps. She can't tell anyone.
She stopped again. Then:
The Treasurer gave me a white rose. It is in the drawer of my desk. I have not touched it.
There was a circle beside an old man's name. In gray pencil. Remy says it means somebody else has the pen. I paid the old man's rent from my wages, so that the line would be true. It was. They took it. And then a quiet man in a gray coat asked who paid the rent before.
The register says who paid it before. For eleven years.
She held the pen for a long time.
She thought about a column of black ink in a big brown book. Paid. Paid. Paid. Month after month. A name in a column. Not even a signature. Only a clerk's copy of a name. Two letters and a word.
I passed my brother on a stair, she had written once, in this book, on a night in the winter. Months ago. When she had not known what she meant by it. When she had only known that she had stood on the great stair of the Bourse and watched a lamp boy with a limp go past her up the steps, with Gage's big hand on his head, lifting him over a step, and something in her had turned over, like a key in a lock, and had not turned back.
She had not written his name then. She had not known it.
She knew it now. Everybody in the city knew it. It was nailed to posts. SOUGHT.
She wrote it.
C. Vane paid an old man's rent for eleven years. Out of his own wage. Even when he had nothing. Remy says so, and so does the register. The old man took him in, Remy says, when he was a boy with nothing in his head.
I was a girl with nothing in my head. Somebody took me in too.
I do not know who.
She put the pen down.
She sat with both hands flat on the table, one on each side of the book, the way she sat at her desk downstairs, and looked at the blue tin.
She did not pick it up. She knew nothing would come.
After a while she got up and went to the round window and stood looking out.
The city was very quiet. The snow lay on every roof and every bridge and every chimney pot, smooth and blue-white under the lamps. The river was black between its white banks. Along the quays on the far side, the Ash lamps burned in their long blue lines, and beyond them, the roofs of the old town went up the hill in steps, jumbled and dark, with here and there a yellow window.
And down to the left, where the river bent, there were the arches.
The Dye-Works arches. A long row of them, low and black, under the old road bridge that nobody used anymore, where the dyers had worked a hundred years ago, before the Ministry moved them out. She could just see them from her window. A line of dark mouths in a wall of dark brick, at the edge of the water. Marjory Kell's arch was the third from the river. It was dark now. It would be dark for a long time.
She let her eyes go along the row.
And near the far end, in one of the last arches, the one nearest the old indigo vats, where the brick was stained blue to the height of a man, there was a light.
Very small. Very low. Yellow, not blue. A candle, or an oil lamp, behind something that was not quite a curtain, so that the light came round its edges in a thin line.
It had not been there last night. She had looked. She looked every night now. She did not know why.
It was there tonight.
Elara stood at the window and watched it for a long time.
Hands stained blue to the wrist. Believed lately returned to the city. Believed to have kept a shop under the Dye-Works arches, at the old indigo vats.
Sought at the Archon's own request.
The Archon has given it to someone else. Someone who does not need a name.
She found that her hand was flat against the cold glass of the window. She did not remember putting it there.
She did not know the woman. She had never seen her. She did not know if it was her at all. It could be anybody, in that arch, with a candle. A tramp out of the cold. A boy with a girl. Anybody.
But she stood with her hand on the glass and watched the little yellow line of light, very far off, at the edge of the black water, and she found that she was saying something. Under her breath. Over and over. Not words, quite. Only a shape that words might go into.
Put it out. Put it out. Please put it out.
The light did not go out.
She went back to the table, after a long time, and sat down, and picked up her pen, and wrote one more line in the book of days, at the bottom of the page, small.
There is a light in the last arch. I hope I am wrong.
Then she closed the book, and put it on the shelf beside the blue tin, and lay down on her bed in the dark with her eyes open, and listened to the city, and did not sleep.
