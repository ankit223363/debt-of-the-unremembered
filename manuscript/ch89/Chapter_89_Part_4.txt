Chapter 89: The Blue Wagon, Part 4 of 5: Thursby
Thursby
The maid was on the stairs when Elara came out into the hall.
She was sitting on the fourth step from the bottom, among the tall jars of dead lilies, with her knees together and her red hands in her lap. A short woman, round in the face and round in the body, in a black dress with a white apron over it that had not been washed in some days. Her cap was crooked. Her face was blotched and swollen from crying, and her eyes were small and red, and she was not crying now. She was only sitting. The way a person sits who has cried until there is nothing left, and does not know yet what to do instead.
She stood up when she saw Elara. She did it badly, the way servants do when they have been caught sitting. One hand went to her cap.
"Ma'am," she said. "I beg your pardon, ma'am."
"Sit," said Elara. "Please."
Thursby did not sit. She stood on the fourth step with her hand on her cap and looked at Elara, and then past her, at the way in to the morning room, and then back.
"Is it done, ma'am?" she said. "The weighing?"
"It is begun," said Elara. "It will take some days."
"And will they sell the house?"
Elara did not answer at once.
"I don't know," she said. "Not yet. The heir is under age. It will depend on what is owed." She paused. "I do not think it will be soon."
Thursby nodded. Slowly. As if that were something, at least.
"Twenty years," she said. "I've been with her twenty years. Since I was a girl. I did her hair every morning. She had beautiful hair. Long, it was, right down her back, and thick, and she'd have it up in the crown every day, with the pins, the Ost-glass ones her mother left her. Five of them. Every day. Even Sundays." Her mouth shook. "They went. Did you know? Before the funeral. All five. I never knew where. She never said. She just did her hair with the black combs that morning, and the pins were gone, and I never saw them again."
Elara did not know what to say.
"I do her hair now," said Thursby. "Every morning. The same as always. Only she doesn't tell me how she wants it anymore. She used to tell me. Higher, Thursby. No, not like that. Again. She was very particular." She wiped her eyes with the back of her red wrist. "Now she just sits. And I do it how I think. And I can't do it like she did. I never could. I can't get the crown right without her telling me."
Elara stood in the black and white hall among the drifts of brown petals and listened.
She did not know why she was listening. It was not the Bank's business. A maid's grief was not part of the estate. It could not be weighed. It would not go in the green audit book.
But she listened.
"Thursby," she said, when the maid had stopped. "The night before you found her. The night of the funeral. Was there anybody in the house?"
Thursby looked at her.
Something came into her small red eyes. Not fear, exactly. Something older and more careful than fear. The look of a servant who has been asked a question about her mistress's business by a stranger, and is deciding how much of the answer belongs to her to give.
"The Bank wants to know?" she said.
"I want to know," said Elara.
Thursby was quiet a long time.
Then she came down off the fourth step. Close. Closer than a maid comes to a lady from the Bank. So close that Elara could smell the starch in her dirty apron, under the lilies. And she said, very low, the way you say a thing in a house where you are not sure the walls are only walls:
"There was a card."
Elara waited.
"On the morning of the funeral," said Thursby. "On the silver tray. In the hall. Cream. Folded. Sealed with red wax. Not a crest on it. Just a circle. Pressed into the wax. A plain circle." She drew it in the air with one red finger. Round. Closed. "She took it off the tray before Master Rowan could see what it said. Put it in her glove. I saw her do it."
"And that night?"
"That night," said Thursby, "after the funeral, she sent us all to bed. Early. The ninth bell. All of us. Pruett and me and the girls in the kitchen and the boy that does the boots. She said she wanted the house quiet. She said she was expecting somebody, and she would let them in herself, and she did not want to be disturbed for anything." Her voice dropped lower. "She'd never done that before. Not in twenty years. Let somebody in herself. She never touched a door handle in her life if there was a servant to touch it for her."
"Did you see who came?"
Thursby shook her head.
"I went up," she said. "Like I was told. To my room, at the top, under the roof. But I didn't sleep. I couldn't. I lay there, and I listened. And at the eleventh bell, or near it, I heard the front open. And shut. And feet in the hall. Two pair. Hers. And another." She stopped. "Light. Very light. Like somebody who didn't weigh much. Or didn't want to be heard."
"And then?"
"And then the morning room. The two of them. And the way in shutting." Thursby swallowed. "And then nothing. A long time. No voices. I listened and listened and I never heard a voice. Not hers. Not anybody's. And that was strange too. Because when she had people in, you always heard her. She had a voice that carried."
Elara stood very still.
"I must have slept," said Thursby. "In the end. Because the next thing, it was light. And I came down to do her fire. And she was sitting by the window. In her black, from the funeral. With her gloves still on. And her face turned to the glass." She stopped. Her mouth shook again. "And I said, Ma'am, you've been up all night, you'll catch your death. And she didn't turn round. And I went round in front of her, and I looked in her face."
She did not go on.
She did not need to.
Elara looked at her a long moment.
"Was there anything left?" she said. "In the morning room. Anything that was not there before. A cup. A glove. Anything."
Thursby thought about it. Hard. Elara watched her think.
"No, ma'am," she said at last. "Nothing. The fire was out. The lilies were the same. Her chair was where it always is. There was nothing." She hesitated. "Only the card. The one with the circle. It was on the floor by her feet. Open. And there was nothing written in it. Nothing at all. Just white."
"Where is it now?"
"I put it on the fire," said Thursby. "I don't know why. It frightened me. A card with nothing in it. I put it on the fire and watched it go."
She looked at Elara with her small red eyes.
"Was that wrong, ma'am?" she said.
Elara thought of the circle in the purple ink, on the page for the funeral. And beside the line for the ninth of the Thaw month, six years ago. Round. Closed. Nothing inside it.
"No," she said. "I don't think it was wrong."
And she reached out, before she knew she was going to do it, and touched the maid's arm. Lightly. Through her white glove. Through the black sleeve. Just for a moment. The way Gage had touched her shoulder in the morning room.
Thursby stared at her hand.
Elara took it away.
"You should sleep," she said. "Tonight. Properly. Ask Pruett to sit with her."
"Yes, ma'am."
"And Thursby."
"Ma'am?"
"Do her hair the way you think," said Elara. "It will be right. She would not have kept you twenty years if you could not do it."
The maid looked at her. And her face crumpled up, all at once, like a child's, and she sat down on the fourth step again among the dead lilies and put her red hands over her face.
Elara went out past her, into the brown afternoon, with Gage behind her, a step back and a little to the left.
The Intake Hall
She did not go straight up to the Fourth Desk when the cab brought her back.
She went down.
She did not decide to. Her feet decided. She came in at the side gate, in the brown dusk, with Gage behind her carrying the ledgers, and instead of turning left for the great stair and the third floor, she turned right. For the back stair. The narrow stone one that the porters used, that went down, and down, six flights, under the Bank, to the river side.
Gage did not ask why. He followed.
It got colder as they went down. It always did. The stone of the walls went from dry to damp to wet, and the air went from the smell of polish and paper to the smell of the river, and then to something else, under the river smell. Sharp and bitter and green. Grayroot. The Bank burned it in little iron dishes in the Intake Hall, all day and all night, to keep the people in the pens quiet. Elara had never liked the smell. She had smelled it, she thought, on the first morning, before she smelled anything else.
The Intake Hall was at the bottom.
It was a long low vault, under the river side of the Bank, with an arched roof of black brick and iron doors at the far end that opened onto the water. A black counter ran across it, near the stair, with a big book on it, and a lamp. And past the counter, in two rows, the pens.
Twelve of them. Six on each side. Iron bars, floor to roof. A gate in each, with a lock. Straw on the floor. A bucket. And in some of them, people.
Not many tonight. Elara counted as she came down the last steps. Three pens with somebody in. An old man, lying on his side in the straw with his face to the wall. A woman, sitting up, rocking, slowly, forward and back, forward and back, with her arms round her knees. Both of them with the look. The empty look. They had come in from the Bourse chute, or from the docks, or from wherever the Bank's carts went. They would go out again, in a day or two, to wherever the book said.
And in pen two, the girl.
She was sitting in the straw by the bars, with her back to them. A small girl. Eight, perhaps. Thin, in a gray shift that was too big for her, with her hair cut short and ragged. And she was humming.
Elara stopped at the bottom of the stair and listened.
Four notes. The same four, over and over. Low, and then up, and then up again, and then down. Very quiet. So quiet you could hardly hear it under the hiss of the grayroot in its iron dishes and the slap of the river against the iron doors. Four notes. And a pause. And four notes again.
She had been humming them, Elara thought, since Midwinter.
"Auditor."
Tom Briggs had come out from behind the counter.
He was a big man. Broad, and red, with a big red face and big red hands, and eyes that hung down at the outer corners like a bloodhound's. He had been Keeper of Intake for nineteen years, and a porter before that, and a boat boy on the river before that, and he moved like a man who had carried heavy things all his life and expected to go on carrying them.
"Mister Briggs," said Elara.
"Didn't expect you down tonight." He looked at her, and at Gage behind her with the ledgers, and at her face. His bloodhound eyes did not change. "Something wrong?"
"No," said Elara. "I only came to see her."
She nodded at pen two.
Tom Briggs looked at the girl too.
"She's well enough," he said. "Eats what I give her. Sleeps. Hums." He was quiet a moment. "Always the same four. Never stops. I've got so I hum them myself, going home. My wife asked me last week what the tune was. I said I didn't know."
"She's still down as held?"
"Still held." He went back behind the black counter and opened the big book, and turned the pages back, a long way, with his big red thumb, and laid his finger on a line. "As held. By the Fourth Desk. Under audit." He read it out slowly, the way he read everything. "Your words, Auditor. Midwinter. I wrote them down just like you said them."
"Has anybody asked for her?"
Tom Briggs looked up.
"Twice," he said.
Elara waited.
"A man from the Treasurer's rooms," said Briggs. "The week after Midwinter. The one who whistles. He wanted her for the Spire cart. To the Choir, he said. He had a paper." He shrugged his big shoulders. "I showed him the book. Held. Under audit. He said audit wasn't a clause. I said it was now, the Auditor said so, and if he wanted it took off he could go up and ask her himself. He went off whistling. He didn't come back."
"And the second time?"
Tom Briggs did not answer at once.
He looked down at the book. At the line under his finger.
"The night before the Inquisitor went north," he said. "Late. Near the tenth bell. A carriage at the river doors. No crest. Gray." He paused. "And a man got down. In a gray coat. Thin. Tired-looking. With his right glove off."
Elara did not move.
"He didn't ask for her," said Tom Briggs. "Not exactly. He came in and stood there, where you're standing, and looked at her a long time. And listened. And then he looked at the book. At that line. Your line." His finger tapped it. "And he said, Good. Like that. Quiet. Good. And then he said, Keep her held, Mister Briggs. As long as the Auditor's line stands. And he went out again, and the carriage went off along the quay."
He looked up at her with his bloodhound eyes.
"I thought you'd want to know," he said.
Elara stood at the bottom of the stair, in the bitter green smell of the grayroot, and did not say anything for a long moment.
The night before he went north. After the funeral, then. After the card with the circle on Cressida Thorne's silver tray. He had come down here, to the river doors, late, on his way out of the city, to stand where she stood and listen to a child hum, and read her one true line in Tom Briggs's book, and say Good.
She did not know what to make of it. She found that she did not know what to make of anything Silas Kross did. Only that it kept turning out, when she looked closely, to be on the same side of the page as the things she had chosen herself.
"Thank you, Mister Briggs," she said.
"You want me to keep doing what I'm doing, Auditor?"
"Yes."
"Right you are."
She went to the bars of pen two.
She did not open the gate. She stood outside the bars and looked down at the girl in the straw, with her back to her, humming. Four notes. Low, up, up, down. And a pause.
Elara did not know the tune.
But she found, standing there, that she was counting the pause. The space between the end of the four notes and the beginning of them again. One. Two. Three. And at three, every time, exactly, the girl began again.
As if she were waiting, every time, for somebody to answer.
As if the answer was supposed to come in the space, and every time it did not, and she started again.
Elara stood by the bars and listened to the space.
And behind her, very quietly, so quietly she thought for a moment she had imagined it, she heard Gage hum.
One note.
Low. Deep. In the space, after the girl's four. Just one. As if he had not been able to help it.
The girl stopped humming.
She stopped. Dead. In the middle of the pause. And the whole long vault went quiet, the grayroot hissing in its dishes and the river slapping at the iron doors and nothing else, and the girl sat very still in the straw with her back to the bars.
And then, slowly, she began again. Four notes.
But slower, this time. And at the end of them she left the space a little longer. One. Two. Three. Four.
Waiting.
Gage did not hum again.
Elara turned round and looked at him. He was standing at the foot of the stair with the ledgers held against his chest and his slate hanging on its cord, and his wooden face showed nothing at all. Nothing. As if he had not made a sound. As if he had never made a sound in his life.
She did not ask him.
She went back up the six flights of the back stair, in the cold, with Gage behind her. And all the way up, in her head, she heard the four notes, and the space after them, and the one low note that had gone into the space and stopped a child humming.
The Summons
Penn was back when she came into the Fourth Desk.
He was standing by his desk nearest the way in, with his coat still on and his face pink from the cold and the stairs, and he jumped when she came through, the way he always jumped. He was seventeen, and pink all over, ears and hands and the end of his nose, and he had been at the Fourth Desk a year and still looked at Gage's bench every time he passed it as if it might bite.
"Auditor," he said. "There's a summons. For you. It came while you were up the Hill. Mister Harrow put it on your desk."
Mister Harrow did not look up from the day ledger. But his pen had stopped.
Elara went down the room to her desk by the window.
It lay in the middle of the green leather, squared to the edge. A long envelope, heavy, cream, with a black border round it a finger wide, the way the Council's papers always had. And sealed with black wax. And pressed into the wax, not a crest, not a key and pen, not anything she had seen on a Council paper before.
A hand. Open. Palm out. The Spire's mark. She had seen it in blue on the doors of the closed black carts that went up Exchange Street on Fridays.
She broke it with her thumbnail, through her glove.
To the Auditor of the Bank of Veridia, at the Fourth Desk.
You are called to attend the Council in its chamber tomorrow, Friday, at the second bell after noon, to witness under clause eleven the reading of a report received this evening by the birds of the Spire from the north.
You will bring the record.
By the hand of the Archon's secretary.
L.
Elara read it twice.
Then she laid it down on the green leather beside the folded note from the Treasurer, the one that said Kindly, and looked at the two of them side by side.
By the birds of the Spire. From the north.
The pigeons. Everybody knew about the pigeons. The Spire kept them, high up, in a loft at the top of the black needle, gray birds with bands on their legs, and they went out with men who went far, and came back with little rolled papers tied to them. Fast. Faster than any horse. Faster than anything. A day and a night from the Glass, they said, if the wind was right.
A report from the north.
From the Glass.
She thought of the line on the Thursday list. Believed north. On the Glass.
She thought of a man on his knees under a dome, holding it up.
And she thought of the Inquisitor, in his gray coat, with his right glove off, standing at the bottom of the back stair, saying Good.
"Is it bad, Auditor?" said Penn, from the far end of the room.
She looked up. He was still standing by his desk in his coat, pink, anxious, the way he always was.
"I don't know yet," said Elara.
"It's from the Archon's man," said Penn. "Mister Lucian. He brought it himself. Up the stairs. I've never seen him on our stairs. He stood just there." He pointed, a little wildly, at the place by Gage's bench. "And he looked at Gage. And Gage looked at him. For a long, long time. And then he gave it to Mister Harrow and went away." He swallowed. "He didn't say anything to me at all."
"No," said Elara. "He wouldn't."
She looked at Gage.
Gage was on his bench, the way he always was, with his slate on its cord and his hands on his knees. He looked back at her. His wooden face showed nothing.
But she thought, though she could not have said why, that he had not liked Mister Lucian looking at him.
"Go home, Penn," she said. "It's late. Thank you."
"Yes, Auditor. Good night, Auditor."
He went, quickly, with a last glance at Gage's bench. And Elara sat down at her desk, and opened the drawer, and took out her book of days. The black cloth one, with the red spine.
She opened it at the day. At the page for today. Thursday. And she wrote it all down, the way she wrote every day down, because tomorrow, or the day after, or a year from now, she might lay her hand on something for the Bank and the Sight might take this day, this one, and then the book would be all she had of it.
She wrote it carefully. In her small neat hand.
The Thursday list. VANE, CORIN still first. New line: Believed north. On the Glass.
Sterling's note. Weigh the estate, Auditor. Kindly.
Cressida Thorne. Emptied. The book clean. The last thing: a pale gray glove, waving.
She stopped. She looked at that line a long time. Then she went on.
Her day book. The ninth of the Thaw month, six years ago. Bank. The Fourth Desk. The girl. Done. With a circle. The same circle as on the day of her brother's funeral.
Thursby. A card with a circle. A visitor at the eleventh bell. Light feet. No voices. The card blank.
Rowan Thorne. He said no. I wrote a true line. I chose it. I think this is what the Inquisitor does.
The girl in pen two. Still held. Briggs says the Inquisitor came to look at her the night before he went north and said Good.
Gage hummed one note. The girl stopped.
A summons. The Council. Tomorrow. The second bell after noon. A report from the north, by the Spire's birds. Clause eleven. Bring the record. L.
She stopped again.
And then, under all of it, after a long time, she wrote one more line. Smaller than the rest. So small that she had to bend close to the page to see it.
I am afraid of what the report will say.
She looked at it.
She had never written that before. Not in six years. Not I am afraid. She had written what she saw, and what was said, and what was done. She had not written what she felt. There had not been much, most days, to write.
She did not cross it out.
She blotted it, and shut the book, and put it back in the drawer.
And at the middle desk, Mister Harrow cleaned his pen on his little square of black cloth, and laid it in its groove, and looked at the window, and the dark, and the blue lamps on the quay, and the first thin flakes of snow beginning to come down past the glass into the black river.
He dipped his pen once more, and wrote one more word at the end of the last line in the day ledger, under the morning's Ash.
Snow.
