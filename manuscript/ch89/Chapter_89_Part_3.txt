Chapter 89: The Blue Wagon, Part 3 of 5: No
No
She heard the front way open before she had moved.
The bolts. Top and bottom. Somebody coming in from the street, with a key. And then feet on the black and white floor of the hall, quick and hard, a young man's feet in boots with iron at the heels, crunching through the dead petals. And Pruett's voice, low, saying something. And another voice answering, louder, shorter. A young man's voice, trying to sound older.
And then a small sound, a sniff, a woman's: Thursby, who had come down the stairs.
And the feet came across the hall to the morning room, and did not knock, and came in.
Elara turned on the small seat.
A cadet stood just inside the way in.
She knew the coat before she saw the face. Gray. The Ministry's gray, but the cut of the cadets, short and tight in the body, with a high collar and a single row of plain gray buttons and no badge on the shoulder, because cadets had not yet earned one. And a cadet's cap, under his arm. And a cadet's haircut, the one they gave them all, at the barber's in Mercer Street: short at the sides, short at the back, so short you could see the pale skin of the head through it, and a little longer on top, combed flat.
He was tall. Taller than Elara. Taller than Pruett, almost, though he could not have been more than sixteen. Thin with it, the way boys are thin who have grown too fast in one winter, all wrists and neck and long bones, so that his cadet's coat, which must have fitted him at the start of the year, was already short in the sleeves.
And his face.
Elara had not seen it before. She was sure of that, though she was never sure of anything. But she felt as if she had. It was a long face, with a straight nose and a strong jaw, the face of a man that had been put, a little too early, on a boy. Serious. Pale. With a frown between the eyebrows that looked as if it lived there. And the eyes were dark, and very steady, and they were looking at her.
They were somebody else's eyes. That was what she thought. They did not go with the face. The face was a man's. The eyes were a girl's. She did not know why she thought that.
He held out something to Pruett, without looking at him. A card. Small and white. Pruett took it, and looked at it, and brought it across the room to Elara, between two fingers, the way a footman brings a card.
She took it.
Plain white. A cadet's card, the kind the Ministry printed for them by the hundred. And on it, in black ink, in a young man's hand, quick and leaning, a name. R. Thorne. And the R was hooked. The tail of it curled back on itself at the bottom, like a fish hook, like the tail of a cat curling round its feet.
"Rowan Thorne," said the cadet. "This is my aunt's house. I live here." He did not come any further into the room. "Pruett says you are from the Bank."
"I am the Auditor," said Elara. "From the Fourth Desk."
He looked at her.
"The Auditor," he said.
"Yes."
"I've heard of you."
She did not say anything to that. A great many people had heard of her. Very few of them had heard anything kind.
"The Treasurer has sent me to weigh your aunt's estate," she said. "The Council must fill her seat. The Bank must weigh what she leaves before it can be filled. There are debts, I am told. And holdings." She paused. "And you."
"Me," said Rowan Thorne.
"You are her heir. Under the law. Since your father's death. And you are under age." She said it plainly. It was only the truth. "The Bank will need to see what is left to you. And what is owed. And to whom."
He did not answer.
He stood just inside the way in, in his short gray coat, with his cap under his arm, and looked at her. And then, slowly, his eyes went past her. To the window. To the rose-colored seat. To the straight gray back and the calm face turned to the brown light.
Something went across his own face then. Elara saw it. It was there and gone so fast that she could not have said what it was. But she knew it. She had felt it on her own face often enough, in the mirror in her three rooms under the Bank roof, on the mornings when she read in her book of days about a day she could not remember.
He looked away from his aunt.
"I'll need the family papers," said Elara. "The deeds of the house. The Council papers. Her private accounts. Any letters to do with money. Her lawyer's name, if she had one." She looked at the little walnut desk. "They're not here. I've looked. There's only the day book here."
"No," said Rowan. "They're not here."
"Where are they?"
He did not say anything for a long moment.
And then, slowly, without taking his eyes off her, he put his right hand flat on the front of his gray coat. On the left side. Over the place where the inside pocket would be. Where something made a square shape under the cloth. Something flat. Thick. Tied, she thought, with tape.
"Here," he said.
"On you."
"Yes."
"All of them?"
"All of them." He kept his hand where it was. "I took them out of her desk the morning we found her. Before the physicians came. Before anybody came." He paused. "Before anybody could come and take them."
Elara looked at the square shape under his hand.
"I shall need to see them," she said. "For the audit."
And Rowan Thorne folded his arms.
He did it slowly. Deliberately. The cap went under one arm, and the other arm came across it, and the hand that had been on his coat went into the crook of his elbow, and he stood there, in the way in of his aunt's morning room, among the dead lilies, sixteen years old, with his arms folded over the papers, and looked at the Auditor of the Bank.
"No," he said.
Just that.
Not loud. Not angry. He did not raise his voice at all. He said it the way you say a thing that you have decided a long time ago, lying awake, going over it, and are now only telling somebody.
No.
Elara looked at him.
She had heard no before. Not often. People did not often say it to the Fourth Desk. They said Of course, Auditor, and At once, Auditor, and I'm sure there's been some mistake, Auditor, and they gave her what she asked for, in the end, because the Bank was the Bank and she was its hand. A few had said no. Men, mostly. Old men, with lawyers. They had said it loudly, and gone red, and talked about their rights, and in the end the Bank had had its way with all of them.
None of them had said it like this.
She looked at his folded arms. At his frown. At the girl's eyes in the man's face, steady, waiting.
And behind her, at the way in to the hall, she heard Gage, who had stepped aside to let the boy in, step back again into his place. Quietly. Not toward the boy. Just back to where he was.
She did not tell him to do anything.
She found that she did not want to.
"May I ask why?" she said.
The Roof
Rowan did not answer at once.
He stood with his arms folded and looked at her, and Elara watched him decide whether to tell her. She could see him doing it. It was all in his face, the way things are in the faces of the young, before they have learned to put them away. He looked at her green coat. At her white gloves. At Gage, by the way in, with his slate. At the green audit book open on the little walnut desk, and the pen beside it, still wet.
And at her face.
He looked at her face a long time.
"Because they're ours," he said at last.
"The Bank—"
"I know what the Bank says." His voice did not rise. It only went harder, a little, at the edges. "I'm a cadet, Auditor. At the Long Office. I've copied out the law about estates forty times. I know what the Bank says. The Bank says it weighs what's left, and then it takes what's owed, and then it gives the rest to the heir. When the heir is of age. And until the heir is of age, the Bank holds it. For him." He paused. "Kindly."
Elara did not move.
"I've seen what the Bank holds," said Rowan. "For people. Kindly. I've seen the lists. I've copied them out too." His arms tightened over his coat. "I don't want the Bank holding anything of ours. Not the house. Not the papers. Not anything. Not after—"
He stopped.
His eyes went to the window again. To the rose-colored seat. And came back.
"Not after what's been done," he said. "To us."
Elara did not answer.
She found that she could not think of anything to say that would not be a lie. And she did not want to lie to him. She did not know why. She lied to people on the Bank's business, sometimes. Not often. Not lies, exactly. Things that were nearly true. But she did not want to say a thing that was nearly true to this boy, in this room, with his arms folded over his family's papers and his aunt sitting emptied by the window.
So she said nothing.
And Rowan looked at her, and something in his face changed. A little. As if her saying nothing had told him more than anything she could have said.
"You were at the Bourse," he said.
It was not a question.
"Yes," said Elara.
"On Midwinter night. When the dome came down."
"When it nearly came down. Yes."
"They say the Auditor was there. For the auction. For the box." He was watching her. "They say you sealed the box."
"I did."
He was quiet a moment.
"I wasn't there," he said. "I was at my desk. At the Long Office. On the late watch. Copying." His voice had gone strange. Lower. "I heard it. The groan. The whole city heard it. Like a ship's hull, Mister Orme said, the old clerk. Like a ship breaking its back on a rock. And then the bells."
Elara remembered the groan. She remembered the bells.
"And then the next day," said Rowan, "the talk. Everybody had a piece of it. Mister Orme. The other cadets. The man who sells chestnuts on Mercer Street. The barber." He stopped. "They said the dome was coming down. They said a man held it up. A harvester. They said he knelt in the middle of the floor with his hands on the stone and held the whole roof up on his back, like a man holding up a cart."
"Yes," said Elara.
"And they said—" He stopped again. His jaw worked. When he went on, his voice was very careful, the way a man's voice is careful when he is carrying something full to the brim across a room. "They said there was a girl. Getting people out. While he held it. Going in and out under the roof. Bringing people out by the arm. Over and over. A girl with short hair, in a borrowed coat, with a cut on her hand."
He looked at Elara.
"My sister got people out," said Rowan Thorne. "While the harvester held the roof."
It was not a question either.
But he was asking.
Elara could see that he was asking. He had been told. By the barber, and the chestnut man, and the old clerk, and the other cadets. Perhaps by others. He had been told, and he had believed it, and he did not know whether he was right to believe it. Because he had not seen. Because nobody who told him had seen, either, not really. Because it was only talk, and the city was always talking, and half of what it said was nonsense.
And she had been there.
She thought of it. She let herself. She did not, often. It was one of the days she had kept. She had been lucky; the Sight had not taken it. It was still there, whole, in her, and in her book of days, every line of it.
The kitchen passage, at the back of the great hall. The smoke. The noise. The terrible noise of the roof, the groan going on and on over her head like a great beast in pain. The people running past her, screaming, falling. Gage's hands under her arms, lifting her, carrying her, as if she weighed nothing. And then, at the end of the passage, where it opened into the hall, she had looked back.
And she had seen him.
A man. Kneeling. In the middle of the floor of the great hall, under the dome. With his hands flat on the stone. With his head down. With his back bent under something she could not see, something enormous, the whole weight of the dome pressing down on him, so that she could see it in the shape of his shoulders. Holding it. Not moving. And the light. There had been a light, round him, in the dark and the smoke. A pale light, like a pillar, going up from his bent back to the cracked roof.
And a girl.
Thin. Quick. Short-haired. In a borrowed coat that was too big for her. Going in under the roof, into the smoke, and coming out again with somebody by the arm. A fat man in a velvet coat. A woman with blood in her hair. A servant girl in a torn apron, crying. Gage had gone in too, three times, and brought out three more, but Elara had hardly seen him. She had been watching the girl. Going in again. And again. With a bloody hand. Not looking up at the roof at all. As if she knew that the man kneeling under it would not let it fall, and did not need to look.
Elara had seen them both.
And she had felt, watching the girl go in under the roof, something she had not felt in six years. She did not have a name for it. She had cried, afterward. In the pillar of light. She had remembered a field of long wet grass, and a boy's hand, and somebody laughing. Her own laugh. She had not laughed in six years. She had not known she could.
She looked at the boy in the way in, with his arms folded and his sister's eyes.
"I saw them both," said Elara.
Rowan's arms came loose.
Not all the way. Not quite. But she saw them loosen. She saw his hands slide a little in the crooks of his elbows, the way a man's hands slide on a rope when he has been holding on too hard for too long.
"You saw her," he said.
"Yes."
"With your own eyes."
"With my own eyes. From the kitchen passage. I saw her bring out four people. Five. I stopped counting." She paused. "She did not look up at the roof once."
Rowan did not say anything.
He stood in the way in of the morning room, among the dead lilies, and looked at her, and his face did a thing that Elara had not seen a face do in a long time. It went young. All at once. The man's face that had been put on the boy too early slid off it, for a moment, and underneath there was only a boy. Sixteen. Thin. Tired. Whose father was dead and whose aunt was emptied and whose sister had gone north on the Glass with a harvester the whole city was hunting.
And then it came back. He put it back. She watched him do it.
But he went on looking at her.
And she saw him open his mouth. And she saw him almost say something else. Something more. It was there, in his face, behind his teeth. She could almost see the shape of it. A question. Or a thing he knew, that he had not told anyone. About the harvester, perhaps. About the man on his knees under the dome.
He shut his mouth again.
He did not say it.
He looked at her a moment longer, with his sister's eyes. And then he looked away, at his aunt by the window, and Elara saw his throat move.
"Thank you," said Rowan Thorne. "For telling me."
A True Line
After he had gone up the stairs, Elara sat for a long time at the little walnut desk and did not write anything.
She could hear him going up. His boots on the stairs, the iron heels, through the dead petals. And then a door, high up, on the second floor at the back. And then nothing. And then, a little later, the maid's crying stopping, all at once, as if somebody had put an arm round her.
The green audit book lay open in front of her.
Estate of Councillor C. Thorne. Weighed this day by the Fourth Desk, under the Treasurer's hand.
That was all she had written. One line. In Cressida Thorne's black ink. The rest of the page was empty. Ruled, and clean, and waiting. Like the pages of the book she had opened with two fingers on a thin wrist an hour ago.
She looked at it.
She knew what she was meant to write next. She had written it a hundred times, in a hundred houses, on the Bank's business. It was always the same. Papers of the estate examined, as follows. And then the list. The deeds. The bonds. The debts. The holdings. Each one with its weight. And at the bottom, the balance. And then, under that, if the heir was under age: Estate to be held by the Bank until the heir is of age.
And she had not examined the papers. Because the heir had them, inside his coat, over his heart, and he had folded his arms over them and said no.
She could make him.
She knew that. She sat at the walnut desk and thought about it, plainly, the way she thought about everything. The Bank could make him. She could go back to the Fourth Desk and write a note to the Treasurer, and the Treasurer could write a note to the Ministry, and the Ministry could send two men in gray to the Long Office, where the cadets sat, and they could take the boy out of his seat and into a room and take the papers out of his coat. It would take a day. Two, perhaps. It would not be difficult.
She thought of the boy's face, when it went young.
She thought of Not after what's been done. To us.
She thought of the line in purple ink, on the page for the ninth of the Thaw month. Bank. The Fourth Desk. The girl. Done.
And the circle.
She picked up the pen.
She dipped it in the black ink. She held it over the clean page, and she thought, very carefully, about what was true.
It was true that she had come to weigh the estate.
It was true that she had looked for the papers, and that they were not in the house.
It was true that the heir had them.
It was true that the heir had not given them to her.
And it was true that, under the law, the heir to an estate had a right to hold the papers of that estate until they were properly asked for in writing, by the Bank, with the Treasurer's seal. She had not asked in writing. She had asked out loud, in a morning room, among dead lilies, with nothing but a folded note in her pocket that said Kindly.
She wrote:
Estate papers held by the heir. Not available to the Bank.
And stopped.
She looked at the line.
It was perfectly true. Every word of it. Estate papers held by the heir. They were. Not available to the Bank. They were not. Not today. Not to her. Not in this room.
She had not lied.
And yet.
She sat with the pen in her hand and looked at the line, and something cold went down her back, slowly, from the nape of her neck to the base of her spine. Not fear. Something else. The feeling you get when you put your foot down on a stair in the dark and it is not where you thought it would be, and you find, catching yourself, that you have been on a different stair all along.
She had not lied.
She had only chosen.
There had been other lines she could have written. They were all true too. Heir refused to give up the papers when asked. That was true. Heir carried papers on his person; Ministry to be informed. That was true. Heir under age; papers to be taken into the Bank's keeping at once. That was true too, or would be, the moment she wrote it, because the Fourth Desk's record was the record, and whatever it said became so.
The record is never wrong, the Treasurer had said.
She understood, sitting at the little walnut desk, for the first time in six years, what that meant.
It did not mean that the record was always true. It meant that the record was always the record. And the record was whatever the hand that held the pen chose to put down. Out of all the true things. One. And the rest left out.
She had chosen one.
She had chosen the one that let the boy keep his papers.
And she thought, very clearly, the way she had thought in front of the purple line: This is what he does.
The Inquisitor.
She had met him four times. In the corridors of the Bank. On the great stair at the Bourse. At the side gate, once, in the rain, by his gray carriage with no crest on its door. Each time he had stopped, and lifted his hat, and said, in his quiet tired voice, Good morning, Auditor. Even when it was not morning. And each time she had felt him look at her. Not the way most men looked. Carefully. As if he were reading a page.
She had heard things about him. Everyone had. That his reports were perfect. That every word in them was true. That nobody had ever caught him out in a lie, not the Council, not the Ministry, not the Archon's own people. And that somehow, all the same, things happened round Silas Kross that nobody had ordered. A man let go who should have been held. A cart that went the wrong way. A list that was one name short. And when anybody looked, there was always the report, perfect, true, and it never said anything that was not so.
It only did not say everything.
This is what he does, thought Elara.
And she sat very still at the little walnut desk with the pen in her hand and the true line drying on the page in front of her, and she did not know whether she was frightened, or glad.
Both, she thought.
She blotted the line. Carefully. With Cressida Thorne's silver blotter, rocking it over the wet ink, one way and then the other. And she lifted the blotter and looked at the paper under it, and there, among the ghosts of a hundred letters written backward, was her own line, backward too, faint and new, like writing seen in a mirror. The one true thing she had chosen, turned the wrong way round.
She looked at it a long time.
Then she shut the green audit book, and stood up, and put on her coat, and buttoned it to the neck.
She left the day book where it was. In the middle of the writing place, squared to the edge, the way Cressida Thorne had left it.
But before she went, she opened it once more, to the ninth of the Thaw month, six years ago. And looked at the line. And the circle.
And then, with one gloved finger, very gently, she turned down the top corner of the page.
Just a little. A small fold. A triangle, the size of a fingernail.
So that it would be easy to find again.
