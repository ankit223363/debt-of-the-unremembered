Chapter 89: The Blue Wagon, Part 2 of 5: Sight
Sight
The morning room was on the east side of the house, and the curtains in it were not drawn.
They were the only ones. Elara saw that as soon as Pruett opened the way in. The tall windows along the east wall had their curtains pulled right back, tied with tasseled cords, and the brown light of the ash-sky came in through them and lay across the room in long dull bars. It was the only daylight in the house. It made the room look like the inside of an old jar of honey.
The lilies were worst here.
They stood on every surface. On the mantelpiece, in a row of white jars. On the little tables. On the floor by the walls, in tall vases. On the windowsills. On the piano, which nobody had played, Elara thought, in a long time, because there was a jar of dead lilies on the shut lid and yellow dust all over it. They had been white. They had been the best lilies, the hothouse ones, from the glasshouses up the river at Larkbridge, that cost more each than a clerk earned in a week. And they were dying, all of them, and dead, and dropping their petals on the pale carpet in brown heaps, and the room was so thick with the sweet rotten smell of them that it was like breathing through a cloth.
There was a fire in the grate. Small, and low. Somebody had kept it in.
And by the middle window, in a high-backed seat covered in faded rose-colored silk, sat Cressida Thorne.
Elara had seen her before. At the Council. At the Bourse, on the night of Midwinter, from a distance, in white silk with three ropes of pearls, standing among the other old families like a tall white candle. A hard woman, Mister Harrow had said. She had looked it. A long straight back, and a long straight nose, and gray hair piled up in a crown on her head, and eyes that went over a room the way an auditor goes over a ledger, looking for the mistake.
She did not look like that now.
She was sitting very straight still. That had not gone. Her back did not touch the back of the seat. Her hands lay in her lap, one over the other, in black kid gloves. She wore a gray gown, plain, high at the neck, with no lace and no jewels. Somebody had put it on her. Somebody had done her hair, too, not well, in a loose knot at the back of her head, with pieces of it coming down. Thursby, Elara supposed. Thursby, who cried upstairs.
And her face was turned to the window.
To the brown light. To the bare black trees outside, and the white houses of the Hill going down toward the river, and the brown haze over the river where the city was. She was looking at it. Or her face was pointed at it. Her eyes were open, gray, clear, perfectly calm. And they did not move. Not when Pruett said, "Councillor, the Auditor's come from the Bank." Not when Elara crossed the room through the brown petals and stood beside her. Not at all.
She was not unhappy. That was the strange thing. Elara had seen people emptied before. Not many. A few. On the Bank's business. In the cold rooms under the vault, and in the Intake Hall. And most of them had a look. A lost look. A child-in-a-crowd look, as if somewhere far down they knew there should be something there and could not find it.
Cressida Thorne did not have that look.
She looked peaceful. Calm. Like a woman sitting by a window on a quiet afternoon, waiting for nothing in particular, with nowhere to be.
"She eats when you put it in front of her," said Pruett, from the way in. "She drinks. She sleeps when Thursby takes her up. She don't speak. Not a word. Not since the morning we found her." He was quiet a moment. "The physicians said she'd go on like this. Years, maybe. They said she was very strong."
Elara did not answer.
She stood beside the rose-colored seat and looked down at the woman in it. At the black gloves, one over the other, in the gray lap. At the calm, still face.
And she took off her right glove.
She did it slowly. Finger by finger. She always did. It was a thing that could not be hurried. She drew the fine white kid off her fingers one at a time, the way you draw off a bandage, and folded it once, and put it in the pocket of her green coat.
Behind her she heard Gage move. One step. On the carpet. Closer.
He always did that, too.
Elara looked at her bare right hand.
It was a thin hand. Pale, from being always gloved. The skin on the back of it was fine and dry, and the knuckles stood up a little, and the nails were cut short and square. It did not look like anything special. It did not look like a hand that could do what it could do.
Ledger-Sight. That was the Bank's name for it. The Auditor's gift. To lay a bare hand on a thing, or a person, and read its account. What was in it. What had gone out of it. The balance. The Bank had had an Auditor with the gift for as long as there had been a Bank. Elara was the latest. She did not know who had been before her. She did not know how she had got it, or when. She only knew that she had it, and that every time she used it, it cost her one day.
One day of her own past. Gone. Burned. The way a harvester burns. A whole day, morning to night, out of her own past. She never knew which one it would be. She only knew, afterward, when she went to her book of days and read it, that there was a day written there that she could not remember living.
She kept count. In the black book. Every day she had given. It was the first thing she had written in the black book, on the first morning, and the count was on the last page, and this morning it said 2,410.
She did not think about the number. She did not let herself.
She reached out her bare hand and laid two fingers on the inside of Cressida Thorne's wrist. Between the black glove and the gray cuff. On the thin skin, where the blood went.
And she looked.
It was like opening a ledger. That was always how it was, for her. She did not know how it was for anyone else. For her, it was like opening a great book and running her eye down the columns. Here is what came in. Here is what went out. Here is what is left. The years. The rooms. The faces. The words. The things loved and the things hated and the things done. Each one a line. Each one with its weight.
She opened Cressida Thorne's book.
And it was empty.
Not short. Not low. Empty. Elara ran her eye down the columns and there was nothing in them. Page after page. Column after column. Every line ruled, neat, ready, the way the lines are ruled in a new ledger before anything has been written. And nothing written. All the years gone. All the rooms. All the faces. All the words. The brother she buried. The niece who ran. The nephew upstairs. The Council seat. The lilies. Everything she had ever loved or hated or done, gone out of the book, so cleanly that you could not even see where it had been.
The balance was nothing.
Elara had never seen a book so empty. Not in the cold rooms. Not in the Intake Hall. Not anywhere. It was not the work of a harvester in a hurry. It was not the work of an accident, or a grief, or a decline. Somebody had done this slowly, and carefully, and with great skill. Somebody had taken everything. Every line. And left the pages clean.
Nearly every line.
At the very end of the book, on the very last page, in the very bottom corner, there was something.
One thing.
Small. Faint. So faint that Elara nearly did not see it. Like a footprint at the edge of a beach, where the sea has come up and gone back and taken all the other prints with it, and left just one, half filled with water, at the very top of the sand.
She bent to it.
A glove.
That was all. A glove. Pale gray. Very fine kid, like her own, but gray, the gray of a dove's breast, the gray of ash on snow. On a hand. A long hand, with long thin fingers inside the glove. Lifting. Slowly. From somewhere a long way off, across a room, or across a hall. Lifting, and turning, and moving from side to side.
Waving.
As if to say Goodbye.
Or as if to say Well done.
And then nothing. The edge of the page. The end of the book.
Elara took her hand away.
She stood very still beside the rose-colored seat. Her bare fingers were cold. She could feel them shaking, a little, and she shut them into her palm so that they would stop. Cressida Thorne had not moved. She had not felt anything. She sat with her calm face turned to the brown window, and the lilies dropped their petals round her, and the fire ticked in the grate.
And somewhere in Elara, somewhere in the six years, a day went out.
She felt it go. She always did. Not which day. Never which day. Just the going. Like a candle going out in a room behind you, in a house where you cannot see the room. A small dark, somewhere, that had not been dark before.
2,411.
She took the white glove out of her pocket and put it back on. Finger by finger. Smoothing each one down.
And behind her, Gage put his big hand, very lightly, on her shoulder, for one moment, and took it away again.
The Day Book
The writing desk stood against the north wall, between two of the tall jars of dead lilies.
It was a lady's desk. Small, and fine, and old. Walnut, Elara thought, gone dark and glossy with years of polish, with thin curved legs and little brass feet like a bird's claws. The top of it folded down to make a flat place for writing, and was covered in green leather, like her own desk at the Bank, but smaller, and finer, and stamped round the edge in gold with a pattern of leaves. Behind the writing place there were rows of little drawers and pigeonholes, each one with a tiny brass knob. And on the writing place, laid out square and neat, the way a hard woman lays things out, there were the things of a hard woman's day.
An inkstand, silver, with two glass wells. One full of black ink. One full of ink of a strange pale purple color, like the color of a lily's shadow. A silver pen. A blotter, in a silver frame, the blotting paper on it covered with the ghosts of a hundred letters written backward. A stick of red sealing wax. A small brass seal. A silver bell.
And a book.
It lay in the middle of the writing place, squared to the edge. A tall thin book, bound in violet leather, faded at the corners. On the front, in small gold letters: C. T.
Her day book. Elara had seen day books like it before. All the old families kept them. A page for every day, and on each page, in the lady's own hand, what had happened. Who came. Who was seen. What was paid. What was owed. What was said at dinner. A record. The Bank liked them. They were often more use, in an audit, than the account books, because people wrote the truth in their day books, mostly, thinking nobody else would read them.
Elara sat down at the little desk.
The seat was too small for her. She did not mind. She opened the green audit book that Gage had carried up from the cab, and laid it to one side, and dipped one of her own pens in Cressida Thorne's black ink, and wrote at the top of a clean page:
Estate of Councillor C. Thorne. Weighed this day by the Fourth Desk, under the Treasurer's hand.
And then she opened the day book.
She did not begin at the end. She never began at the end. The end was where the trouble was, and if you began there, you only saw the trouble, and not how it came. She began at the front. And the front was this year, the year that had just begun, after Midwinter. Not many pages. A dozen, perhaps, written on. Then nothing.
She read them.
The writing was small and upright and very neat. Each letter separate, like a row of soldiers. And it was in the purple ink. The lily-shadow ink. All of it. Elara had never seen anybody use purple ink for a day book. It made the pages look strange, as if they had been written by lamplight in a room full of flowers.
Midwinter. The Bourse. The Lady's masque. Wore the white. The pearls. Spoke to the Pellows and to the Duke of A. who was rude. The Treasurer bid ten thousand for Lot 41. Lost it. Bank sealed it. Then the dome. I will not write about the dome.
The day after. Pruett says the city is talking. I do not care what the city says.
A. ill. They say. I have not been sent for. I will not go unless sent for.
A. dead.
That was all. Two words, in the purple ink, and a long white space under them. Elara looked at the space. Then she turned the page.
Rowan here. He is to stay. He has the look of his father. He does not talk.
The funeral to be at St. Clement's. On Sunday. A plain box. Aurelian would want plain. A card has come.
And on the next page, the page for the funeral, only a mark.
A small circle. Drawn with the pen in the purple ink, carefully, in the middle of the page. Round. Closed. Nothing inside it.
And after that, the pages were empty.
Elara sat and looked at the circle for a long time.
She had seen it before. She did not know where. That was the trouble with her. There were a great many things she had seen before and did not know where, because the day she saw them in was gone. But she knew this mark. She knew it the way you know a face in a crowd and cannot put a name to it. A circle. Plain. Closed. In somebody's careful hand.
She did not write it down in the audit book.
She began to turn the pages back.
Not for anything. She told herself that, afterward, many times. She was not looking for anything. She was only doing what an auditor does, which is to go back through the record, slowly, page by page, and see how things came to be the way they are. Debts did not come from nowhere. Holdings did not come from nowhere. They had a history, and the history was in the day book, and so she went back through it.
Last year. Dinners. Councils. Debts paid, to the day, every one, as Mister Harrow had said. Lilies ordered from Larkbridge, every week, the bill paid on the Friday. A quarrel with the Duke of Ashcombe about a wall. A visit from her brother, A., that went badly. Another that went worse.
The year before. The same. Dinners. Councils. Lilies.
The year before that.
Elara turned the pages. Her gloved fingers moved on the thin paper, and the purple ink went by under her eyes, small and upright and neat, year after year, and she read it all, and it was all the same. The life of a hard woman on the Hill. Ordered. Careful. Paid to the day.
Five years.
Six.
And she came to the Thaw month.
She did not mean to stop there. She did not know, afterward, why she did. Only that her fingers slowed as they turned the pages, the way your feet slow when you come near a place on a road that you know, without knowing why you know it.
The Thaw month. Six years ago. The month when the ice goes out of the river and the snow goes off the roofs and the whole city drips.
The first day. The second. A dinner. A Council. The lilies.
The eighth.
The ninth.
She stopped.
The page for the ninth of the Thaw month was nearly empty. Most of the pages were full, at least half full, with the small upright purple writing. This one had only one line. In the middle of the page. Small. Neat.
Bank. The Fourth Desk. The girl. Done.
And beside it, a little to the right, a circle.
The same circle. Plain. Closed. Drawn carefully, in the purple ink, with nothing inside it.
Elara looked at it.
She did not move. She sat at the little walnut desk in the morning room full of dying lilies with the day book open in front of her, and she looked at the line on the page, and she did not move at all.
The ninth of the Thaw month.
She knew that date.
She did not have to think to know it. She did not have to go to her book of days. It was the first line in her black book, the very first, written on the first morning in her own hand in her own ink at her own desk. She knew it the way she knew her name. Better than her name. Her name had been given to her, on a slate, by a man on a bench. This she had written herself.
I do not remember anything from before the morning of the ninth of the Thaw month, six years ago.
The morning she woke.
The morning she found herself sitting at the long desk by the window at the far end of the Fourth Desk, in a dark green coat she did not know, with white gloves on her hands, and a pen in her fingers, and a blue tin in the corner of the desk that smelled of oranges. And a man on a bench by the way in with no hair and a slate, who stood up, and came down the room, and wrote on the slate, and held it up.
Your name is Elara. You are the Auditor. I am Gage.
The ninth of the Thaw month.
Bank. The Fourth Desk. The girl. Done.
Her hands had gone cold.
She noticed it from a long way off, the way you notice that a room has gone cold when you have been reading by the fire and the fire has gone out. Her fingers inside the white gloves. Cold. Colder than the room. Colder than they had been when she touched Cressida Thorne's wrist. They lay on the open page of the day book, on either side of the line in the purple ink, and she could not feel them.
The girl.
Done.
She read it again. And again. As if it might say something different if she read it enough times. As if the purple ink might move.
It did not move.
And behind her, by the way in, she heard Gage take one step forward on the carpet, and stop.
She did not turn round.
She sat very still at the little desk with the cold in her hands, and the circle on the page, and the sweet rotten smell of the lilies all round her. And she thought, very clearly, in a voice inside her head that did not seem to be her own: Somebody did this to you. On purpose. And wrote it down. And drew a circle by it. As if it were a debt that had been paid.
And then she thought, more quietly:
Cressida Thorne knew.
She looked across the room. At the rose-colored seat by the window. At the straight gray back, and the loose knot of gray hair, and the calm face turned to the brown light.
Cressida Thorne had known, for six years, who the girl at the Fourth Desk was.
And now Cressida Thorne did not know anything at all.
