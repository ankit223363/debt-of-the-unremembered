Chapter 89: The Blue Wagon, Part 1 of 5: The Thursday List
The Thursday List
The Thursday list came up to the Fourth Desk at the third bell, and CORIN VANE was still the first name on it.
Elara read it standing at her desk by the window, with her gloves on.
She always read the lists standing. She did not know why. It was one of the things she did that she had found herself doing on the first morning, six years ago, and had never thought to stop. Stand up. Read it through once, top to bottom, without stopping. Then sit down and read it again, slowly, with a pencil.
The list was one sheet of heavy cream paper, folded in three, with the Ministry's gray seal broken across the fold. It came up every Thursday from the Ministry to the Bank, in a leather case, carried by a boy in a gray coat who did not look at anybody and went away again at once. It held the names of everyone the Ministry wished the Bank to know about. Debtors who had run. Harvesters who had not reported. People whose accounts were to be frozen, whose boxes in the vault were to be held, whose names, if they came to any counter in the building, were to be sent upstairs at once.
There were thirty-one names on it this week.
The first one was in heavier ink than the rest. It had been the first name on the list every Thursday since Midwinter.
VANE, CORIN. Harvester. Unregistered since Midwinter. Description as before. Not to be approached. Report and hold.
And under it, in a smaller hand, a new line she had not seen before:
Believed north. On the Glass.
Elara read it twice. Then she folded the list in three again, along the old folds, and sat down, and read the rest with her pencil, the way she always did. Thirty names. She did not know any of them. She made a small mark by each one, a tick, to show that the Fourth Desk had seen it. Then she got up, and went to the cupboards, and filed it.
The Fourth Desk was a long narrow room on the third floor of the Bank, at the river end, and the cupboards took up the whole of the wall opposite the windows. Black. Tall. Old. Each one with a brass number on the door, worn smooth and dull by a great many hands. One to fourteen, in a row. The lists went in nine. They always had. There were six years of Thursday lists in cupboard nine, in bundles tied with gray tape, one bundle for each month, and before that, other people's years, going back and back into the dark at the bottom of the cupboard, where nobody had looked for a long time.
She opened nine. She laid the new list on top of the others. She shut it.
She did not look at eleven.
Eleven was two doors along. It was taller than the others, and wider, with a double door and a heavier lock, because eleven was the Charter cupboard. The Bank's Charter lived in it, in a box of black wood. And the old letters of the first Treasurers, in bundles. And the seals of the Bank, the old ones, the ones nobody used anymore. And, since the night of the Bourse, at the back of it, behind the Charter box, on a folded blanket, a young man named Remy Castellan.
Elara did not look at it.
She went back to her desk.
The room was quiet. It was always quiet. The three tall windows at the far end looked out over the river, and this morning the river was gray and slow and low, with a skin of dirty ice along the edges by the steps, and the far bank was hidden in a brown haze. Ash, still. It had been falling, on and off, since Midwinter. Not much. A little each day. A fine gray dust, like the dust that comes off a sack of flour. It lay on the windowsills outside, and on the roofs, and on the water, where it made the ice look old. The Ash lamps along Lantern Quay, below, were still burning blue. They had been burning blue by day for a long time now. There were notices on the posts, in the Ministry's gray, that said REPAIRS IN PROGRESS. Nobody believed them.
There were three desks in the room.
Hers was the one by the window, at the far end, where the light was best. A long dark desk with a green leather top, gone pale where her arms had rested on it for six years. Her inkstand. Her pens, in a row. Her black book, shut, at the right-hand corner, where it always lay. Her book of days, in its black cloth with the red spine, in the drawer. And at the back of the desk, in the corner, a small blue tin.
She did not open the tin. She did not need to. She knew what was in it.
The third desk, nearest the way in, was young Penn's, and it stood empty this morning, because Penn was out on the Bank's business until noon. The middle desk was Mister Harrow's.
Mister Harrow had sat at it for twenty-two years. He was sitting at it now, with his back very straight, writing in the day ledger in his small, neat, sloping hand. He was a small neat man altogether. Gray. Gray hair, cut close. Small gray whiskers, trimmed square, like two little brushes, on either side of his face. A gray coat with all its buttons done up. His umbrella stood in the corner behind his seat, furled tight, black, with a horn handle. Elara had never seen it open. Mister Harrow did not open umbrellas indoors. He did not hold with it. He had told her so on her first morning, six years ago, and she had written it down, and it was the second thing in her book of days, after her own name.
He looked up as she sat down.
"Is he still on it?" said Mister Harrow.
"Yes."
"First?"
"Yes."
Mister Harrow made a small sound through his nose. It was the only sound he ever made that was not a word. Elara had never been able to tell what it meant. Disapproval, perhaps. Or interest. Or only that his nose was cold.
"They say he held the roof up," said Mister Harrow. "At the Bourse. My wife's sister's boy was in the street. He says the whole dome was coming down and a man held it. With his hands. Like a man holding up a cart so another man can get the wheel off." He dipped his pen. "I said that was nonsense, of course."
"Of course," said Elara.
"A man can't hold up a roof."
"No."
Mister Harrow wrote a line in the day ledger. Then he stopped, and looked at what he had written, and looked out of the window at the gray river, and wrote one more word at the end of the line, the way he did every morning.
Ash.
The weather. Mister Harrow wrote the weather in the day ledger every morning. Nobody had asked him to. It was not in any rule. He had simply done it for twenty-two years, one word at the end of the first line, and nobody had ever told him to stop.
And on the bench by the way in, Gage sat.
He was always there. That was the first thing anyone learned about the Fourth Desk. The bench was bare oak, plain, without a back, set against the wall just inside, so that anybody who came into the room had to walk past it. And on it sat Gage, the way a stone sits in a field. He had no hair at all, on his head or his face, not even eyebrows. His face was broad and flat and plain, like something carved out of wood by a man who had not had much time. He wore a gray coat cut by the Bank's own tailor, very good, very plain. And round his neck, on a cord, hung a slate. A small square slate, in a wooden frame, with a stub of white chalk tied to it on a string.
Gage did not speak. Elara did not know if he could not, or would not. She had never asked. In six years she had never heard him make a sound. When he wanted to say a thing, he wrote it on the slate and held it up. Mostly he did not want to say things.
He was looking at her now.
He did not write anything. He only looked. With his small pale eyes in his wooden face. The way he looked at her every Thursday, when she came back from cupboard nine.
She looked back at him.
And then she opened her black book, and dipped her pen, and began the day.
Kindly
The note came at the fourth bell.
A porter brought it. One of the old ones, from the front hall, in the Bank's dark blue with the brass buttons, who walked as if his feet hurt and knocked on the open way in before he came through it, though there was nothing to knock on but the frame. He stopped at Gage's bench, the way everybody did, and waited for Gage to look at him. Gage looked. The porter came on down the room to Elara's desk and held out a folded paper on a small silver tray.
"From the Treasurer's rooms, Auditor," he said. "By hand."
Elara took it.
It was folded in four, and sealed with a blob of dark red wax, and pressed into the wax was the Treasurer's seal. A key, crossed with a pen. Her name was on the front in a hand she knew. Tall. Loose. Graceful. A hand that went up and down the page in long loops, like a man walking easily downhill.
The Auditor. The Fourth Desk.
She broke the seal with the edge of her thumbnail, through the glove, and opened it.
Auditor,
A sad matter, and a delicate one, which I would rather you saw to yourself than leave to the clerks.
You will have heard, I think, that Councillor Cressida Thorne has not been seen at the Council since her brother's funeral. I regret to tell you the reason. She was found in her morning room on the Hill on the morning after that funeral, sitting by her window among her lilies, quite well in body, and quite emptied. The physicians have been. There is nothing to be done. It seems she took it hard, the loss of her brother so soon after her niece ran off, and something gave way. An unfortunate decline.
The house has been quiet since. But the Council must fill her seat, and the Bank must weigh what she leaves, before the seat can be filled. There are debts, I understand. There are holdings. There is a nephew, a minor, who will need to be provided for.
I should be grateful if you would go up to the house today and begin. Weigh the estate, Auditor. Kindly.
D. S.
Elara read it twice.
Then she laid it flat on the green leather of her desk and looked at it.
Kindly.
It was the last word before the initials. It stood on its own, after a full stop, with a little space round it, like a word that had been added after the rest of the note was finished. As if the Treasurer had signed his name, and then looked at it, and dipped his pen again.
Elara had worked for Dorian Sterling for six years. She had read a great many of his notes. He did not, as a rule, ask her to do things kindly.
She thought about what it might mean.
She thought it might mean what it said. That he was sorry, a little, for the Thorne woman, and for the house on the Hill, and for the boy, and wanted the Bank to go gently.
She thought it might mean the opposite. Kindly, the way a man says it who is holding a door open for you and does not intend you to come back through it. Weigh it kindly. Weigh it quickly. Weigh it quietly. Find what you are meant to find and nothing else.
She did not know.
She had found, in six years, that she did not often know what Dorian Sterling meant. She knew what he wrote. She knew what the ledgers said. Those were the things the Fourth Desk dealt in. The record is never wrong, he had told her once, in his rooms on the second floor, with the roses on the table and the long row of dead Treasurers looking down from the wall. Men are wrong. Memory is wrong. The record is never wrong.
She had written that down too, in her book of days.
She was less sure of it than she had been.
"Mister Harrow," she said.
He looked up.
"I am going up to the Hill this morning. To Councillor Thorne's house. On the Treasurer's business." She folded the note again, in four, along its creases, and put it in the inside pocket of her coat. "I shall want the Thorne ledgers. The household account and the Council account, both. And the vault book for the Thorne boxes. And a clean audit book."
"The green one?"
"The green one."
Mister Harrow got up and went to the cupboards. Elara heard him open four, and then six, and then four again, and his small neat hands moving among the ledgers, taking down what she wanted. He did not ask why. He never asked why. It was one of the things she liked about him.
She stood up and took her coat from the peg.
It was dark green. A plain heavy wool, cut long, buttoned high at the neck, with a high collar that turned up. She had worn it every winter for six years. She did not know where it had come from. It had been hanging on the peg behind her desk on the first morning, and it had fitted her, and nobody had ever said it was theirs.
She put it on. She buttoned it. She took her white gloves off, and laid them on the desk, and took a clean pair from the drawer, and put those on instead, finger by finger, smoothing each one down.
White. Kid. Very fine. The Bank gave the Auditor a new pair every month. She went through them faster than that. She changed them before she went anywhere, and when she came back, and whenever they had touched anything that she did not want to keep on her hands.
And on the bench by the way in, Gage stood up.
He did not need to be told. He never needed to be told. When Elara went anywhere outside the room, Gage went too. He walked a step behind her and a little to the left, the way he had done for six years, and he did not say anything, and he did not look at anything except her and whatever was in front of her. He stood up now and waited by the way in with his slate on its cord against his chest, and his plain wooden face showing nothing at all.
Mister Harrow came back with the ledgers.
Three of them. And the green audit book on top. He gave them to Gage, not to her. That was the way of it. Gage carried. Elara wrote.
"Will you want a cab, Auditor?" said Mister Harrow.
"Yes. The Bank's. At the side gate."
"I'll send down."
"Thank you, Mister Harrow."
He went back to his desk. He sat. He picked up his pen. And then he paused, and looked at her over the top of his small gray whiskers, and said, in his small gray voice:
"I knew the Councillor a little, Auditor. Years ago. When she first came on the Council. She was a hard woman. But she paid her debts. Every one, to the day." He was quiet a moment. "I hope it goes gently with her."
Elara looked at him.
She did not know what to say. She did not often know what to say, when people said things like that. She had found, in six years, that she did not have a great many of the words for it.
"So do I," she said.
And she went out, with Gage behind her, a step back and a little to the left, carrying the ledgers.
The House on the Hill
The cab went up the Hill slowly, because of the ice.
Elara sat inside with the ledgers on the seat beside her and her gloved hands folded in her lap, and watched the city go by through the small square window. Gage sat on the box with the driver. He always did. He said nothing to the driver, and the driver said nothing to him, and the cab went up through the old streets of the lower town, past the fish market and the chandlers and the long gray front of the Ministry, and over the Lantern Bridge.
There were white barriers on the bridge.
New ones. Elara had not seen them before. White painted wood, waist high, set across the footways at both ends, so that people on foot had to stop and wait to be let through, one at a time. And gray coats standing by them. Four at this end. She counted them. They let the Bank's cab through without stopping it. One of them lifted a hand to his hat as it went by. Half a salute. The kind you give to a thing, not a person.
On the far side the road began to climb.
The Hill was where the old families lived. Elara had been up it a great many times, on the Bank's business, and it always looked the same. The houses got bigger as you went up, and further apart, and whiter. The streets got wider and quieter and cleaner. The snow on the pavements was swept, here, into neat gray heaps at the edges. There were trees. Bare now, black against the brown sky, with their branches full of old ash like dirty lace. There were railings, tall and black and spiked, round little gardens where nothing grew in winter. There were fanlights over the front ways, half-moons of colored glass, and brass knockers shaped like lions and hands and fish, polished every morning by somebody who was not seen.
The Thorne house stood near the top.
The cab stopped in front of it, and Gage got down, and opened the cab, and held out his hand, and Elara took it and stepped down onto the swept pavement. And stood, and looked up.
It was a tall house. Four floors, and a row of small windows under the roof. Cream-colored stone, smooth and pale, with a flight of white steps going up to a wide front way painted black, with a brass knocker in the shape of a woman's hand holding a ball. Tall windows on every floor, in rows, with pale stone round them like picture frames.
And every curtain drawn.
Every one. On every floor. From the ground to the roof. Heavy curtains, Elara saw, gray or dark green, pulled shut tight behind the glass, so that the windows looked like eyes with the lids down. In a house full of daylight, on the Hill, in the morning. As if somebody inside had died, and they were waiting for the hearse.
The front step had not been swept.
There was old snow on it. And ash on the snow. And on the top step, by the black front way, a single lily, lying on its side, brown, with its petals gone soft and curled like old paper. As if somebody had carried an armful of them out of the house and dropped one, and not come back for it.
Elara looked at the lily for a long moment.
Then she went up the steps, and Gage came after her with the ledgers, and she lifted the brass hand and let it fall.
The sound went into the house and did not seem to come back.
They waited.
After a long time she heard feet. Slow. On a stone floor. And bolts going back, two of them, top and bottom. And the black front way opened a hand's width, and a face looked out at them.
A man. Tall, and thin, and not young, in a black coat with silver buttons, the coat of a house servant. He had a long pale face with a long nose, and his eyes were red at the rims, as if he had not slept, or as if he had.
"Yes?" he said.
"The Auditor," said Elara. "From the Bank. On the Treasurer's business. To weigh the estate of Councillor Thorne."
The man looked at her. At her green coat, and her white gloves, and her face. Then past her, at Gage, with the ledgers, and his slate on its cord, and his wooden face. He did not seem surprised. Elara thought he was past being surprised by anything.
"Pruett," he said. "I'm the footman. I'm all there is, now, of the men. The rest have gone." He opened the way wider. "Come in, Auditor. She's in the morning room. She's always in the morning room."
Elara went in.
And the smell came at her.
Lilies. She had known it would be lilies. She had smelled them on the step, a little, the one dead one. But in here it was not a little. It was everything. It filled the hall from the floor to the roof, thick and sweet and heavy, so heavy it was almost something you could touch. And it had gone wrong. That was the thing. It was not the smell of lilies in a vase on a table. It was the smell of lilies left too long. Sweet, still, but under the sweetness something else, something brown and soft and wet, like fruit going over in a bowl. Like a sickroom. Like the inside of an old coffin.
She stood in the hall and breathed it, and her eyes watered, and she did not let them.
The hall was black and white. The floor was marble, in big squares, black and white, like a game board. The walls were white, and went up and up, past the first floor and the second, all the way to the roof, where there was a dome. A small round dome made of colored glass, in panes, red and blue and yellow and green, like a church window laid on its back. On a bright day it would have thrown colored light all down the hall onto the black and white floor. Today the sky was brown with ash, and the colored glass let in only a dim muddy glow, and the hall was full of shadows.
And there were lilies everywhere.
In vases. On every table. On the stairs, on every step, in tall white jars. On the floor, in buckets, by the walls. Hundreds of them. Elara could not count them. Big white lilies, the hothouse kind, with long throats and yellow dust inside. Some of them were still white. Most of them were not. They had gone brown at the edges, and then all over. Their heads had dropped. Their petals had come away and lay on the black and white floor in drifts, brown and curled, like dead leaves in an autumn lane. And the yellow dust had fallen out of them and lay everywhere, on the tables and the floor and the stairs, in little drifts, like pollen, like ash.
Somebody had kept bringing them. Elara understood that. Every day, after the Councillor went the way she went, somebody had gone on bringing lilies into the house. Because she had always had lilies. Because nobody had told them to stop. And nobody had taken the old ones away.
And somewhere in the house, upstairs, a woman was crying.
Elara heard it. Thin and high and steady, the way a person cries who has been crying for a long time and has forgotten how to stop. It came down the stairs between the dying lilies and filled the hall, very faint, under the smell.
"That's Thursby," said Pruett. "The maid. She was the Councillor's own. Twenty years." He did not look up the stairs. "She does that. Most days. It doesn't help anything. But she does it."
He turned and walked across the black and white floor, through the drifts of brown petals, toward a tall white way in on the left side of the hall. His shoes made a soft whispering sound in the petals.
"This way, Auditor," he said. "She won't know you. She doesn't know anybody. But she doesn't mind people. She just sits."
