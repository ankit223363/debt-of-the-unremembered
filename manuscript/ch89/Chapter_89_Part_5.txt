Chapter 89: The Blue Wagon, Part 5 of 5: Cupboard Eleven
Cupboard Eleven
It was dark when Mister Harrow went home.
He went at the sixth bell, the way he always did. He blotted the last line in the day ledger, and closed it, and squared it on his desk. He cleaned his pen on the little square of black cloth he kept for it, and laid it in the groove of his inkstand. He stood up. He took his coat from the peg and put it on and buttoned it. He took his hat. He took his umbrella from the corner, furled tight, black, with its horn handle, and held it under his arm, not open.
"Good night, Auditor," said Mister Harrow.
"Good night, Mister Harrow."
He stopped at the way in. By Gage's bench. He stopped there every night, for a moment, the way a man stops at a gate to look back at a field.
"Was it bad?" he said. "Up the Hill?"
Elara looked at him from her desk by the window.
"Yes," she said.
Mister Harrow nodded slowly. His small gray whiskers moved.
"I'm sorry for it," he said. And went.
She listened to his feet go down the long corridor, and down the stairs, neat and even, until she could not hear them anymore. And then she sat for a while longer at her desk, in the light of her one lamp, with the windows black behind her and the river black beyond them, and the blue Ash lamps along the quay making small cold blurs in the dark.
Gage sat on his bench.
She got up.
She went to the cupboards. Past nine. To eleven. The tall one. The double one. And she took the key from her pocket, the big brass Charter key on its own ring that only the Auditor and the Treasurer had, and she put it in the lock, and turned it, and opened the doors.
It was dark inside.
Dark, and deep. Eleven went back a long way into the wall, further than the other cupboards, because it had been made long ago to hold more. Shelves on both sides, going back, loaded with bundles of old letters tied in faded red tape, and wooden boxes, and the old seals of the Bank laid in rows on velvet. And at the very back, on its own shelf, the Charter, in its box of black wood with the silver corners.
And behind the Charter box, on the floor of the cupboard, a small light.
A candle. A stub of one. The end of a tallow candle, no longer than a thumb, standing in a little pool of its own grease on a tin lid. It burned low and yellow and smelled of mutton fat, and its small light lay on the bundles and the boxes and the black wood of the Charter, and on a young man.
He was sitting on a folded blanket with his back to the end wall of the cupboard and his knees drawn up and a book open on them.
Remy Castellan.
He looked up when the doors opened. He did not start. He never started anymore. He had been in the cupboard so long that the doors opening had become just a thing that happened, like the bells.
"Auditor," he said.
"Remy."
He was thinner than he had been. Elara saw that every night, and every night it was a little more. He had been thin to start with, a gray thin young man with dark eyes and a long pale face, but now he was thinner, and grayer, and his eyes were bigger in his face. He had not been outside the cupboard by day since the night of the Bourse. At night, sometimes, when the Bank was empty, she let him out to walk the long room, up and down, and to go down the back stair to the porters' privy, and to wash at the pump in the yard. Then he came back. He always came back. There was nowhere else for him to go. There were people in the city looking for him, from more than one house, and none of them meant him well.
He had no gloves on. He never did, in the cupboard. His hands lay on the open book, thin and pale, with the pen callus on the right one and the ink stain on the cuff. And on the back of the left one, in the candlelight, the brand. A small V, old and shiny and pale, burned into the skin when he was four years old, by a woman who had bought him and wanted everybody to know it.
Elara sat down on the floor of the cupboard, inside the doors.
She did not usually. She usually stood. She brought him bread, and cheese, and a jug of water, and stood in the doors for a little while and asked him if he needed anything, and he said no, and she went away. Tonight she sat. On the bare boards. In her green coat. With her back against one of the shelves.
Remy watched her do it. He did not say anything.
"I went up the Hill today," said Elara.
"To the Thornes'," said Remy.
"How did you know?"
"I heard Harrow say it." He shrugged a little. "I hear everything, in here. It's like being inside a drum."
Elara was quiet a moment.
"Cressida Thorne," she said. "The Councillor. She has been emptied."
Remy did not move.
"Not by accident," said Elara. "Not by grief. Somebody did it. Slowly. Carefully. All of it. Every line. I've never seen a book so empty." She stopped. "She sits by her window among her lilies. She eats when they feed her. She doesn't know anybody. She never will again."
Remy looked at her for a long moment in the candlelight.
Then he looked down at his book.
It was a small book. Thick. Bound in black, worn to brown at the corners. Elara had seen it many times. He wrote in it every night. She did not know everything that was in it. Names, mostly. She knew that. Names, one under the other, in his small tidy hand. People he had known. People he had sold, or helped to sell, in the years when he worked for the woman who branded him. People who had been in the cold rooms under the Sanguine Vault. And beside some of the names, a small drawing.
A flower.
A little five-petalled flower, done in a few strokes of the pen. Beside the names of the dead.
She had asked him, once, why a flower. He had said, Because nobody else will give them one.
He turned the pages now. Elara watched them go by in the candlelight. Names. Names. A flower. Names. Three flowers in a row. Names. Until he came to a page that was half full, and found the end of it, and dipped his pen in a little stoppered bottle of ink that stood by his knee.
And wrote.
She watched the pen move. Small. Tidy. Careful.
Cressida Thorne. Councillor. The Hill.
And then he stopped.
The pen hung over the page, beside the name. In the place where the flower would go. Elara watched it hang there. She watched the small drop of ink gather at the tip of it, and grow, and not fall.
Remy did not draw anything.
He lifted the pen away. He wiped it on the edge of the bottle. He put the cork back in the bottle and pushed it down with his thumb.
"No flower," said Elara.
"She's not dead," said Remy.
"No."
He looked at the name on the page. At the empty space beside it.
"Not yet," said Remy quietly. "Not a flower yet."
And he shut the book, and held it on his knees with both his thin hands, the branded one and the other, and looked at the little candle, and did not say anything else.
Elara sat on the floor of cupboard eleven, in the smell of tallow and old paper, and looked at him.
She thought of the book in Cressida Thorne's wrist. Every line ruled, ready, clean. Nothing written.
And she thought of Remy's book, on his knees. Full of names. Every one of them written down, by hand, by somebody who had decided they should not be forgotten. And a flower beside the dead.
She did not know why the thought made her eyes sting.
She got up.
"I'll bring you more candles," she said. "Tomorrow. That one won't last the night."
"Thank you, Auditor."
She went to close the doors.
And Remy said, behind her, very quietly:
"She knew, didn't she. The Councillor. Something about you."
Elara stopped with her hand on the door.
She did not turn round.
"Yes," she said.
And shut the doors, and locked them, and stood for a moment with her forehead against the cold black wood of cupboard eleven, in the dark, before she went back to her desk.
The long room was very quiet when she turned round.
The one lamp on her desk made a small round island of yellow at the far end, by the black windows, and the rest of the room was dark. The three desks. The row of black cupboards with their dull brass numbers. Penn's empty seat. Mister Harrow's, squared and tidy, with the day ledger shut on it and the umbrella gone from the corner. And the bench by the way in, and Gage on it, a big pale shape in the dark, not moving.
Outside, the snow was coming down thicker now. She could see it against the glass, in the light of her lamp, big soft flakes going past and down, slowly, one after another, into the black. It made no sound. It never did. But the room felt quieter for it, the way a room does when snow is falling outside, as if the whole city had pulled a blanket up over its head.
She walked down the room toward her lamp.
Her feet were quiet on the boards. She counted them without meaning to. Eleven steps from cupboard eleven to the end of Mister Harrow's desk. Seven more to hers. She had never counted them before. She did not know why she counted them now.
She sat down at her desk.
The black book lay where it always lay, on the right-hand corner, shut. Waiting for the day's number.
The Total and the Slate
She sat at her desk by the black window and opened the black book.
Not the book of days. The other one. The one she kept on the right-hand corner of her desk, shut, always, where she could see it. It was a plain book, bound in black cloth, no bigger than her hand, with nothing written on the cover. The Bank had not given it to her. She did not know where it had come from. It had been on her desk on the first morning, beside the blue tin, and she had opened it and found it empty, and the first thing she had ever written in it was the line about the ninth of the Thaw month.
And after that, the days.
She turned to the last page.
It was ruled, like a ledger, in two columns. On the left, the date. On the right, a number. And the number went up, line after line, page after page, in her own neat hand, all the way down from the top of the book to here. A running total. Like the balance at the foot of an account.
The last line said:
2,410.
She dipped her pen.
Under it, on the left, she wrote today's date, in the Bank's way, with the day of the week after it. Thursday. And beside it, on the right, after a long moment, she wrote the new number.
2,411.
And she sat and looked at it.
She did not usually look. She wrote the number and shut the book and did not think about it. That was the rule she had made herself, in the first year, when the number was still small. Write it. Shut it. Don't look. Because if you looked, you began to think about what the number meant, and that was no good to anybody.
She looked.
2,411.
Two thousand, four hundred and eleven days. Given. Burned. Gone. One at a time. Every time she laid a bare hand on a thing for the Bank and read its account. Two thousand, four hundred and eleven mornings she had woken up to and could not remember. Two thousand, four hundred and eleven evenings. More than six years of days. More days than she had in her book of days, all of it, from the first morning to tonight.
Where had they come from?
She had never asked herself that before. Not properly. She had always thought of them as coming out of the six years, one here and one there, like coins out of a purse. But there were not enough days in six years to pay that many. There were not enough by a long way.
So they had come from before.
From the time she could not remember. The time before the ninth of the Thaw month. The Sight had been taking from that, too. Reaching back past the first morning, into the dark, where she could not see, and taking. A day. And a day. And a day. Out of a childhood. Out of a long wet field and a boy's hand and somebody's laugh.
She had not known.
She sat very still with the pen in her hand.
Bank. The Fourth Desk. The girl. Done.
They had put her here. On the ninth of the Thaw month. Somebody had. Cressida Thorne had known, and written it, and drawn a circle. They had put her at this desk with the gift in her hands, and let her use it, day after day, for six years, on the Bank's business. And every time she used it, it took a little more of whatever had been there before.
Until there would be nothing.
Until she was as clean as the pages of Cressida Thorne's book.
She put the pen down.
She put it down very carefully, in the groove of her inkstand, the way Mister Harrow put down his. And she shut the black book. And laid her two gloved hands flat on top of it, one on the other.
And then, because her hands wanted something to do that was not writing, she reached to the back corner of the desk and took the blue tin.
It was a small tin. Flat and square, as wide as her palm. Blue, gone pale at the corners where the paint had rubbed off, and dented on one side, as if it had been dropped on stone. On the lid there was a picture, faded nearly away: an orange tree, small and round, in a white pot, with oranges on it like little suns. And round the edge, in curly gold letters, half gone: SWEETS OF THE SOUTH.
It had been on her desk on the first morning. Beside the black book. She did not know whose it was. She had decided, after a while, that it must be hers, because nobody else had ever asked for it.
She opened it.
The smell came up. It always did. Oranges. Faint, now. Very faint. After six years, and however many years before that, it was hardly there at all. You had to put your face close to smell it. But it was there. Sweet and sharp and bright, the smell of a thing that grew somewhere warm, a long way from here, under a sun that was not hidden by ash.
There were no sweets in it. There had never been, in all the time she had had it. There were only two papers.
Two small twists of waxed paper, flattened out and folded. Wrappers. From barley sugars. She had found them on a windowsill at Number Fourteen, in the lower town, in the middle of the winter, on the Bank's business, and she had not been able to say why she wanted them, and she had taken them anyway. They smelled of burnt sugar and lemon. They were the kind of sweet a tired man keeps in his coat pocket and eats one at a time, at night, on long watches, so that he will not fall asleep.
She knew whose they were. She had known since the first time she smelled them. She had not written it down.
She sat with the tin open in her gloved hands and breathed the oranges, very faint, and the burnt sugar, and looked at nothing.
And for a moment, just a moment, something came.
Not a picture. Not a day. Smaller than that. A feeling. The feeling of a tin like this one being held out to her, open, by a hand bigger than hers. And the smell of oranges, much stronger, so strong it filled her whole head. And somebody saying, Only one, Ellie. Only one. They have to last.
And her own hand going in. Small. With dirt under the nails.
And then it was gone.
She sat very still and waited for it to come back. It did not come back. They never did. They came, sometimes, these little pieces, out of the dark before the ninth of the Thaw month, when she smelled a thing or heard a thing or touched a thing. A long wet field. A hand. A laugh. Come on, Ellie. Keep up. Don't let go. And now this. Only one. They have to last. Each one so small, and so quick, that she could never be sure afterward that she had not dreamed it.
She would write it down. She always wrote them down. In the book of days, on a page at the back that she kept only for these. There were not many lines on that page. There were more than there had been at Midwinter.
Only one, Ellie.
A boy's voice, she thought. Not a man's. A boy who was bigger than her, and had a tin of sweets from the south, and was making them last.
She shut the tin.
She put it back in the corner of the desk, where it always was. Squared to the edge.
And she sat with her hands flat on the black book, one on the other, and looked out at the dark river, and the snow beginning, and did not let herself think the thing she was thinking.
And behind her, by the way in, she heard the chalk.
A small sound. Dry. Squeaking a little. The stub of white chalk on its string, moving on the slate.
She turned round on her seat.
Gage was writing.
He sat on his bench in the dark by the way in, beyond the edge of her lamplight, with the slate on its cord held up in front of him in one big hand, and the chalk in the other, and he was writing. Slowly. Carefully. The way he always wrote, in big square letters, pressing hard. She could not see the words. Only the shape of his bent head, hairless, pale in the dark. And his hand moving.
He did not write often. He had written a great many times in the first year, when there was so much she did not know. Your name is Elara. You are the Auditor. I am Gage. And This is your desk. And This is Mister Harrow. He is kind. And Do not go down to the vault alone. And later, less. Months, sometimes, with nothing. And then, on the night of the Bourse, in the cab, coming back through the burning streets, more than he had written in years.
A wagon. A blue one. I drove it.
She had it in her book of days. Every word. She had read it so many times that she did not need to read it. Children. A boy. Tall. Who would not let go of his sister's hand. And his sister. Two braids.
And then: Not yet.
And he had pointed up. At the roof of the cab. At the sky. At somebody, somewhere, who was listening.
She watched him write now.
He stopped. He looked at what he had written. He did not rub any of it out.
And he got up off the bench.
He came down the long room toward her, out of the dark, into the edge of the lamplight. Big. Slow. His heavy feet quiet on the boards. His wooden face showing nothing. And he stopped beside her desk, by the black window, and held up the slate. Turned it to the window, so that the light from the blue Ash lamps on the quay below came in through the glass and lay on it, cold and pale.
And she read it.
I drove the blue one. The girl was in it. The boy was not.
She read it again.
The girl was in it. The boy was not.
She looked up at Gage.
She knew the boy. She thought she knew him. She had his face somewhere, in a day she had kept. A man on the ninth step of a kitchen stair at the Bourse, sitting, with a stiff leg out across the step, looking up at her. A lamp boy on the great stair, with a limp, whom Gage had lifted over a step with his hand on his head. A man on his knees under the dome, holding it up. She had a line in her book of days, written in her own hand on the night of the Bourse, that she had read so often she could have said it with her eyes shut.
I passed my brother on a stair tonight and did not stop him.
She knew.
And she did not know.
She had never let herself. She had written it, and read it, and shut the book. Because a thing written in her book of days was a thing she could not remember, only read about. And reading about a brother was not the same as having one. And because if she let herself know, all the way, then she would have to know what it meant. That he had been taken too. That he had been the boy who would not let go of her hand, and they had made him let go. That he had gone one way, and she had gone the other, in the blue one. And that somebody had done this. On purpose. And written it down.
"What boy?" said Elara.
She heard herself say it. Her voice was very small in the long dark room.
She did not mean, I don't know. She knew. She meant, Tell me. She meant, Say it. Write it. His name. Put it on the slate where I can see it, in chalk, in your big square letters, so that it is written down and true and I cannot not know it anymore.
"What boy, Gage?"
Gage did not write anything.
He looked at her for a long time, in the cold blue light from the window, with his small pale eyes in his wooden face.
Then he lowered the slate. He let it hang on its cord against his chest, with the words still on it, unwiped.
And he put out his big hand, slowly, the way you put out a hand to a bird you do not want to frighten.
And laid it on her head.
On her hair. On the top of her head, where the two old ridges were, at the back, that she had found with her fingers on the first morning and never understood. The marks of two braids, worn so long and so tight, once, by somebody, that they had left their shape behind.
He did not press. He did not stroke. He only laid his hand there, heavy and warm, and left it.
The way you lay a hand on a child's head at the end of a long day, when there is nothing to say that would help, and the child knows it, and you know it, and you are both still there.
