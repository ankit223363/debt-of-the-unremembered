Chapter 59: Fold It Small, Part 5 of 5: Kross at the Door
Kross at the Door
She did not want to.
She stood on the gray stone of the hall with Corin's gloved hand round hers and did not want to show the Inquisitor anything. Not her hand. Not her face. Not one more thing of hers in this plain gray house where he already had her mother's book and her father's last breath and her brother's question.
But Corin let go of her hand.
Not quickly. Slowly. The way you let go of a rope when you have seen that the boat is tied up and will not drift. And stood back a little, on his crutch. And looked at her. And did not say anything.
So she held out her hand to Silas Kross.
He came the last few paces across the hall. And took it. Not the way Corin had. Not to hold. The way a doctor takes a hand. By the wrist, with two fingers, lightly. And with his other hand, he took hold of the end of each finger of Silas's old black glove, one at a time, and drew it off. Carefully. A finger at a time. The way you take a glove off somebody who has been hurt.
And looked at her bare hand, in the gray light of the hall.
He looked at it for a long time.
At the cut on the ball of the thumb. At the knuckles. At the nails, short, uneven, one broken. At the small hard bump on the side of the second finger, where the pen had rested for fifteen years.
He touched the bump. With the tip of one finger. Very lightly.
"Your mother had one of these," he said.
Lyra did not move.
"Here," said Kross. "The same place. From the needle, she said. Not the pen. She stitched more than she wrote." He did not look up from her hand. "She used to say that writing was for people who expected to be listened to, and stitching was for people who didn't, and she knew which she was."
He let go of her wrist.
He held out the glove to her. She took it. She did not put it on.
"You know what she'd say," said Kross. "About what you did up there."
"No."
"No. You wouldn't. You were nine." He looked at her. "She'd say you paid too much. She always said that, when anybody folded. You paid too much, you always pay too much, there's always a cheaper hand." A pause. "And then she'd say she'd have done the same."
"Did she?"
Kross did not answer that.
He turned his head and looked up the stair. Toward the white room at the top. And Lyra saw something go across his face. The shadow of a bird across a field.
"He's dying," said Kross.
The hall was very quiet.
"You know that," said Kross. "You saw it. I watched you see it, on the barge, in the reeds, through a glass, when you didn't know I was watching. He's been dying since the cellar. Perhaps since before. The doctor comes every morning. He says a week. Perhaps less." He looked back at her. "That's why he's here. Not because I want something from him. Not any more. Because he's warm here. Because the nurse listens, and when he taps, somebody answers. Because the stairs at Crown Walk would kill him in a day, and the barge would have killed him in a night, and the litter nearly did." His voice did not change. "I brought him up the north road on a litter because Vell would have taken him to the Health. And the Health would have given him a sweet."
Lyra stood in the gray hall with her bare hand at her side and the glove in it.
"You could have let him go home," she said. "To die at home. In his own bed."
"I offered," said Kross. "For the square."
"That was a price. Not an offer."
"Yes," said Kross. "It was."
He did not say he was sorry.
She did not think he was. Not for that. She thought he was sorry for a great many things, in his tired gray eyes, and that was not one of them. He had wanted the square more than he had wanted to be kind. And he did not pretend he had not.
She found, a little to her surprise, that she respected that.
"It's gone now," she said. "The square. Where none of you can follow. Not you. Not him. Not the man with the circle." She held his eyes. "So you can't buy it. And you can't take it. And you can't burn it. It's in my father's hand. And when he goes, it goes with him."
Kross looked at her for a long moment.
"Yes," he said quietly. "I heard."
And something happened at the corner of his mouth. That place again. Where a smile would be.
"That was better than burning," said Silas Kross. "Much better. I should have thought of it." He looked at her bare hand. "I'd not have paid with that, though."
"What would you have paid with?"
He did not answer.
He turned to Corin.
Corin was standing a little way off, on his crutch, by the foot of the stair. Watching. He had not moved. He had not said anything since he let go of her hand. Lyra had felt him there, all the time, the way you feel a wall at your back in the dark.
"Mister Vane," said Kross.
"Inquisitor."
"You told me something in my study," said Kross. "About a stone. And a crypt. And three hours."
"Yes."
"I'll not write it down." Kross's voice was very even. "I'll not write down what you told me in this house. Not for the Ministry. Not for the Council. Not for anybody." He paused. "But I'm not the only one who can count. The Auditor's ledger says the case in the Archon's vault weighed what it ought. In the spring he'll take it out to open it. And it won't open. And he'll take an axe to it. And there'll be nothing inside but a board from the same tree." He looked at Corin. "And then he'll want to know who held up the dome of the Memory Bourse on Midwinter night with his bare hands. Half the city saw it. Somebody will have a name."
Corin did not say anything.
"There's a list on Thursday," said Kross. "Every Thursday. For the Watch, and the Enforcers, and the gates. I sign it. Names. Faces. Hands." He did not look away. "I can leave a name off one Thursday. Perhaps two. Not three. Not with the Archon reading it. He reads everything once. He never forgets a thing he has read."
"So I should go."
"So you should go," said Kross. "Tomorrow night. Not tonight. Tonight the gates are full of carts going out with the ash from the Bourse, and every cart is searched. Tomorrow night the snow's coming again, Harlan says, off the hills, and the gates will be half asleep. Go north. Go out by the dyke road, where you came in, and then keep going. Past the reeds. Past the hills." He paused. "Past the Glass, if you can."
The Glass.
Lyra saw Corin's face change. Very slightly. At the word.
"Why the Glass?" said Corin.
Kross did not answer.
He looked at Corin for a long moment. And then, slowly, his eyes went down. To Corin's hands, on the grip of the crutch. In the gray wool gloves, over the white linen, over the black fingertips.
"Because nobody can burn anything there," said Kross. "And because nobody can take anything there, either. Not a face. Not a name. Not a jar." He looked up. "It's the only place in the world, Mister Vane, where a man with a hand's width of gray left could sleep without being afraid of what he'd wake up without."
Nobody said anything.
Kross turned away. Back toward the study door. Toward the fire, and the plain chairs, and the dark old desk with the green cloth book on the corner of it.
And stopped in the doorway, with his back to them.
"Miss Thorne," he said.
She waited.
"You can come every day," he said. Without turning round. "By the front door. Knock as you knocked this morning. Three and two. Wren will let you in. You can sit with him as long as you like. Every day. Until." He paused. "And when it's time, I'll send Wren for you. Wherever you are. Day or night."
Lyra did not answer.
She could not.
"And Miss Thorne," said Kross.
He turned his head. Just his head. Over his shoulder. And looked at her, across the gray hall, with his tired gray eyes.
"The dark is the day with its eyes shut," said Silas Kross.
And went into the study, and shut the door.
Lyra stood in the empty white hall and stared at the shut door.
Behind her, Oren let out a breath. Long and slow, through his nose. And said, very quietly, the first thing he had said in the whole of the plain gray house.
"Saints and all their candles."
And behind Oren, by the front door, very straight in her white coat with the twelve silver buttons, Wren stood with her hand on the latch and her pale young face turned toward the study. And Lyra saw that her lips were moving. Very slightly. Without sound.
Humming. Three notes.
And then stopping. As if she were waiting for somebody to hum the fourth.
Lyra did not hum it.
She put Silas's old black glove back on her bare right hand, the hand that could not write, and went to the door. And Wren opened it. And the gray light came in, and the cold, and the white stone of the Spire, and the sound of a single pigeon, somewhere high up on a ledge, calling.
And she went out, and down the five plain steps, and into the lane.
She heard the crutch come after her.
Tap. Swing.
Tap. Swing.
She did not look back. But she slowed down. So that it would not have to hurry.
Go
She told him on the steps of Saint Bride's, at the sixth bell, with the light going.
Not the crypt steps. The others. The three wide shallow stone steps outside the church door, under the porch, where the lamp hung in its glass case, not yet lit. Where Linus Hale had come up with his stick on Sunday, Silas said, and could hardly get up them, and been given tea. Where Kross had sat, three days ago, on the bottom one, beside Silas, at the sixth bell, and left a sweet paper folded eleven times.
She sat on the top one.
The snow had stopped. The sky over the river was the color of a bruise, purple and yellow and gray, and the roofs of the city below the churchyard wall were going dark one by one, the way a field goes dark when a cloud comes over. Down on Lantern Bridge, the blue lamps were already burning. They had never stopped. She could see them from here, a line of small cold blue lights strung across the dark water, like beads on a thread.
Corin came out of the church and sat down beside her.
It took him a long time. He did it the way he did everything now. In stages. The crutch first, leaned against the stone of the porch. Then one hand on the wall. Then the good leg bending. Then the bad leg, stuck out straight in front of him down the steps. Then down. Onto the cold stone. With a breath let out through his teeth.
He sat beside her. Not close. Not far. The way you sit beside somebody on a step.
He had the Ledger in his lap. He always had the Ledger in his lap now.
Neither of them said anything for a while.
They had come down the Hill in the early afternoon, the three of them, past the Watchmen on the corner of the Crescent, who had not looked at them, past the end of Ferrule Street, where the black carriage was gone and the oil lamp over the door of the Lamplighter's Rest had been lit, though it was day, by somebody who remembered how. Down to the river. Over the bridge. Up the lane. Two hundred and twelve. And into the crypt, where Silas had looked at their faces and not asked anything, and Mrs. Doyle had put bread in front of them, and Juno had sat up on her blankets with her whistle in her fist and said Well?
And Lyra had told them. All of it. In front of everybody. Because they had earned it.
She had told them about the white room, and the nurse who listened, and the fourteen taps on her palm. And her name. Lyra. After two years.
And she had told them about the square, and her father's closed hand, and the letters going out of her like leaves off a tree in a high wind.
Juno had cried. Gideon had not. Gideon had looked at her bare right hand, when she took off the glove to show them, the way he looked at chalk marks. And then he had gone to the clear space on the floor by the fire, with his stub of blue chalk, and written on the stone, big, the way he had said he would. LYRA. A boot, and a fork, and a gate with a belly, and a tent. And then, after a long moment, frowning, beside it, very slowly, a second word. One he had not known before. He had made Silas show him. Four letters. A gate with a belly, and a comb with three teeth, and a tent, and half a moon standing on its flat side.
READ.
And under it, an arrow. Pointing at Corin's Ledger.
"For when you can't write," Gideon had said to her. "You can still read. So you read it. And he writes it. That's two people. That's enough."
She had not been able to say anything to that.
And now it was the sixth bell, and she was sitting on the top step of Saint Bride's in the cold beside a man who did not know her, and the lamps on Lantern Bridge were burning blue.
"You're going to say something," said Corin.
She looked at him.
"I don't know you," he said. "But I know that. You've been going to say something since we came off the Hill. You keep starting to, and then you count something instead." He did not look at her. He looked at the bridge. "Say it."
Lyra looked down at her gloved hands in her lap.
"You have to go," she said. "Tomorrow night. Like he said."
"Yes."
"You and Oren. And Juno and Gideon, if they want. And Big Gideon, if Gideon goes. North. By the dyke road. Past the reeds. Past the hills." She swallowed. "Past the Glass."
"Yes."
"And I'm not coming."
The sky over the river went on getting darker.
Corin did not say anything for a long time.
Then he said, "Because of your father."
"Yes."
"Because he's dying, and you can go every day, by the front door, and sit with him. Until."
"Yes."
"And because—" He stopped.
"Rule one," said Lyra.
He turned his head and looked at her.
"No lies between us," she said. "Not even the kind ones. It's in the book. In your hand and mine. I can't write it any more. But I can still say it." She did not look away. "So I'll tell you the whole of it. Not just the kind half."
He waited.
"It's because of my father," she said. "That's true. That's the biggest part. I'm not leaving him in that white room to die with the Inquisitor's nurse writing down his taps. I'm going to be there. Every day. I'm going to hold his hand, the one that isn't closed, and let him count to fourteen on my palm, as many times as he likes. And when he goes, I'm going to be holding it." Her voice did not shake. "That's true."
"Yes."
"And the other part is this." She looked at the bridge. At the line of small cold blue lights. "If you stay, he'll put you on a form. Not Kross. The other one. The one with the circle. In the spring, when the case won't open. He'll want to know who held up the dome. And somebody will have a name. And he'll draw a circle by it." She was quiet for a moment. "And you'll end up in a white room too. Or worse. In a chair at a table with a pen in your hand and a blank page and a circle in the corner. Like Linus. Not knowing your name."
Corin did not say anything.
"And I'd rather you didn't know me somewhere far away," said Lyra, "than didn't know me in a white room. Where I could come every day by the front door and sit with you. And you'd look at me the way you look at Mrs. Doyle."
She stopped.
She had not meant to say that last part. It had come out.
She did not take it back.
The lamp in the porch above them flickered, and caught. Somebody inside had lit it. Silas, perhaps. Or Mrs. Doyle. A small yellow flame, in its glass case. It threw their two shadows down the three stone steps in front of them, long and thin, side by side, on the snow.
"That's the whole of it," said Lyra. "Both halves. You go. I stay."
Corin was quiet for a very long time.
Then he opened the Ledger.
In his lap. To the last page with writing on it. She could see it in the yellow lamplight. Her letter from the roof, at the top. His square capitals under it, crooked. HER FATHER LOVED HER LIKE THE SEA. And then her two wandering lines, like the tracks of beetles in dust, from the hall of the plain gray house this morning. And under those, a clean space.
He took the stub of pencil out of his coat.
And held it in his gray-gloved fingers, which could not feel it. And bent over the page. And wrote. Slowly. In square capitals. Crooked. One letter at a time. She watched every one of them come.
WEDNESDAY. SAINT BRIDE'S STEPS. SIXTH BELL.
SHE SAYS GO. SHE SAYS SHE IS STAYING WITH HER FATHER UNTIL.
SHE SAYS SHE WOULD RATHER I DID NOT KNOW HER FAR AWAY THAN DID NOT KNOW HER IN A WHITE ROOM.
He stopped.
He looked at what he had written for a long time.
Then he went on.
I AM GOING. BECAUSE SHE ASKED. AND BECAUSE SHE IS RIGHT, AND SHE IS USUALLY RIGHT. MALAKOR SAYS SO.
BUT I AM WRITING THIS DOWN SO THAT I DO NOT FORGET IT:
SHE IS COMING AFTER.
He put the pencil down on the page.
And turned the Ledger round, on his lap, so that the words faced her. And held it out. On the flat of his hand. Like a tray.
Lyra looked at it.
SHE IS COMING AFTER.
"You can't make me come," she said. "By writing it."
"No," said Corin. "I can't."
He did not take the book away.
"But you can read it," he said.
Lyra sat on the top step of Saint Bride's in the yellow lamplight with the dark coming down over the river, and looked at the crooked square capitals on the page. SHE IS COMING AFTER. She could read every letter. Every one. She knew them all. She would always know them. She would never be able to make a single one of them again.
She read it again.
And again.
She found she was counting the letters. She could not help it. She had counted all her life.
Sixteen.
"Gideon wrote read on the floor," she said. "With an arrow. At your book."
"I saw."
"He said that's two people. You write it. I read it." Her voice cracked down the middle, like a cup. "He said that's enough."
"Is it?"
She did not answer.
She reached out, instead, with her right hand. The one in Silas's old black glove. The one that could not write. And put one finger on the page. On the last line. On the crooked square S at the beginning of it. And traced it. The way she had traced the silver letters on the green cloth book on her father's desk, when she was small, and he was not looking.
S. H. E.
All the way to the end.
A. F. T. E. R.
Her finger left a small mark on the page, at the end. A smudge. From the cut on her thumb, under the glove, bleeding a little through the worn-through fingertip. A small red spot, beside the R.
She took her hand away.
"Leave it there," she said.
Corin looked at the small red spot on the page.
"That's not yes," he said.
"No."
"It's not no."
"No." She stood up. All at once. The way Remy did things. Before she could think. "It's my mark. That's all. It's the only one I've got left." She looked down at him, sitting on the step with the Ledger open in his lap and his stiff leg stuck out down the stairs. "You'll have to decide what it means. You're the one who can write."
She turned to go in.
"Lyra," said Corin.
She stopped. In the doorway. Under the lamp.
It was the second time he had said it. Not reading it. Saying it. The second time since the dome.
She did not turn round.
"You talk like a kettle," he said.
She stood in the doorway of Saint Bride's with her back to him, and her shoulders went very still.
And then she said, without turning round, in a voice that was very small and very steady and only a little cracked:
"A kettle is useful. A heron just stands about."
And went in.
And down the twenty-three steps into the crypt, counting.
And Corin sat alone on the top step, in the yellow lamplight, with the Ledger open in his lap, and looked at the small red spot beside the last letter of the last line for a long time. Until the light was gone from the sky over the river altogether, and there was nothing left but the lamps on the bridge, burning blue, and the one above his head, burning yellow.
Then he took the stub of pencil, and under the red spot, very small, in crooked square capitals, he wrote:
I THINK IT MEANS YES.
And then, smaller still, under that:
I DO NOT KNOW HER. BUT I KNOW THAT.
