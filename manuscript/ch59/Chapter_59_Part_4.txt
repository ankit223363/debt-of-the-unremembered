Chapter 59: Fold It Small, Part 4 of 5: Fold It Small
Fold It Small
It lay on her palm. Small and tight and white, with a smear of her own blood dried brown on one corner.
She looked at it.
She had three choices. She had thought of them all the way up the Hill, under the blue lamps, counting, and all the way up the scrubbed pale stair, and she had not been able to make them into more than three, however she turned them.
She could give it to Kross. He would burn it in the plain grate in front of her, without opening it. She believed that. She did not know why, but she did. And her father would go home to Crown Walk this afternoon in the Inquisitor's own carriage, and be carried up the five steps and in at the dark green door and put to bed in his own room, with the green curtains and the moon clock. And Aunt Cressida and Ro would come home to him. And the circle would stop at the first name.
And K would be gone. Whoever K was. Burned. The last piece of her father's last brave thing, burned in the grate of a man who had her mother's book on his desk and would not give it back.
She could keep it. In her list pocket. With the vial and the letter and the strip from the ledger. She could walk out of this house with it, and down the Hill, and keep it safe, and never open it. And her father would stay in the white room with the nurse who listened. And the circle would go on to the second name, and the third. And one day, perhaps soon, perhaps not, somebody with long thin white hands would come for whoever had it. For her.
Or she could open it.
She looked at the square on her palm.
She would not open it. She had known that since the crypt, since Corin said don't, in a voice that did not sound like his, as if something in him knew what the rest of him did not. Her father had not folded it for her to open. He had folded it so that it would go under. So that it would not be found.
And it had been found. Twice. By Linus, in the chair. And by her, on the clasp.
And by Kross. In her pocket. With his eyes.
It must go under again, she thought. Somewhere none of them can follow.
Fold it small. Fold it small, Aurelian, and let it go under.
Her mother's words. In her father's mouth. In a warm study, in the rain, two autumns ago. Corin had said them to her in the crypt, in his flat Ministry voice, telling her everything.
You think of the thing. As hard as you can. All of it. And while you think of it, you fold. And with every fold, the thing goes into the paper, a little more, and out of you, a little more.
It burns. Folding. It burns as much as anything else.
She had never burned anything.
Not once. Not a candle. Not a breath of wind. She had watched Corin do it, all through the autumn and the winter, his little things going up one by one like paper in his gray hands. She had watched Vespera, and Hollis, and the mages at the Bourse with their vials. She had watched her father burn every room in himself on purpose. She had never done it. She had never wanted to. Her mother's people had done it, over the water, where she came from. Her mother's daughter had not.
She did not know how.
She looked at her father's hand on the blanket. The right one. With the long finger tapping. Very faintly now. Nine. On the white wood.
And she knew where it had to go.
Not into the paper. It was already in the paper. Her father had put it there. She did not need to fold the thing into the paper. She needed to fold the paper into something. Into somewhere. Into the one place in this city that nobody, not Kross, not the man with the circle, not the man with the long white hands, would ever think to look. Or be able to.
Except for the children. I'll keep the children. As long as I can.
The last room in him. The one he had not burned.
She took his right hand.
She lifted it, gently, off the white wood of the table, in the middle of a tap, and held it. In both of hers. And turned it over. Palm up. The long thin cold hand, all bones and knuckles and blue veins, with the nails cut very short and very neat.
And put the folded square in the middle of his palm.
And closed his fingers over it.
One by one. The little finger first. Then the next. Then the next. Then the first. Then the thumb, last, over the top of them all. The way the silver hand on the black case had been closed. A closed hand. Over something small.
And she held his closed hand in both of hers.
And shut her eyes.
And thought of it.
Not of K. She could not think of K. She did not know K. She had no face to think of, no name, no voice. Only a fold. A pale flat place.
So she thought of the square.
Of the square itself. The thing in her father's hand. Small and tight and white, with her blood on one corner. Of where it had been. In his waistcoat pocket, the last night. Under his left hand on his knee, being folded smaller while his evening went out through the other. Slipping into the dark of the chair. Linus's blue fingers, finding it. The silver hand on the black case, closing over it. The cold room. The ice. The lift. The chute. Oren's arm. The crypt. Her cut thumb. Her list pocket. All the places it had been, all the hands it had been in, all the people who had looked at it and not seen it, and all the people who had.
And she began to fold.
Not the paper. She did not touch the paper. It was inside his closed hand. She folded with her own hands round his. She folded the way you fold your hands to pray, and then fold them again, and again. Pressing. Closing. Each time, a little tighter. A little smaller. Round his closed hand, round the square inside it, as if she were folding his whole hand, and the square with it, down and down and down into something no bigger than a seed.
And with every fold, she felt it go.
Not the square. Something of hers.
She had not chosen it. She had not known she would have to choose. But the first time she folded, she felt something leave her, and she knew at once what it was, the way you know at once which tooth is aching.
Her hand.
Not her hand itself. What her hand knew. The thing her right hand had learned when she was five, sitting at the nursery table at Crown Walk with her tongue between her teeth and her mother's hand over hers, guiding the pen. L. That's your letter. That's you. Down, and along. Now again.
L.
She felt it go.
Out of her fingers. Out of the muscles of her hand, and her wrist, and the place behind her eyes where the shape of it lived. Into her father's closed hand. Into the fold. She felt it leave her the way a bird leaves your hand when you open it. All at once. A small warm weight, and then nothing.
She almost stopped.
She understood. In that moment. With her eyes shut and her hands round her father's, in the white room, with the Choir singing very faint and very high through the stone wall. She understood the rule. It was her mother's rule. She did not know how she knew it. It came up out of her the way the fourth note of the song had come up out of her at the table of loves, when Wren hummed the first three. As if it had always been there, waiting.
You pay with the hand you would use to unfold it.
That was the price. That was always the price, with folding. Not a memory. Not a winter. Not a face. The thing you would need to undo it. So that it could not be undone. Not by you. Not ever.
And what would she need, to undo this? To find it again? To tell anybody where it had gone, and what it was, and how?
She would need to write it down.
If I can't write it, nobody can make me write it.
Not Kross. Not the man with the circle. Not anybody.
She folded again.
Y.
Gone. Out of her hand. A small warm weight, and then nothing.
R.
Gone.
A.
Her own name. All four letters. The first thing she had ever written. On a slate, at the nursery table, with her mother's hand over hers. Gone. Into her father's closed hand.
She went on folding.
She could not stop now. She knew that. If she stopped now, half-folded, it would be like a door left half-open. Worse than open. Worse than shut. So she went on. And the letters went. One after another. Not in order. The way leaves come off a tree in a high wind. T. H. O. R. N. E. Her father's name. Gone. B. D. G. The ones she had learned next. M. P. S. W. Gone. The way her hand made a loop at the top of a K. The way it crossed a T. The small quick lean of it, forward, as if it were in a hurry. Her hand. Hers. The hand that had written to Ro, eight times, nine times, ten times, and never been answered until this morning. The hand that had written A kettle is useful in a man's book, under his square capitals. The hand that had written, on a roof, in the snow, at the second bell, It's still there. Under this. I promise.
All of it. Going. Into her father's closed hand. Folded small, and smaller, and smaller.
She did not cry. She could not afford to.
She folded.
And at the very end, when there was almost nothing left, when her hand round her father's was only a hand, a thing that could hold and grip and carry and tap and do nothing else at all with a pen, she felt the last letter go.
It was not one she had expected.
It was C.
The round open curve of it. Like a door half open. Like a mouth about to say something. The first letter of a name she had written, in the last two weeks, more than any other name in the world. In a book with HARBOUR TIDE TABLES on the cover. In the margins of square capitals. On a roof, in the snow. Your name is Corin Vane.
She felt it go.
And her father's hand, inside hers, moved.
She opened her eyes.
His closed hand was lying in both of hers on the white blanket. Closed. Quite tight. The knuckles white. And she knew, without opening it, without looking, that there was nothing in it any more. Nothing she could have found. Nothing anybody could have found. The square had gone under. Into the last room. Into the place where he kept the children. Where it would stay as long as he did.
And he was looking at her.
Not at the window. Not at the Spire. At her. With his gray eyes. And the light at the end of the long road had come back into them, just for a moment, faint, very far off. As if he had felt something put into his hand, and closed his fingers over it, and known what it was.
His closed hand opened. Just a little. Enough for one finger.
And it tapped. On her palm.
Once.
And then twice, quick.
Heard.
And closed again.
And his eyes went back to the window. To the white stone of the Spire, and the pigeons on the ledges, and the children singing, very high and very sweet, somewhere above the gray sky.
Lyra knelt beside the bed and held his hand and did not move.
After a while, the Choir stopped singing.
After a while longer, her father's finger began to tap again, very faintly, on the white blanket. Four. Two. Nine. Three.
She let go of his hand, at last. Gently. And laid it on the blanket. Still closed.
And she looked down at her own right hand, in her lap. At her fingers. At the cut on the ball of her thumb, opened again, bleeding a little. At the small hard bump on the side of her second finger, where the pen had rested for fifteen years.
It looked exactly the same.
She did not know yet. Not really. Not in the way you know a thing. She knew it the way you know, in the first moment after you have been hit, before it starts to hurt, that something has happened.
She got up off her knees.
She went to the small white table, where the nurse's notebook lay, with the pencil in it to keep the place. And she opened it, to a clean page at the back. And took the pencil in her right hand. The way she had held a pencil every day of her life since she was five.
And bent over the clean white page, to write her name.
And her hand did nothing.
It held the pencil. It held it quite well. The fingers were in the right places. The point was on the paper, at the top, at the left, where you begin.
And it did nothing. It did not know what to do. It did not know what came next. It sat there, on the clean page, with the pencil in it, like a horse at a gate that has forgotten the way home. She looked at the page. She knew what her name looked like. She could see it in her head. Four letters. She could read it, if somebody else wrote it. She knew she could.
She could not make her hand make it.
She tried. She pressed the pencil down, and moved it. And a line came. A line. Going nowhere. Wandering across the white page like the track of a beetle in dust. It was not a letter. It was not anything.
She tried again.
Another line. Another beetle track.
She put the pencil down.
She stood by the small white table in the white room with the white stone of the Spire in the window and looked down at two wandering lines on a clean page in a nurse's notebook. And the cut on her thumb bled a little onto the paper, a small red spot, beside the lines.
And that was all.
She closed the notebook. And put the pencil back in it, to keep the nurse's place. And stood for a moment with her hand flat on the cover.
Then she put Silas's old black glove back on.
And went to the door, and opened it, and went out onto the landing. Where Wren was standing, very straight, in her white coat. And the small old nurse in gray beside her, with her pale water eyes, looking at Lyra's face.
The nurse did not say anything.
She did not have to.
Lyra knew that she had heard. Everything. Every fold. Every letter. Through the shut door, through the white wall. Because she could not not hear.
"I won't write it down," said the nurse softly. "What I heard. I'll not write it. Not for him. Not for anybody." She looked at Lyra. "I don't think I could, now. I don't think there's anything to write."
Lyra looked at her.
Then she went past her, and past Wren, and down the scrubbed pale stair. Counting. Fourteen. Thirteen. Twelve.
She stepped over the fifth step.
She did not know if she would ever be able to write the number five again.
She found she did not care.
The Name She Cannot Write
Corin was standing in the hall when she came down.
Not sitting. Standing. At the foot of the stair, on the gray stone, leaning on the crutch, with his wool-gloved hands on the grip of it and his stiff leg out to one side. Oren was behind him, in the doorway of the study. And behind Oren, in the study, by the fire, Kross. In his shirtsleeves. With his back to them all, looking at the flames.
Corin was looking up the stair.
He had been looking up it for a long time, she thought. The way you look up a stair when somebody you are waiting for has gone up it.
She stopped on the bottom step.
She did not know why she stopped. She had meant to walk straight past him, the way she had walked past him all morning, without looking. She had decided it in the night. She stopped on the bottom step of the Inquisitor's scrubbed pale stair, in Silas's old coat and her mother's burned gown and Silas's old gloves, and looked at him.
And he looked at her.
Not politely. Not kindly. Not the way he looked at Mrs. Doyle, or at the arches. He looked at her the way he had looked at Kross across the study when he said that didn't happen. Closely. Carefully. As if she were a page written very small, and he had just found a figure on it that did not add up.
"What did you do?" he said.
She did not answer.
"You've done something," said Corin. "Up there. Something's gone. I can see it." He frowned. "I don't know how I can see it. I don't know what you looked like before. But I can see it. You came down the stair like somebody coming down from a fire."
You came down the stair like somebody coming down from a fire.
She almost laughed.
She did not. She did not trust what would come out of her if she opened her mouth. She stood on the bottom step and held on to the scrubbed rail with her gloved hand and looked at him.
And then she held out her right hand.
She did not mean to. Her hand did it. The way her hand had gone to the list pocket in Kross's study. It came up, by itself, in Silas's old black glove, worn through at one fingertip. And held itself out to him. Palm down. Fingers a little curled. The way you hold out a hand to show somebody a cut.
Corin looked at it.
Then he let go of the crutch with one hand, the right one, and leaned on it with the other, and reached out. And took her hand. In his gray wool glove, over the white linen, over the black fingertips he could not feel. And held it. And turned it over. And looked at the palm, in Silas's glove, as if there would be something written on it.
There was nothing written on it.
There would never be anything written by it again.
"Pencil," said Lyra.
It came out very small. Very steady.
Corin looked at her.
"Have you got a pencil," said Lyra. "In your coat. You always have one. It's like your name, you said."
He let go of her hand. He felt in Fenton's coat, which he was still wearing because there was nothing else, in the inside pocket, past the Ledger, with the side of his gray-gloved hand. And found the stub of pencil. And held it out to her.
She took it.
And he took out the Ledger, too. Without being asked. And opened it, there, in the hall, on the flat of his hand, to the last page with writing on it. Her letter, from the roof. Your name is Corin Vane. Mine is Lyra. And under it, his square capitals, crooked, from yesterday. HER FATHER LOVED HER LIKE THE SEA. I FELT IT. DO NOT FORGET. TOMORROW. THE HILL. NOT BY HERSELF.
And below that, a clean space.
He held it out to her. On the flat of his gloved hand. Like a tray.
She looked at the clean space on the page.
She looked at her own writing, above it. Your name is Corin Vane. Small and quick and leaning forward, as if it were in a hurry. She could read it. Every word. She knew what every letter was. She could see the loop at the top of the Y, the way her hand had always made it, ever since the nursery table.
She put the point of the pencil on the clean space.
At the left. Where you begin.
And tried to make her name.
And a line came. A line going nowhere. Wandering across the clean page of Corin's Ledger like the track of a beetle in dust, under her own handwriting from the roof, under his square capitals. It was not a letter. It was not anything.
She lifted the pencil.
She looked at the line.
She tried again. Smaller. Slower. Thinking of it. L. Down, and along. She knew that. She knew it. She had known it since she was five. Down, and along.
Another line. Wandering. Going down a little, and then off sideways, and then stopping, like a beetle that has found a crack.
She gave him back the pencil.
Corin took it. He looked at the two lines on the page. He looked at them for a long time. And then he looked up at her.
"You can't write," he said.
"No."
"You could. On the roof. On Monday night. And yesterday you—" He stopped. He looked down at the page. At her letter, above the two lines. Your name is Corin Vane. Mine is Lyra. "You could."
"I could."
"And now you can't."
"No."
"What did you do?"
Lyra held on to the rail.
Rule one, she thought. No lies between us. Not even the kind ones. It was in his book. In her hand and his. She could not write it any more. But she could still say it.
"I folded it," she said. "The square. The thing my father folded on the last night. I didn't give it to him." She did not look at the study door. "And I didn't keep it. And I didn't open it. I folded it again. Into my father's hand. Into the last room in him. The one he kept for us. Where nobody can follow." She swallowed. "My mother's people did it. Over the water. Folding. It burns. Like everything else. And you pay with the hand you'd use to undo it." She looked down at her own gloved hand on the rail. "So I paid with my hand. My writing. All of it. So nobody can ever make me write it down. Not where it went. Not what it was. Not anything."
Corin did not say anything.
He stood at the foot of the stair, leaning on his crutch, with the Ledger open on the flat of his hand and the two wandering lines on the page, and looked at her.
"Your letters," he said at last. "To your brother."
"Yes."
"And your name."
"Yes."
"And—" He looked down at the page. "And the book. Mine. You wrote in it. On every page. Silas said. I read it all last night. You wrote in the margins, when I'd got something wrong. A kettle is useful. A heron just stands about." He looked up. "You can't do that any more."
"No," said Lyra. "You'll have to get things right by yourself now."
She meant it to be sharp. Quick. A little cross. The way she said things.
It did not come out like that. It came out cracked down the middle, like a cup.
Corin looked at her for a long moment.
And then he did a thing she did not expect.
He shut the Ledger. And put it under his arm, against his ribs, with the crutch. And with his free hand, the right one, in its gray wool glove, he reached out and took her right hand again. Off the rail. The one in Silas's glove. And held it. Not to look at it. Just held it. The way you hold the hand of somebody on a stair, to help them down the last step.
"I'll write it," he said.
She looked at him.
"Whatever you need written," said Corin. "Your letters. To your brother. Anything. You say it, and I'll write it. In square capitals. They'll be crooked. I can't feel the pencil." He did not let go of her hand. "But I'll write it."
Lyra stood very still on the bottom step.
Your name is Corin Vane. Mine is Lyra. You found me in the rain at the door of the Inkwell, in the autumn, and I said "You're the harvester," and you said "Yes," and you didn't say anything else, and I hated you. Then I didn't.
She had written that. On a roof. In the snow. Two nights ago. The last thing she had ever written to him.
He did not know who she was.
And he was holding her hand on the stair, and saying I'll write it.
She did not trust herself to say anything. So she did not. She held on to his hand, in its gray wool glove, and stepped down off the bottom step onto the gray stone of the hall. And stood there.
And behind them, in the study doorway, Captain Oren of the Thorne house guard, twenty-two years, made a sound in his throat, and turned round, and looked very hard at the empty white wall.
And in the study, by the fire, the Inquisitor of the Ministry of Records turned round from the flames.
He had heard. Lyra knew that. He had heard all of it. Every word. I folded it. Into my father's hand. Where nobody can follow.
He did not say anything.
He came out of the study. Past Oren. Into the hall. And stopped, a few paces away from them, on the gray stone, in his shirtsleeves and his unbuttoned waistcoat. And looked at Lyra. And then, for a long moment, up the scrubbed pale stair. Toward the white room at the top. Where her father lay with his closed hand on the blanket, tapping.
And then back at her.
"Show me your hand," said Silas Kross.
