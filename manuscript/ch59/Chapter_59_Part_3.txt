Chapter 59: Fold It Small, Part 3 of 5: The Bargain
The Bargain
"That didn't happen," said Corin.
Everybody looked at him.
He was sitting in the plain wooden chair by the fire with his crutch against the arm of it and his wool-gloved hands on his knees. He was not looking at Lyra. He was looking at Kross. And his face had the look it had when he read a page of a ledger and found a figure that did not add up.
"The garden door," he said. "Lord Thorne in his shirtsleeves, in the rain, with a book in his hand. Take this, K. That didn't happen."
Kross did not move.
"I was there," said Corin. "Yesterday. In a crypt. For three hours. I walked the whole of his last evening, from the eighth bell to the end, beside him, the slow way. I felt the chair under him. I heard the clock. He sat at his desk from the time the visitor went out through the painted shelves until the ninth bell struck. Folding. He never got up. He never went to the garden door." Corin's voice was very flat. "And there was no book. The visitor had a book. A register, in blue cloth. Not that."
The fire crackled.
Lyra stood very still beside the desk.
She looked at Corin. And then at Kross. And she saw Kross's face change.
Not much. It was a face that did not change much. But something went across it, very fast, the way the shadow of a bird goes across a field. And then it was gone, and the face was still again.
But it was not the same stillness as before.
"You walked it," said Kross.
Corin did not answer.
"The stone," said Kross softly. "Lot 41. A Lord's Last Evening. Taken whole." He was looking at Corin the way Lyra had once seen him look at her, across the hole in the floor of the dome, at a quarter to midnight. Very closely. Very carefully. As if Corin were a page written in a small hand. "The Archon's man carried a black case out of the garden door of the Bourse on Monday night. And the Archon's secretary carried it into the Fourth Desk of the Bank yesterday morning at the first bell. And the Auditor of the Fourth Desk weighed it. And wrote, in her own hand, weighed and agreed." He paused. "I had a man at the Bank door. He told me so before the second bell."
Nobody said anything.
"And all the while," said Kross, "it was in a crypt. With you."
He did not say it angrily. He said it the way a man says something he has been almost sure of, and is now quite sure of, and finds that he does not know whether to be glad.
Oh, thought Lyra. Oh, Corin. He was fishing. And you bit.
She saw Corin understand it too. A moment after her. She saw it go into his face, the way the cold had gone into the gray of his hands. That he had just told the Inquisitor of the Ministry of Records, in his own study, the one thing that nobody in the city except the people in that crypt had known.
He did not look away from Kross.
"Rule one," said Corin. "No lies between us. Not even the kind ones." His voice did not shake. "It's her rule. Not yours. But I'm not going to sit in a room and let somebody tell her a lie about her father's last night. Not even you." He looked at Kross steadily. "Especially not you."
Kross looked at him for a long moment.
And then, very slowly, something happened at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The place where a smile would be, if there were one. Lyra had seen that once before. In the glass of a mirror, at Crown Walk, on her own face.
"No," said Kross quietly. "No, I don't suppose you would." He turned to Lyra. "He's right. It didn't happen like that. Not on the last night."
"Then when?"
"Another night." He did not look away. "Another garden door. A long time before. In the rain." A pause. "I'm sorry. I put two nights together to make it simple. I shouldn't have." He glanced at Corin. "I forgot there was somebody in the room who'd been there."
Lyra looked at him.
She did not know if she believed him. She did not know if he was lying now, or had been lying before, or was telling a truth so folded up inside other truths that it might as well be a lie.
She let it go.
Not because she believed him. Because there was a sound upstairs. Tapping. On wood. Four. Two. Nine. Three. Over and over. And she could not stand in this room much longer while her father tapped in the room above it.
"What do you want?" she said.
Kross went to the desk.
He did not touch the green cloth book. He went past it, to the tea tray, and poured a cup of tea from the brown pot, and held it out to her. She did not take it. He put it down on the desk in front of her, beside the book, and stood back.
"On the last night," he said, "your father folded something. While Linus Hale was taking his evening. With his left hand, under the table, on his knee. Very small." He looked at her side. At the list pocket. "I've known that for two years. Linus told me. Not in words. He never said a word about it to anybody. But I sat with him once, a year ago, in his room at the top of the Lamplighter's Rest, and asked him about that night. And while he talked, his left hand on the table was folding a corner of his handkerchief. Over and over. Small." Kross's voice was very quiet. "He didn't know he was doing it. But I did."
Lyra said nothing.
"I've been looking for it ever since," said Kross. "Two years. In the house on Crown Walk. In your father's chair. In Linus Hale's room. In your aunt's. In the Bourse, in the Lady's cupboards." He paused. "I never thought to look on the outside of the case. Under the clasp. Where anybody could see it." Again the place where a smile would be. "That was clever. That was Linus."
"Why do you want it?"
"So that I can burn it."
Lyra stared at him.
"Here," said Kross. He nodded at the small plain grate, with the coal burning well. "Now. In front of you. Without unfolding it. Without looking. So that nobody can ever open it. Not you. Not me. Not anybody." His gray eyes did not move from hers. "Not him."
He did not say who him was.
He did not need to.
"Linus Hale was emptied on Midwinter night," said Kross. "In his room. At his table. With a pen in his hand and a blank page in front of him, and a circle in the corner of the page. They carried him out of there this morning. He's in the Ministry now, in a white room, with a nurse. He doesn't know his name." He did not look away. "There was a list, Miss Thorne. On Friday. On gray paper. Three names. Three people who had held the case your father's evening was in. Hale, L. Was the first. There was a circle beside it." He paused. "He has drawn his circle on the first name. And then he'll go on to the second. And the third."
"Who are they?"
"The second is your aunt."
Lyra did not move.
"Thorne, C. Councillor's widow. By proxy," said Kross. "She held it. For a year. After Linus gave it to her. And then she sent it to the Bourse." He looked at her steadily. "She's at the Crescent now. With your brother. She doesn't know she's on a list. She was crying, this morning, my man at the Crescent says. She doesn't know why."
"And the third?"
Kross did not answer at once.
"The third is somebody you don't know," he said at last. "Somebody who weighed the case, and held it, under clause eleven. And wrote weighed and agreed yesterday morning in her own hand, knowing it wasn't." His voice had gone very quiet. Very even. Too even. "Somebody I would not like to see on another form."
Corin had gone very still in his chair by the fire.
Lyra saw it. She did not know why.
"So," said Kross. "That's what I want. The square. To burn. And if you give it to me, I'll do three things. I'll take your father out of this house this afternoon, in my own carriage, and drive him down the Hill to Crown Walk, and carry him up the five steps and in at the front door, and put him to bed in his own room. And I'll put two of my own men on the door, and no others, and the papers will say he's in his family's care. And I'll go to the Crescent tonight and tell your aunt and your brother to go home to him."
Lyra did not breathe.
"And the second name on the list," said Kross, "will have nothing that anybody could take. Because there'll be nothing left of it. And neither will the third. And the circle will stop at the first name." He looked at her. "That's the third thing."
The fire crackled.
Upstairs, the tapping went on. Four. Two. Nine. Three.
"And if I don't?" said Lyra.
Kross was quiet for a moment.
"Then I'll not take it from you," he said. "I could. You know I could. I could have Wren take it out of your pocket on the stair. I could have the Watch take it off you at the corner of the Crescent. I could have had it on the bridge this morning, or the lane, or the church step." He did not raise his voice. "I haven't. I won't. I'll not take it from you, Miss Thorne. It's your father's. He folded it. You'll give it, or you'll keep it."
"Why?"
"Because he'd want it that way," said Kross. "Your father. And so would she."
He did not say who she was.
He did not need to.
Lyra looked at him for a long time.
At the tired gray eyes. At the deep lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth. At the plain white shirt, and the gray waistcoat, unbuttoned, and the small tight square in its pocket, folded eleven times. At the green cloth book on the corner of his desk, with her mother's silver stitches leaning back on it, as if they were looking over their shoulder.
Because the dark is the day with its eyes shut.
"I want to see him," she said. "First. Before I decide anything."
Kross nodded.
"Wren will take you up," he said. "The nurse is with him. She'll go out, if you ask her." He paused. "She listens. That's all she does. She sits by him and listens to him tap, all day, and writes down what he taps. It's how I knew about the four and the two and the nine and the three. It's how I knew to answer him." He looked at her. "She won't listen to you, if you tell her not to."
Lyra turned to go.
And then turned back.
"The book," she said. "My mother's book."
"Yes?"
"Whatever I decide. About the square." She looked at the green cloth, and the gold tree, and the silver thread. "I want it back."
Kross looked at the book for a long moment.
Then he looked at her.
"No," he said gently. "I'm sorry. Not that."
And for the first time since she had walked into the plain gray house, Lyra saw something in his face that she understood. Completely. At once. Without needing to be told.
It was the face of somebody who has been asked to give up the last thing they have of somebody they loved.
She had seen it in the glass. Two years ago. When Aunt Cressida came to take Rowan away.
She did not say anything else.
She went out of the study, past Oren in his chair by the window, past Corin in his chair by the fire. And Wren was waiting in the hall, at the foot of the scrubbed pale stair, in her white coat with the twelve silver buttons. And Lyra went up the stair behind her, counting. One. Two. Three. Four. And stepped over the fifth step, with the small dark ring on it where somebody had sat in the dark with a cup. Six. Seven.
And the tapping got louder.
The Better Nurse
The room at the top of the house was white.
Not like the hall. The hall was white the way a house is white when nobody has bothered to make it any other color. This was white on purpose. White walls. White ceiling. White painted boards on the floor. A white iron bed against the far wall with white sheets and a white blanket. A white chair beside it. A small white table. And a window, tall and narrow, with the white stone of the Spire filling it almost from edge to edge, so close that Lyra could see the grain in the stone and the little ledges where pigeons sat, gray against the white.
It was the cleanest room she had ever been in.
It smelled of nothing at all.
And in the white bed, propped on white pillows, under the white blanket, with his hands on top of the blanket, was her father.
Lyra stood in the doorway.
Wren had stopped at the top of the stair, and not come in. Lyra could feel her there, behind her, on the landing. Very still. Waiting.
She did not turn round.
She looked at her father.
He was thinner. That was the first thing. He had been thin on the barge. Thin in the cellar on Saltmarsh Row, two weeks ago, when Corin carried him out over his shoulder in the dark. He was thinner now. His face had fallen in round the cheekbones and the temples, the way a tent falls in when you take out the poles, and his long pale hands on the white blanket were all bones and knuckles and blue veins. His hair had been cut. Short. Very neatly. By somebody who knew how. And he had been shaved, and washed, and dressed in a clean white nightshirt buttoned to the throat.
Somebody had taken great care of him.
His eyes were open. Gray. Looking at the window. At the white stone of the Spire.
And his right hand, on the blanket, was tapping.
Not on the blanket. On the edge of the small white table beside the bed. With one long finger. On the white painted wood.
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
Four.
Tap. Tap.
Two.
And a pause. And then:
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
Nine.
Tap. Tap. Tap.
Three.
And a longer pause. And then again. Four.
Witness. Hold. Fold. Home.
Over and over. On and on. The way the tall clock in his study used to tick. As if he would go on tapping until somebody came.
And beside the bed, in the white chair, there was a woman.
She was small and old and neat, in a plain gray dress with a white collar and white cuffs, like a nurse in a hospital. Her gray hair was pinned up under a white cap. She had a notebook open on her knee, and a pencil in her hand. And her face was turned, not toward the bed, but toward the window. With her eyes shut.
Listening.
Lyra saw her pencil move. Without looking. On the page of the notebook on her knee. A small mark, every time Lyra's father tapped. A line. Four lines. Two lines. Nine lines. Three lines. Down the page, in rows, like the count in a ledger.
She listens. That's all she does.
The woman in the white chair opened her eyes.
She looked at Lyra in the doorway. She had pale eyes. Very pale. Almost no color at all. Like water in a white bowl.
"Miss Thorne," she said. Her voice was soft. Gentle. A nurse's voice. "He's been waiting for you."
"How do you know?"
"He told me." The woman held up her notebook. "This morning, before the first bell, he stopped tapping four, two, nine, three. For the first time since they brought him here. And he tapped something else. Over and over. For an hour." She turned a page. "Fourteen. Fourteen. Fourteen."
Lyra did not move.
"And then he went back to the old one," said the woman. "But I wrote it down. I write everything down." She looked at Lyra with her pale water eyes. "I don't know what it means. The Inquisitor said you would."
Fourteen.
There are fourteen stairs to the first landing. I've counted them every day since I was nine.
Lyra could not speak.
"Will you go out?" she said at last. Her voice came out very small. "Please. I'd like to be alone with him."
The woman in gray looked at her a moment longer.
Then she shut the notebook, and put the pencil in it to keep the place, and stood up. She was very small standing. Smaller than Lyra.
"Of course," she said. "I'll be on the landing. With the cadet." She came to the door. And stopped beside Lyra. And looked up at her. "I won't listen," she said. "The Inquisitor said I wasn't to, if you asked. And you asked." She paused. "But I'll hear him. If he taps. I can't not hear. I'm sorry. It's how I am."
She went out.
And shut the door.
And Lyra was alone in the white room with her father.
She went to the bed.
She did not sit in the white chair. She knelt on the white boards beside the bed, the way she had knelt on the barge, in the dark, in the reeds, with the fog on the water. And took off Silas's old black glove. And put her bare hand, the cut one, flat on the white blanket beside his tapping hand.
He went on tapping. Nine.
She waited.
Three.
And then she turned her hand over, palm up, on the blanket. And slid it, very gently, under his. So that his long finger, coming down for the next tap, came down not on the white wood of the table but on her palm.
Four.
On her palm.
He stopped.
His finger lay on the middle of her palm, where it had come down. Quite still. Long and thin and cold, with the nail cut very short and very neat.
And his eyes moved.
Slowly. Away from the window. Away from the white stone of the Spire. Down. Across the white blanket. To his own hand. And under it, hers.
And up.
To her face.
Lyra did not breathe.
She had seen him look at her before. On the barge. In the cellar. Once or twice, in the two years since he went quiet, at Crown Walk, when Mrs. Hobb brought him down to sit by the fire in the hall and she sat on the bottom stair. He had looked at her. The way you look at a picture on a wall that has been there so long you have stopped seeing it.
This was not that.
He was looking at her. At her. At her face. With his gray eyes. And something was moving in them. Far back. Very far back. Like a light in a window at the end of a long road, seen from a great way off, at night, in the rain.
His finger moved on her palm.
Not four. Not two. Not nine. Not three.
It moved slowly. And pressed. And lifted. And pressed again. Counting.
She counted with it. She could not help it. She had counted all her life.
One. Two. Three. Four.
Five. Six. Seven.
Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven.
Twelve. Thirteen.
Fourteen.
And stopped.
And lay on her palm. Still.
And her father looked at her with the light at the end of the long road in his eyes and his finger on her palm and said, in a voice like an old door that has not been opened for two years:
"Lyra."
She put her head down on the white blanket.
She did not make a sound. She knelt on the white boards beside the bed with her forehead on the blanket by his hand and her own hand under his, and her shoulders shook, and she did not make a sound, because the nurse was on the landing and she could not not hear.
And her father's other hand came up off the blanket.
Slowly. The left one. With the plain gold ring on it, worn thin. And came across. And came down. On her head. On her short burned hair.
And stayed there.
Except for the children. I'll keep the children. As long as I can.
He had kept her.
Through two years of nothing. Through the cellar and the barge and the reeds and the fire on the hay and the litter up the north road and this white room. Through every room in him that he had burned on purpose, so that there would be nothing left for them to ask. He had kept her. Her name. And fourteen stairs.
She stayed like that for a long time.
Until the light from the window, off the white stone of the Spire, had moved across the white boards from the foot of the bed to the head of it. Until, high up at the top of the Spire, the practice bell rang again, and a moment later, very faint, very high, very sweet, the Choir began to sing. Children's voices. Coming down through the white stone. Through the wall. Into the white room.
She lifted her head.
Her father's hand slid off her hair onto the blanket.
He was looking at the window again. At the Spire. Listening to the children sing. His eyes were open, and gray, and the light at the end of the long road was going out of them. Slowly. The way it had come.
His right hand on her palm began, very faintly, to tap.
Four.
She looked at him.
She looked at him, and knew.
Not from anything anybody had said. Not from Silas, or the nurse, or Kross. From his face. From the fallen-in cheeks and the blue veins and the bones of his hands. From the way his breath went in and out, very shallow, very slow, with a small catch at the top of it, like a man going up a stair who has to stop on every step.
He had kept her as long as he could.
And it was nearly as long as he could.
Two.
She reached into the list pocket of her mother's gown, and took out the folded square.
