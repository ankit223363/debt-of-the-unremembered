Chapter 59: Fold It Small, Part 2 of 5: The Inquisitor's House
The Inquisitor's House
The door was opened by a girl in white.
Not a servant. Lyra knew that at once. A servant would have been in black, or gray, or a cap and apron. This girl was in a white coat, plain and close-fitting, buttoned up to the throat with a row of small silver buttons. Twelve of them. Lyra counted. White breeches, and white stockings, and plain black shoes. Her hair was fair and pulled back very tight and very smooth into a knot at the back of her head. Her face was pale and young and quite still, the way a face is still when it has been taught to be.
She was perhaps nineteen.
Lyra had seen her before. Twice. On Monday night, in the gallery of the Memory Bourse, in a white mask, sitting very straight in a box three along from the Lady's. And then again, without the mask, at the table of loves, with her hands shaking. I collected Sergeant Moll tonight. He's in Room Nine. He touched my hand. Once, and twice. What does it mean?
And Lyra had taken off her glove and tapped on the girl's palm. Heard.
"Wren," said Lyra.
The girl in the white coat looked at her.
Not at her face. At her hands. At Silas's old black gloves, too big, worn through at one fingertip. And then up, at her face, under the hood. And something moved in the still young face that had been taught to be still. Just for a moment. Like a fish moving under ice.
"Miss Thorne," said Wren. Her voice was quiet and very correct. A clerk's voice. "The Inquisitor is expecting you. And the gentleman." Her eyes went past Lyra. To Oren. To Corin, on the crutch, at the bottom of the five steps. And stayed on Corin a moment longer than they needed to. "Both gentlemen. Will you come in?"
Lyra did not move.
"How long has he been expecting us?"
Wren hesitated.
It was a very small hesitation. Half a breath. But Lyra saw it.
"Since the first bell," said Wren. "He was up before it. He's been sitting in his study. He made tea." Another half-breath. "He never makes tea. I've been his cadet for two years. I've never seen him make tea."
She stood back from the door.
Lyra went in.
She did not know what she had expected. She had thought about it all the way up the Hill, under the blue lamps, counting. The Inquisitor's own house. A man who could walk across the burning floor of the Memory Bourse with his hands behind his back as if he were walking through a field. A man who stood on a gilt chair inside the bronze doors and looked at every face. A man who sat on a church step beside a doctor at the sixth bell and left a sweet paper folded eleven times. She had thought his house would be like a trap. Dark and close and full of things you could not see.
It was not.
It was empty.
That was the word. Not bare. Empty. The way a house is empty when somebody has just moved in, or is just about to move out. A narrow hall, very clean, with a floor of plain gray stone and walls of plain white plaster. No pictures on the walls. No rug. No table with a bowl on it for letters. No hooks for coats. A plain wooden stair going up on the left, with no carpet, the wood scrubbed pale. Two doors on the right, both shut. And at the end of the hall, a third door, standing a little open, with light coming through it, and a smell. Tea. And coal smoke. And something else, very faint, that Lyra did not know at first and then did.
Peppermint.
She stood in the hall and breathed it.
Behind her, Oren came in. He stood just inside the door with his good hand on the hilt of his sword and looked at the empty white walls and the scrubbed stair and the shut doors, one by one, the way a soldier looks at a room he might have to fight in. His face did not change. But his hand did not leave the hilt.
And then Corin. Up the five steps, one at a time, on the crutch. Slowly. Into the hall. He stopped beside Oren and leaned on the crutch and breathed hard, and looked round. And his eyes went to the stair. And stayed there.
Lyra looked too.
On the fifth step of the stair, in the middle, there was a small dark mark on the scrubbed pale wood. Round. The size of a coin. As if somebody had sat there once, a long time, in the dark, with a cup of something hot, and set it down.
Kross waited on Wren's stair, on the fifth step, in the dark.
That was in Corin's book. She had read it. Not this stair. Another one. Wren's, in the Ink Quarter, at Mrs. Marta's. But it was the same thing. A man who sat on the fifth step, in the dark, and waited.
Wren shut the front door behind them.
And then she did a thing that Lyra did not expect.
She turned round, with her back to the door, and put out her hand. Her right hand. Bare. Palm up. Toward Lyra. Not to shake. Just held out, flat, between them, in the empty white hall. The way you hold your hand out for a bird to land on.
Lyra looked at it.
Then she took off Silas's old black glove. And put out her own hand. And laid two fingers on the girl's palm.
And Wren tapped. With one finger. On the back of Lyra's hand.
Once.
And then twice, quick.
Heard.
Lyra stood very still.
"Sergeant Moll," said Wren, very low. So low that Oren, three paces away, could not have heard it. "He's in Room Nine still. At the Ministry. He's all right. They haven't touched him. The Inquisitor said not to." Her eyes did not leave Lyra's face. "I go in every morning. With his breakfast. And I tap it on his hand. And he taps it back." She swallowed. "I thought you'd want to know."
"Thank you," said Lyra.
Wren nodded. Once. Quickly. As if she did not want to be thanked, or did not know what to do with it.
And then, still very low, she said: "The tune. The one you hummed me. On Monday. The fourth note." She hesitated. "I hummed it to him. To Moll. This morning. All four notes. And he cried." She looked down at their hands. "He's never cried. Not once, in Room Nine. Not even when they took his papers."
She took her hand away.
And turned. And was a cadet again. Straight-backed and still and correct, in her white coat with the twelve silver buttons.
"This way, Miss Thorne," she said, in her clerk's voice. "The Inquisitor's study is at the end of the hall."
Lyra put her glove back on.
She looked at Oren. And then at Corin.
Corin was looking at Wren. Not the way he looked at strangers. Not the polite kind look. Lyra knew that look now. She had had it from him for a day and a half. This was different. He was looking at Wren the way he had looked at the word in blue chalk on the floor of the crypt. As if it were a word he could read, and did not understand, and could not stop looking at.
"I know you," he said.
Wren turned her head.
"Sir?"
"I know you." Corin's voice was quite flat. Puzzled. "From before. I don't know where. You lied for me. Once. I think." He frowned. "There's a stone. A beach stone. In my coat. Gray, with a white line round it. You gave it me."
Wren's still face went quite white.
She did not answer. She looked at Corin for a long moment, with her pale young face gone the color of her coat. And Lyra saw her mouth open, a little, as if she were going to say something. And then shut.
She turned and walked down the hall to the open door at the end, and stood beside it, very straight, with her hands at her sides. Waiting.
Lyra looked at Corin.
"You remember her," she said.
She had not meant to say it. It came out of her before she could stop it. And it came out wrong. Too sharp. Too quick. The way her voice came out when she was hurt and did not want anybody to know.
Corin looked at her.
"Not really," he said. "The stone. The lie. Not her." He was quiet. "It's in the book. Wren. Inquisitor. Lied for me in the autumn. Gave me a beach stone. I read it last night." He looked down at his gloved hands on the crutch. "I know her the way I know Oren. From the book, and from before. I don't know why some of it's still there and some of it isn't."
I know why, thought Lyra. Because you didn't need to burn her.
She did not say it.
She turned and walked down the hall toward the open door, and the smell of tea and coal smoke and peppermint, and the girl in white beside it.
Behind her, Oren said, very low, to Corin: "Mind your feet. There's a step."
And the crutch came after her, on the gray stone. Tap. Swing. Tap. Swing.
Wren pushed the door open.
And stood back.
"Miss Thorne, sir," she said, into the room. "And the gentlemen."
And from inside the room, a voice that Lyra knew. Calm and quiet and very even. A voice that had never once in its life needed to shout.
"Thank you, Wren. Come in, Miss Thorne. All of you. Please. Sit down." A pause. "There's tea."
Green Cloth
The study was the only room in the house that looked as if anybody lived in it.
It was small. Smaller than her father's. Square, with one window looking out at the back, at a high garden wall and, above the wall, very close, the white stone of the Spire going up and up into the gray sky until you had to tip your head right back to see where it ended. There was a fire in a small plain grate, coal, burning well. There was a rug in front of it, gray, plain, worn. There were two chairs by the fire, plain wooden chairs with arms, and a third chair, the same, by the window. And there was a desk.
It was the only fine thing in the house. Lyra saw that at once. Dark wood, old, very good, with brass handles on the drawers worn bright by hands. The kind of desk you inherit. There was nothing on it but a tea tray, with a brown pot and four plain white cups, and a small paper of sweets, and a book.
She did not look at the book.
Not yet. She made herself not look at it. She looked at the man.
He was standing by the fire.
She had seen him many times now. On the church step at the sixth bell, beside Silas. At the tower window, lifting his hat. At the table of loves, with his hand on hers. At the edge of the hole, smiling. On a gilt chair inside the bronze doors with the dome burning over his head. She had never once seen him without his long plain gray coat and his hat.
He was not wearing them now.
He was in his shirtsleeves. A plain white shirt, open at the throat, and a gray waistcoat, unbuttoned. His hair was dark, and going gray at the sides, and cut short. He was not tall. Not short. Thin, but not like Silas, not a heron. Thin like a blade. His face was the face she knew, quiet and still and very careful, with deep lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth, and gray eyes. But without the hat, and the coat, and the gilt chair, it looked—
Tired.
That was the word. He looked like a man who had not slept for a long time. Not one night. A great many nights. Years of them.
He had a cup of tea in his hand. He put it down on the mantelpiece when she came in.
"Miss Thorne," said Silas Kross.
She did not answer.
He looked past her. At Oren, in the doorway, with his hand on his sword. At Corin, behind Oren, on the crutch.
"Captain," he said to Oren. "Your shoulder. Is it bad?"
Oren did not answer either.
Kross did not seem to mind. He looked at Corin.
"Mister Vane," he said. Very quietly. "I'm glad you're alive. I saw the dome stop. From the doors. I didn't know who it was. Not then." He looked at Corin's wool-gloved hands on the crutch. "I know now."
Corin looked at him.
Lyra watched Corin look at him. And she saw that he knew him. Not from the book only. From before. From somewhere deep, where the gray had not reached. His face went still and careful, the way it went still when he was reading a room, and his gloved hands closed on the crutch.
"You let me run," said Corin. "In the autumn. From the Ministry. You let me run to see where I'd go."
"Yes."
"And who'd help me."
"Yes."
"And you followed. All the way. Down the river. Up the north road. To the Bourse."
"Yes," said Kross. "I did."
He said it quite simply. Not proud. Not sorry. The way a man says yes, that is my coat.
"Sit down," he said. "Please. Mister Vane, you should sit. You're gray to the face." He moved one of the plain chairs a little nearer the fire. "Captain. There's a chair by the window, if you'd rather have your back to the wall. I know I would."
Oren looked at the chair by the window.
Then, slowly, he went and sat in it. With his back to the wall. And his sword across his knees. And his eyes on Kross.
Corin sat in the chair by the fire. Slowly. With the crutch beside him.
Lyra did not sit.
She stood in the middle of the gray rug and looked at the desk.
At the book.
It lay on the left-hand corner of the dark wood. By itself. Squared to the edge of the desk, the way you square a thing you care about. Not large. A little bigger than a hand. Bound in cloth. Green cloth. Faded at the corners to the color of moss, and worn smooth at the spine, and soft. The kind of green that her mother had loved. The green of the curtains in the study at Crown Walk.
And on the front of it, in thread, stitched.
A tree. In gold. With its branches spread. And in the branches, a crown.
And under the tree, smaller, in silver thread, in a line, the words.
Lyra did not have to go nearer to read them. She knew them. She had known them all her life. She had traced them with her finger on the corner of her father's desk when she was too small to read, and spelled them out when she was six, and said them to herself in the dark when she was nine, after the carriage went away and her mother did not come back, over and over, under the blankets, the way you hold a stone in your hand.
She went nearer anyway.
She went to the desk, and stood beside it, and looked down at the green cloth book with the gold tree and the silver words. Close. So close she could see the stitches.
They were small. Very small, and very even, and they leaned back. Every letter, leaning back a little, as if it were looking over its shoulder. Her mother's hand. Her mother's stitches. The same lean as the letters on the height chart on the nursery wall at Crown Walk, the ones that said L. and R. in her mother's ink, with the lines going up the wall, year by year.
The dark is the day with its eyes shut.
She stood and looked at it.
She did not touch it.
She wanted to. She wanted to so badly that her hand in Silas's old glove was shaking at her side. She wanted to put her fingers on the silver thread and trace the letters the way she had when she was small. The dark. Is the day. With its eyes. Shut. She wanted to pick it up and hold it against her chest and walk out of this plain gray house with it and never let anybody touch it again.
She did not touch it.
"Ro asked me a question," she said.
She did not turn round. She said it to the book.
"My brother. He's at our aunt's. On the Crescent. He hasn't spoken to me for two years. And this morning he sent me a letter with one question in it." She looked at the silver stitches. "He saw this. In your case. On Sunday. In our aunt's hall."
Behind her, Kross did not say anything.
"It was on my father's desk," said Lyra. "For nine years. On the left-hand corner. Like that. By the lamp. My mother stitched it the winter before she went. Ro watched her. He was seven. He remembers the needle going in and out." Her voice was quite steady. "And after my father went quiet, it wasn't there any more. And nobody knew where it had gone. Not my aunt. Not Bertram. And Bertram always knows."
She turned round.
Kross was standing by the fire, in his shirtsleeves, with his hands at his sides. Looking at her.
"So that's Ro's question," said Lyra. "And now it's mine." She did not raise her voice. "Why do you have my mother's book?"
The fire crackled.
Outside the window, very high up, at the top of the white Spire, a bell began to ring. Not a church bell. Smaller. Higher. A practice bell. Calling the Choir.
Kross did not look away from her.
"Because she gave it to me," he said.
Lyra stood very still.
"When?"
"A long time ago."
"Before she went?"
He did not answer that.
"It was on my father's desk," said Lyra again. "For nine years after she went. Every day. I saw it. Ro saw it."
"Yes," said Kross. "It was. That's where she wanted it kept. On his desk, where he would see it every day. Until it was time." His gray eyes did not move from hers. "And then she wanted it given to me."
"Time for what?"
Kross did not answer.
"Who gave it to you?" said Lyra. "If she was gone. If my father was—" She stopped. "Who took it off his desk and gave it to you?"
"He did," said Kross.
The practice bell at the top of the Spire stopped ringing.
In the quiet after it, Lyra heard Oren, in his chair by the window, let out a breath. And Corin, by the fire, move his crutch. And somewhere in the house, upstairs, a long way off, a sound. Very faint. Tapping. On wood. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. And then a pause. Tap. Tap.
Four. Two.
She did not move.
"My father," she said.
"Two autumns ago," said Kross. "On the last night. Before the ninth bell. Before Linus Hale." His voice was very quiet now. Very even. "He came to the garden door of his own house, in the rain, in his shirtsleeves, with that book in his hand. To somebody who was leaving. And gave it to them. And said, Take this, K. She'd want you to have it. Take it, and don't come back." He looked down at the book on the corner of his desk. "So they took it. And didn't come back. For a long time."
Lyra looked at him.
She looked at him for a long time.
Somebody who was leaving. To them. K.
"You're telling me it was given to somebody," she said slowly. "Not to you. To somebody. And they gave it to you."
Kross did not answer.
"Or you're telling me it was you."
He did not answer that either.
He only looked at her. With his tired gray eyes. And then, after a long moment, he reached out to the desk, and picked up the small paper of sweets that lay by the tea tray, and took one out. A peppermint. White and round and smooth. And held it out to her, on his open palm.
The way Pallister had held one out on the stairs of the house on Crown Walk, every Midwinter, when she was small.
"Peppermint?" said Silas Kross.
Lyra looked at the white sweet on his palm.
"No," she said.
"No," said Kross. "No, I didn't think so." He put it back in the paper. And folded the paper over it. Once. Twice. Three times. Again. And again. Small. Very small. Eleven times. Until it was a tight hard square no bigger than her thumbnail. And put it in his waistcoat pocket.
She watched his fingers do it.
She knew those folds. She had seen them on the church step. On a sweet paper, left behind. Eleven times folded.
Fold it small.
She did not say anything.
"Your father is upstairs," said Kross. "In the room at the top. At the back. With the window on the Spire." He nodded at the ceiling. "You can hear him. He's been tapping since the first bell. Since before it. As if he knew you were coming." He paused. "Perhaps he did."
"I want to see him."
"You will."
"Now."
"In a moment," said Kross. "First, there's a thing I need to ask you. One thing. And then you can go up, and sit with him, as long as you like." He looked at her. Steadily. "And I think you know what it is."
Lyra's hand went to her side. To the bodice of her mother's gown. To the list pocket sewn flat into the seam.
She did not mean it to. It went by itself.
And Kross's gray eyes followed it. And stayed there.
"Yes," he said softly. "That."
