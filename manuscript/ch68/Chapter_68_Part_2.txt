Chapter 68: A Thing Put Back, Part 2 of 5: Through the City
Through the City
Veridia was waking up.
It woke up slowly in winter, and grudgingly, like an old man getting out of bed. The sky over the roofs was the color of dishwater. The snow from the week before had gone gray and hard in the gutters, piled up in long dirty ridges along the edges of the streets, with black holes in it where people had thrown out their ashes. The cobbles between the ridges were wet and black and shining, and Juno ran on them carefully, on the sides of her feet, the way she had learned to, because the middle of her feet still did not like being stood on.
She went through Tallow Street, where the coal carts were already lined up outside Abner Cole's yard, the horses steaming, the coal men in their leather hoods shouting to each other. Through Comb Street, where the shutters were coming down off the shop fronts one by one, with a bang and a rattle. Through the little crooked lane behind the tannery, where you held your breath. Down the Rope Street stairs, three at a time, with her hand on the cold iron rail.
And everywhere, people were talking about the light.
They had been talking about it for eleven days. Since Midwinter. Since the night the dome came down at the Bourse and the pillar of white light went up out of the ground into the sky over the river, and stood there, for as long as it takes to breathe a hundred times, and the snow that was falling turned gold as it fell through it. Everyone in the city had seen it. Everyone. You could not have missed it. And everyone, for a moment, as they stood in the street with their faces turned up to it, had remembered something.
Something small. Something they had forgotten. Something that had gone, a long time ago, to the lamps or the Ministry or the jars, and that they had not even known was missing until it came back. A smell. A face. The sound of a voice. Juno had remembered her father's hands. Just his hands. Brown and square and nicked with little scars from his tools, turning a clock over, gently, to look at its back. And his whistle. The two notes he whistled when he came in the door, so you would know it was him.
It had lasted as long as the light. Then the light had gone out, and the memory had gone with it, and she could not get it back. She only knew she had had it.
Everyone was like that. Everyone had had something for a moment, and lost it again. And so everyone talked about it. In the queues at the pumps, at the stalls, on the stairs. What did you get? Mine was my mother singing. Mine was the dog we had when I was small. Mine was my wedding. I'd forgotten my own wedding. And then, lower: they say a man held the roof up. They say he stood in the middle of the floor with his hands over his head. They say he had gray hands.
Juno did not say anything to anybody. She ran.
At the bottom of the Rope Street stairs she came out onto Lantern Quay, and slowed down, and walked. You did not run on the quays. Not this week.
The lamps were lit.
They stood all along the edge of the quay, between the cobbles and the river wall, every thirty paces, the tall black iron posts with their glass globes on top. Ash lamps. They had stood there all Juno's life, and they had always been dark in the day and white at night. Now they were lit in the day. And they were blue.
A pale, thin, cold blue, like the blue at the bottom of a candle flame, or like ice with the light behind it. Burning inside their globes in the gray morning, all along the quay, as far as she could see, right down to the docks. They made a faint sound, if you stood close. A thin high whine, like a gnat. Nobody stood close.
There were notices pasted on the posts. The same notice, on every one. REPAIRS IN PROGRESS. THE PUBLIC IS NOT TO TOUCH THE LAMPS. BY ORDER. Juno could read it now. REPAIRS took her a while. But nobody was repairing anything. The lamps just burned, blue, day and night, and the notices went soft in the damp and began to peel.
And there were gray coats.
Two of them at the foot of the Rope Street stairs. Two more halfway along the quay, by the steps down to the water. Two on the end of Lantern Bridge, one on each side, with their hands behind their backs, looking at everyone who went over. Enforcers. In their long gray coats and their flat gray caps, with short swords at their hips and carbines slung on their backs. There had always been some on the quays. Not like this. Not in pairs, every hundred paces, watching.
Juno walked past them with her hands in her pockets and her eyes on the cobbles and her face blank and bored, the way you walked past gray coats if you had any sense. One of them looked at her. She felt it. She did not look back. She did not walk faster. She went on along the quay at the same steady pace, a skinny girl in a too-big coat with her scarf over her chin, nobody, nothing, and felt the packet against her chest with every step.
At the corner where the quay met the lane to the Fish Stairs, there was a wall. An old brick wall, the side of a warehouse, black with soot. It was always covered in notices. Sales and sermons and lost dogs and the dates of the hangings.
There was a new one on top of all the rest. Big. Black letters on yellow paper.
She stopped and read it. She had read it before. She read it again anyway. Every time.
VANE, CORIN.
HARVESTER, FORMERLY OF THIS MINISTRY.
LAME IN THE LEFT LEG. GRAY TO THE SHOULDER.
TO BE HELD ON SIGHT.
And under it, smaller, a drawing. A man's face. Long and thin and tired, with deep lines and dark eyes and a jaw that needed a shave. It was not very good. It did not look much like him. But somebody had drawn on it, since yesterday. In charcoal. Round the man's head, a ring. A rough circle, like the gold rings round the heads of the saints painted on church walls.
And under the drawing, in the same charcoal, in big wobbly letters that leaned to the right, someone had written one word.
ROOF.
Juno looked at it for a long moment.
Then she turned away, and went on, quickly now, into the lane, toward the river and Saint Bride's, with the packet against her heart and the gray coats behind her and the blue lamps whining in the gray morning.
Saint Bride's Lane
Saint Bride's Lane went up from the river.
Not gently. It went up the side of the old town the way a goat goes up a cliff, in steps. Steps cut into the rock, and steps built of old brick, and steps of wood where the brick had fallen away, with landings between them where the lane turned a corner and squeezed between two leaning houses so close that their upper floors almost touched overhead. Two hundred and twelve steps, from the river wall at the bottom to the church gate at the top. Lyra had counted them, the first time. Juno counted them every time now, because Lyra was not here to.
She counted them under her breath as she climbed. One. Two. Three. On the sides of her feet.
The lane was very narrow. In places she could have touched the houses on both sides at once. They were old and black and crooked, with little windows crammed in anyhow under the eaves, and doors at the top of short flights of their own steps, and washing hung out on poles above the lane, stiff and gray with frost. Water dripped from the eaves. The steps were wet and worn and slippery, with a gutter down the middle of them where the rain ran in the wet months and where now a thin black ice lay. It smelled of old stone and coal smoke and cabbage and the river.
Forty. Forty-one. Forty-two.
A cat watched her from a windowsill. A woman with a bucket came out of a door, and looked at her, and went back in.
Seventy. Seventy-one.
Her feet hurt. She went on.
At the hundredth step there was a landing, a little square of flat stones with a pump on it, and a stone trough with ice in it, and an old man sitting on the edge of the trough in a blanket, smoking a pipe. He nodded to her. She nodded back. She had seen him every time. She did not know his name. He was always there, at the hundredth step, smoking, as if he were guarding it.
"Gray coats in the lane last night," said the old man, around his pipe. Not looking at her.
Juno stopped.
"At the top?" she said.
"At the top. By the gate. Four of 'em. Smoking. Stamping their feet. Waiting." He took the pipe out of his mouth and looked at it. "Went off at the second bell after midnight. Didn't go in."
"Thank you," said Juno.
The old man put the pipe back in his mouth.
She went on up. A hundred and one. A hundred and two. Quicker now. On the sides of her feet, with her hand on the cold wet wall.
A hundred and fifty. The lane turned and went under an arch, a low dark tunnel through the bottom of a house, and she went through it with her head down, and came out the other side into gray light.
Two hundred.
Two hundred and twelve.
And there was the church.
Saint Bride's was not a church for the Hill. Mrs. Doyle always said so. It was small and squat and black with soot, a low square tower and a low long roof, built of rough gray stone that had gone black with three hundred years of the old town's smoke. It sat at the top of the lane on a little shelf of rock, crowded in by the backs of houses on three sides, with a tiny churchyard in front of it full of leaning stones, and a wall round the churchyard, and a gate in the wall. Over the door there was a lamp in an iron cage, an ordinary oil lamp, not an Ash lamp. It was lit. It was always lit. Mrs. Doyle saw to that.
The gate was shut.
There was nobody in the lane. No gray coats. Only the trodden snow by the gate where boots had stood and stamped for a long time, and a scatter of pipe ash on the snow, and a twist of chewed tobacco, and a little brown puddle where someone had spat.
Juno looked at the trodden snow for a long moment.
Then she looked up and down the lane. Both ways. Carefully. At every window. At every door. At the roofs.
Nothing.
She went to the gate and opened it, quietly, and went through, and shut it behind her. Up the short path between the leaning stones. Up the three steps to the church door. Under the caged lamp.
She knocked. Three. Then two. The way you knocked at Saint Bride's.
There was a long pause. Long enough to be frightening.
Then a bolt went back, on the inside. And another. And the door opened a crack, and an old red face looked out at her round it, in a rusty black shawl, with a broom held in both hands like a pike.
"Oh," said Mrs. Doyle. "It's you." The broom came down. "Get in, love. Get in, quick. There were men."
"I know," said Juno. "The old man at the trough said."
"Old Tull. He'd tell the Saints their own business, that one." Mrs. Doyle pulled the door wider and Juno slipped through, and the old woman shut it behind her and shot both bolts, and stood with her back against it, breathing hard. "Four of 'em. Half the night. I sat behind this door with the broom the whole time. Didn't sleep a wink." She looked at Juno. And her red face changed. "What is it? You've got a look."
Juno put her hand flat on her chest. On the packet.
"Is Gideon down there?" she said.
"Where else would he be?" said Mrs. Doyle. "He's always down there. Him and his da. Why?"
Juno did not answer. She was already going down the dark nave between the empty pews, toward the low door in the corner that led to the crypt stair, as fast as her feet would let her.
Behind her, Mrs. Doyle said, "Saints preserve us," and picked up her broom, and came after.
The Crypt
Twenty-three steps down.
They were steep and narrow and old, cut into the rock under the church, and they went down in a straight line into the dark with a rope along the wall to hold. Juno counted them. She always did. On the sides of her feet, with her hand on the rope.
At the bottom there was warmth.
That was the thing about Saint Bride's crypt. Upstairs, the church was as cold as a church. Down here, there was the brazier. A big iron basket of coals on three legs, in the middle of the floor, glowing orange, that Doctor Silas kept going day and night with coal that Abner Cole sent up the lane in sacks and did not ask to be paid for. The heat came off it in a steady wave, and the orange light came off it too, and lay on the floor and the walls and the low round roof.
The roof was the best part. Lyra had said so, the first time. It was made of round arches, one after another, all the way along, low and curved and close over your head, built of small yellow-brown bricks gone dark with age. Like being inside a loaf of bread, Lyra had said. And it was. Like being inside a great warm loaf, with the crust curving over you, and the orange glow of the oven in the middle.
There had been beds down here. Rows of them, in the autumn. Fifty-two children, Doctor Silas said, on straw pallets between the pillars, coughing, forgetting things, with their sweet breath. Most of them were gone now. Some to their mothers. Some to the step women's houses. Some to the churchyard. There were only six left, on six pallets at the far end, under the last arch, asleep in a row under gray blankets, with a candle burning on a box beside them.
And the walls were covered in chalk.
Blue chalk, mostly. Some white. Some red. Words, drawn big and careful on the old bricks, all round the crypt, at the height a boy could reach. The same five words, over and over. Juno could read them now.
LYRA.
READ.
V.
DA.
SAUL.
Over and over and over. On the walls, on the pillars, on the floor round the brazier. On the side of the big copper kettle. On the bottom step. LYRA READ V DA SAUL. LYRA READ V DA SAUL. Some of them very old now, from the autumn, smudged and faded. Some of them fresh. SAUL had a line drawn under it, every time, since the fire.
Gideon was sitting by the brazier.
He was small. Smaller than Juno, though he was a year younger and that was not much. Thin, with a pale pointed face and short dark hair that stuck up at the back, and big ears. He had a knife in one hand and a bit of wood in the other, and he was whittling it. A boat. It always was. There were little wooden boats all along the edge of the brazier's base, a whole fleet of them, flat-bottomed and pointed and lopsided, each one a bit better than the last.
He did not look up when she came in.
Behind him, against the wall, under the nearest arch, there was a man.
He was very big. As big as the captain, nearly. A docker's body, broad and heavy, with great sloping shoulders and arms like hams and hands like shovels. He had a broken nose, flattened sideways, and an old scar on one cheek in the shape of a hook. He was sitting on the floor with his back against the brick wall and his legs stretched straight out in front of him and his two huge hands lying on his knees, palms down, the way someone had put them there. He was looking at the brazier. He had been looking at the brazier, Juno knew, since she left yesterday at dusk. He did not blink much. He did not move at all.
Big Gideon. Gideon's da.
There was chalk on the wall over his head too. One word. Bigger than all the others. DA.
"Juno," said a tired voice.
Doctor Silas was at the table at the side of the crypt, under the second arch, with a lamp and a kettle and a cup. He was very tall and very thin, like a heron standing in a pond, with long legs and a long neck and a long gray face, and spectacles that sat crooked on his nose because one of the arms was bent. He looked as if he had not slept for a week. He always did. He had his sleeves rolled up and his hands were red from washing.
"You're early," he said. "Is your mother well? Are your feet..."
He stopped. He was looking at her face. And he put down his cup.
"What is it?"
Juno did not answer him. She went across the crypt to the brazier and knelt down on the warm floor in front of Gideon, and he looked up at last.
His eyes were gray. Flat gray, like a puddle on a cloudy day. There was nothing in them. There never was. Not since the Bourse. Not since the four winters he had spent down there with Lady Vivienne taking it out of him, a little at a time, every feeling he had. He looked at Juno the way you would look at a chair.
"You're early," he said. Flat. Short. Not a question.
"Gideon," said Juno. "It's come."
The knife stopped.
She put her hand inside her coat and took out the oilcloth packet and held it out to him on her two flat palms. Like a plate.
Gideon looked at it.
He did not take it. He did not reach for it. He sat by the brazier with the knife in one hand and the half-made boat in the other and looked at the packet on her palms, and his flat gray face did not change at all.
But the knife in his hand had begun, very slightly, to shake.
Behind them, at the foot of the stairs, Mrs. Doyle had come down with her broom. She stood there, breathing hard. Doctor Silas had got up from the table. Nobody spoke.
"From him?" said Gideon.
"From him," said Juno. "A man brought it. All the way from the end of the world. In a turnip cart."
Gideon put down the knife. Very carefully. On the floor, beside the boats. He put down the half-made boat beside it.
Then he looked at the packet again. And then at Juno. And then he said the thing he always said, about everything, in his flat short voice.
"I can read five words," he said. "Read it to me."
Read It Slow
She opened it by the brazier.
They all came to watch. They did not mean to crowd her, she could tell, but they did. Doctor Silas came and sat down on the low stool on the other side of the brazier, with his long knees up round his ears and his long red hands hanging between them. Mrs. Doyle stood behind Juno with her broom, breathing. Even two of the children on the pallets at the far end had woken, and were sitting up under their gray blankets, watching with big eyes.
Gideon did not move from where he sat. He only turned a little, so that he could see.
Juno undid the thong. It was knotted tight, and her fingers were cold, and it took a while. Then she unfolded the oilcloth. It was stiff and shiny and smelled of bread and smoke and something sweet and burnt, and when she opened it there was a fine gray dust in the creases. Ash, she thought. Or just dirt from the road.
Inside there was a piece of paper.
One piece. Folded once. Thick, creamy, old paper, the edge on one side rough and ragged where it had been cut out of a book, along the stitching, with a knife. She could see the tiny holes where the thread had gone through.
She unfolded it.
And it was covered in his writing.
Top to bottom. Edge to edge. The big leaning capitals in gray pencil, crowded together, line after line, some of them going uphill and some going down. In two places a letter had been crossed out with a little line and another letter written over it, small. At the very bottom, the last lines were squeezed in so tight that they ran into each other.
And under them, in the last finger's width of paper, two lines in smaller letters. Very careful. Every letter made slowly, like letters cut out of wood.
She found those first. Her eyes went to them as if they had been pulled.
IT SAYS QUICK. GO QUICK. C.
And under it, smaller still, right at the edge:
JUNO. READ IT SLOW.
She stopped breathing for a moment.
He had written her name. Again. Not in the book. On this. On the thing that mattered.
READ IT SLOW.
"What does it say?" said Gideon.
Juno looked up. He was watching her face. Flat gray eyes.
"At the bottom," she said. "It says, it says quick, go quick. And a C." She swallowed. "And then it says Juno. Read it slow."
Gideon looked at her for a moment.
"Then read it slow," he said.
And Juno took a breath, and turned the paper round so that the top of it was at the top, and held it close to the orange light of the brazier, and began.
The first word was easy. It was THE. She knew THE. It was everywhere. The second word was harder. It began with P, and then it had a U, and then two T's, and then an I and an N and a G. She sounded it out under her breath. Puh. Uh. Tuh. Ing. Putting. She said it out loud.
"The putting."
The third word was BACK. She knew BACK. It was on the Back Lane sign by the chandler's.
"The putting back," said Juno.
The crypt was very quiet. The brazier ticked. Big Gideon, against the wall, did not move.
The next line began with FOR. Then ONE. Then WHO. She knew WHO. Then a word with H and A and S. Has. Then LOST. Lost was easy, she had seen it on the dog notices. Then A. Then THING.
"For one who has lost a thing," read Juno.
She went on. NOT. BURNED. Burned took a while. The U in the middle confused her. TAKEN. OR. GIVEN. AWAY.
"Not burned," she read. "Taken. Or given away."
She looked up. Doctor Silas had taken off his crooked spectacles and was holding them in his hand, and his long gray face was very still.
She looked down again and went on.
It was slow. It was so slow. Every word was a wall to climb, and some of the walls were very high. YOU she knew. WILL she knew. NEED was hard, the two E's, until she said it out loud and heard it. A THING OF YOUR OWN. A REAL ONE. A THING YOU WERE THERE FOR. IT MUST BE A WARM ONE.
She read each word out loud as she got it. One at a time. With a gap between. And the gaps got longer as the words got harder. And nobody hurried her. Nobody said anything at all. They sat round the brazier in the warm orange light under the arches, the tall tired doctor and the old woman with the broom and the small flat-faced boy, and listened, the way you listen to rain beginning, one drop at a time.
And Juno read. Slowly. Out loud.
The way he had told her to.
