Chapter 68: A Thing Put Back, Part 4 of 5: Gid
Gid
The gold went in.
Juno saw it go. The thin bright thread of it, drifting on Gideon's breath across the little space, touching the big man's face. His broken nose. His mouth, a little open. And going in. The way warm breath goes into a cold room. The way water goes into dry ground. It did not hesitate. It did not push. It touched him and it went in, and the thread grew thinner, and shorter, and fainter, until there was only a last small glimmer of it, like the last spark of a fire, at the corner of his lips.
And then nothing.
The jar in Gideon's hand was empty. Clear glass. Not even a glow.
And Big Gideon sat against the wall with his son's small hand flat on his huge palm and the candle burning on the floor beside him, and looked at the brazier.
Nothing happened.
Juno waited. They all waited. The candles burned. The brazier ticked. Mrs. Doyle stood with her red hands folded on top of her broom and did not move. Doctor Silas had taken off his spectacles again and was holding them so tight that Juno thought the other arm would bend too.
Nothing.
The big man looked at the fire. His face did not change. His eyes did not move.
It can take a long while, the page said. Sometimes it does not take at all.
Gideon sat beside him. He did not move either. He sat against the wall with his shoulder against his father's arm, and the empty jar in one hand and his father's hand in the other, and looked at his father's face. Up. From below. With his flat gray eyes.
Waiting.
Juno began to count again. She could not help it.
Ten. Twenty. Fifty.
One of the candles on the pillars guttered and went out, and a thin thread of smoke went up from it.
A hundred.
If it does not take, it was not warm enough. Or it was not yours.
It was his, Juno thought, fiercely. It was his. It had his name on it. H.297. GIDEON H. AGE 8. It was the warmest thing the Lady ever took. She said so. It was warm enough. It was warm enough. It was...
Two hundred.
She stopped counting.
Doctor Silas made a small movement. He started to stand up. Mrs. Doyle put out one red hand without looking at him and laid it on his arm, and he sat down again.
And then Big Gideon blinked.
That was all. Just a blink. Slow. Heavy. The way a man blinks when he has been staring at something for a long time and his eyes have dried. The big lids came down over the eyes, and stayed down for a moment, and came up again.
And when they came up, the eyes were not looking at the brazier.
They were moving.
Slowly. Very slowly. Like something heavy being rolled along the ground. Away from the fire. Away from the orange glow. Sideways. Down. Along the floor. Past the candle. Past his own legs stretched out in front of him. Past the small boots beside them.
Up.
To the face beside him. The small pale pointed face, turned up toward him from below, in the candlelight, with its big ears and its short dark hair sticking up at the back.
And stopped there.
And stayed.
Big Gideon looked at his son.
Not through him. Not past him. At him. Juno saw it. They all saw it. The big broken-nosed face had not changed, not really. There was no smile on it. But the eyes had something in them now that had not been there before. Not for a long time. Not since the lift at the Bourse, and the cold room, and the four winters.
They knew what they were looking at.
The big man's mouth moved.
It moved slowly, stiffly, as if it had forgotten how. The lips came together. Came apart. Something worked in the thick throat. And a sound came out. Rough and low and cracked, like a door that has not been opened in years.
"Gid."
Doctor Silas dropped his spectacles.
They fell on the brick floor with a small clatter, and one of the lenses cracked, and he did not even look down. He was staring at the big man against the wall with his mouth open.
Mrs. Doyle made the sign of the Saints. Forehead, heart, left shoulder, right. Her red hand was shaking.
And Juno did not do anything at all. She stood in the middle of the crypt with the candles all round her and the tears running down her face, and could not move.
"Gid," said Big Gideon again.
Slower. Surer. As if he had found the word and was holding on to it.
Gideon did not answer.
He sat against the wall beside his father, looking up at him, with his flat gray eyes, and the empty jar in one hand and his father's hand in the other. And his face did not change. Not at all. Not a flicker.
But his hands were shaking.
Not the small fine tremble from before. Much worse. Hard. Both of them. The hand with the jar and the hand under his father's hand. Shaking so hard that the empty jar rattled against the brick floor, tink, tink, tink, and his father's huge hand on his palm shook with it.
Gideon looked down at them. At his own two shaking hands. As if they belonged to someone else.
"Why are they doing that?" he said.
Flat. Puzzled. Like a boy asking why the kettle was whistling.
"Why are they doing that? I'm not doing it. I don't know why they're doing that."
Nobody answered him.
Juno could not. Nobody could. She stood there and looked at him, the small flat-faced boy against the wall with his father looking at him and his hands shaking and shaking and not knowing why, and she cried. She cried for him. She cried the way he could not. For both of them. For all of it. Out loud, in the middle of the crypt, with her hands over her mouth, and did not care.
And Big Gideon, beside his son, lifted his other hand off his knee.
Slowly. Heavily. As if it weighed as much as a sack of coal. Up. Across. Over.
And laid it on the top of the boy's head.
On the short dark hair that stuck up at the back. The whole of the huge scarred palm, covering Gideon's head like a cap.
And left it there.
One Thing
That was all he had.
They found it out over the next hour, slowly, the way you find out the size of a room in the dark, by feeling along the walls. Doctor Silas, with his cracked spectacles back on his nose and one lens starred like ice, knelt in front of the big man and asked him questions. Quiet ones. Simple ones. What is your name? Where are you? Do you know this place? Do you know me?
Big Gideon did not answer any of them.
He did not seem to hear them. His eyes did not leave his son's face. They stayed there, on the small pale pointed face beside him, the way a lamp stays lit in a window. When Silas spoke, the eyes did not turn. When Mrs. Doyle came and knelt and touched his arm and said, Gideon, love, it's Mrs. Doyle, from the church, you know me, the eyes did not turn.
Only when the boy moved did they move. When Gideon shifted his shoulder against the wall, the eyes followed. When Gideon turned his head, the eyes followed. When Gideon, after a long time, got up, stiffly, on his stiff feet, to fetch his gloves from where he had dropped them, the big head turned, slowly, heavily, and the eyes followed him across the floor, and came back with him.
And the hand.
The huge scarred hand had come down off Gideon's head after a while, and lain on the boy's shoulder instead. And when Gideon got up, it stayed in the air for a moment, where his shoulder had been, open, the fingers a little curled. And when Gideon came back and sat down again, it came down on his shoulder again. As if that was where it lived.
"That's all," said Doctor Silas at last.
He sat back on his heels. He looked very tired. And very strange. As if he had seen something he would need a long time to think about.
"That's all there is," he said. "His name for the boy. And knowing the boy's face. And the hand." He shook his head slowly. "He doesn't know where he is. He doesn't know me. He doesn't know his own name, I think. I don't think he knows Saul is gone. I don't think he knows anything at all, except that." He looked at Gideon. "Except you."
Gideon did not say anything.
He sat against the wall with his father's hand on his shoulder and his own hands in his lap. They had stopped shaking so hard. Now there was only the small fine tremble again, like before. He had looked at them several times, frowning a little, as if they were a puzzle he could not work out.
"One thing," he said.
"One thing," said Silas.
Gideon thought about it. Juno could see him think. His flat face did not change, but there was a small line between his eyebrows.
"It's enough," he said.
And Juno knew that he did not feel it. That he could not feel whether it was enough or not. That he had thought about it, the way he thought about everything, flat and plain, and worked it out, the way you work out a sum, and the answer had come out: enough.
And she knew, too, looking at him, that it was the truest thing she had ever heard anybody say.
Gideon put his hand in his coat pocket. And took out a piece of chalk. Blue. A stub, worn down almost to nothing, the last of a new stick that Mrs. Doyle had bought him at the chandler's after the harvester went north with the old one.
He leaned forward, away from the wall, with his father's hand still on his shoulder. And on the warm brick floor in front of him, between his own boots and his father's boots, where the candle on the floor shone brightest, he began to write.
He wrote slowly. The way the harvester wrote. Every stroke a separate thing. He made the first letter. Then he stopped and looked at it. Then he made the second.
Juno watched. They all watched.
Gideon could not read. He could write five words. He had learned them the way you learn a song, by their shapes, one stroke after another, without knowing what the letters were. LYRA, because she had given him chalk. READ, because she had read to him. V, because it was the first letter he ever knew. DA. SAUL.
He had never written a sixth.
He made three letters. A big round open one. A straight up-and-down one. And a straight one with a round belly on the right side.
G. I. D.
He sat back against the wall and looked at it.
GID.
The chalk was nearly gone. There was only a crumb of it left, between his finger and thumb. He looked at that too. Then he put it in his pocket, carefully, the way you would put away something valuable.
"How did you know how to make it?" said Juno. Her voice was hoarse.
Gideon looked at the word on the floor.
"He said it," he said. "I heard it. And I thought, what does that look like. And I took the G from the front of my name. The doctor showed me once, on the wall, my whole name. GIDEON. And I took the front of it." He looked at the three letters. "It's the front of my name. That's what he calls me. The front."
Big Gideon, beside him, was looking at him. Not at the floor. At him. The way he had looked at him since the gold went in.
Gideon looked back up at his father.
And then, very slowly, as if he were trying something he had read about and did not quite understand, he leaned his head sideways. Until it rested against the big man's arm. Against the rough brown wool of the sleeve, just below the shoulder.
And stayed there.
And the big hand on his shoulder, very slightly, tightened.
Nobody in the crypt said anything for a long time. Mrs. Doyle went round, after a while, and blew out the candles one by one, except the one on the floor by Big Gideon, which she left. Doctor Silas picked up the empty jar from where it had rolled and held it up to the brazier, and looked at it, and put it in his pocket. And Juno sat down on the bottom step of the crypt stair, where she always sat, with the cut-out page from the green book folded in her lap and her hand flat on top of it, and watched the two Gideons sitting against the wall in the light of one candle, the big one and the small one, side by side.
On the floor in front of them, in blue chalk, between their boots, the word said GID.
The sixth word.
The Doctor on the Stair
Doctor Silas came and sat on the step above her.
He did it slowly, folding his long legs up like a heron folding itself onto a nest, with his knees up round his ears and his long red hands hanging between them. The stair was narrow. His knee was by her shoulder. He smelled of carbolic and coal smoke and tea.
For a long time he did not say anything. He sat and looked at the two Gideons against the wall, the way she was looking at them.
"I've been a doctor for thirty-one years," he said at last. Very quietly, so that his voice would not carry across the crypt. "I've set bones and cut out bullets and pulled babies into the world backward. I've sat with more people dying than I could count. I've watched this city burn itself away a little at a time, every winter, into those lamps, and I've done what I could, which was nothing much." He took off his cracked spectacles and looked at the starred lens. "And I have never once, in thirty-one years, seen anybody get anything back."
Juno did not say anything.
"Not once," said Silas. "You burn a thing, it's gone. Somebody takes a thing, it's gone. That's the first thing you learn in this city. The Ash goes one way." He put the spectacles back on. "And today a boy sat against a wall and breathed on his father, and the Ash went the other way."
He was quiet for a moment.
"Do you know what that means?" he said.
Juno thought about it.
"It means Gideon's da knows him," she said.
"Yes," said Silas. "Yes. That's what it means here. Tonight. To them." He rubbed his long gray face. "But think, Juno. Think what it means everywhere else. There are jars. Hundreds of jars. Thousands. In the Ministry vaults. In the Bank. In the cellars under the Bourse, the ones that didn't burn. On the shelves of every Ash merchant on Lantern Quay. Every one of them with a label on it. A name. A number. A child's first winter. A soldier's last morning." His voice had gone very low. "Every one of them was taken out of somebody. And some of those somebodies are still alive."
Juno stared at him.
"If you had the jar," said Silas, "and you had somebody who loved them, who could hold it till it was warm. And say their name." He stopped. "That jar was Gideon's. It was taken out of him. It was still his. He gave it back."
"It says you can't put back what you don't give," said Juno slowly.
"Yes. That's the price. Gideon will never have that day now. Never. The gulls and the humming. It's gone out of the jar and into his father, and it will never come back to him." Silas looked at the boy against the wall. "Every time you put a thing back, somebody has to let it go. It's not a way of getting things for free. Nothing is."
"No," said Juno.
"But it's a way," said Silas. "It's a way. Which is more than anybody in this city has had for a hundred years."
They sat on the step in silence.
At the far end of the crypt, under the last arch, one of the sleeping children turned over and coughed in its sleep. A small dry cough. Silas's head turned toward it at once, the way a mother's does.
"Those six," he said, very low. "I don't know what was taken out of them. I don't know where it went. The Lady's jars are gone, most of them, in the fire. The ones that weren't went in the black carts to wherever the black carts go. There's no label. There's no jar." He was quiet. "There's nothing to put back."
Juno thought of the black cart. Everybody near the river thought of it, on Fridays.
"Somebody should find out where the jars go," she said.
Silas looked at her for a long moment over his starred spectacles.
"Yes," he said. "Somebody should." And then, quickly, the way grown-ups do when they have said too much to a child: "Not you. You hear me? You've done enough this winter for ten grown men. Your feet are still mending. Your mother would have my hide."
Juno did not answer that.
She looked down at the folded page in her lap. At the big leaning letters showing through the back of the paper, faint, in reverse. She had read every word of it. Every wall. She had climbed them all, one at a time, on her own.
And at the bottom, in the smallest letters, he had written her name.
"I'll keep this," she said. "The page. If Gideon doesn't mind. I'll keep it safe. With the book."
"I think," said Doctor Silas, "that is exactly what the harvester would want." He unfolded himself from the step, slowly, joint by joint, and stood up, bending his head under the low arch. "Now. I'm going to make tea. Strong tea. With too much sugar in it. And then I'm going to sit down for a very long time and not think about anything at all."
He went off toward the kettle. And Juno sat on the bottom step with the page in her lap, and thought about jars, and about the black cart that came on Fridays, and about somebody finding out.
The Bottom Step
Later, when the tea had been drunk, and the six sleeping children had woken and been fed bread soaked in milk, and Mrs. Doyle had gone up to see to the church, Gideon came and sat beside her on the bottom step.
He did not ask. He just came. He got up from beside his father, and the big hand came off his shoulder and stayed in the air where it had been, and the big head turned to follow him across the floor. And Gideon sat down on the step beside Juno, close, the way he always did, with his knees up. From there, his father could still see him. Juno noticed that. He had sat where his father could see him.
He had a boat in his hand. One of the little wooden ones from the row along the brazier. A flat one, with a square front and a square back, very neat, the best one yet.
He held it out to her.
"For you," he said.
Juno looked at it. "Why?"
Gideon thought about it, the way he thought about everything.
"Because you read it," he said. "All of it. Slow. I couldn't."
She took the boat. It was smooth and light and still a little warm from his hand. On the bottom of it, cut very small with the point of his knife, there was a letter. V. The first letter he ever knew.
"It's a barge," said Gideon. "Like Hessa's. Before it burned. Flat at the front, so it can push."
"Thank you," said Juno.
Gideon nodded. He did not say you're welcome. He never did.
They sat on the step. Across the crypt, Big Gideon was looking at them. At his son. With his huge hands on his knees, palms down. And Juno thought that if you came down the stair now for the first time and saw him there, you would think he was only a big tired man resting against a wall. You would not know that this morning he had been nobody.
"Juno," said Gideon.
"What?"
"Teach me."
She turned her head.
He was not looking at her. He was looking at the page, folded in her lap. At the big crooked letters showing through the back of the paper.
"The letters," he said. "All of them. Like Lyra taught you. A to Z." He was quiet for a moment. "I want to read it. Myself. Not have it read. I want to look at it and know what it says."
"Why?" said Juno. "You know what it says. You've done it."
"For next time," said Gideon.
She stared at him.
"There'll be a next time," he said, flat, as if he were telling her the coal was low. "Not for him. He's had his. But somebody else. There's always somebody else. And I want to be able to read it to them. If they can't." He looked at the six children at the far end of the crypt, sitting up now on their pallets with their bowls of bread and milk. "Like you read it to me."
Juno looked at him for a long time. At the flat pale pointed face. At the gray eyes with nothing in them. At the hands in his lap that had still not quite stopped shaking.
Then she unfolded the page on her knee, very carefully, and turned it so that it faced him, and put her finger on the first word.
"That's a T," she said. "Like a post with a bar on top. That's an H. Two posts with a bar between. That's an E. A post with three arms. T. H. E. The."
Gideon looked at it.
"The," he said.
"It's everywhere," said Juno. "Once you know it you see it everywhere. On every sign. It's like a dog that follows you."
"The," said Gideon again. He reached out, slowly, and touched the T with one fingertip. Then the H. Then the E. "Post with a bar. Two posts. Three arms."
"That's it."
And across the crypt, against the wall, Big Gideon's head turned a little at the sound of his son's voice. And his big hand on his knee moved, the fingers curling and uncurling, slowly, once, as if it were trying to remember how to reach.
They stayed on the step for an hour. Juno taught him nine letters. He made her go over the R three times, because the leg confused him. When she left, he was sitting by the brazier with a new stub of chalk that Mrs. Doyle had found for him, drawing them on the floor around GID, one after another, very slowly, with his tongue between his teeth.
T. H. E. R. A. I. N. B. W.
His father watching him.
