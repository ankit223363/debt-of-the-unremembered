Chapter 86: Kross, Part 3 of 5: Spent
Spent
The pale one closed his book.
Lyra heard it. A small, soft, flat sound, like a hand laid on a table. It was the first sound he had made all morning. He had stood on the black glass since dawn with the book open in his left hand and the silver pencil in his right, writing, watching, writing, and the pages had turned under his thumb without a sound. Now he shut it. He slid the silver pencil into the loop on the spine. He put the book into the inside pocket of his near-white coat and buttoned the pocket.
Then he took off his hat.
It was a gray hat, with a narrow brim. He held it in his gloved hands for a moment, and turned it round once, the way a man turns a hat round when he is about to go into a house where somebody has died. Then he put it back on, a little further back on his head than before, so that his face was in the light.
Lyra had not seen his face properly before. She had seen it in the distance, at dawn, a pale smudge under the gray hat. She saw it now, close.
It was not an old face, and it was not a young one. It was smooth. Very smooth. Smoother than a face should be, the way a stone in a river is smooth. There were no lines in it at all. Not round the eyes. Not round the mouth. And the eyes were pale. So pale that they hardly had a color. Like water in a white cup.
He was looking past her. Past Kross. At the man on his knee on the glass behind her.
"The harvester, Inquisitor," said Ferrand.
His voice was light. Pleasant. The voice of a man in a shop, asking a clerk to fetch down a box from a high shelf. It was the first time Lyra had heard it, and she had been afraid of it all morning, since the first sight of that pale shape at dawn, that she had thought it would be a terrible voice. Cold and hard and loud. It was not. It was a nice voice. That was worse.
"The harvester," he said again. "Now, I think. While he's down."
Kross did not turn round.
He was standing three paces from Lyra, with his sword point down on the glass and his bare right hand closed into a fist. He did not look at Ferrand. He looked at Lyra, for a moment more, as if he were making sure of something. And then his eyes went past her. Not to Oren. To the place on the glass, a little behind her and to her left, where Corin was.
Lyra did not turn to look. She knew where Corin was. She could feel him there. She had always been able to feel where he was, since the very first night, in the room of drawers, with her sword at his throat. She could feel him now, behind her, on his knees on the black glass, with his gray hand on the glass and his gray face turned toward her, half of it alive and half of it still.
Kross looked at him for a long moment.
"Spent," he said.
He said it flatly. Not loud. The way a man at a market says the price of a sack of flour: a fact, nothing more, take it or leave it.
Ferrand's head went on one side.
"Spent," he said. Pleasantly. As if he were trying the word, to see how it tasted.
"Look at his face," said Kross.
And now he did turn. He turned his head, slowly, and looked at the pale one over his shoulder. And Lyra, standing in front of him with her bent sword wavering in her bleeding hands, saw his face change again. Not much. It went harder. The way water goes harder at the edges of a pond, on the first cold night of the year.
"Look at it," said Kross. "Look at his left side. The hand. The face. You've read the reports. You know what that is."
"I know what it is," said Ferrand.
"Then you know what it means. He's burned to the edge. Past the edge. He has been burning since the chapel. Since before. Every day on this glass he has been carrying the dead, and they have been eating him, and he has nothing left to burn but the last of himself. Look at him. He can barely kneel."
Lyra wanted to turn round. She wanted to see Corin's face, and see if it was true. She did not turn round. She kept her eyes on Kross and her sword up. But she could feel it, behind her. The stillness of him. Corin had not moved since she stood up. Not a hand. Not a breath that she could hear.
"The Archon wants him alive," said Kross. "Those are the words. Alive. You heard them in the white room, the same as I did." He paused. "And he won't live through another fight. Not one. Put a hand on him now, and he'll burn the last thing he has to throw you off, and then there will be nothing to bring back. Nothing for the Archon. Nothing for anyone. A gray thing on the glass that doesn't know its own name."
The quiet came back.
The big quiet of the Eye, with no hum under it. Lyra heard her own blood going in her ears. She heard Kross breathing. She heard, behind her and far off, at the dome, a very small sound, which was the child in Marrit's arms, beginning to cry and being hushed.
Ferrand looked at Corin.
He looked at him for a long time. Lyra could not see what he was looking at, because it was behind her. She could only see Ferrand's pale face, and his pale eyes, moving. Slowly. Up and down. The way a man's eyes move when he is reading a page he does not quite believe.
And then the pale eyes came back to Kross.
"How kind of you," said Ferrand, "to worry about the Archon's wishes, Inquisitor."
Kross said nothing.
"I'll make a note of it."
Kross said nothing.
"You've been very careful this morning," said Ferrand. "I've been watching. I watch everything, as you know. It's my work. You were careful on the glass. You were careful with the captain. You let him drive you a long way, before you took it. I wondered, for a time, whether you meant to take it at all." He smiled. It was a small, friendly smile. "And now you're careful with the harvester. And you were careful just now with the girl."
Lyra felt Kross go still.
It was not much. He did not move. But she was three paces from him, and she was watching him, and she felt it, the way you feel a dog go still at your side when it hears something in the hedge.
"The girl has it," said Ferrand. "The stroke. I saw it. You saw it. Not as well as the captain. But she has it. And you stood there with your bare hand, Inquisitor, and you looked at her, and you closed your hand." He shook his head, slowly. "I'll make a note of that, too."
"She's nothing," said Kross. "A girl with a bent sword. The Archon didn't ask for her."
"No," said Ferrand. "He didn't."
He let the word sit there for a moment.
And then he turned. Not toward Kross. Not toward Corin. Away from them all, on his heel, neatly, the way a clerk turns from one desk to go to another. He turned toward the dome.
Toward the open way in at the front of it, black and square, in the great curved wall of fused stone and iron with the rust running down it in long red tears. Where the iron steps went down, and down, into the dark under the glass. Into the bunker. Into Sola's room, with the map table, and the old charts, and the lamps, and the journal lying on the table where Corin had left it.
"Then the book," said Ferrand.
And he began to walk.
He walked calmly. Not quickly. His near-white coat moved round his ankles, and his gray boots went down on the black glass one after the other, neat, with a small click each time. He walked straight toward the dome, and the line of Ashborn in front of it, and the open dark of the way in.
And from under his coat, as he walked, he took out the rod.
Lyra saw it come out. A short silver rod, as long as a man's hand, with a twist in it. Like a screw, or like the shell of a sea snail. It twisted to the left. The wrong way, Lyra thought, like a screw that would only ever come loose. And Ferrand held it loosely in his gloved right hand, at his side, as he walked, the way a man holds a walking stick he does not need.
The Ashborn stood in his way.
Kestrel was in the middle of the line, with her spear held low across her body. Sef on her left. Oda and Brin on her right, Brin leaning on his spear because of his ribs. Marrit at the end, with the child pressed against her. They stood in front of the dome, and Ferrand walked toward them, and none of them moved.
And behind Lyra, Corin said, very quietly, "Kestrel. Let him by."
Lyra heard it. She did not know if Kestrel heard it. It was very quiet.
But Kestrel looked past Ferrand, at Corin, across the glass. For a long moment. Her lined gray face did not change.
And then she stepped aside.
She stepped to the left, one long step, and lifted the point of her spear, and the others stepped aside with her, as if they had been told to by somebody they could not see. Sef and Oda and Brin. And Marrit, holding the child, with her face like stone. They stepped aside and left a gap in the line, and Ferrand walked through it without looking at any of them, the way a man walks through a gate that has been held open for him.
And Lyra understood why Corin had said it. She understood it a breath after Kestrel did.
Because there was somebody standing in the way in, in the open dark at the front of the dome.
Somebody who had not been there a moment before.
Nothing to Take
It was the man.
The silent man. The one who knocked. The one with no name.
Lyra had last seen him that morning, at dawn, before the fight, before any of it. He had been sitting on the gray stones of the shore, far off across the black glass, with his back to the Eye and his face to the south, the way he always sat. Tansy had been beside him. He had not looked round when the iron men came. He had not looked round when Oren walked out onto the glass with his sword. Lyra had looked for him, once, in the middle of the counting, and he had been there still, a small dark shape on the far gray shore, not moving.
She did not know how he had got here.
She did not know when. She had not seen him cross the glass. Nobody had. She was sure of that, afterward, because she asked them all, and none of them had seen him come. Not Kestrel. Not Sef. Not Marrit, who saw everything. He had not been in the way in when the fight began. He had not been there when Oren went down. And now he was.
He was standing in the open way in at the front of the dome. In the black square of it, just inside, where the light from the white sky stopped and the dark began. He stood with his feet apart on the iron sill and his long arms hanging at his sides. His hands were still wrapped in the strips of cloth Tansy had tied round them, after the Erasers in the Ridges, and the cloth was brown with old blood. His face was in the shadow. All Lyra could see of it was the line of the jaw, and the gray stubble on it, and the eyes.
The eyes were looking at Ferrand.
And Ferrand was walking toward him.
He had not stopped. He had walked through the gap in the Ashborn line, and he was still walking, straight toward the way in, with his near-white coat moving round his ankles and the left-turning rod held loose at his side. He was ten paces from the man. Eight. Lyra did not think he had seen him yet. He was looking down, at the iron sill, the way a man looks at a step to make sure of his footing.
Six paces.
And Ferrand looked up.
Lyra saw him see the man. She saw it in his back, first. A small check in his walk, half a step, as if he had trodden on something soft. Then he went on. Not slower. Not faster. As if a man standing in a doorway were nothing at all to a man like him. As if men had stood in his way before, in a great many doorways, and it had never mattered.
And as he went on he lifted the rod.
Not high. Just a little. He lifted it from his side and held it out in front of him, pointing at the man in the way in, the way you might point a finger at somebody across a room to show them where to stand. The left-turning silver rod, with its twist like the shell of a sea snail, held out level in his gloved hand.
Lyra knew what it was for.
She had never seen one used. She had never seen one at all. But she knew what it was for the moment she saw it pointed, the way you know what a knife is for. The rod was a harvester's tool. A thing for taking memories without a harvester. A thing for men like Ferrand, who could not take with their hands, to take with.
And he was pointing it at the man.
Four paces.
The rod began to turn.
Lyra saw it. Slowly. In Ferrand's hand. It turned to the left, the way its twist went, like a screw going into wood. And as it turned, a light came up along it. Not the pale light that had come up off Oren's wrist into Kross's hand. A different light. Thin and gray and sharp, like the light on the edge of a knife. It ran along the twist of the silver from Ferrand's hand to the tip, round and round, and at the tip it reached out.
She saw it reach. A thread of it. Fine as a hair. Gray. Out across the four paces of air between the tip of the rod and the man in the way in. Reaching for his face. For his forehead. For the place behind the eyes, where Corin always said the memories were, the place you reached for first.
It touched him.
And it found nothing.
Lyra did not know how she knew that. She was too far away to see anything but the thin gray thread and the man's shadowed face. But she knew it the moment it happened. Everybody on the glass knew it. You could feel it. It was like the moment when you put your foot down in the dark for the last step of a stair, and there is no last step, and your foot comes down on the floor too soon, and your whole body jolts.
The gray thread went into the man, and there was nothing there for it to take.
Not nothing the way an empty room is nothing. Nothing the way a well is nothing when it has no bottom. The thread went in, and in, and kept going, and did not stop, and did not find a floor. Lyra saw it go thin. Thinner. The gray light on the rod went pale, and paler, as it poured out of the tip and into the dark behind the man's eyes and never came back.
And Ferrand flinched.
It was the only time Lyra ever saw him do it. Before or after. His whole body went back from the rod, as if it had burned him. His hand opened. The rod jerked in his fingers and nearly fell, and he caught it, and pulled it back against his chest, and the thin gray thread snapped off at the tip and was gone.
He had stopped walking.
He was standing two paces from the man, on the iron sill, with the rod held against his coat. He was breathing fast. Lyra could see his shoulders going up and down. For the first time all morning, he looked like a man who did not know what to do next.
And the man looked at him.
He had not moved. Not when the thread touched him. Not when it went in. He stood in the way in with his wrapped hands at his sides and his face in the shadow, and looked at Ferrand, and his face did not change at all. It was the same face he had at the door every month, knocking. The same face he had on the shore, facing south. The same face he had in the Ridges, when he tore the springs out of the iron men with his bare hands and did not seem to notice that his hands were cut to the bone.
No one's face.
Lyra had thought of it like that, sometimes, in the night. No one. It was not his name. They did not know his name. But it was what he was. A man that the rod could find nothing in. A man with nothing left inside to take.
And then he stepped aside.
Not far. One step. To the left, on the iron sill, out of the middle of the way in, into the shadow by the edge of it. He did it slowly, and quietly, and politely, the way a man in a narrow street steps aside to let a cart go by. And he stood there with his back against the fused stone of the wall, and his wrapped hands at his sides, and left the way open.
The black square of it. The iron steps going down into the dark.
Ferrand stood and looked at it.
He looked at the man, against the wall. He looked at the open dark. He looked at the rod in his hand, with its twist of silver gone dull and gray, as if all the light had run out of it. Lyra saw his smooth face work. Saw it try to be the face it had been all morning, calm and pleasant and curious, and not quite manage it.
And then it managed it.
She saw that happen too. Saw the calm come back down over his face like a lid closing on a box. He straightened his back. He slid the dull rod back under his coat. He looked once more at the man by the wall, a long, slow, careful look, the look of a man memorizing something so that he can write it down later.
And he went in.
Past the man. Close. Close enough that his near-white coat brushed the cloth on the man's wrapped hands. The man did not move. Ferrand did not look at him. He stepped through the way in, out of the white light and into the dark, and Lyra heard his boots on the first of the iron steps. Click. And the second. Click. And then the dark had him, and she could not see him anymore, but she could hear him going down. Click. Click. Click. Neat. Even. Down and down, into the bunker under the glass, toward the map table and the old charts and the journal.
And the man stood by the wall, in the shadow of the way in, with his back to the stone. And turned his head. And looked across the black glass, past Ferrand, past the dome, past all of them.
At Oren.
Oren on his knee in the white light, with the sword held across his body like a spade.
The man looked at him for a long moment. His face did not change. It never changed. But Lyra saw his wrapped right hand come up, a little way, from his side. Just a little. And the fingers move inside the cloth. Folding. Smaller. And smaller. The way she had seen him fold a scrap of cloth, once, in the Ridges, by the fire, until it was the size of a fingernail.
As if he were folding something away.
As if he were putting something somewhere safe.
