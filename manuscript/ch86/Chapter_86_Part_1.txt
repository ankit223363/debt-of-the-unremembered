Chapter 86: Kross, Part 1 of 5: Noon
Noon
Their reflections fought upside down beneath them in the black glass, Oren's heavy and Kross's thin, and the ringing of their swords was the only sound in the Eye.
But not at first. At first there was no sound at all.
Lyra knelt on the glass beside Corin with her bleeding hand held against her chest and watched them stand there. Face to face. A few paces apart. In the full white light of noon, with the sun straight overhead, so that neither of them had any shadow at all. Only the reflections. Under the glass, under their feet, another Oren and another Kross, upside down, standing on the underside of the world, face to face, with the drowned roofs of Applegarth far below them.
Four of them. Two above, two below. Not moving.
Kross had not drawn.
He stood with his bare hands at his sides and looked at Oren. Not at Oren's sword. At Oren's face. The way he had looked at her father, Lyra thought, in the white room at the Spire, at the end, when he came in quietly and stood by the bed with his hat in his hands. Steadily. Without anything in his face that she could read.
Then he put his right hand inside his coat, on the left side, and drew his sword.
She had not known he carried one.
It was not like Oren's. Oren's sword was long and heavy, broad at the base and heavy at the point, a soldier's sword, made for cutting through things. Kross's was thin. Plain. Shorter. A narrow straight blade with no pattern on it and a plain steel guard and a grip wound with gray cord. It was the kind of sword a clerk might carry, if a clerk carried a sword. It came out of the sheath under his coat without a sound.
He held it low. Point down. By his right foot.
The same way Oren was holding his.
Lyra saw that and something went cold in her stomach. Oren held his sword low by his left boot, in the lie, the way he had taught her. The Vance lie. And Kross stood with his thin sword low by his right foot, and his weight on his back foot, and his left shoulder a little forward. A mirror. Not the same. Turned round. The way your reflection in a glass is you, turned round.
She did not know where Kross had learned to stand like that.
"Lyra," said Corin beside her. Very low.
She did not look at him. "I know."
"His feet. Look at his feet."
"I know."
She did not know what she knew. She only knew that the two men on the glass were standing the way two men stand who have been taught by the same kind of teacher, and that one of them was Oren, who had been taught by his father with a stick across the knuckles, and the other one was the Inquisitor of the Ministry, who had sat at her father's bedside and folded peppermint papers into small hard squares and taken her name off the Thursday lists and on again.
And behind Kross, a step behind his left shoulder, the cadet.
Wren. Lyra knew her name now. It was in Corin's book, and she had heard Corin say it, in the Fold Hills, reading it to himself. WREN. CADET. SHE LIED FOR ME. SHE GAVE ME THE STONE. She was standing very straight in her white coat with its silver buttons, behind Kross, the way Lyra had seen her stand at the door of the Inquisitor's house and at the foot of the white stair in the Spire. Her face was white as her coat. Her hands were at her sides. And her eyes were moving. From Kross to Oren. From Oren to Kross. Not missing anything.
And off to the left, alone, the pale one.
He had stopped walking. He stood a little way off, in his near-white coat, with his black book open on his hand and his silver pencil lifted. Waiting. With his head a little on one side, and a little polite smile, the way a man stands at the edge of a dance floor waiting for the music to start.
Lyra hated him.
She had not known she could hate anybody so much. She hated him more than she had hated the Lady, more than she had hated the man with the gentle voice whose face she had never seen. She hated his clean coat and his clean gloves and his silver pencil. She hated that he had stood on the slope above Last Thorn while the tents burned, writing. She hated that he was standing there now, writing, waiting to write down whatever happened to Oren.
She made herself look away from him.
At Oren.
He was standing very straight. She knew what it cost him. She had seen him come up out of the gap this morning, sideways, with his head bent, and she had seen the way he moved. His back was bad. It had been bad since Sola's throw by the iron horse, five days ago, and the night in the Ridges had made it worse, and the wall this morning had made it worse than that. He had been on his knees an hour ago. He had leaned on his sword like a stick.
He was not leaning now.
He stood on the black glass over Applegarth with his sword low and his feet set and his shoulders back, and he looked at Kross the way she had seen him look at a man only once before. On Crown Walk, when she was eleven, at the bottom of the front steps, at a drunk lord who had come to the door at midnight shouting for her father. Oren had not said anything to the lord. He had only stood on the bottom step and looked at him. And the lord had gone away.
Kross did not go away.
The silence went on. The sun stood straight overhead. Under their feet, upside down, the reflections stood too.
And then Oren moved.
Lyra had watched him do it a thousand times. In the yard behind the smithy at Haverstock. In the shadow of the iron horse. On the stone floor of the crypt at Saint Bride's, with a broom handle, when he was teaching her the parry and she would not get it right. She knew how he moved. Big and slow until he was not, and then all at once.
He came forward one step, and his sword came up off the glass, and it was not slow.
It came across, high, from the right, a heavy flat stroke at the height of Kross's neck. The kind of stroke that does not try to be clever. The kind that tries to take a man's head off. Lyra heard it come. A hum, like a big bee going past.
And Kross's thin sword was there.
Not meeting it. Kross did not meet it. A thin blade cannot meet a heavy one head on, Oren had told her, it will bend or break. Kross's sword came up and touched Oren's, at an angle, along the flat, and turned it. Just a little. Just enough. The heavy blade slid along the thin one with a long scraping hiss and went past Kross's shoulder, a hand's width off, and Kross was not there anymore. He was a step to the left.
And the sound came.
It went out across the Eye. Steel on steel. A long ringing scrape that turned at the end into a note, high and thin and clear, like a glass bell struck with a spoon. On the Eye, where there was no hum and no wind and nothing at all to stop it, it went out and out across the glass and did not fade. Lyra heard it go. She heard it reach the far shore, she thought. She heard it go on.
The only sound in the Eye.
And under the glass, under their feet, the reflections fought too.
She saw them. She could not stop seeing them. The other Oren, upside down, swinging his heavy sword in a flat stroke at the other Kross's neck, and the other Kross's thin sword coming up to meet it and turning it, and the other Kross stepping aside. The same. Exactly the same. Two fights. One on the glass and one under it, with the drowned village far below them both, as if the dead of Applegarth were watching from their square with their faces turned up, as if it were a fair, and this was the show.
Oren came again.
Another stroke. From the left this time, low, at Kross's knee. Kross's blade went down to meet it, turned it, the long hiss of steel, the ring. Kross stepped back.
Again. High. A downward cut at the shoulder. Kross turned it. The ring. A step back.
Again.
Lyra began to count.
She did not mean to. She never did. The numbers came, one with each ring of the steel, the way they had come on the stairs at Crown Walk, every day, since she was nine. One. Two. Three. Four. Each one a stroke of Oren's sword. Each one a ring, going out across the silent Eye.
And beside her, very low, so low she almost did not hear him, Corin was counting too.
With her. On his fingers. On the glass. One gray finger for each ring of the steel, lifting and coming down again on the black.
Five. Six. Seven.
Eight.
Nine.
He stopped at nine. She heard him stop. She felt his hand go still on the glass beside hers.
She went on counting for both of them.
Better
Oren was better.
Lyra saw it within a dozen strokes. She did not want to see it, at first. She did not want to hope. Hoping, on a morning like this, was like putting your weight on a step you have not tested. But by the tenth ring of the steel she could not help it, and by the twelfth she was sure.
Oren was better.
Not faster. Kross was faster. Kross's thin blade went where it was going before Oren's heavy one had finished getting there, every time. And not cleverer, either. Kross was clever. Every stroke Oren made, Kross turned. He did not meet them. He never met them. He touched them along the flat and turned them, a little, just enough, and stepped aside, and the heavy blade went past him and rang. Over and over. It was beautiful to watch, the way Kross did it. Like watching somebody pour water from one cup into another without spilling a drop.
But Oren was better.
Lyra did not know how to say how. She thought about it afterward, for a long time, and the nearest she could come to it was this: Oren knew what a fight was for, and Kross did not.
Kross was turning strokes. That was all he was doing. Each stroke as it came, one at a time, turned and let past. As if every stroke were a separate thing, a problem on its own, to be answered and put away. And he answered every one. Perfectly.
Oren was not making separate strokes. Oren was making one long thing, the way a man digging a ditch is making one long thing, even though every swing of the spade is separate. Each stroke led into the next. Each one moved Kross a little. A step to the left. A step back. A step to the left again. And each step put Kross a little further from where he wanted to be, and a little closer to where Oren wanted him.
Back.
Kross was going back.
Lyra saw it. A step with every stroke. Not much. Half a pace. A pace. But back. Away from the dome. Away from the line of Ashborn spears. Toward the broken iron men lying on the glass, and the one still kneeling with its arm lifted. Oren was driving him back across the black glass the way a farmer drives a bull out of a field, not by fighting it, but by always being in the place it wants to go.
Fifteen. Sixteen.
Kross was tired.
She saw that too. She had not seen it at first, because his face did not change. It never changed. But his breathing changed. She could hear it now, between the rings. Short. Quick. Through his nose and then, at the eighteenth stroke, through his mouth. And his feet. They had been neat, at the start. Exact. Every step placed. Now, at the twentieth stroke, she saw his heel come down on the glass a little wrong, a little too far back, and slide.
He caught it. He was quick. But he had slid.
He was a man who had walked across the Glass by night for a week, Lyra thought. Who had slept by day, on the hot glass, under canvas, if he slept at all. Who had stood on this glass all morning without moving, watching. Who had not had water since yesterday, if what Oren said about the casks on their backs was true.
And he was not a soldier. That was the other thing. He held his sword well, and he moved well, and he had been taught by somebody who knew how. But he had not been a soldier. He had not done it every day, for twenty years, in the rain and the mud, until it was in his bones.
Oren had.
Twenty-two.
The heavy blade came down, and Kross turned it, and stepped back, and his heel slid again, and this time he did not catch it so well, and for a moment his thin blade was out of line.
Oren's sword went through the gap.
Not far. Not into him. Kross twisted away. But the point of Oren's sword caught Kross's left sleeve as it went past, and Lyra heard the cloth tear. A long dry rip. And when Kross stepped back, his left sleeve was cut open from the elbow to the wrist, and the gray cloth hung down in a flap, and under it his forearm was bare, and there was a thin red line across it.
Blood.
Not much. A scratch. But blood.
And behind Lyra, at the dome, the Ashborn breathed.
She heard it. All of them at once. A long breath out, the way a crowd breathes out at a fair when the man on the rope has slipped and caught himself. Kestrel and Sef and Marrit and Oda and Brin. And the child, in Marrit's arms, who had been silent since the iron man fell, made a small sound.
They had not been breathing. None of them. Lyra had not noticed. They had been standing in their line in front of the dome with their spears held low and their gray-lined faces set, not breathing, the way you do not breathe when you are watching somebody you love walk along the edge of a roof.
And now they breathed.
Kross looked down at his arm.
He looked at the cut sleeve, hanging, and the thin red line across his bare forearm, for a moment. His face did not change. Then he looked up at Oren.
And something happened at the corner of his mouth. Lyra saw it. She did not know what it was. It was not a smile. It was not a frown. It was the thing that happens to a man's mouth when he has been told something he already knew, and is a little sad to hear it said out loud.
Oren did not wait.
He came on. Twenty-three. Twenty-four. Heavier now. Faster. The long thing he was making with his sword going on and on, the way the ditch goes on, and Kross going back in front of it. Step. Step. Past the first broken iron man, lying on its face. Kross stepped over its outflung arm without looking down. Past the second, kneeling, with its arm lifted. Kross went round it, close, and Oren's blade came round the other side of it and rang off the iron of its shoulder in a shower of sparks, and the kneeling iron man rocked, and did not fall.
Lyra had her bleeding hand pressed against her chest so hard that it hurt. She did not feel it.
Thirty.
And beside her, Corin had stopped counting on his fingers. She did not know when. His gray hand was lying on the black glass beside hers, open, still. He was watching Oren with his whole face. The right side of it, alive, his mouth a little open, his eyes wide. And the left side, gray, still, smooth, like the face of a statue that has been half-carved and left.
"He's winning," said Corin. Very low. As if he did not dare say it any louder.
"Yes," said Lyra.
"Is he?"
"Yes."
She said it again in her head. Yes. Yes. He's winning. She let herself think it. She let herself put her weight on it, the untested step. She watched Oren drive the Inquisitor back across the black glass in the white noon light, with the reflections fighting under them upside down, and the heavy sword ringing, and she thought: he is going to win. He is going to win, and Kross is going to fall down on the glass, and the pale one will have to write it in his little book, and then we will go home.
Thirty-four. Thirty-five.
Kross was a long way from the dome now. Further than Lyra had thought. Oren had driven him back past all the broken iron and out onto the open glass, close to where the pale one stood with his book. The pale one had not moved. He was writing. Calmly. His silver pencil going. Watching them come toward him with his head on one side, as if they were two men having a slightly interesting argument in a street.
And behind the pale one, the cadet. She had come after Kross as he went back, exactly one step behind his left shoulder, all the way across the glass. Her hands were at her sides. Her face was white. She was watching Kross's back.
And Lyra saw her face, and something in it made her stop counting.
Wren was not watching Kross the way you watch somebody who is losing.
She was watching him the way you watch somebody who is about to do something.
Thirty-six.
Oren set his feet.
Lyra knew it. She knew the way his weight went down into his heels. She knew the way his sword went down. Low. Behind him. By his left heel. The point almost touching the glass. His left hip turned toward Kross. His weight on his back foot. As if he were tired. As if he were giving up.
The lie.
He was going to do it. The cut. The family stroke. The Vance cut. The one his father had beaten into him with a stick across the knuckles for twelve years, until he got it right at nineteen, and then ten thousand times more. The one he had used six times in the war. The one he had given her, in the shadow of the iron horse, so that if he lost it, somebody would still have it. A finishing stroke.
He was going to finish it.
And Lyra, kneeling on the black glass with her bleeding hand against her heart, stopped breathing, the way the Ashborn had stopped breathing. And watched.
