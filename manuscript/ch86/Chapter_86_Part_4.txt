Chapter 86: Kross, Part 4 of 5: Down
Down
Kross moved.
Lyra had almost forgotten him. He had been standing three paces from her all this time, with his sword point down on the glass and his bare right hand shut. She had kept her bent sword up at him the whole time Ferrand was walking, without knowing it, the point wavering in her bleeding hands. She had watched Ferrand. She had watched the man. She had not watched Kross.
Now he moved, and she remembered him, and her sword came up a little higher.
He did not look at it.
He bent, and laid his own thin sword down on the black glass at his feet. Carefully. The way you lay down a thing you mean to come back for. Then he straightened, and took a gray glove out of his coat pocket, and put it on his right hand.
He did it slowly. Lyra watched him. He drew the thin gray leather on over his long fingers one at a time, and smoothed it down over the back of his hand, over the thin white scar, and buttoned it at the wrist. Then he looked at his gloved hand, front and back, the way a man checks his hands after washing them.
And then he picked up the sword again, and turned his back on her, and walked toward the dome.
He walked quickly. Not running. But quickly, the way a man walks who has remembered he left a pot on the fire. Past the kneeling iron man, with its arm still lifted. Past the broken one, face down. Toward the gap in the Ashborn line, which had not closed, and the open way in, where the silent man still stood against the wall in the shadow.
And as he went, without turning his head, he said: "Cadet."
Just the one word. Not loud. The voice of a man calling a dog to heel.
Wren had not moved all this time.
She had stood out on the glass where she had stopped, behind Kross's left shoulder, while he fought. When he went back, she went back. When he took the stroke from Oren, she was there. When Lyra came in with the cut, she was there, three paces behind, her hands at her sides. Lyra had seen her out of the side of her eye the whole time, a small dark shape in a gray coat too big for her, and had not looked at her once.
She looked at her now.
Wren was standing very straight. Her chin was up. Her hands were at her sides, and they were shut into fists, and Lyra could see the knuckles, white, from where she stood. Her face was whiter than her knuckles. Her eyes were on Kross's back.
And for a moment she did not move.
Lyra saw it. One breath. Two. Wren standing there on the black glass with her fists shut and her chin up, not moving, while Kross walked away from her toward the dome. As if her feet had grown into the glass. As if she were thinking about not going.
Then she went.
She went after him. Not running. Walking quickly, the way he walked, a step behind. Her short boots went down on the glass one after the other. Past the iron men. Past the gap in the Ashborn line. She did not look at the Ashborn as she went through. She did not look at Kestrel, who was standing with her spear lifted and her lined face hard, watching her go by. She did not look at Lyra.
But as she went past Oren, she looked at Oren.
Lyra saw it. Wren turned her head, just a little, and looked down at the big man on his knee on the glass, with the sword held across him like a spade. And her face did something. Lyra could not see what. It was only for a moment. Then Wren looked away, and her chin came up again, and she went on.
Kross reached the way in.
He stopped on the iron sill, where Ferrand had stopped. The silent man was against the wall, a pace to his left, in the shadow. Kross did not look at him. He looked down, into the dark, at the iron steps going down. Lyra could not see his face. Only his back, in the gray coat, and the left sleeve hanging open in its cut flap, and the thin red line on the bare forearm under it.
And he went down.
He went into the dark, and the dark had him, and Lyra heard his boots on the iron steps. Not the way Ferrand's had sounded. Ferrand's had been neat. Even. Click, click, click. Kross went down fast. Two steps at a time. She heard the steps ring under him, a quick hard clatter, going down.
And Wren went after him.
She stopped on the sill. For a moment. Just a moment, at the edge of the dark. She looked down into it. And then she looked to her left, at the silent man, against the wall.
The man was looking at her.
Lyra saw it. The first time all morning that the man had looked at anybody but Ferrand and Oren. His shadowed face was turned toward Wren, and his eyes were on her. And Wren looked back at him. And neither of them moved, for a breath.
Then Wren went down into the dark.
And the way in was empty again, except for the man.
Lyra lowered her sword.
She had not known it was still up. Her arms dropped, and the bent blade swung down and touched the glass, and the point of it scraped. Her legs were shaking so hard that she had to put the point on the glass and lean on it, like a stick. Her right hand was wet. She looked at it. The blood had run down over the grip and onto the guard and onto the bent blade, a long dark line. She did not feel it.
She turned round.
Corin was behind her. Not where he had been. He had got up. She did not know when. He was on his feet, a few paces off, between her and Oren. He was standing badly, the way a man stands on a deck in a storm, with his feet too wide apart and his body leaning. His left arm hung at his side. His left hand, gray to the wrist, hung open. His face was turned toward the dome.
He came toward her.
He did not say anything. He came across the few paces of glass between them, unsteadily, and he put out his right hand, his living hand, and caught her by the wrist.
Through her sleeve.
She felt his fingers close on the cloth of her cuff. Not on her skin. He had been so careful, all the way across the Eye. Since the dark under the dome. Since the moment he nearly took the last thing she had of her mother. He had not touched her skin once. He caught her wrist through the cloth of her cuff, and held it, so hard that she felt the bones of her wrist press together.
And they stood there.
They stood on the black glass in the white light, Lyra with her bent sword point down and her bleeding hand, Corin holding her wrist through her sleeve, and they looked at the open way in at the front of the dome. At the black square of it. At the silent man against the wall, beside it, in the shadow.
And they listened.
They could not hear anything.
That was what Lyra remembered most, afterward. Not the fight. Not the stroke. Not Oren's face. The listening. Standing on the black glass with Corin's hand round her wrist, and the white sun hanging over them, and the reflections of them hanging upside down underneath, and listening with everything she had, and hearing nothing.
No voices. No boots on the iron steps. No ring of steel. The way in was a black square in the stone and the dark inside it was complete, and no sound came out of it at all.
She counted.
She did not mean to. She had been counting all morning. She could not stop. One. Two. Three. She counted the way you count between the lightning and the thunder, to know how far away the storm is. Ten. Twelve. Twenty. Corin's fingers did not loosen on her wrist. Thirty. Forty.
Behind them, at the dome, the Ashborn had not moved. They stood in their broken line with their spears, Kestrel and Sef and Oda and Brin and Marrit with the child, and they watched the way in, and nobody spoke. The child had stopped crying. Marrit had her hand over its head, holding it against her shoulder.
Fifty. Sixty.
And Oren, behind Lyra, on his knee. She did not turn to look at him. She could not. But she could hear him breathing. Slow. Even. The breathing of a man who is not afraid, because he does not know that there is anything to be afraid of.
Seventy. Eighty.
Ninety.
She stopped counting.
She did not decide to stop. It was simply that the numbers ran out. She got to ninety and could not think what came after it. She stood with her mouth a little open and her wrist in Corin's hand and the number gone out of her head, and the quiet went on, and on, and the dark in the way in did not change.
A long nothing.
And then a sound.
One sound. From down in the dark. From a long way down. It came up the iron steps and out of the way in and across the black glass, small and short and quick, and then it was gone.
Lyra did not know what it was.
She thought about it, afterward, for the rest of her life, and she never knew. It was not a voice. It was not a word. It was not the ring of steel, or the clatter of boots, or the bang of a door. It was short. A breath long. Less. Something between a cough and a gasp. The sound a man might make if he trod on a stair in the dark and the stair was not there.
And then nothing.
Corin's fingers went tighter on her wrist. So tight it hurt. She did not pull away.
And beside the way in, against the wall, in the shadow, the silent man turned his head.
Not toward the dark. Away from it. South. Out across the black glass, past the dome, past all of them, toward the far gray shore and the Glass beyond it, the way he always looked.
As if whatever had happened down there was a thing he had already seen.
Up
They heard him before they saw him.
Boots, on the iron steps. Coming up. Slowly. Not two at a time, the way Kross had gone down. One at a time. A long pause between each one. Ring. And a pause. Ring. And a pause. The sound of a man climbing a long stair who is very tired, or who is carrying something heavy, or who does not want to get to the top.
One pair of boots.
Lyra listened for the second. For Wren's short quick steps. For Ferrand's neat click. She listened so hard that her ears hurt. And there was only one pair of boots, coming up out of the dark, ring, and a pause, ring.
And then, a long way behind, a second pair.
Lighter. Quicker. Stumbling, a little. Catching up, and then stopping, and then coming on again, as if whoever it was kept forgetting how to climb.
Two.
Only two.
Corin's hand was still round her wrist. She felt it go tighter, and then, slowly, let go. He let go of her wrist and stood beside her with his living hand hanging at his side, open. She did not look at him. She was looking at the way in.
Kross came out of the dark.
He came up the last of the iron steps and out onto the sill, into the white light, and stood there. He was alone. Lyra looked behind him, into the dark, and there was nobody behind him, not yet, only the sound of the second pair of boots still coming up, a long way down.
He was wiping something.
That was the first thing she saw, before she saw his face. He had something in his bare left hand, and he was wiping it on the back of his gloved right, slowly, carefully, the way a man wipes a table knife on a cloth after he has eaten. A blade. Not his sword. His sword was in its sheath at his side. This was a short blade. As long as Lyra's hand. Thin and narrow and pointed, like a letter knife, the kind a clerk keeps on his desk for opening the post. He was drawing it across the gloved fingers of his right hand, from the guard to the tip, one way only. And the gray leather of the glove, where the blade went through it, came away dark.
He did it twice. Three times. Then he looked at the blade, front and back, and at his glove, and he slid the short blade into a place inside his coat that Lyra could not see, and it was gone.
And he looked up.
His face was the same. Lyra had thought it would be different. She had thought, standing on the glass listening to the silence, that whatever had made that one short sound in the dark would have done something to his face. It had not. It was the same tired, closed, careful face it had been all morning. Wet. A little gray at the mouth. But the same.
Behind him, Wren came up out of the dark.
She came up the last steps with one hand on the wall, the fused stone, feeling her way, as if she could not see. She stepped out onto the sill beside Kross and stopped there, and stood, and her hand stayed flat on the wall, holding her up.
She was white. Not pale. White. The white of paper, of chalk, of the gray-white of the Ash on the far side of the Glass. Her lips were white. Her eyes were very wide and very dark and they were not looking at anything. They were looking straight out across the black glass at nothing at all, the way the eyes of a person look when they have just seen something and are still seeing it.
She did not look at Kross.
She did not look at any of them.
And Ferrand did not come up.
Lyra waited for him. She could not help it. Even though she knew. Even though she had known since the one short sound. She stood on the glass and looked at the black square of the way in behind Kross and Wren, and waited for the neat boots, and the near-white coat, and the gray hat, and the pale pleasant face.
They did not come.
Kross spoke.
He spoke to all of them. To the Ashborn at the dome, and to Lyra and Corin on the glass, and to Oren on his knee, and to the silent man against the wall, and to the white sky. Not loudly. He did not need to. On the Eye, with no hum, a voice carried as far as you wanted it to.
"Ferrand fell," he said. "On the iron steps. In the dark."
Nobody moved.
"The steps are steep," said Kross. "And they are old. And he did not know them. He was in a hurry." He paused. "He fell a long way."
He looked down at his gloved right hand. At the dark place on the leather. And then he looked up again.
"A great pity," he said.
And he said it, Lyra thought, exactly the way Ferrand would have said it. Pleasantly. Lightly. The voice of a man in a shop.
Nobody said anything.
Not Kestrel. Not Corin. Not Lyra. They stood on the black glass in the white light and looked at him, and he stood on the iron sill and looked back at them, and the silence went on, and on, and was full of the thing that nobody was saying.
And Lyra, standing there, found that she did not know what she felt.
She had hated Ferrand. She had hated him since dawn, since the first sight of the pale shape under the gray hat. She had hated his book and his pencil and his pleasant voice, and the way he had said Then the book, and the way he had looked at Oren on his knee as if he were a thing in a shop window. She had wanted him to fall on the glass. She had wanted Oren to cut him in half.
And now he had fallen, in the dark, on the steps, and she did not feel glad.
She did not feel sorry, either. She felt nothing at all. She felt the way you feel when you have been carrying something heavy up a long hill, and you get to the top, and put it down, and stand there, and your arms do not know yet that they are empty.
Kross came down off the sill.
He came across the glass toward them. Not quickly. Wren came after him, one step behind his left shoulder, the way she always did. But she was walking badly. Stiffly. Like a doll on a string. Her hands were at her sides, and they were open now, not fists, and they were shaking.
As he came, Lyra saw his coat.
The gray coat, with the left sleeve cut open. He had buttoned it. It had not been buttoned when he went down. It was buttoned now, all the way up to his throat. And at the front of it, on the left side, where the inside pocket would be, something made a square shape under the cloth. Something flat. The size of a small book.
And at the top of the square, where the coat did not quite close over it, a corner showed.
A corner of black leather. Worn soft at the tip. With the end of a silver pencil, in a loop on the spine, catching the white light.
Lyra saw it, and then she did not look at it, because she knew without being told that she should not look at it. She looked at Kross's face instead.
And behind him, back in the dark, down the iron steps, in the room under the glass, Lyra knew without being told what was lying on the map table.
She did not have to go down and see. Corin went down, later, that night, with a lamp, and came back up and told her, but she had known before he told her. The journal. Malakor's journal. Lying on the map table where Corin had left it, under the old charts and the lamps, with its black leather cover and its faded red edges and its swollen pages. Untouched. Unopened. Exactly where it had been.
Nobody had taken the book.
Kross stopped in front of them.
He stopped a pace from Corin. Close. Closer than he had ever been. And he stood and looked at him, at his face, at the right side of it, alive, and the left side of it, gray and still, for a long moment.
And Lyra, beside Corin, saw that Kross's hands were shaking too.
Just a little. Just the fingers of the left hand, the bare one. She would not have seen it if she had not been so close. He held the hand down at his side, and closed it, and the shaking went into the fist and stopped.
And then he spoke.
Keep Counting
"Your face," said Kross.
He said it to Corin. Quietly. Not the voice he had used for the Ashborn and the sky. A smaller voice. The voice of one man speaking to another across a narrow table.
Corin did not answer at once.
Lyra watched him. He stood with his feet too wide apart on the glass, leaning, his gray left arm hanging, and he looked back at the Inquisitor. Straight at him. He did not look away. She had seen Corin look away from people his whole life, from their eyes, from their faces, the way a man looks away from a bright window. He did not look away now.
"Yes," said Corin.
Just that.
Kross looked at the gray side of his face a moment longer. At the still cheek, and the smooth gray skin round the eye, and the corner of the mouth that did not move when Corin spoke. Lyra saw him look at it the way a doctor looks at a wound. Carefully. Without turning away. Measuring how deep it went.
"Then you won't hide in Veridia for long," said Kross. "Don't try."
Corin said nothing.
"They'll know you the moment you come through the gate. Half a face like that. There isn't another in the world. Every Enforcer in the city has your description. Every clerk at every desk. Every boy who sells papers on the bridges." Kross paused. "You can't go home and be quiet, harvester. Whatever else you do. You can't go home and sit in a room and wait for it to pass. It won't pass."
He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, lower still:
"Go where you're needed. And be quick."
Lyra did not understand it. She looked at Corin, and Corin's face, the living side of it, did not understand it either. A small line came between his eyebrows. He opened his mouth.
But Kross had already turned away.
He turned to Oren.
Oren was still on his knee, where he had gone down. He had not moved all this time. Not when Lyra did the cut, not when Ferrand went in, not when the one short sound came up out of the dark. He knelt on the black glass with the sword held across him like a spade, and he looked at it, and turned it a little in his hands, this way and that, so that the white light ran up and down the blade.
Kross stood over him.
And then, slowly, the way a man bends whose back is hurting him, he went down. Not to his knees. To his heels. He crouched on the black glass in front of Oren, so that their faces were level, a pace apart.
Oren looked up from the sword.
He looked at Kross the way he had looked at him before. Politely. Puzzled. A stranger in the road.
"I'm sorry, Captain," said Kross.
Oren's frown deepened a little.
"It had to be seen," said Kross.
He said it very quietly. So quietly that Lyra, a few paces off, only just heard it. And she did not know who it was meant for. She did not think it was meant for Oren. Oren could not understand it. Oren did not know what had been taken, or that anything had been taken at all. She thought, afterward, that it was meant for somebody who was not there. Somebody a long way off. Somebody in a white room at the top of a tall white tower, who would read Kross's report, and want to know that it had been done.
It had to be seen.
Kross stood up.
He stood up slowly, and Lyra heard his knees crack. He took the other glove out of his coat pocket, where it had been folded small all this time, and shook it out, and put it on his left hand. Over the long fingers. Up to the bare forearm, where the cut sleeve hung. He buttoned it at the wrist.
Then he looked at his two gloved hands, front and back. Gray leather. Clean on the left. Stained on the right.
And then he looked at Lyra.
She was standing a pace behind Corin, with her bent sword point down on the glass. She had not lifted it again. She did not think she could. Her right hand was still bleeding, slowly, down the grip, and it had made a small dark pool on the black glass by the point of the blade, and the pool had a white sky in it.
Kross came to her.
One step. Two. He stopped in front of her, close. Closer than he had stopped in front of Corin. So close she could see the gray at the roots of his short hair, and the fine lines round his eyes that the white light showed up, and a small cut on his lip she had not seen before. So close she could smell him. Dust and sweat and the dry, stale smell of a man who has been walking in the heat for a week.
She did not step back.
He looked at her for a long moment. Not at her face. At her right hand, on the grip, bleeding. At the bent blade. At the way her feet were set, on the glass, the left a little forward, the weight in the back heel.
And then at her face.
"Keep counting," he said.
So quietly she was not sure he had said it at all.
And then he turned, and walked away.
Lyra stood on the black glass and did not move.
Keep counting.
She knew it. She knew the words. She had known them all her life without knowing where they came from. They were her mother's words. The last words, the message left for her, when she's grown, when she comes asking. Tell her to keep counting. They were the words at the end of her own letter to Ro, the one somebody else had written down for her in pencil, in big leaning capitals, because she asked them to. Keep counting, Ro. She had not known, when she asked for them, why those were the words she wanted at the end.
And now he had said them. The Archon's man. In his gray coat. With his stained glove. On the black glass in the middle of the Eye, after he had taken the stroke out of Oren and let a pale man fall in the dark.
How did he know them?
Who had told him?
She stood there with her bent sword and her bleeding hand and the question going round and round in her like water going round a drain, and she could not find the bottom of it.
And Kross walked away.
He walked across the glass, back the way he had come at dawn. Toward the far shore. Toward the place where his Enforcers had stood, before, beside the iron men, and where three of them were still standing now, Harrow and Pike and the third man, at a long distance, small dark figures against the gray of the shore, with the canvas and the water casks. They had not come out onto the glass again since they carried the bearded man back. Lyra had forgotten them.
Wren went after Kross. One step behind his left shoulder. Her short boots on the glass.
And at the way in, Harrow and Pike came, when he called them. Lyra had not heard him call. But they came, across the glass, and went down the iron steps into the dark with a lantern, and were gone a long time. When they came up again, they were carrying something between them, long, wrapped in a canvas sheet, the kind you stretch over yourself to sleep under in the day. They carried it slowly. It was heavy. One end of it, where the canvas did not quite cover it, showed a gray boot.
Nobody said anything.
The Ashborn stood back from the way in to let them pass. Kestrel stood with her spear held upright, not across her body, the way the Ashborn stood for the dead. Even this one. Lyra saw it and did not understand it, and then she did.
Harrow and Pike carried their dead man across the glass after Kross.
And the little line of them went away over the Eye toward the far gray shore. Kross in front. Wren behind his shoulder. The two Enforcers and their long canvas bundle. They got smaller and smaller on the black glass in the white noon light, with their reflections hanging upside down under them, smaller and smaller, until they were only marks.
And just before they were too small to see, Wren looked back.
Once.
Lyra saw it. A small white face, turned over a gray shoulder, far off, across the glass. Looking back at the dome. At the Ashborn. At Lyra and Corin. At Oren on his knee. For one breath.
Then it turned away, and the marks went on, and the gray shore had them.
The Eye was empty.
Lyra let out her breath.
She had not known she was holding it. It came out of her in a long, shaking rush, and her knees went, and she sat down on the black glass where she was, hard, with her bent sword across her lap. She sat there. The glass was hot under her. She did not care.
Corin sat down beside her.
Not touching. A hand's width away. He sat with his gray hand in his lap and his living hand flat on the glass, and they did not speak.
Behind them, at the dome, she heard the Ashborn begin to move. Kestrel's voice, low, giving orders. Marrit, hushing the child. Brin, groaning as he lowered himself to the ground with his cracked ribs. Somebody laughing. Short. Not happy. The laugh a person laughs when they find they are still alive and do not yet know what to do about it.
And in front of them, Oren.
He had not moved. He was still on his knee on the glass, in the white light, with the sword across him. Turning it in his hands. Slowly. This way and that. Looking at the edge, and the flat, and the guard, and the grip, with its leather worn smooth and dark by twenty years of his own palm.
Looking at it the way you look at a letter from somebody you are sure you once knew.
