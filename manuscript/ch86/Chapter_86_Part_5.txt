Chapter 86: Kross, Part 5 of 5: The Hand
The Hand
Vespera came to her first.
Lyra did not see her come. She was sitting on the hot black glass with her bent sword across her lap and Corin a hand's width away, and she was looking at Oren, and then there was a shadow over her, and a smell of smoke and old ink and the bitter green herbs Vespera kept in a little bag at her belt, and a thin old voice said, "Give it here."
Lyra looked up.
Vespera was standing over her. She was not tall, and she was not young, and the white light of the Eye was hard on her. It showed every line in her face. Lines round her eyes, and round her mouth, and across her forehead, deep as the cracks in dry mud. Her hair was gray and cut short and ragged, as if she had done it herself with the little bird-shaped scissors at her belt. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. Her hands were blue to the wrist, stained deep with old indigo that no washing would ever get out, and her thin arms above them were marked with old ink, the way they always were. Lines and marks and small dark letters, some of them faded almost to nothing and some of them new.
"Where were you?" said Lyra. Her voice came out strange. Thin. As if it belonged to somebody else.
"On the far side of the dome," said Vespera. "In the shade. With my eyes shut." She held out her hand. "I'm too old to watch men fight. I've watched enough of it. I sat and listened, and I counted the rings of the steel, the same as you." She paused. "I lost count, near the end. Give me your hand, girl."
Lyra gave it to her.
She had to let go of the sword to do it. Her fingers did not want to open. They had been shut round the grip so long, and so hard, and the blood had dried between them and the leather, so that when she opened them at last the skin of her palm came away from the grip with a small wet tearing sound. She heard it more than felt it. Then the feeling came, and she shut her eyes.
"Yes," said Vespera. "I know. Hold still."
She knelt. It took her a long time. Her knees cracked, and she put one hand flat on the glass to let herself down, and grunted. Then she took Lyra's right hand in both of hers and turned it over, palm up, and looked at it.
Lyra looked too. She had not meant to. She could not help it.
It was bad. The old cut from the Ridges ran across the palm from the base of the first finger to the heel of the hand, and the strips of cloth she had tied round it that morning were gone, torn away somewhere in the counting, and the cut had opened along its whole length. It was a deep red line, wide in the middle, with the edges pulled apart. Blood was still coming out of it, slowly, the way water comes out of a cracked jar. There was blood all over the palm, and in the creases of the fingers, and dried black under the nails. And across the middle of it, at a slant, there was a new cut. Thinner. Shorter. Where the notch in the grip had dug in when she struck.
"You held a sword with that," said Vespera.
It was not a question. Lyra did not answer it.
"You did the stroke with that," said Vespera. "With a bent blade. On no water and no sleep." She looked up. Her old eyes, pale brown, like weak tea, met Lyra's. "You're a fool."
"Yes," said Lyra.
"Well," said Vespera. "At least you're an honest one."
She took the little bag from her belt and opened it. Inside, wrapped in a scrap of oiled cloth, was a lump of something gray-green and soft, like old cheese, that smelled so bitter that Lyra's eyes ran. Vespera pinched off a piece of it with her fingers and rolled it between them until it was soft and warm, and then she pressed it into the cut.
Lyra made a sound.
"Yes," said Vespera. "That's the worst of it. Breathe."
Lyra breathed. In. Out. The pain went up her arm to the elbow, white and hot, like a wire pulled tight, and stayed there, and then, slowly, began to go dull. Vespera pressed the gray-green stuff all along the cut, from one end to the other, with her thumb, firmly, the way you press the seam of a pie shut. Then the new cut, the short one. Then she took a strip of clean linen from her sleeve, where she must have had it ready, and began to wind it round Lyra's hand.
She wound it tight. Round the palm, and between the thumb and the first finger, and round the wrist, and back. Her old hands were quick and sure. Lyra watched them. They were the same hands that had inked the lines on Corin's chest and ribs, the grounding lines, the ones Lyra read to him when he could not read them himself.
"Can you close it?" said Vespera.
Lyra tried. Her fingers came halfway, and stopped.
"Good enough," said Vespera. "Don't hold anything with it for three days. Not a cup. Not a spoon. Not a sword. Especially not a sword." She tied off the end of the linen with a knot so small and neat that Lyra could hardly see it. "If you hold a sword with it before the three days are up, I'll take it off at the wrist myself, and save you the trouble."
"Yes," said Lyra.
Vespera did not let go of her hand.
She sat back on her heels on the black glass, slowly, with her knees cracking again, and held Lyra's bound hand in both of hers, in her lap, and looked at her. Not at the hand. At her face.
"Now tell me," she said. "Quietly. Just to me. Do you still have it?"
Lyra did not understand.
"The stroke," said Vespera. "The cut. He stood there with his hand open and looked at you, and he could have taken it, and he didn't. I heard Kestrel say so, just now, to Sef. She doesn't say things she isn't sure of." She paused. "But I want to hear it from you. Is it there? Look for it."
Lyra looked.
She shut her eyes, the way she did at night, and looked for it inside herself. The shadow of the iron horse. The sun going down red behind the black ridge. Oren's big hands on her shoulders, turning her. Feet. Hip. Turn. The lie, low behind the left heel. The drive of the back foot. The gate swinging. The long flat curve. The hiss of the air.
It was there.
All of it. Every part, in its place. Not as good as his. Her feet still wanted to be too close together. Her hands still came up too soon. But it was there, whole, the way a song you know is there, waiting, when you open your mouth to sing it.
She opened her eyes.
"Yes," she said.
Vespera let out a breath.
She did not smile. Vespera did not often smile. But something in her old face loosened, a little, the way a knot loosens when you have been pulling at it for a long time and it suddenly gives.
"Then keep it," she said. "Keep it the way you'd keep a coal in a pot on a long walk. Blow on it every morning. Do it in your head, if you can't do it with your hands. Every morning. Every night. Don't let a day go by." She gave Lyra's bound hand a small, hard squeeze, and let it go. "It's the only place it is now. You know that."
"I know," said Lyra.
"The only place," said Vespera, "except in that gray coat."
She got to her feet. It took her even longer than kneeling had. She put one hand on Lyra's shoulder to push herself up, and Lyra felt how light she was, how thin the old fingers were, and how much they shook.
Then Vespera turned to Corin.
She looked down at him for a long moment, the way she always looked at him now, since the Eye. Searching. Reading him, almost, the way she read the old letters on the walls of the bunker. Then she bent, with a grunt, and put her thumb on the corner of his mouth. The left corner. The gray one.
"Say it," she said.
"Corin," said Corin.
"The other one."
"Corin Vane."
The left side of his mouth was slow. Lyra heard it. The V came out soft and a little wrong, because the lip on that side did not quite close. But it came.
"Still there," said Vespera. "Good. Still on the edge." She took her thumb away. "You didn't burn anything. Just now. In all that."
"No," said Corin.
"Not when he went down. Not when she stood up. Not when the pale one walked by you." She looked at him hard. "You wanted to."
"Yes," said Corin.
"But you didn't."
"No."
Vespera nodded, slowly.
"Then that's two of you that did something hard today," she said. "And I'm too old for more of it. I'm going to sit in the shade."
And she went, slowly, across the glass toward the dome, with her ragged gray hair and her blue hands and her old inked arms bare in the white light, and did not look back.
What Stroke
Lyra went to Oren.
She got up off the glass. It was harder than it should have been. Her legs did not want to hold her, and her bound right hand was no use for pushing, so she pushed with her left, and swayed, and Corin put out his living hand and caught her sleeve, and held it until she was steady. Then he let go.
She left the bent sword on the glass.
She walked the few paces to where Oren knelt. The glass was so hot now that she could feel it through the soles of her boots. The sun had moved. It was not straight overhead any longer. It had begun to go down the white sky toward the west, and the shadows of things had come out from under them again: the shadow of the dome, long and curved, and the shadow of the kneeling iron man, with its lifted arm, and Oren's shadow, a dark lump on the glass beside him.
She knelt down in front of him.
Not close. A pace away. The way Kross had crouched. So that their faces were level.
Oren was still looking at the sword. Turning it. He did not look up.
"Oren," said Lyra.
He looked up.
And his face changed. Not the puzzled look. That went. Something else came instead, warm and quick, the way the sun comes out from behind a cloud on a windy day. His eyes crinkled at the corners. His big mouth, in the brown beard, came up on one side.
"Lass," he said.
Lyra's throat shut.
She had not known until then how afraid she was. That he would look at her the way he had looked at Kross. Like a stranger in the road. She had not let herself think it. But it had been there, under everything, the whole time she was walking toward him, and now it was gone, and she found that she could not speak.
"Lass," said Oren again. Gently. "You're bleeding."
She looked down. The linen Vespera had wound round her hand was already showing a red line across the palm.
"It's all right," she said. "Vespera bound it."
"Aye. She binds a thing well, that one." He looked at the hand a moment longer. Then his eyes came back to her face, and the crinkle at the corners went, a little. "Something's happened. Hasn't it?"
Lyra did not answer.
"Something's happened," said Oren slowly. "I can feel it. Like when you wake in the night and the fire's gone out and you don't know yet what's wrong, only that the room's colder." He frowned. "There was a man. In a gray coat. He was talking to me."
"Yes."
"He said he was sorry."
"Yes."
"What for?"
Lyra looked at him.
She looked at his big tired face, with the beard gone gray at the chin, and the old white scar through the left eyebrow, and the broken nose. His eyes, waiting. Puzzled and kind. And she thought: I could tell him. I could tell him now, all of it. What he had. Who gave it to him. How many years. What it was for. And then he would know what he had lost, and he would have to carry that, as well as the losing.
Or she could not tell him.
"Can you stand?" she said.
Oren thought about it.
"Aye," he said. "I think so. I'm stiff. I've been down on this knee a long while, I think." He looked down at it, on the glass, as if he were surprised to find it there. "Why was I down on my knee?"
"Stand up," said Lyra. "I'll help you."
She held out her left hand. He took it. His hand was huge round hers, rough and hot and dry, with the cut across his own palm from the way in at the dome, bound in a dirty strip of cloth. He leaned on her, and she leaned back, and he came up off the glass with a groan, slowly, the way a big man gets up who has been down too long, one knee and then the other, and stood.
He was still holding the sword. In his other hand. By the middle of the grip, like a spade.
He looked at it.
"This is mine," he said.
"Yes."
"I know it's mine. I know the feel of it." He weighed it in his hand, up and down. "I've had it a long while. Years. I can feel that." He turned it, and the white light ran down the blade and caught on the notch near the tip. "But I'll be cursed if I know what I've been doing with it all these years. It's a heavy thing to carry about for nothing."
Lyra said nothing.
She stood back from him. Two paces. Three. On the black glass, in the long afternoon light, with the dome behind her and the Ashborn moving about in its shadow and Corin sitting on the glass a little way off, watching.
And she set her feet.
She did not have a sword. She did not need one. She set her feet apart on the glass, the left one forward and the right one back. She let her weight go down into her back heel. She turned her left hip toward him. And she brought her empty hands down, low, behind her, by her left heel, the left one under the right, as if she were holding a grip that was not there.
The lie.
Oren watched her.
And then, slowly, she did it. Not fast. Slowly. The way he had done it for her in the shadow of the iron horse. Here. And here. Now the hip. Now the hands. She drove the back foot. She turned. Her body went round like a gate from the ground up. Her empty hands came up off the heel and round and up, in the long flat curve, and stopped at the top of it, high, where the stroke ends.
And she held it there.
And looked at him.
Oren's face did not change for a long moment.
Then he smiled. The same warm smile. Kind. Interested. The smile of a man watching a child at a fair do a trick with three cups and a pea.
"That's a fine shape, that," he said. "Like a scythe going through long grass. You see the old men do it in the hay fields in summer. All of them in a line. Round and up, round and up, and the grass going down in front of them." He nodded. "It's a good shape. Somebody taught you that well."
Lyra's hands came down.
"Who showed you that, lass?" said Oren.
She looked at him.
"You did," she said.
Oren laughed.
Not unkindly. A short, surprised laugh, deep in his chest, the laugh of a man who has been told he once danced at a wedding when he knows quite well that he has never danced in his life.
"Me?" he said. "Saints, no. I'm no hand at a thing like that. I'm a—"
And he stopped.
Lyra watched him stop. She watched his mouth stay open on the next word, and the word not come. Watched the puzzled frown come back between his eyebrows. Watched him look down at the sword in his hand, held like a spade, and then up at her, and then down at the sword again.
"I was a soldier," he said slowly. "I know that. I was in the war. I remember the war. The mud. The rain. The tents. The bad bread. The men I was with." He named them, under his breath, as if he were counting them. Lyra did not know the names. "I remember all of that. I was a captain. They called me Captain." He looked at her. "But I can't think, lass. I can't think what I did with my hands all day."
Lyra did not say anything.
She could not.
She stood on the black glass in the long light and looked at him, and he looked back at her, a big puzzled man holding a sword like a spade, and behind her she heard Corin get up off the glass, slowly, with his crutch, and come toward them.
Oren looked past her at Corin.
"Lad," he said.
"Oren," said Corin.
"Your face, lad." Oren frowned. "What's happened to your face?"
"I burned something," said Corin. "This morning. Before."
"Aye," said Oren. "Aye. I remember that. You burned the old woman. The aunt. And I held the—" He stopped again. His mouth worked. "The way in. I held the way in, with my back. Didn't I?"
"Yes," said Corin.
"That's right." Oren nodded, slowly, as if he were pleased to have found that, at least, where he had left it. "That's right. I held the way in." He looked down at the sword. "And then something else happened. Something with this."
Nobody answered him.
And Oren, after a while, nodded again, the way a man nods when he has asked a question and seen from your face that it is not a question you can answer, and has decided to let it go for now.
"Well," he said. "Well. It'll come back to me, I daresay. Things do."
He looked round. At the dome. At the Ashborn in its shadow. At the broken iron men on the glass, and the long shadows, and the white sky going yellow at the western edge.
"It's hot," he said. "I'm dry as a bone. Is there water, lass?"
"Below," said Lyra. "In the cistern. I'll bring you some."
"Ah." He nodded. "Later, lass. I'll sit a while first."
And he walked away from them, toward the dome, carrying the sword across his body in both hands, the way you would carry a spade back from a field at the end of a long day. Not hurrying. Looking about him at everything, the glass and the sky and the dome, with that same calm, puzzled, interested face, like a man in a town he has never seen.
Lyra watched him go. All the way to the shade of the dome, where he sat down with his back to the warm stone, and laid the sword across his knees, and turned it in his big hands, slowly, this way and that, with the light running up and down the blade. Looking at it as if it were written in a language he couldn't read.
And beside her, Corin stood on the glass with his crutch and his gray hand and his half-still face, and did not touch her, and did not speak, and stayed.
They stood like that for a long time.
The sun went on down the white sky. The shadows went on growing out from under things. Somewhere behind the dome, Sef was singing under his breath, a low song with no words, the kind the Ashborn sang when they were working with their hands. Brin had stopped groaning. The child was asleep; Lyra could see it from here, a small heap against Marrit's shoulder in the shade, with one fat arm hanging down.
"He said it to me," said Lyra at last.
Corin turned his head.
"Kross," said Lyra. "At the end. When he came up close. He said, Keep counting."
Corin did not say anything for a moment. The living side of his face was very still. Then he said, slowly, "I heard."
"They're my mother's words."
"I know."
"How does he know them?"
Corin looked away, out across the glass, toward the far gray shore where the little line of marks had gone. The left side of his face caught the long light and did not move.
"I don't know," he said. "I don't know, Lyra." And then, after a while, lower: "But he didn't say it the way Ferrand said things. He said it the way you'd pass somebody a message. Quick. So nobody else would hear."
Lyra thought about that.
She thought about the gray coat, and the stained glove, and the small cut on his lip. About the bare hand, open, and then closed. Good, he had said, when he saw she had the stroke. Good. About the one short sound in the dark. About It had to be seen. About Wren, looking back once over her shoulder before the gray shore had her.
She could not make it come out even. It was like a sum that has a number missing from the middle of it. However many times she went through it, the bottom line would not add up.
So she did the other thing. The thing her mother had told her to do.
She counted.
Not the strokes this time. Not the seconds in the dark. She turned round on the black glass, slowly, and counted the people.
Corin. One. Beside her, with his crutch.
Oren. Two. In the shade of the dome, with the sword across his knees.
Vespera. Three. Sitting near him, with her eyes shut and her old inked arms folded.
Kestrel. Four. Standing at the way in, with her spear upright, looking down into the dark where Ferrand had gone.
Sef. Five. Somewhere behind the dome, singing.
Oda. Six. Sitting on the glass with her spear across her knees, rubbing the shoulder Oren had put back for her.
Brin. Seven. Lying flat on his back in the shade with his hands on his cracked ribs and his eyes on the sky.
Marrit. Eight. And the child. Nine.
And the man. Ten. Lyra looked for him, and he was not by the way in anymore. He was walking. Away across the glass, south, alone, toward the far shore where he had sat at dawn. Back to Tansy, she thought. Eleven. Tansy, on the shore, waiting for him with her eyes shut, singing to nobody.
Eleven.
She counted them again, to be sure. Eleven. All of them.
Not Arran. Arran was under his cairn at the start of the short way. Not Hask. Not Sola, who had gone into Corin and out of the world. Not the bearded man whose name she did not know. Lyra counted them too, in her head, the ones who were not there. She did not know any other way to do it.
But the eleven were there. Today. Now. On the black glass at the end of a long day, alive.
Count them. Every day. Keep them safe.
"Eleven," she said.
Corin looked at her.
And then, slowly, he lifted his living hand, and began to count them too, on his fingers, the way he had counted the strokes with her at noon. One. Two. Three. All the way to nine.
And stopped, because he had run out of fingers on that hand, and the other one was gray.
Lyra put out her bound hand, palm up, beside his. Not touching. A hand's width away. And held up two of her fingers, over the linen.
Ten. Eleven.
They stood there on the glass with their hands held out side by side in the long yellow light, not touching, and between them, they had enough.
