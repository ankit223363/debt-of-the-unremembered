Chapter 86: Kross, Part 2 of 5: The Taking
The Taking
Oren went in.
Lyra had seen him do it once before, in the shadow of the iron horse, slowly, so that she could learn it. Step by step. Here. And here. Now the hip. Now the hands. She had done it herself a hundred times since, in her head, at night, lying on the glass with her eyes shut, and once with a real blade in the Ridges, and once that very morning on the stair of the dome, when it had killed a man whose name she did not know.
She had never seen him do it fast.
It was like nothing. That was the first thing she thought. It was like watching a door that had been shut all your life suddenly not be there. The heavy blade was low behind him, by his heel, and then it was not. It was coming. It came up from the left hip, all of him behind it, his back foot driving into the glass, his body turning like a gate swinging on its post, and the sword came round and up with the whole weight of the turn in it, in a long flat curve, toward the place under Kross's right arm where a man cannot easily guard.
And the sound. There was a sound. Not the ring of steel. A low sound. The sound of the air being cut, a long hiss, like a sheet being torn very slowly.
It was perfect.
Lyra knew it was perfect. She did not know how she knew. She knew the way you know a note in a song is right before your mind has had time to think about it. Every part of it was in the place it had to be. The feet. The hip. The turn. The hands, low and then high. The edge of the blade, tilted exactly, so that it would go in and not glance. Twelve years of a stick across the knuckles. Ten thousand times more. Six times in the war. All of it, in one long breath, coming round.
It was going to kill him.
She saw that, too. Just before it happened. There was no parry for it. There was nowhere for Kross to go. He was on the wrong foot, with his heel slid back, with his thin blade out to the side where the last stroke had left it. The heavy blade was coming in under his arm and there was nothing in the world that could stop it.
And Kross let his sword go wide.
Lyra saw it and did not understand it. Kross did not try to bring his blade back. He did not try to meet Oren's stroke. He did the opposite. He let his own thin sword swing out and away, out to his left side, far out, his arm straight, as if he had simply stopped caring about it. As if it were a thing he was holding for somebody else and had grown tired of.
And he stepped in.
Into the stroke. Not away from it. Toward Oren. Close. So close that the heavy blade, coming round in its long curve, was suddenly coming round behind him, and not at him, because he was inside the curve, inside the reach of it, chest to chest with Oren, almost. The way a man stepping in close to a swinging door is safe from the edge of it.
And he put out his right hand.
His bare right hand. The one with no glove. Lyra had seen it on the glass that morning, pale, with long fingers and a thin white scar across the back of it. He put it out, quite gently, the way you put out a hand to steady a man who has tripped on a step, and he laid it on Oren's wrist.
Just laid it there. On the inside of the wrist. Where the skin is thin and you can see the blue lines under it.
And the stroke stopped.
It did not slow. It stopped. As if it had come to the end of a rope. The heavy blade, which had been coming round with the whole weight of Oren behind it, simply was not coming round anymore. It hung in the air behind Kross's back, the point of it a hand's width from the gray cloth of his coat, and stayed there.
And there was a light.
Lyra saw it. She had seen it before. She had seen it at the ruined chapel, the first time, and in the cellar under the Wicks, and in a hundred other places since. She had seen it every time Corin put his hand on somebody and took. A small light. Not bright. A pale light, like the light that comes up off a pond on a cold morning, where Kross's bare fingers touched Oren's wrist. It came up out of Oren's skin like breath on a window. Pale and thin and fine. And it went into Kross's hand.
Into it. Up through the long fingers and into the palm and away up the bare forearm, where the thin red line of the cut was, and it was gone.
It took no time. No time at all. A breath. Less.
And then Kross took his hand away, and stepped back.
And Oren stood there with his sword in the air.
Lyra saw his face. She was close enough. She saw it change.
It did not change much. That was the terrible thing. If his face had changed much, if he had cried out, or gone white, or fallen, she would have understood it. She had seen people lose things before. She had seen what it did to them. The people in Veridia that Corin had been to, who sat very still afterward with their hands in their laps. Her own face, in the mirror in the boarding house, the morning after the Wicks.
But Oren's face did not do any of that. It just went... puzzled.
That was all. A small frown came between his eyebrows. The look of a man who has walked into a room to get something and cannot, now that he is standing there, remember what it was. He looked at his own hands, up in the air, holding the sword. He looked at the sword.
And Lyra watched the sword turn in his hands.
Not fall. Turn. His grip changed on it. She saw his fingers move. They had been holding it the way a soldier holds a sword, the way Oren had held it every day for twenty years, the way he had taught her: the right hand high under the guard, the left hand low on the pommel, the thumbs just so. And now they were not. Now his hands slid apart on the grip. The right hand went down. The left hand came up. And he was holding the sword the way you hold a spade. Or a hoe. Or the handle of a rake, in a field. Both hands together, near the middle, the blade out in front of him like a thing you might dig with.
Like a tool.
As if he had found it lying on the ground, and picked it up, and did not quite know what it was for.
He lowered it. Slowly. He looked at it, with that small puzzled frown.
And then his knees went.
Not all at once. The right one first. It just folded under him, the way a knee folds when you have been standing too long in one place and somebody comes up behind you and taps it. He went down on it. On the black glass. Hard. Lyra heard the knee hit. And he stayed there, down on one knee, with the sword held like a spade across his body, and his head a little bent, and that frown.
He did not try to get up.
And the black glass under him showed another Oren, down on one knee, upside down, hanging from the knee like a man hanging from a branch, with the sword across him. And above them both, the white sun.
Nobody made a sound.
Not the Ashborn, at the dome. Not Corin, beside her. Not the cadet. Not the pale one with his book. Lyra heard nothing at all. No breath. No wind. The Eye was quiet the way the Eye was always quiet, with no hum under it, and the quiet was so big and so complete that she could hear the blood in her own ears.
And then, from somewhere a long way off, she heard a voice saying, "No."
She did not know it was hers until she heard it again.
"No. No. Oren. No."
Kross stood back from him.
He had stepped back three paces. His own thin sword hung loose in his left hand. He had not used it. He was breathing hard, through his mouth, and his face was wet. He stood with his feet apart on the glass and looked down at the man on his knee in front of him, and his bare right hand hung at his side with the fingers a little open, the way a hand hangs when it has just put down something heavy.
And his face.
Lyra saw his face, and for a moment she did not hate him, because there was nothing in it to hate. There was nothing in it at all. It was the face of the man in the white room at the top of the Spire, and the man on the glass at dawn, and the man who had given away his coins to a widow and hummed in the dark. A tired man's face. Closed. Still. But under the stillness, just for a breath, there was something that Lyra had seen before. She had seen it in Corin's face, every time, afterward.
She had seen it in Corin's face in Veridia, at the end of a job, when the person he had taken from looked up at him and did not know him.
Then it was gone. And Kross straightened, very slowly, the way a man moves whose back is hurting him, and stood with his bare right hand hanging open at his side.
And he spoke.
Not to Oren. Not loudly. He did not look up. He said it to the glass, almost.
"It's a beautiful stroke," said Silas Kross. "Your father would have been proud of it."
Oren looked up.
He looked up at Kross with that small frown, as if a stranger had stopped him in the road and asked him the way to a town he had never heard of.
"What stroke?" he said.
And Lyra, on the black glass, with her bleeding hand against her chest, made a sound she had never made before in her life. It was not a word. It came from somewhere a long way down, under her ribs, under everything. It was the sound a dog makes when you step on it in the dark.
Corin's gray hand closed on her arm.
Through her sleeve. Not on her skin. Through the cloth, the way it had to be in the Eye, where he could take and burn again, and where a touch on bare skin could cost her the last thing she had of her mother. His fingers closed on her arm through the cloth so tight that it hurt, and held, and did not let go.
She did not look at him. She could not look away from Oren.
Oren, on his knee, looking at the sword in his hands. Turning it over. Looking at the edge, and the flat, and the guard, and the grip, with its leather worn smooth by twenty years of his own palm. Looking at it the way Lyra had once seen a very old woman in the Wicks look at a picture of her own wedding. Politely. Kindly. Trying to be interested. Not knowing who anyone in it was.
He had lost it.
Not the sword. The sword was in his hands. He had lost the thing that made it a sword. The twelve years. The stick across the knuckles. The ten thousand times. The six times in the war. The lie and the hip and the turn and the long flat curve. All of it. One touch. One breath. Gone up through a pale bare hand like water up a wick.
If I lose it, somebody will still have it, Oren had said to her, in the shadow of the iron horse.
She had thought he meant if he died.
She had not thought he meant this.
The Cut, Again
Lyra stood up.
She did not decide to. She did not think about it at all. Afterward, when she tried to remember the moment, there was nothing there to remember: no thought, no choice, no voice in her head telling her what to do. One moment she was kneeling on the black glass with Corin's hand closed on her arm through her sleeve. The next she was on her feet, and his hand was gone, and she was walking.
She heard Corin say her name. Once. Behind her. She did not stop.
Her sword was on the glass where she had laid it down when Oren went out to fight. She had not meant to lay it down. She had meant to hold it, all the way through, so that she would be ready. But her right hand was cut across the palm from the Ridges, and torn open again from the fall on the iron steps that morning, and somewhere in the counting she had put the sword down so that she could press her hand to her chest and stop the blood. Now she bent and picked it up.
It was a poor thing. It had never been a fine sword, even on the day she first held it. And it was worse now. The edge was notched in three places from the Ridges, where it had met the iron of an Eraser's arm. The blade had a bend in it, a little way above the guard, so that if you sighted along it you could see it was not quite true. It was too light. It had always been too light.
She held it in her right hand, and her right hand screamed.
She felt the cut across her palm open wider as her fingers closed on the grip. She felt it the way you feel a thing a long way off, a bell ringing in another street. She felt the blood come, warm, and run down between her fingers and onto the leather of the grip, and make it slippery. She did not care. She shifted her fingers on the grip until they held, and she put her left hand under the right, low, near the pommel, the way he had taught her.
The way he had taught her.
He was on his knee in front of her. She had to step past him. She did not step past him. She stepped over him, almost, over his outflung leg, her boot going down on the black glass a hand's width from his knee, and she did not look down at him, because if she looked down at him and saw that puzzled frown again she would not be able to do anything at all.
And she stood between him and Kross.
Kross had not moved. He was standing where he had stopped, three paces back, with his thin sword loose in his left hand. He watched her come. He did not lift the sword. His face was wet, still, and closed, and he watched her with the same tired, careful look he had watched everything with since dawn.
"Miss Thorne," he said.
She did not answer him.
She set her feet.
She did not have to think about that, either. That was the strange thing. Her feet knew. They went apart on the black glass, the left one forward, the right one back, the weight going down into the back heel. Her hips turned. Her left hip toward Kross. Her right shoulder back. And her hands brought the sword down, low, behind her, by her left heel, the point almost on the glass.
The lie.
As if she were tired. As if she were giving up.
She was tired. That was true. She had walked across the Glass for a week, and she had not slept since the night before last, and she had not drunk since yesterday morning, and her hand was bleeding, and her sword was bent. Her legs were shaking. She could feel them shaking. The lie was not much of a lie.
But she was not giving up.
Feet. Hip. Turn. Hands low, then high. The edge, tilted. Don't swing it with your arms. Swing it with the ground.
Oren's voice. In her head. In the shadow of the iron horse, with the sun going down red behind the black ridge, and his big hands on her shoulders, turning her. Again. Again. No. Again.
He had it, in her head. He still had it there, in her head, even though he did not have it anywhere else in the world anymore. She thought of that, standing on the black glass with her sword down by her heel. She thought: it is in me. It is the only place it is now. In me.
And she went in.
The back foot drove. She felt the glass under her boot, hard and smooth and slippery, and she drove into it anyway, and it held. Her hip turned. Her whole body turned, like a gate, from the ground up, and the sword came up off the glass by her heel and round and up, in the long flat curve, with all of her behind it. All of her. Every bit of her that was left.
And there was the sound. The hiss. The long, slow tear of the air.
It was not perfect. She knew it was not perfect, even as she did it. Her feet were a little too close together. Her hands were a little too high, too soon, because the cut in her palm made her grip short. The bend in the blade made it turn, a little, at the top of the curve, so that the edge was not quite where it should be. It was not Oren's stroke.
But it was the stroke.
It was the same shape. It came from the same place. It went to the same place, under the arm, where a man cannot easily guard. It was the Vance cut, done by a tired girl with a bent sword and a bleeding hand on a lake of black glass, and it was true. And it was coming.
Kross barely met it.
Lyra saw his face change, at the last moment. Just the eyes. They went wide. And his thin sword came across his body, fast, so fast she hardly saw it move, the blade flat, the point down, and her sword hit it.
The ring was enormous.
It was the biggest sound she had ever heard on the Eye. It went out across the black glass in a ring, like a stone dropped in a pond, and came back from the dome, and from the broken iron men, and from somewhere far off on the shore. She felt it in her hands. She felt it in her teeth. Her sword bounced. It jumped back off his blade as if it had hit a wall, and her hands, wet with blood, nearly lost it, and she stumbled.
But Kross stumbled too.
She saw it. He went back. A whole step. His thin sword had held, but only just, and the weight of her stroke had come down through it into his arm and into his body, and it had moved him, and his heel slid on the glass again, the way it had slid when Oren was driving him, and this time he went down on one hand.
One hand. His bare right hand. Flat on the black glass, to catch himself.
And then he was up again. Quick. Before she had got her own feet back under her. He was up and standing, three paces off, with his sword up in front of him at last, and his chest going up and down, and the bare hand he had caught himself with held a little away from his body, open.
And he looked at her.
Not the way he had looked at her before. Not tired. Not careful. He looked at her the way you look at a thing you have been hunting for a long time and have found at last in a place you did not expect.
He had seen it.
Lyra knew that he had seen it. She knew what he was seeing. The stroke. The Vance cut. He had just taken it from Oren, all of it, every year of it, in one breath. It was in him now. He knew it the way Oren had known it, better than anybody else alive. And he had just seen it come at him again, from her. A little wrong. A little short. Done with a bent sword. But the same.
He knew she had it.
And she saw him look at her right hand. The bleeding hand, on the grip of her sword. Bare. She had no gloves. She had never had gloves. She saw him look at the bare skin of her hand, and the bare skin of her wrist above it, and then at his own bare right hand, held a little away from his body, open.
She understood.
He could take it. Now. From her. The same way. Step in close, inside the curve, and lay his hand on her wrist, gently, the way you steady somebody on a step. One breath. And then she would be standing here on the black glass with her sword held like a spade, and a small puzzled frown, and the Vance cut would be gone out of the world for good. Not in Oren. Not in her. Only in him. Only in the Archon's man, in his gray coat.
She could not stop him. She knew that. She had done the stroke with everything she had. There was nothing left in her for another one. Her legs were shaking so hard now that she could hardly stand. If he stepped in, she could not keep him off.
She stood there and waited for it.
She did not lower her sword. She held it up in front of her, in her bleeding hands, with the point toward him, wavering. She would not lower it. She would stand here holding it until he came and took it out of her. That was all she could do now, and so she did it.
Kross looked at her.
And he did not come.
She watched him not come. She watched him stand there, three paces off, with his bare hand open, and his chest going up and down, and his eyes on her face. She watched him think about it. She was sure he thought about it. She saw it in him, the thing he could do, sitting there behind his eyes, like a man looking at a coin lying in the road and deciding whether to bend down for it.
He did not bend down.
He closed his bare hand. Slowly. Into a fist. And he lowered his sword.
Not all the way. The point went down until it was pointing at the glass between them. And he stood like that, with his sword down and his bare hand shut, and looked at her, and something went across his face that she did not understand at all.
It looked, she thought afterward, a little like relief.
"Good," said Silas Kross. Very quietly. So quietly that she was not sure, afterward, that she had not dreamed it. "Good."
And he turned his head, and looked past her, at the pale one.
