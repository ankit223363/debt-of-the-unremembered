Chapter 51: Three Times Over, Part 2 of 5: The Pressmen's Yard
The Pressmen's Yard
The Pressmen's Yard was the only quiet place in the Ink Quarter, and it was quiet the way a grave is quiet.
You came into it through an arch. A low round stone arch, black with a hundred years of soot, cut through the ground floor of an old printing house on Quill Row. The printing house was empty. Its windows were boarded up, and somebody had painted on the boards, in big white letters that had run in the rain, TO LET. APPLY KEYS PRESS. The arch went through underneath it like a tunnel, ten paces long, and at the far end of the tunnel there was a gate. An iron gate, tall, with spikes along the top. It stood half open. It had stood half open for years, Juno said. The bottom of it had sunk into the ground on one side, and the hinge had rusted, and it would not shut any more and it would not open any further. You had to turn sideways to get through.
Silas could not get the barrow through it.
So they stopped in the tunnel, in the dark under the arch, just inside the gate. Silas put the handles down. And from where Corin sat, on the barrow, with his back against the front board and the blanket round his shoulders, he could see through the bars of the gate into the yard beyond.
It was square, and not very big. Twenty paces across. The backs of four tall printing houses stood round it, black and blank, with only a few small windows high up, and all of those were dark. Three of the houses had been empty for years. The fourth, on the far side, was the Keys Press. Corin could hear its presses through the wall, faint and deep, thud, thud-thud, the way you hear your own heart when you lie with your ear on the pillow. But no light came from it into the yard. The Keys Press had its doors on the other side, on Sallow Steps.
In the middle of the yard stood the broken press.
It was enormous. The biggest press Corin had ever seen. An old flat-bed press, from the days before the rolling presses, with an iron frame as tall as two men, painted black once and now red-brown with rust, with great iron legs like an animal's and a great iron screw at the top as thick as a tree trunk, and a long iron bar sticking out of the side to turn it. The bar had snapped, halfway along. The broken end lay in the snow at the foot of the press, half buried. The bed of the press, where the paper had once gone, was full of snow. And somebody had hung things on it. Strings. Lines of string stretched from the top of the iron frame out to iron rings in the walls of the houses all round, like the spokes of a wheel. And on the strings, frozen stiff in the cold, there were sheets of paper.
Old printed sheets. Hundreds of them. Hung up to dry years ago, and never taken down, and left there through every winter since. The rain had washed them, and the sun had faded them, and the frost had stiffened them, so that they stood out on their strings in the dark like the pages of a book that had been opened and frozen and never closed again. You could not read them any more. The ink had run off them, long ago. They were just gray. Gray sheets on gray strings, very still, all round the black press in the middle of the yard, with the snow lying on top of each one in a thin white line.
There was no wind. They did not move.
"Why did nobody take them down?" said Corin.
"Old Japhet," said Juno. She was sitting beside him, with her knees up under the blanket. Her voice was very low. "He had this yard. He was the last pressman who worked this press. He hung those sheets the day he died. The day before the big strike, when the Keys Press men came and broke the bar, because he wouldn't come out with them. He went home that night and died in his chair. His heart." She looked at the frozen gray sheets. "Nobody wanted to take them down after. They said it was bad luck. They said if you took down a dead man's last sheets you'd never print a straight line again."
"How do you know all that?"
"Gran told me." Juno pulled the blanket tighter. "She told me all the stories of the Quarter. Every yard. Every house. Grandpa used to work here, when he was young, before the Ministry took him on as a night porter. He knew Old Japhet. He used to bring me here when I was little and lift me up so I could touch the bottom sheets." She stopped. "Before."
Corin did not say anything.
He knew what before meant. Before old Terrence came home from the Ministry one morning eight years ago, from his last night on the Night Door, and sat down in his chair by Marta's range, among all those clocks, and never got up again. Not really. He still sat there now. He still rubbed his thumb on a key that was not there. He did not know Juno's name. He did not know Marta's name. He did not know his own.
Juno wore the key round her neck on a string. The real one. Brass, with the Ministry sign on its bow. NIGHT DOOR. III.
Corin saw her hand go to it now, under her shawls, and hold it.
Silas was standing in the mouth of the tunnel, at the Quill Row end, with his back to them, looking out into the lane. He had his hands in the pockets of his old black coat and his shoulders hunched. Every so often he took his spectacles off and wiped them and put them back on. He was keeping watch. Nobody had told him to. He just did it, the way he did everything, without being asked and without saying anything about it.
They waited.
It was very cold in the tunnel. Colder than outside. The stone of the arch held the cold the way the stone of the crypt did, and gave it back. Corin's breath went up in front of his face in a gray cloud and hung there and went away. His bad leg ached, a deep dull hot ache all along the back of the thigh, and the knee felt as if it were full of wet sand. His hands, under the blanket, did not feel anything at all. He looked at them once, to be sure they were still there. Gray. Stiff. Still. They had not shaken since the bottom of the Hill.
He thought about the garden.
He had not meant to. He had meant to think about Lucian, and what to say to him, and what to ask. He had the questions ready. He had written them down in the Ledger in the crypt, before they left, in a list, the way Lyra made lists. Who signs. Who says yes. Where the paper goes. What happens to the children after the House of Rest. What he knows about Monday night. But every time he tried to go through the list in his head, he found himself somewhere else. On a narrow stair between two high walls. With his black hand round an iron bar. Looking at a woman on her knees in the frost, with a trowel, planting bulbs on top of bulbs.
He found that the tune was going round in his head.
Not the three notes. All of it. Up, and up, and hanging. And down, and round, and home. It had been going round in his head all evening, he realized, ever since he had hummed it for Lyra. Under everything. Like the presses through the wall.
He did not know what to do about that. So he did nothing.
Beside him, Juno said very quietly, "Corin."
"Yes?"
"What's it like? Being able to do what you do?" She did not look at him. She was looking at the frozen sheets. "Taking things out of people. Out of the dead. Burning them."
Corin thought about it.
"It's like being very rich," he said at last, "in a city where everything costs more than you've got."
Juno was quiet for a moment.
"I'm never going to do it," she said. "Burn anything. Not ever. Not one thing. Not if it would save the whole city." She swallowed. "Not after Grandpa."
"I know."
"Is that wrong?"
"No," said Corin. "No. I think it might be the bravest thing anybody's ever said to me."
She looked at him then, sideways, in the dark. She did not say anything. But she leaned against his shoulder, a little, the way she had leaned against Abner's hand on the bridge. And stayed there.
The Ministry bell rang the quarter. Then the half.
At the mouth of the tunnel, Silas took his hands out of his pockets.
He did not turn round. He did not say anything. He just stepped back, very quietly, out of the mouth of the arch and into the shadow against the tunnel wall. And stood there. Flat against the stone, tall and thin and black, like a crack in it.
And from out in Quill Row, faint, through the thudding of the presses, Corin heard footsteps. Quick and light and even. Coming along the black ice of the lane.
And a voice. Soft and precise. Counting.
"Two hundred and eleven. Two hundred and twelve. Two hundred and thirteen."
The footsteps stopped at the mouth of the arch.
Lucian
He stood in the mouth of the arch, a narrow black shape against the yellow light of Quill Row, and did not come in.
Corin could see him quite clearly from the barrow. The long black coat, buttoned to the throat. The leather folder under one arm. The slim black book with its silver clasp held flat against his chest, with his fingers spread over it. His short black hair shining in the light from the lane. He had a scarf on now, a gray wool scarf wound twice round his neck, very neat, with the ends tucked in. And a pair of black gloves.
He was looking into the tunnel. Into the dark. At the barrow, and the gray blanket, and the FEVER card on its stick.
His mouth pressed itself together.
"Harvester Vane," he said.
He said it quietly. Not as a question. The way a man reads out a name at the top of a form.
Corin did not answer at once.
Then he pushed the blanket off his shoulders, slowly, and let it fall onto the boards of the barrow. And sat there, in the dark, in his old gray coat, with his gray hands on his knees, where the light from the lane could reach them.
"Scribe Lucian," he said.
Lucian's large dark eyes went to his hands. And stayed there.
"I knew," said Lucian. "On the stair." He did not move out of the mouth of the arch. "I knew by your hands. You kept them under the coal man's cape, but you could not keep them still, and when they moved I saw the coal dust crack on the knuckles, and what was under it was gray. And by the leg. The Watch report from the Bridewell stables, last Saturday morning. An iron rod, in the back of the left thigh, above the knee. The fugitive was not found. I read it on Saturday afternoon. Three times." He paused. "And by your voice. You tried to talk like Mister Cole. You did it quite well. But you said Afternoon, sir the way you said It was correct in Toby's office in the autumn. With the same breath before it." Another pause. "I notice breaths."
"You didn't tell anybody."
"No."
"You told the stair where you were going."
Lucian's mouth pressed itself together very hard.
"I always count the stair," he said. "I always say where I am going next. Out loud. To the stair. It is a habit. It helps me not to forget." He looked down at the black book against his chest. "I said it three times today. I do not usually say it three times." A very small pause. "I hoped you were listening."
Nobody said anything for a moment.
The presses thudded through the wall of the Keys Press. Thud. Thud-thud. Out in Quill Row, somebody went past the mouth of the arch, a man with a sack on his back, singing to himself, and did not look in. Lucian did not turn round. But Corin saw him count the footsteps going past. His lips moved. One. Two. Three. Four. And stopped when the man was gone.
Then Silas stepped out from the shadow against the tunnel wall.
Lucian saw him and went white. Whiter than he was. He took one step back, into the lane, and his hand went flat over the black book as if somebody had tried to take it.
"Doctor Silas," he said.
His voice did not go up. That was the strange thing. It stayed exactly as soft and precise as before. But his face had gone the color of paper.
"Scribe," said Silas.
"You are—" Lucian stopped. He looked at the black book. He opened it. He did not look at the page. He closed it again. "You are to be collected tomorrow. At the sixth bell. By the Inquisitor himself."
"I know."
"You should not be here."
"Neither should you," said Silas.
Lucian looked at him for a long moment.
Then he did something Corin did not expect. He looked back over his shoulder, once, at Quill Row. Up the lane, and down it. And then he turned round, and looked at the iron gate at the end of the tunnel, behind the barrow, standing half open with its foot sunk in the ground. And he counted the bars. Corin watched his lips. Eleven. He counted them again. Eleven. And a third time.
"Eleven," said Lucian. "Good." He took a breath. "Not here. Not in the arch. People look into arches. In there." He nodded at the yard beyond the gate. At the broken press, and the frozen sheets. "Nobody looks into Old Japhet's yard. Nobody has looked into it for twelve years. It is bad luck."
"I can't get the barrow through that gate," said Silas.
"Then he must walk," said Lucian. He looked at Corin's leg. "I am sorry. I know he cannot walk. But he must. Through the gate, and to the press. Twenty-two steps. I counted them. I have counted them every Saturday night for three years. It is the long way." His mouth pressed itself together. "I will wait."
So Corin walked.
He got himself off the end of the barrow the way he had got off Abner's cart that afternoon, with the crutch first and the good leg after and the bad leg swung out stiff in its splints like a plank. Juno slid off beside him on the sides of her wrapped feet, and caught at his coat to steady herself, and he let her. Silas came round and took his other arm. And the three of them went through the gate together, sideways, one at a time, through the gap where the gate stood half open. And across the snow of the yard. Crutch, good foot. Crutch, good foot. Past the first line of frozen sheets, which hung just above Corin's head and did not move. Twenty-two steps. Lucian had been right. Corin counted them too.
At the press, Silas let go of his arm. Corin leaned his back against the great rusty iron leg of it, and stood there, on the good foot, with the crutch under his arm, breathing hard. Juno sat down in the snow at the foot of the press, on the broken end of the iron bar, with her knees drawn up.
Lucian came through the gate last.
He did it very carefully, turning sideways, and his long black coat caught on the rusted edge of the gate and he stopped and unhooked it, and looked at the place where it had caught, and brushed it twice with his gloved fingers. Then he came across the yard. He did not walk on the snow where they had walked. He walked beside it, in a straight line, his own line, setting each narrow polished shoe down exactly in front of the other. Corin saw his lips move. Nineteen. Twenty. Twenty-one. Twenty-two.
He stopped at the press.
He stood there with his back very straight and the folder under his arm and the black book against his chest, three paces from Corin, under the frozen gray sheets, and the snow on top of the sheets above his head was white, and his face under it was white too. And Corin saw that he was shaking. Not his hands. He kept his hands flat on the book, and they did not move. All the rest of him. His shoulders, and his knees, and the edges of his long black coat. A fine fast shaking, all over, like a wire in the wind.
He saw Corin see it. His mouth pressed itself together.
"I am very frightened," said Lucian. "I want you to know that. I want it written down somewhere. I am not here because I am brave. I am not brave. I have never been brave. I am here because I have checked it three times, and it comes out the same each time, and I do not know how to make it come out differently."
"Checked what?" said Corin.
Lucian did not answer that.
"Three questions," he said instead. "I will answer three questions. Only three. And then I will go home the long way and go to bed at the second bell after midnight, and I will write in my own book that I came through Old Japhet's yard tonight and saw nobody. And that will be correct." He looked at Corin. "Because after tonight I will not have seen you. Do you understand? Whatever I tell you, I will not have told you. I will have only told you about a form that was not filled in correctly."
Corin looked at him.
Three questions.
He had five on his list. In the Ledger, in his coat, over his heart. Who signs. Who says yes. Where the paper goes. What happens to the children after the House of Rest. What he knows about Monday night. Five. And there were a hundred more behind those five that he had not written down because there was no room on the page. About a garden on a roof. About a woman in gray. About a voice.
And he could ask three.
He thought about Silas, standing at his shoulder in the snow, who was going to be collected tomorrow at the sixth bell by the Inquisitor himself. About fifty-one children on pallets in the crypt of Saint Bride's, with their eyes open, watching the lamp. About a girl of six who had hummed three notes, twice, tonight, and then stopped.
He made himself not think about the garden.
"Three," he said.
Lucian nodded once, very small and very fast.
"Ask," he said. "Carefully. I will answer exactly what you ask. Nothing more. I always answer exactly what is asked. It is the only way I know to be correct."
The Black Book
Corin took a long time over the first one.
He stood with his back against the cold iron leg of the press and looked at the young man in black, and at the frozen gray sheets above him, and thought about how to say it. Lucian waited. He did not hurry him. He stood quite still, shaking his fine fast shake, with his gloved hands flat on the black book, and waited the way a clerk waits at a counter for somebody to finish filling in a form.
"The notice on the door of Saint Bride's," said Corin at last. "Pallister signed it. Under his name, somebody else wrote Seen. Approved. And pressed a gray seal beside it that I couldn't read." He kept his voice even. "Who wrote those two words, and where will he be on Monday?"
Lucian's mouth pressed itself together.
"That is two questions," he said.
"It was one breath."
Lucian looked at him. For a moment Corin thought he would refuse. Then, very slightly, something in the narrow pale face moved. It was not a smile. Lucian did not seem to know how to smile. But it was the place where a smile would have been.
"Yes," he said. "It was one breath. I noticed." A pause. "I will count it as one. Because it was asked in one breath. That is a rule. It is my rule. Nobody else knows it."
He opened the black book.
He did it with great care. He undid the silver clasp with his gloved thumb, and it made a small sharp click in the quiet of the yard. He opened the cover. And Corin saw that the pages inside were covered, every one of them, from edge to edge, in very small, very neat, very black writing. Lines and lines of it. Not a blot. Not a crossing-out. Not a single letter out of place.
Lucian turned the pages. He did not look for the place. He knew where it was. He turned to it the way Corin turned to the page in his Ledger that said OREN at the top.
Then he held the book out, open, at the length of his arm. Not to Corin. Into the air between them. So that Corin could see the page, in the faint gray light that came down into the yard from the sky, and the faint yellow light that came through the gate from Quill Row, but could not touch it.
"I will not read it to you," said Lucian. "You will read it. If you read it, I have not told you anything. I have only held a book open in a bad light."
Corin leaned forward from the press, on his crutch. Juno got up out of the snow and stood beside him on the sides of her feet, holding his coat. Silas came and stood at his other shoulder, with his crooked spectacles catching the light.
At the top of the page, in letters a little larger than the rest, it said:
MIDWINTER. MONDAY. HIS EXCELLENCY.
And under it, a list. Each line with a bell at the front of it.
Sixth bell. Rises. The garden, with her Excellency. One quarter hour.
Seventh bell. The Office of Public Health, at Saint Bride's (A. P.). Not attending. To be told when done.
Ninth bell. The Council. Midwinter sitting. The Treasurer to report on the year.
Second bell after noon. The Choir, at the Spire. To listen only.
Fourth bell. Rests. Not to be disturbed for any cause.
Eighth bell. Dines. Alone.
Ninth bell. The Bourse. Masked. By the garden door, not the front. The Lady to be told at the eighth bell, and not before.
Carries his four stones.
Midnight. The box. With the Fourth Desk (clause eleven).
First bell after midnight. Home by the river road.
And at the very bottom of the page, in the same small neat black hand, but a little apart from the rest, as if it had been added later:
All written. All checked. Three times.
Corin read the page through once. Then again. Then a third time, without meaning to, and realized he was doing it the way Lucian did it, and stopped.
Nobody said anything for a long moment.
The presses thudded through the wall. The frozen sheets hung above them, gray on gray, very still.
"The Bourse," said Juno at last, in a whisper. "He's going to the Bourse."
Lucian did not answer her. She had not asked him a question. He just held the book open, at the length of his arm, in the bad light. His hand was quite steady now. It was only the rest of him that shook.
Corin looked at one line. The Lady to be told at the eighth bell, and not before. Vivienne would not know until an hour before. She would not know who was coming to her Midwinter night until the carriages were already going up Exchange Street.
He looked at another line. Seventh bell. The Office of Public Health, at Saint Bride's (A. P.). Not attending. To be told when done.
He felt Silas, at his shoulder, go very still.
And he looked at the line that stood by itself. Under the ninth bell, under the Bourse. In the same small black hand as everything else. As if it were the most ordinary thing in the world. As ordinary as Dines. Alone.
Carries his four stones.
He opened his mouth.
"Is that your second question?" said Lucian at once.
Corin shut his mouth again.
He looked at the young man in black. At his narrow pale face under the frozen sheets. At his dark eyes, very wide, watching Corin over the top of the open book. Waiting. Not hoping. Not afraid. Just waiting, the way a clerk waits, to see which box would be ticked.
What stones? he wanted to say. What does a man like that carry stones for? Where? Why four?
And that would be his second question. And it would be gone. And Silas would still be collected tomorrow at the sixth bell, and the carts would still come at the seventh, and Corin would know what was in a man's pocket.
"No," said Corin.
Lucian looked at him a moment longer.
Then he closed the book. Gently. And did the silver clasp up again with his thumb. Click. And held it flat against his chest, with his fingers spread over it.
"That was the first question," he said. "Who wrote the two words. His Excellency wrote them. In his own hand, at his own desk, on Thursday, at the third bell after noon. I placed the paper before him myself. He read it once. He never reads anything twice. He does not need to." His mouth pressed itself together. "He took up his pen and wrote Seen, and then Approved. And then he pressed his own seal beside it. The gray one. He keeps the gray wax for things he has seen with his own eyes." A pause. "And where he will be on Monday, you have read. I have not told you. You read it, in a bad light."
He looked at the gate behind them. He counted the bars again. Eleven.
"Two more," he said.
