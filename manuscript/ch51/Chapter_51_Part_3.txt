Chapter 51: Three Times Over, Part 3 of 5: The Bank's Own Books
The Bank's Own Books
It was Juno who asked the second one.
Corin had not meant her to. He was still standing against the press, looking at the list in the Ledger in his head, trying to choose between where the paper goes and what happens to the children, when Juno, beside him, holding his coat, said in a small hard voice:
"Who gets paid?"
Lucian's eyes went to her.
They stayed on her for a moment. On her sharp white face, and her three shawls, and her feet in their blanket wrappings on the snow. Corin saw him count something. He did not know what. The shawls, maybe. Or the breaths she took.
"Is that your question?" said Lucian.
Juno opened her mouth. She looked at Corin. She shut it.
Corin looked at her. At her chin, set. At her eyes, very bright. Because I pulled forty sacks today and it cost me two toes, and I want it to be for something.
He thought about the shelf. About the whitewashed room full of jars, and the gray light in them that moved when you came near. About kindnesses, for the ladies of the Hill, to put in their tea. About crates with brown card labels on them going out of the wet door on the high tide, to men in the north who wanted children's clean cold for the mines. About the drum boys with their hooks, and the six carts with blankets in them and hot bricks for the children's feet.
Somebody paid for all of that. Somebody was paid.
"That's the question," said Corin. "Who gets paid. For the children."
Lucian nodded once.
"That is the second question," he said. "I will answer it."
He did not open the black book this time. He opened the leather folder instead. He untied the black ribbon, one loop and then the other, the way Corin had seen him untie it in Toby's office in the autumn. And he took out a single sheet of paper. Not white. Pale green. Ruled in faint blue lines, with columns, and numbers in the columns, written in a small round clerk's hand that was not Lucian's.
He held it out at the length of his arm, into the bad light, the way he had held the book.
"This is the monthly abstract of the Fourth Desk," he said. "Of the Oblivion Bank. For the month before last. The Bank sends one to the Office of the Archon at the end of every month. I copy it. Three times. One copy is placed before his Excellency. One copy goes to the Treasurer's office. One copy goes into the Archive, on the Second Level, in a box with the year on it, where nobody will ever look at it again." His mouth pressed itself together. "This is not any of those three. This is a fourth. I make a fourth. Every month. I have made a fourth every month for three years. I do not know why."
Corin did not believe that. He did not say so.
He leaned forward on his crutch and read.
It was a long column of names, down the left side. Not people's names. The names of accounts. The Lantern Bridge Lamps. The Crescent Gas. The Ministry Steps (repair). The Choir (allowance). The Office of Public Health (allowance). And in the next column, numbers. Sums of money. Some small. Some very large.
And near the bottom, by itself, a little apart, like the line in the black book that said Carries his four stones, there was one line in a different color of ink. A brownish red, like old blood.
Sundries, the Hill. Received of the Office of Public Health, being the Dispensary receipts for the month (by the Lady's house, by the glass, at the agreed rate).
And beside it, a number.
Corin looked at the number for a long time.
He had been a Senior Investigator of the Harvesting Section. He had been paid by the Ministry, once a month, in an envelope, for nine years. He knew what a clerk was paid, and what a constable was paid, and what a Senior Investigator was paid. He knew what a house on Tallow Street cost a year, and a room in it, and a loaf, and a bottle of the cheap ink from Sallow's. He knew what the whole of Mrs. Marta's rent came to, in a year, because she had told him on the night she nearly lost it.
The number beside Sundries, the Hill was more than all of those things put together. Many times more. It was more money than Corin had ever seen written down in one place, for one month.
For one month.
And it was the month before last. The month when Ned was still alive.
"By the glass," said Silas, at Corin's shoulder. His voice was very quiet. "At the agreed rate."
Lucian did not answer him. Silas had not asked a question.
Below that line there were three more. In the ordinary black ink.
Out of Sundries, the Hill: to the Office of Public Health, its allowance for the month.
Out of Sundries, the Hill: to the Treasurer's office, one tenth (D. S.).
Out of Sundries, the Hill: to the Council, the balance. For the Crescent Gas, and the Lantern Bridge Lamps, and the lamps of the Hill.
Corin read them twice. Then he looked up from the green paper, out of the dark of the yard, past the frozen sheets and the half-open gate and the black tunnel, at the faint yellow light of Quill Row.
And he thought of the Crescent.
Of the long white curve of houses, all the way round the top of the Hill, like a smile. Of the tall green iron posts along the road, on both sides, with the glass lamps on top of them. Lit. All of them. Even at the first bell after noon, on a gray winter day, burning with their soft steady white-blue flame behind the glass, so that the whole of the Crescent seemed to glow.
The city pays for the gas, Lyra had said.
The city did not pay for the gas.
Polly paid for the gas. Kit Flynn, with his wooden spoon. Ned, who forgot how to swallow. Gideon, at eight years old, in the selling room, in the chair with the straps.
He heard Juno make a sound beside him. Very small. She had read it too. She could read now. Not well, not fast, but she had been learning, with Lyra, on the barge and in the crypt, and she had got far enough. She had got as far as Crescent. She was holding his coat so tight that he could feel her fist through it, against his side.
"The lamps," she said. "On the Hill. The ones that are on all day."
Lucian did not answer her.
He was looking at the green paper in his own hand. Not at the figures. At the edge of it, where his gloved thumb held it. And for the first time since he had come into the yard, he was not shaking. He had gone quite still. As still as the frozen sheets.
"The Treasurer signs the abstract," he said. Quietly. Not to anyone. "At the bottom. In green ink. Seen and agreed. D. S. Every month." A pause. "His Excellency does not sign it. His Excellency reads it once. He never reads anything twice."
He put the green paper back in the folder. He tied the black ribbon. One loop. The other.
"That was the second question," he said. "Who gets paid. The Health gets paid. The Treasurer gets paid. And the lamps." He looked up at the gray sky over the yard, at the narrow square of it between the four black houses, as if he could see the Crescent from here. As if he could see the glow of it on the clouds. "Every lamp on the Hill. And the Lantern Bridge. All seven arches."
He looked back at Corin.
"I copy it," he said. "Three times. Four. And every night I go home the long way, past the printers, so that I will not have to walk under them."
The Doctor and the Scribe
Corin turned his head and looked at Silas.
He did not say anything. He did not need to. Silas was standing at his shoulder, tall and thin in the snow, with his long arms hanging at his sides and his crooked spectacles catching the light from the gate. His face was quite still. Corin had seen it like that once before. On Monday, in the arches, when Nell came running down the slope to say that Ned had stopped breathing.
"Ask it," said Corin quietly.
Silas looked at him.
"It's yours," said Corin. "The third one. Ask it."
Silas did not move for a moment. Then he took off his spectacles, slowly, and held them in his long fingers, and looked at the young man in black without them. Corin had never seen him do that. Without the spectacles his face looked younger, and much more tired, and the eyes in it were gray and very clear.
"The House of Rest," said Silas. "For the Simple and the Aged. Beyond the tanneries, on the east road, where the river bends. I have stood at that gate four times this winter and been given a peppermint." His voice did not rise. "What happens to them, Scribe? To the children? After they go in at that gate?"
Lucian looked at him.
And Corin saw something happen in the narrow pale face that he had not seen before. Not fear. The fear had been there all along. Something under the fear. Something that looked, for a moment, almost like shame.
"That is the third question," said Lucian.
"Yes."
"I will answer it." He took a breath. "I have the numbers. I copy the Health's books, too. Every week. They come up the pipe on a Saturday. I copied them this afternoon, after the garden, before the stair." He closed his eyes. He did not open the book. He did not need to. "Since the first week of the autumn, three hundred and twelve persons have been admitted to the House of Rest for the Simple and the Aged. Forty-one are there tonight. Of the rest, one hundred and eight are written Died, of the river fever. And one hundred and sixty-three are written Discharged. To the Bank."
Nobody said anything.
The presses thudded through the wall.
"To the Bank," said Silas.
Lucian opened his eyes.
"The House of Rest is not a house," he said. "It is a gate. A gate and a long room with beds in it and men in brown coats who are very kind and give sweets. They keep them three days. Sometimes four. They give them the sweets three times a day, and warm milk with the sweets, and a blanket each. And on Friday morning, before dawn, a cart comes. From the Lady's house. A plum-colored cart, with a driver in a plum scarf. And it takes the ones who are ready." His voice did not change at all. It was soft and quick and precise, every word set down separately, like a coin on a table. "Ready is the Health's word. It means that they have stopped asking for their mothers."
Juno made a sound.
Lucian did not look at her.
"The cart takes them to Cinder Lane," he said. "To the coal chute behind the Bourse. And down. You have seen down, I think, Harvester. And from down they are—" He stopped. He looked at the black book against his chest. "The Health's book says treated. The Lady's book says something else. I do not copy the Lady's books. Nobody copies the Lady's books."
"Remy does," said Corin, before he could stop himself.
Lucian's eyes went to him very fast. And stayed. And then, very slightly, he nodded. Just once. As if a box had been ticked that he had not known was on the form.
"And after they are treated," he went on, "the Bank takes them. Discharged, to the Bank. The Intake Hall, on Lantern Quay. Warden Briggs's hall. They are registered as Slates, and given numbers, and sent to the docks. To carry. Or to the mills. Or—" His mouth pressed itself together. "The quiet ones, the ones who were very young, and very empty, and very still, the Choir has first choice of those. For the allowance. I have seen the forms."
Corin felt the cold of the iron press against his back, all the way through his coat. All the way into his spine.
He had walked through Warden Briggs's Intake Hall in the autumn. He had seen the line. The long gray line of people shuffling forward to the desks, with their eyes on nothing. He had seen Felix, in the line. A big young man who smiled at nothing and carried what he was told to carry.
He had not looked at how young some of them were. He had not thought to look.
"And the fifty-one," said Silas.
His voice had changed now. It had gone very low and very rough, like a cart going over stones. He was holding his spectacles so tightly in his long fingers that Corin thought the mended arm would snap.
"That is a fourth question, Doctor," said Lucian.
"I know it is."
Lucian looked at him for a long time.
Then he turned his head and looked at the iron gate at the end of the yard. He counted the bars. Eleven. He counted them again.
"I will not answer a fourth question," he said. "I said three. I always do what I say." He looked back at Silas. "But I will tell you about a form. A form that is not yet filled in. It is the Health's form for Monday. Report of Removal, Saint Bride's. It has a box on it for the number of persons removed. And a box for the time. And a box for remarks." A pause. "It will come up the pipe to the Office of the Archon on Monday at noon. I will read it. Three times. And whatever is written in the box for the number, I will copy it exactly. I always copy it exactly."
He looked down at his gloved hands on the black book.
"If the number is fifty-one," said Lucian, "I will copy fifty-one. If the number is four, I will copy four. If the box says none, and the remarks say the crypt was found empty, the persons having been removed in the night by persons unknown—" He stopped. "Then I will copy that. Correctly. And I will place it before his Excellency. And he will read it once."
Silas did not say anything.
He stood in the snow with his spectacles in his hand and looked at the young man in black, and his tired gray eyes were very bright. Then he put the spectacles back on. Crooked. And looked down at the snow.
"And me," he said. Very low. "Tomorrow. The sixth bell."
"That is a fifth question," said Lucian.
"It isn't a question," said Silas. "It's a man asking what time he's to be hanged."
Lucian was quiet for a moment.
"You will not be hanged," he said. "The Inquisitor does not hang people. He does not need to." He looked at Silas over the black book. "He asked for you himself, Doctor. On Thursday, in the Long Office. I was there with the Council papers. He asked for you because you wrote four letters. He said, A man who writes four letters will write a fifth, and the fifth will be to somebody who can read. He said, I would like to have a little talk with him before that." Lucian's mouth pressed itself together. "He said it very kindly."
"They all say things very kindly," said Silas.
"Yes," said Lucian. "Yes. They do."
And for a moment the two of them stood there under the frozen sheets, the tall tired doctor and the thin frightened scribe, and looked at each other. And Corin, against the press, thought that he had never seen two men understand each other so completely, without either of them saying a single thing that the other had not already known.
Things I Had
"That is three," said Lucian. "I will go now."
But he did not go.
He stood under the frozen sheets with the black book against his chest and the folder under his arm, and he did not move. Corin watched him. He watched him look at the gate, and count the bars, eleven, and look back at the press. And look at the gate again. As if there were something he had to do before he could go through it, and he did not know how to do it.
It was Juno who spoke. Of course it was Juno.
"They'll read him," she said.
She said it to Corin, not to Lucian. Low and quick and fierce, holding his coat.
"Tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever. That man in gray. The Inquisitor. He reads people. Moll said. He sat Moll down in a room and tapped on the desk and read him like a book. And he'll read him." She jerked her chin at Lucian without looking at him. "And he'll find this. Tonight. Us. The yard. Silas. He'll find all of it, and then he'll know where we are, and where Silas is going to be, and what we know about Monday, and—" She stopped. She swallowed. "You could take it out. Couldn't you? Tonight. Out of him. Before he goes. So there's nothing there to find."
The yard was very quiet.
Corin did not look at Lucian. He looked at Juno.
"I don't take from the living," he said.
"But you could."
"I don't." He kept his voice level. "I never have. Not once. I take from the dead, Juno. From what's left in them after. That's what a harvester is." He looked down at his gray hands on the crutch. "The ones who take from the living are the ones in the selling room with the chair with the straps. And the men in the white rooms at the Ministry. And—" He did not finish.
Lucian, we all make mistakes. Let me help you not to make that one again.
He looked up.
Lucian was looking at him. His face had gone the color of the snow. He had taken one step back from the press, and then another, until his shoulders were nearly touching the nearest line of frozen sheets. And he was holding the black book against his chest with both hands now, both gloved hands spread over it, as if it were a shield.
"Please," said Lucian.
His voice was not soft and quick and precise any more. It had gone high and thin, like a boy's.
"Please. Don't." He took a breath that shook. "I know you could. I know what harvesters can do. I have read the Regulations. All of them. I know Regulation Nine, and what it does not say. I know what a Senior Investigator could do to a man if nobody was watching, in a yard, at night." He swallowed. "Please leave me my memories."
"I wasn't going to—"
"Please," said Lucian again, over him. "Even this one. Especially this one."
And then he did something that Corin would remember for a long time.
He opened the black book again. Not to the Midwinter page. To the back. To the very last pages, after all the lines and lines of small black writing. And he turned it round, and held it out at the length of his arm, into the bad light, the way he had before. But this time his hand shook so much that the page shook with it.
At the top of the last page, in the same small neat black hand as everything else, it said:
THINGS I HAD.
And under it, a list.
My mother's voice. (Spring, three years ago. A form not filled in correctly. He helped me.)
The way to my old school, and the name of the master. (Summer, three years ago. A date copied wrong. He helped me.)
How to whistle. (Autumn, three years ago. A door left open. He helped me.)
The name of the dog we had. Brown, with one white ear. I remember the ear. Not the name. (Winter, two years ago. He helped me.)
The taste of oranges. (Spring, two years ago. He helped me.)
Why I count. (Summer, last year. I don't know what I did. He helped me.)
My own birthday. (Autumn. This autumn. The week of the scribe. He helped me.)
There were more. The list went on down the page, line after line, each with its little note in brackets after it, the season and the year and the small mistake. And at the end of each one, the same three words. He helped me.
Corin read the list once. He could not read it twice.
Beside him, Juno had let go of his coat. She was standing quite still on the sides of her wrapped feet in the snow, with her hands at her sides, staring at the page.
"I write them down," said Lucian. His voice was thin and very steady now, the way a voice is steady when it is holding something very heavy. "After. When I notice they are gone. Sometimes it takes me a long time to notice. The oranges took a month. I bought one at Midwinter, from a barrow on the Ministry Bridge, because I thought I liked them, and I ate it, and it tasted of nothing. Of wet paper." His mouth pressed itself together. "So I write them down. Here. In the back. Where he will not look, because he never reads anything twice, and he read this book once, on the day I was given it, when it was empty."
He closed the book. Slowly.
"He is very kind about it," said Lucian. "I want you to understand that. He is always very kind. He never shouts. He puts his hand on my shoulder and says, Lucian, we all make mistakes. And then he helps me. And afterward I feel better. I feel calm. Much calmer than before. I go back to my desk and I copy everything three times and I do not make that mistake again. Not ever." He looked down at the book. "Because I cannot remember how."
Nobody said anything.
The frozen sheets hung above them in the dark, gray on gray.
"If he finds out about tonight," said Lucian, "he will help me. And I will not have been here. I will not have stood in Old Japhet's yard and held a book open in a bad light and told a doctor how many there were. I will go to my desk on Tuesday and copy the Health's report three times and I will not know what it means." He looked up at Corin. "And I would rather the Inquisitor found it. Do you understand? I would rather he read it out of me in a white room and put me in a jar on a shelf than have it taken away and be calm."
He was crying. Corin saw it. Not much. A little shine at the edges of his large dark eyes, in the light from the gate. He did not wipe it. His hands were on the book.
"It is the first thing in three years," said Lucian, "that I have done on purpose."
Corin looked at him for a long time.
Then he let go of the crutch with one hand, and leaned his weight back against the iron leg of the press, and reached inside his coat. Over his heart. And took out the Ledger.
He opened it on his good knee, against the press, awkwardly, with one gray hand. He turned to the back. To the page with THOSE I OWE at the top. And he took the stub of pencil out of the spine, and he wrote, under Juno. Two toes. Forty sacks.
He wrote it slowly, in his square capitals, so that it could be read in a bad light.
LUCIAN. I PROMISED HIM I WOULD TAKE NOTHING FROM HIM. EVER. NOT EVEN TO SAVE US.
Then he turned the book round, the way Lucian had turned his, and held it out at the length of his arm. Into the air between them.
Lucian looked at it.
He read it. Corin watched his lips move. Once. Twice.
Three times.
"That is correct," said Lucian, in a whisper. "Thank you." And then, very fast, as if the words had got out before he could stop them, "I am sorry. I was not to say thank you. I am not to be thanked. That is my rule."
"Whose rule?"
"Mine," said Lucian. "I made it. It is one of the ones I have left."
