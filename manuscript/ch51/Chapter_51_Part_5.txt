Chapter 51: Three Times Over, Part 5 of 5: Red Light on the River
Red Light on the River
They went back across the river the way they had come. Dead.
Silas laid the gray army blanket over them again in the tunnel under the arch, and tucked it in at the sides, and picked up the handles, and the world went dark and close and smelled of carbolic. And the wheels began to turn on the black ice of Quill Row with their slow grinding. And the presses thudded through the road, through the boards of the barrow, through Corin's back.
He lay under the blanket with Juno small and stiff beside him and did not think about anything for a long time. He let the city come to him through the dark. The ink. The hot metal. The chestnuts. A man laughing in a doorway. The great iron sign of Sallow's Inks creaking on its arm above them as they went under it. The Midwinter song from somewhere high up, the one about the three lamps and the long road, sung by a different voice this time, a man's, deep and drunk and happy.
Then the presses got fainter. And the cobbles changed. Bigger, rounder, older. And the smell of the river came up through the carbolic, cold and brown and full of weed.
Silas stopped the barrow.
Corin felt him put the handles down. Gently. Not the way he put them down at a barrier. The way he put them down when he wanted to rest. There was no voice calling Hold there. There was no brazier. There was only the sound of the river, very close, slapping on stone. And the wind, which had come up while they were in the yard, cold and thin, coming down the river from the north.
"Where are we?" said Juno, very small.
"The Ministry Bridge," said Silas's voice above them. "The top of it." A pause. "I need a moment. Lie still."
They lay still.
Corin heard Silas breathing, hard and slow, leaning on the parapet. He heard the wind in the iron lamp-posts of the bridge, a thin high whistling. He heard, far off down the river, a ship's bell ringing, two strokes and then two more, the way they rang them at the Saltgate when the tide turned.
And then Silas said, in a different voice, a low quick careful voice, "Vane."
"Yes?"
"Can you sit up? Slowly. Don't take the blanket off. Just look."
Corin sat up.
He did it slowly, the way Silas said, with the blanket still over his head like a hood and pulled close round his face, so that from outside he would look like a dead man who had been propped up, or a heap of something under a cloth. And he looked out from under the edge of it, over the side of the barrow, through the iron railings of the bridge, at the river.
It was very dark. The river was black. Blacker than the sky. On both sides of it the lamps of the city went away along the embankments in two long lines, yellow, getting smaller and closer together until they met in the far distance downstream, toward the Saltgate, like the two sides of a road going away to a point. And between them, the black water. Moving. He could see it move only where the lamps touched it, little broken pieces of yellow light that slid and wobbled and broke up and came together again.
Upstream, to the north, there were no lamps. The city ended. The embankments ended. There was only the river, going away into the dark between low black banks, toward the locks, toward Long Pond and the Mill Cut and the Tanners' Ditch and everything that lay beyond. Toward Brindle. Toward Withy Reach. Toward the peat cuttings, and a turf barn with a broken roof, the third one, where Hessa owed a goat.
"There," said Silas, very low. "Upstream. Under the far bank. Past the last lamp. Watch."
Corin watched.
For a long time there was nothing. Just the dark, and the black water, and the low black line of the far bank against a sky that was only a little less black.
And then a light.
Red.
It was very small and very far away. A single red point, low down on the water, under the far bank, where the river went round the first bend north of the city. It showed for a moment. Then it was gone. Then it showed again. Then gone. Three times. Short. Like a door opened and shut, and opened and shut, and opened and shut.
Then nothing.
Corin did not breathe.
He had never seen it. But he knew it. He knew it the way he had known Harlan's voice at the foot of the Tanners' Bridge. From other people's faces. From Oren, on Monday night, in the arches, with the snow melting on his shoulders and his big face set. At the Brindle toll bridge. A red lantern. It flashed three times at the willow bend, north. Moll knew it. Moll said, That's Vell.
A black skiff. A man in a round hat with a plain blank face, and a fishing rod he never fished with. Rowed by another man. And a red lantern with a shutter on it, that opened and shut.
Going north.
"It's going upstream," said Silas. "I've been watching it while I got my breath. It came out from under the Ministry Bridge, below us, while we were on the bridge. Right under us. A small boat. Very low. Two men in it, I think. No lamp. And then when it got past the last lamp on the embankment, it showed that." He was quiet a moment. "It was signaling to somebody. Up there. Further up."
Corin looked at the dark where the red light had been.
He thought of a barge. A long low black barge called the Patient Grace, with a split rudder pin that Hessa had hidden under her bunk so that the other barges would leave without her. He thought of a turf barn with a broken roof. He thought of Hessa, big and red and furious, with her arms crossed, saying Slower. And Wat, with his shoulder that had been hooked, and his sapper's hands. And a baby called Robin, eleven months old, in a box by the stove. And an old man on a bunk with a blanket over him, tapping. Four. Two. Nine. Three.
Go further than hay goes. That was the last word Oren had sent her. On Tuesday. By Ellis, by mouth.
Had she gone? Had she got the message? Was she still at the barn? Or further? He did not know. Nobody knew. That was the point. That was what Marta had said, on the first night. Tell the barn to Corin, and nobody else. By mouth. Not to Moll. Not to anybody. If nobody knows, nobody can be made to tell.
And a red light, going north, under the far bank, in the dark. Signaling to somebody further up.
"It might be nothing," said Silas.
"It might."
"It might be smugglers. It might be the Nym boats. It might be anybody."
"It might."
Silas did not say anything else. He did not believe it either.
Juno had sat up too, under the blanket, beside Corin. She was staring at the dark upstream with her mouth set.
"Gran's chain," she said. "The step women. They can get a word up the river. Through the locks. The lock-keepers' wives. The bargemen's wives. The women who take in the washing at Brindle." She said it fast, low, the way she said things on the roofs. "Gran says she can get a word from Tallow Street to Brindle in a day, if it's walked hard, door to door. Faster than a horse, she says, because a horse has to stop at the barriers and a word doesn't."
"A day," said Corin.
"A day," said Juno. "If we start it tonight."
Corin looked at the black water.
A day. Tonight was Saturday. A day was Sunday night. If they started it tonight. If every woman on the chain was home, and awake, and willing. If the barriers let the word through. If Hessa was still at the third barn, and not further, and not somewhere else. If.
And the red light was already on the river. Going north. Tonight.
"Start it," he said.
And under the blanket, beside him, very quietly, Juno began to cry. Not loud. Not for long. Just a few hard dry sobs, with her fist pressed against her mouth so that nobody on the bridge would hear. And then she stopped, and wiped her face on the gray army blanket, and said in a perfectly steady voice:
"I'll tell Gran. As soon as we're back. She'll start it from the step."
Silas picked up the handles.
The barrow tipped, and the wheels began to grind, and they went down off the top of the Ministry Bridge toward the far bank, toward the river parishes, toward Saint Bride's. Corin lay back under the blanket in the dark and the carbolic.
He did not see the red light again. He looked for it, through a gap at the edge of the blanket, all the way down the slope of the bridge. It did not come back.
But he knew it was there. Out on the black water, past the last lamp, going north. And he knew that he could not go after it. He could not walk to the end of the crypt. He could not get up the church steps without two people holding him. He could not do anything, tonight, for anybody on that river, except lie on his back on a dead man's barrow and write it down.
So when they were back in the lane behind Saint Bride's, and Oren had carried him down, and he was on his bench under the skulls with the lamp on the floor, that was what he did. He opened the Ledger. And under the line about Lucian, in his square capitals, he wrote:
SATURDAY NIGHT. MINISTRY BRIDGE. A RED LIGHT ON THE RIVER, GOING NORTH, PAST THE LAST LAMP. THREE TIMES. VELL? THE CHAIN STARTED FROM MARTA'S STEP. A DAY, IF IT'S WALKED HARD.
And under that, smaller:
Hessa. Wat. Robin. Lord Thorne.
And under that, because it was all he had:
Please be further than hay goes.
The Step
Lyra had written in the Ledger while he was gone.
She gave it to him as soon as Oren had set him down on the bench. Before she asked anything. She opened it at the page where he had written about the garden, and pointed, and he saw her hand under his own, under Polly hummed three notes. Lyra's hand. Small and quick and leaning forward a little, the hand of a girl who had been taught her letters by a governess on Crown Walk and then had written lists in the back of a stolen ledger all autumn on barges and in cellars.
Ninth bell. Polly again. Three notes. Then a fourth. Then she stopped and looked at the lamp for a long time and did not do any more. Mrs. Doyle was sitting with her. Mrs. Doyle says Polly's mother sang to her. Mrs. Doyle does not know the mother's name. Nobody does. L.
And under that:
A fourth note, Corin. Write it in your book. I'm writing it in mine.
Corin read it three times. He found that he was doing it the way Lucian did, and this time he did not stop.
Then he told her.
He told her all of it, in order. Rule one. The barrow, and Harlan's voice at the foot of the Tanners' Bridge. Get them out of the cold. The Ink Quarter, and the black ice, and the frozen sheets in Old Japhet's yard. Lucian at the gate, shaking his fine fast shake, saying I hoped you were listening. The three questions. The black book held out in a bad light. Carries his four stones. The green paper, and the brown-red line, and the Crescent gas. Three hundred and twelve. Ready is the Health's word. It means that they have stopped asking for their mothers. The form for Monday, with its box for the number. If the box says none, I will copy that. Correctly. The back page of the black book. Things I had. He helped me.
And then the list.
He watched her face while he told her that part. Hale, L. Thorne, C. E., Auditor.
At her aunt's name she did not move at all. She sat on the floor under the grating with her back against the wall, in Gideon's thin jacket and her mother's gown, with her hands in Madame Hesper's gloves folded in her lap, and she did not move. But something went out of her face. The way color goes out of a cloth when it is washed too many times.
"She's on it," said Lyra.
"Yes."
"Because she held the box."
"Yes."
"And he put a circle by somebody's name."
"By Master Hale's."
Lyra was quiet.
"Not hers," she said at last.
"Not this time."
She looked down at her gloved hands. Corin knew what she was thinking. He did not need Ledger-Sight to know it. He could see it in the line of her shoulders. A woman in white silk in a white room, with five silver pins in her hair where there should have been six, and a silver cup on a table between them. As long as nobody says it out loud, he stays afraid. And she stays—
"I hate her," said Lyra, very quietly. "I've hated her for two years. Since the hall. Since your sister has done this, darling. I hated her last night with her pins and her cups and her lilies and her poor Agnes. I hated her so much I thought my teeth would break." She stopped. "And I don't want him to put a circle by her name. I don't want it. Isn't that stupid?"
"No," said Corin.
"It feels stupid."
"It isn't." He looked at her across the little vault, under the skulls. "She's your father's sister. She sat by your father's bed at Fallowfield and cried, Rowan says, and your father took his hand away. She paid a nurse to keep your father alive for two years. She stood over a dead scribe in a shell mask with a silver cup." He kept his voice low. "She's all of that. All at once. You don't have to choose which one to be afraid for."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment.
Then she nodded, once, and looked away at the wall.
And Silas, in the arch, said: "The children."
They both looked at him.
He had not sat down. He had not taken off his coat. He was standing in the arch beside Oren's crate with his hands in his pockets and the snow melting off his shoulders, the way he had stood that morning with the notice in his hand, and his long tired face in the lamplight was very calm. Calmer than Corin had seen it all week.
"The children," he said again. "Monday. The seventh bell. If the box on his form says none, then the crypt has to be empty. All of it. By Monday at dawn." He looked at Lyra. "Fifty-one children who can't walk, my lady. Who need feeding with a spoon. Who need turning in the night. In the snow. With every cart in the city being poked with hooks. Where?"
Lyra did not answer at once.
She looked down at her lap. At her gloved hands. And then she unfolded them, and reached into the left side of her mother's gray gown, into the seam at the waist, into the little list pocket that Madame Hesper had sewn there a week ago. And took out a folded paper.
It was the letter she had written to the Duke while they were gone. Corin could see her hand on the outside of it, small and quick and leaning forward. His Grace the Duke of Marrow. By Tibbs. By hand.
She did not unfold it. She held it in her lap.
"I wrote it at the ninth bell," she said. "After Polly. Before you came back. I didn't know what Lucian would say. I didn't know about the form, or the box. I only knew it was Monday at the seventh bell and there were fifty-one of them and nowhere to put them." She turned the letter over in her fingers. "And then I thought about my mother."
Nobody said anything.
"She stood in the Duke's gallery," said Lyra, "eleven years ago, in the dark, with all those glowing jars, and she said to him, Ansel, if they ever sell this house, buy it. And keep it shut. And don't let her in." Her voice was quite steady. "He told me that last night. In the carriage. On the way to Crown Walk." She looked up. "He did. He bought it. At the auction. He kept it shut. He has the only key. Nobody has been inside it for two years. Not the Watch. Not the Inquisition. Not my aunt. Nobody."
Corin looked at her.
"It's on the Hill," said Lyra. "Right on the Hill. On Crown Walk. Three streets from the Crescent. It's the last place in the whole city that anybody would look for fifty-one children from the river parishes. They never look on the Hill. They never look up." She swallowed. "It's got forty rooms. It's got a nursery at the top with a fire in it. It's got kitchens, and a laundry, and a cistern full of water under the yard that my father had dug when I was small, because he said the city water would kill us all one day. It's got beds. It's got blankets in the press on the second floor, Oren knows, he used to carry them up for the housekeeper every autumn." She looked at Oren's broad back in the arch. "Doesn't it, Oren?"
"Forty-one blankets in the long press, my lady," said Oren, without turning round. "Wool. Mrs. Burrows counted them every autumn."
"Forty-one blankets," said Lyra. "And it's empty. And it's shut. And the Duke has the key." She held up the folded letter. "So I asked him for it."
The vault was very quiet.
Out in the long hall, the children breathed. The braziers ticked. Mrs. Doyle had begun to sing again, very softly, by Polly's pallet. The song about the three lamps and the long road.
Silas took his hands out of his pockets.
He looked at Lyra for a long time, with his tired gray eyes behind his crooked spectacles. Then he looked at Corin. Then he looked out of the arch, down the long hall, at the pallets in the dark.
"Then you'll need the night," he said.
"Yes."
"Tomorrow night. All of it. From dark to dawn. With nobody watching the door."
"Yes."
"There's a young man sitting on the wall by the lych gate," said Silas, "who has been eating the same pie since the ninth bell this morning, and writing down everybody who goes in or out of that church." He took off his spectacles. He looked at them. He put them back on. "He'll be there tomorrow. Unless he's got something better to write down."
Corin understood before Lyra did.
"No," he said.
"Yes," said Silas.
"Silas—"
"They're coming for me anyway, Vane. At the sixth bell. The Inquisitor himself." His voice was very calm. "You heard him. You heard Lucian. A man who writes four letters will write a fifth. They're coming whether I'm in the crypt or on the step or halfway up the Ministry Steps with my hands in the air. So I'll be on the step." He looked out of the arch. "The front step. The big one. Under the door where they nailed the notice. In my coat, with my bag, at the sixth bell. Where the boy on the wall can see me, and the Inquisitor can see me, and everybody in the street can see me. And when they take me, they'll take me from the front. And the boy will go with them, to write it down. Because it'll be the most important thing that's happened at Saint Bride's all winter, and he won't want to miss it."
Nobody said anything.
"And then you'll have the night," said Silas. "From the back door. Amos's door. All of it."
Lyra had got up off the floor. Corin had not seen her do it. She was standing in the middle of the vault with the Duke's letter in her hand, very white.
"You could run," she said. "Tonight. Now. You could go out of Amos's door and be across the river before morning. Abner could take you north. Mother Grue could hide you on the Saltmarsh. You could—"
"I could," said Silas. "And then who goes out on the step?"
She did not answer.
"I said I wouldn't let them go," said Silas gently. "On Friday. I said it to Vane, and I said it to myself, and I said it to Ned, in the next vault, with his tin soldier on him. I said I didn't care what it cost me." He looked down at his long hands. "I find I meant it. It's a strange thing, my lady. I've never been sure I meant anything in my life before. I'm sure of this."
He put his hands back in his pockets.
"Send your letter," he said. "Get me my key. And let me go and sit with Polly for a bit. If she's going to do a fifth note tonight, I'd like to hear it."
And he turned round in the arch, and went out into the long hall, tall and thin and stooping under the low stone ceiling, toward the braziers, and Mrs. Doyle's singing, and the pallet where a girl of six lay with her eyes open, watching the lamp.
Corin sat on his bench under the skulls and watched him go.
Then he opened the Ledger. To the back. To THOSE I OWE. And under LUCIAN, in his square capitals, he wrote:
DOCTOR SILAS. THE FRONT STEP. THE SIXTH BELL.
And he did not write anything after it. Because it had not happened yet. And because, for once, he did not know what he would write when it had.
