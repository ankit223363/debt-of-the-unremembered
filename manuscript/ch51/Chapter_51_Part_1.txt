Chapter 51: Three Times Over, Part 1 of 5: The Barrow
The Barrow
It was Silas who thought of the barrow.
They were sitting in the side vault at the sixth bell, all of them who could sit, with the lamp on the floor between them and the skulls looking down. Corin on his bench with the Ledger open on his knee. Lyra on the floor under the grating. Oren on his crate in the arch, with his back to them and his face to the long hall, listening the way he always listened. Gideon by the blue chalk shapes, with his knees drawn up and his new carved barge in his hands, turning it over and over. Juno on her pallet against the wall with her wrapped feet up on a folded blanket and her face turned to the ceiling, very quiet, the way she had been quiet ever since Silas looked at her toes.
And Silas himself, standing, because he could never sit for long. He walked up and down the little vault, three long steps one way and three long steps back, with his hands behind him and his head bent under the low round ceiling, like a heron in a box.
"The eighth bell," he said, for the third time. "The Ink Quarter. The long way. Past the printers."
"Yes," said Corin.
"And you can't walk there."
"No."
"And Juno can't walk there."
Juno did not say anything. She looked at the ceiling.
"And Gideon can't go, because every porter and watchman and kitchen boy in the Bourse knows his face. And Lyra can't go, because after last night every lady on the Hill knows hers, and so does the Inquisitor. And Oren can't go, because Oren has one arm, and a sword the Watch would like very much to see, and a face the Watch would like very much to hang." Silas stopped walking. "And I can't go, because I'm to be collected tomorrow evening and they'll be watching me already."
"Will they?" said Corin.
Silas looked at him over his crooked spectacles.
"Wouldn't you?"
Corin did not answer that.
"So," said Silas. "Nobody can go. Good. That's settled." He began to walk again. Three steps. Three steps. And then, in the middle of the third step back, he stopped. Quite still. With one long foot in the air.
"Except the dead," he said.
They all looked at him.
"The dead go everywhere in this city," said Silas slowly. He put his foot down. "Did you know that? Nobody stops them. Nobody asks them where they're going. A dead man can go across the river at midnight and up through the Ink Quarter and past every Watch house and every drum boy and every barrier in Veridia, and nobody so much as lifts the blanket, as long as there's a card on the barrow that says what he died of." He looked down at Corin. "You did it on Friday. At the Fish Stairs. You were dead on Friday, Vane, in front of two hundred people and the Under-Secretary of the Office of Public Health, and he looked you in the face and gave you a sweet."
"I remember."
"I'm the parish doctor of Saint Bride's," said Silas. "I push that barrow every night. Every night of this winter I've pushed it, to the dead-house behind the Fish Hall, or to the parish ground, or to the Health's own cart at the Tanners' Bridge. Every Watch constable between here and the Ministry Steps knows me pushing it. They don't even look at me now. They look at the card, and they step back, and they hold their breath." He took off his spectacles and wiped them on the end of his waistcoat, and put them back on, crooked. "They're watching me, yes. Let them watch me. Let them watch me do what I do every night. Push the dead."
Nobody said anything for a moment.
Then Juno, on her pallet, said to the ceiling, very quietly, "Two dead."
Silas looked at her.
"Two dead," said Juno. She turned her head on the blanket and looked at him. Her sharp face was very white. "There's room. On the barrow. A big man and a small girl. It's a big barrow. I've seen it." She swallowed. "I can't walk. Fine. You said so. But I can lie down. I'm good at lying down. And I know the Ink Quarter. Every yard. Every arch. Every printers' alley where a man who counts things would go the long way. Corin doesn't. He knows the streets. He doesn't know the yards."
"Juno—" said Lyra.
"He doesn't," said Juno. Her voice went up. "He doesn't. I grew up on those roofs. I know which yard has a gate that shuts and which one has a gate that doesn't. I know where you can stand in the dark and not be seen from the street. I know where the presses are going late tonight, because it's two days to Midwinter and every printer in the Quarter is doing almanacs and song sheets and they'll be at it all night with the doors open, and the noise—" She stopped. She took a breath. "The noise is good. The noise is very good. Nobody hears anything in the Ink Quarter when the presses are going. You could shout. You could scream. Nobody would come."
There was a little silence in the vault.
"Who's going to scream?" said Gideon, from the floor.
Juno did not answer.
Corin looked at her. At her white face on the blanket, and her wrapped feet up on the fold, and her sharp chin set. He knew that look. It was the look she wore on the roofs before she jumped a gap that she should not jump. He had seen it on the roofs in the autumn, the first night she caught him. He had seen it at the top of the Fish Stairs.
He thought of her two toes, purple-black, like grapes left too long on the vine. After Midwinter. Then somebody else will do it.
"Rule one," he said to her quietly. "Why do you want to go?"
Juno looked back at him.
"Because I pulled forty sacks today," she said, "and it cost me two toes, and I want it to be for something." Her chin wobbled. She made it stop. "That's why."
Corin did not say anything for a long moment.
Then he looked at Silas.
"Two dead," he said.
Silas looked at Juno. Then at Corin. Then at the low ceiling of the vault, as if he were asking the skulls on the shelf what they thought. Brother Anselm. Brother Hugh. Brother Dunstan. The one at the end with no name, only a little painted cross.
The skulls did not say anything. They never did.
"Two dead," said Silas at last, heavily. "Saints help me. Two dead."
Lyra got up off the floor.
She had not said anything else. She went to the end of the bench where Corin's coat lay folded, the old long gray coat with the deep right pocket, and picked it up, and shook it out, and held it for him. And while he was getting his arms into it, sitting on the bench, with Oren's good hand steadying his shoulder from behind, she said very low, so that only he could hear:
"I want to come."
"I know."
"I'm not going to."
"I know."
"Somebody has to write to the Duke. Tonight. About tomorrow. Marta can get it to Tibbs by morning." She pulled the coat straight on his shoulders. Her hands stayed there a moment. "And somebody has to stay with them." She did not look out of the arch at the long hall. She did not need to. "Polly hummed. Twice. Somebody has to be here in case she does it again."
Corin put his gray hand over hers on his shoulder.
"Then you'll hear it," he said.
"Then I'll write it down for you," said Lyra. "In your Ledger. In my hand. And you'll read it when you come back." She took her hand away. "So come back."
"Rule one," said Corin.
"Rule one," said Lyra, and went to fetch the card.
Fever
The barrow was waiting in the lane behind the church, where Abner's cart had waited that afternoon.
It was a long low handcart with two big wheels and two long handles, made of plain gray wood gone soft and silver with weather, with a flat bed of boards and low sides, like a coffin with no lid. There was a rope coiled at the front end. There was a gray blanket folded at the back end, old army wool, heavy and coarse and smelling of carbolic, so strong that it made Corin's eyes water. And nailed to a stick at the front, standing up where everyone could see it, there was the card.
It was a square of white board, painted with black letters by somebody who was used to painting signs. Doctor Silas had had it made in the autumn, when the fever started, at a sign-painter's on the Saltmarsh road, and he had paid for it himself.
FEVER.
KEEP BACK.
BY ORDER OF THE PARISH DOCTOR, SAINT BRIDE'S.
Oren carried Corin out to it. One arm. Ready, sir. The way he had that afternoon, with his face going dark red and the cords standing out in his neck and no sound at all. He laid Corin down on the boards of the barrow on his back, carefully, with the bad leg in its splints straight out along the bed, and the crutch beside it, and the Ledger inside his coat over his heart where he always kept it.
Then he lifted Juno in beside him.
She did not weigh anything. She was a bundle of shawls and a boy's jacket and a sharp white face. Oren set her down on the boards next to Corin, on his left side, small and stiff, with her wrapped feet next to his good ankle. And then Silas shook out the gray army blanket and laid it over the two of them, all the way up, over their faces, and tucked it in at the sides.
The world went dark and close and smelled of carbolic.
"Breathe slow," said Silas's voice from somewhere above. "Dead people breathe slow. If anybody lifts the corner, you've been dead since the fourth bell and you're not happy about it."
"Nobody will lift it," said Juno's voice, very small, beside Corin's ear.
"Nobody ever has." A pause. "Yet."
The barrow tipped. Silas had picked up the handles. Corin felt his head go down and his feet go up, a little, and then level again. And then the big wheels began to turn under him on the frozen ruts of the lane, with a sound like a slow grinding of teeth.
He lay in the dark and listened to the city.
It was strange, seeing it that way. Not seeing it. He had lived in this city all the life he could remember, and walked every street of it, and climbed over half its roofs, and he had never once gone through it like this, on his back, under a blanket, blind. It came to him only as sounds and smells and the feel of the road under the wheels. And he found, lying there, that he knew where they were by those alone. Better than he would have thought.
The ruts of the lane. Then cobbles, big and round and uneven, the old cobbles of the river streets that had been laid when his grandfather's grandfather was young. The barrow jolted on them, and his bad leg jolted with it, and he set his teeth. The smell of the river came up, cold and brown and full of weed, and the sound of it, a long low slapping of water on stone. They were going along the wharves. A dog barked from a doorway and somebody shushed it. A woman was singing somewhere above them, behind a window, a Midwinter song, the one about the three lamps and the long road, that every child in the river parishes knew. She had a thin sweet cracked voice, and she kept forgetting the words in the second verse and humming instead.
Then the wheels went hollow, and loud, and the whole barrow began to climb.
The Tanners' Bridge. Up the hump of it. Corin felt himself tip back, feet down now, and Silas's breathing above him got harder. Over the top. Down the other side, faster, with Silas leaning back on the handles to hold it.
And at the bottom of the bridge, at the far end, a voice called out.
"Hold there!"
The wheels stopped.
Corin did not breathe at all.
Beside him, under the blanket, he felt Juno go rigid. Her elbow was against his ribs. He could feel her heart going through it, very fast, like a bird's.
Boots on the cobbles. Two pairs. Coming close. A smell of hot iron and coal smoke and wet wool. A brazier, somewhere near, crackling. And then a different voice. Young. A boy's voice, high, trying to be deep.
"Load?"
Corin knew that voice. He had heard it that afternoon, at the bottom of Exchange Street, when a small drum boy with a red face stepped out in front of Abner's brown mare and held up his hand. Load? Where to?
The same boy. A different barrier. They moved them about, he supposed. Or the boy had been on duty all day, and all night, in the cold, with his hooks and his black cap and his little square box on his hip.
"Fever," said Silas's voice above the barrow. Tired. Flat. The voice of a man who has said the same word a hundred times this winter. "Two. A man off the Saltmarsh boats and a girl from the rope-walk. Since the fourth bell. For the dead-house behind the Fish Hall."
There was a pause.
"The card says keep back," said Silas. "I'd keep back, son. If I were you."
Another pause.
Then Corin heard the boy breathe in. A small sharp breath, through the nose. And hold it.
He was holding his breath. The way everybody did when they came near the fever barrow. As if the fever could get in through the air. As if it were a smell.
Footsteps. The second pair of boots, the heavier ones, coming round the side of the barrow. Corin felt the boards shift very slightly as somebody leaned on the side. A man's voice, low, older, rough.
"You want a hook in it, sir?"
He was not talking to Silas. He was talking to someone else. Someone further off. By the brazier, perhaps.
And from further off, from by the brazier, another voice answered.
Corin knew this one too. He had never heard it. But he knew it. He knew it from Oren's face, on Tuesday, in the arches, telling him about the Lantern Bridge. Taller than me. Our mother's face. No beard. A little white scar in the left eyebrow. He knew it the way you know a song you have only heard described.
It was a calm voice. A deep one. Very tired.
"No," it said. "Let the doctor through."
"Sir."
The boots went away from the side of the barrow. The boards shifted back.
"Doctor," said the calm deep tired voice, a little closer now. "How many tonight?"
Silas did not answer at once. When he did, his voice was careful.
"These two, Captain."
"Commander," said the voice. Not sharply. As if it did not matter much. "How many this week?"
Another pause.
"Nine," said Silas. "Counting these."
The voice did not say anything for a while. Corin lay under the blanket in the dark and the carbolic and listened to the brazier crackling, and the river slapping under the bridge, and Juno's heart going like a bird against his ribs. And Harlan Vance standing by a fever barrow at the foot of the Tanners' Bridge in the snow, not saying anything.
"Go on, then," said Harlan at last. "Get them out of the cold."
It was a strange thing to say about the dead.
Silas did not say anything about it. He just lifted the handles. And the barrow tipped, and the wheels began to turn again on the cobbles, with their slow grinding, and the brazier crackled away behind them, and the voices.
They went on for a long time before Juno breathed out.
"That was him," she whispered. Her mouth was right by his ear under the blanket. "Wasn't it? Oren's brother."
"I think so."
"He didn't look."
"No."
"Why didn't he look?"
Corin lay in the dark and thought about that. About a tired man by a brazier at the end of a long day, who had marched four hundred men into the city and held every lock from the Saltgate to Long Pond, and put drum boys on every corner with twelve-foot hooks. Who had stood on the Lantern Bridge on Tuesday and told his own brother, Never do that in this city. And then tapped Heard with one finger, hidden, where nobody could see.
Get them out of the cold.
"I don't know," said Corin. "Maybe he didn't want to know what was under it."
The barrow turned a corner. The cobbles changed. Smaller now, and flatter, and slick with something. And a new smell came up through the carbolic, very strong, black and oily and bitter, a smell Corin knew better than almost any smell in the world. A smell he had walked through every morning for years, on his way to the Ministry, before the autumn.
Ink.
And under it, through the boards of the barrow, through the wheels, through the road itself, a sound. A deep slow thudding. Like a heart. Like a great many hearts, all beating, all out of time with each other. Thud. Thud-thud. Thud.
The presses.
"We're in," said Juno.
The Ink Quarter
Silas lifted the corner of the blanket off their faces in a dark alley behind a paper warehouse, and Corin saw the Ink Quarter for the first time since the autumn.
It was night. But it was not dark. That was the first thing. The Ink Quarter was never dark, two days before Midwinter. It was the busiest week of the printers' year. Every press in the Quarter was running, and every printing house had its doors open to let the heat out, and its lamps lit, and the light came out of the doors and the windows and the cracks in the shutters in long yellow bars, and lay across the snow in the lanes, and up the black walls opposite.
The lanes were narrow. Narrower than anywhere else in the city. The houses leaned over them on both sides, tall and thin and old, five floors and six, with their upper storeys built out over the lower ones so that at the top they nearly touched, and only a crack of sky showed between, black, with no stars. Every house was a printing house, or a binder's, or a paper-seller's, or a type-founder's, or an ink-maker's. The signs hung out over the lanes on iron arms, swinging a little, creaking. Corin knew most of them by heart. A black hand holding a quill. A golden book. A pair of crossed keys, for the Keys Press, which did the Ministry's forms. A great black bottle with a white label, for Sallow's Inks, which had a shop at the bottom of every lane in the Quarter and made the ink that every clerk in Veridia wrote with.
And the snow in the lanes was black.
Not all of it. But most of it. The printers threw their wash-water out of the doors, the water they cleaned the type in and the rollers and the ink pans, and it ran down the lanes in the gutters and spread out over the snow and froze, so that the whole of the Ink Quarter was paved with black ice, shining in the lamplight like a road made of glass. Corin had slipped on it every winter he could remember. Everybody slipped on it. There was a saying in the Quarter, that you could tell a man who had worked there ten years by his bruises.
And over all of it, under all of it, through all of it, the sound.
The presses.
Corin had forgotten how loud they were, at Midwinter. A hundred of them. More. Big flat-bed presses with iron frames as tall as a man, and little hand presses on tables, and the new rolling presses that the Keys Press had brought in from the north last year and nobody else could afford. Each one going thud, and then creak, and then thud, as the men pulled the great iron bars and the platens came down on the paper and went up again. Hundreds of them, all at once, all out of time. Thud. Thud-thud. Creak. Thud. The sound came up through the cobbles into your feet. It came through the walls. It got inside your chest and beat there with your heart, until you could not tell which was which.
Juno had been right. You could have screamed in the Ink Quarter, two nights before Midwinter, and nobody would have come.
And the smell. The ink first, black and oily and bitter, the good ink and the cheap ink, soot and oil and something sharp under it like vinegar. Then hot metal, from the type-founders, where they poured the lead into the little molds. Then wet paper, a soft gray smell, like rain on cloth. Then lamp oil. Then men working hard in hot rooms. Then, from somewhere, very faint, chestnuts roasting on a brazier, and onions frying, and beer.
The printers were making the Midwinter sheets. Corin could see them through the open doors as Silas pushed the barrow along the lanes. Men in leather aprons and shirt-sleeves, with their arms black to the elbows, working the bars of the presses. Boys running between them with stacks of white paper. Girls at long tables folding the printed sheets, fast, fast, without looking, the way Lyra's hands folded things. And everywhere, hanging up to dry on strings stretched from wall to wall across the rooms, and out across the lanes from window to window over their heads, hundreds and hundreds of printed sheets, like washing. Song sheets. Almanacs. Broadsides. Midwinter cards, with pictures of three lamps on them, and a long road, and a star. They hung on their strings in the cold air with the ink still wet, and the snow drifted down between them and made little gray spots where it landed.
And on every wall of every lane, pasted up over each other, layer on layer, the notices.
NOTICE OF REMOVAL.
WANTED.
BY ORDER OF THE OFFICE OF PUBLIC HEALTH.
Sweets will be given.
Corin looked away from them.
He was sitting up on the barrow now, with his back against the front board and his bad leg out along the bed and the gray blanket round his shoulders like a cloak. Juno was sitting beside him, small, with her knees drawn up and her wrapped feet tucked under the blanket. Silas had pulled the barrow into the mouth of an alley and put down the handles and was leaning on the wall, breathing hard, with his spectacles misted over from the cold.
"Where are we?" said Corin.
"Bell Alley," said Juno at once. "Behind Sallow's big warehouse. Gutter Lane's the next one over." She pointed with her chin. "The Inkwell's down that end."
Corin looked down the lane where she pointed.
He could not see the Inkwell from here. But he knew where it was. A printing house with a faded painted sign along its front, and behind it the dead end of Gutter Lane, where everybody threw what they did not want to pay to have taken away. He had stood there in the autumn, on the first morning. The morning everything began. He had looked down at a man lying on that heap of rubbish in the gray rain, with his eyes open, and nothing at all behind his eyes. Clean as a Slate. Never seen the like, the Watch constable had said.
Pell Dorran. Scribe, grade three. Who ate bread and dripping on the boiler-room steps every day so he could save his money in a sock in his boot for a sister who was twelve years old.
Death by misadventure, the form had said. Being in drink.
And now Corin knew the rest. Lyra had told him last night. He had seen the white shell mask bending over that man every time he shut his eyes for three months, and now he knew whose face had been behind it. There were things in that scribe's head about your sister, darling. And I could not let anybody else have them.
He looked away from Gutter Lane.
The Ministry bell began to ring.
It came across the river from the far bank, from the great bell tower on the Ministry Steps, slow and deep and enormous. The bell that every other bell in the city rang by. The bell that old Amos at Saint Bride's listened for. Corin felt it before he heard it, through the noise of the presses, a long low shudder in the air like a door being shut very far away. And then he heard it.
One.
Two.
He counted. He could not help it. Lyra had given him that, like a cold.
Six. Seven.
Eight.
The eighth bell.
The copying room until the eighth. Home by the Ink Quarter. The long way. Past the printers.
"He'll be leaving now," said Corin.
"Then he'll be here in half an hour," said Juno. "If he walks like he walks on a stair. He'll come over the Ministry Bridge, and down Quill Row, and he won't go along Gutter Lane because that's the short way and he said the long way." She was frowning, working it out, the way she worked out roofs. "The long way from Quill Row to anywhere is round by the Pressmen's Yard. Through the arch, and across, and out the other side by the Keys Press, and up Sallow Steps. Nobody goes that way unless they want to go the long way. It's dark. It's got a gate that doesn't shut. And there's a press in there that's been broken for three years and nobody's fixed it, so it's the only yard in the Quarter that's quiet." She looked at Corin. "If I wanted to be found by somebody, and I didn't want anybody else to see me found, that's where I'd go."
Corin looked at her.
"You think he wants to be found?"
Juno looked back at him with her sharp white face.
"He told a stair where he was going," she said. "Three times. Out loud. With you sitting on it." She pulled the blanket tighter round her knees. "Nobody tells a stair anything by accident, Corin. Not a man like that."
Silas, against the wall, took off his misted spectacles and wiped them on his waistcoat and put them back on.
"The Pressmen's Yard," he said heavily, and picked up the handles.
