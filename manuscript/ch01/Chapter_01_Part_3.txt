Chapter 1: The Scribe in the Ink Quarter, Part 3 of 5: Send a Harvester
The Ministry of Records
The Ministry of Records stood at the top of a hill in the middle of Veridia, and it had been built to make you feel small.
Corin felt small. He stood at the bottom of the great stone stairs with the two coats over his arm and the long wooden case under it, and tipped his head back, and looked up, and up, and up.
It was black. That was the first thing. Not painted black. Built black, out of a dark gray stone that had gone darker still with a century of coal smoke, until now it was the color of a wet slate roof at night. It was enormous. It filled the whole top of the hill, and its walls went straight up from the stairs for six stories without a single window, smooth and blank and sheer, like the side of a cliff. Above that, the windows began. Tall, narrow, pointed windows, hundreds of them, in long rows, with stone frames carved into the shapes of leaves and vines and twisting ribbons. Above the windows there were towers. Dozens of them, thin and sharp and spiked, rising up into the low gray sky like the points of a crown, or the spines on the back of some great black animal. Iron weathervanes stood on top of the tallest ones, turning slowly in the smoky wind.
And over the great doors at the top of the stairs, carved into the stone so big that each letter was taller than a man, were the words:
WHAT IS WRITTEN IS TRUE.
Above the words, the sign. The open book. And above the book, the eye. Closed. Carved in stone, three times the height of a house, its long stone lashes resting on its long stone cheek. It looked, Corin thought, like the eye of something sleeping. Or something pretending to sleep.
There were people on the stairs. Hundreds of them. Clerks, mostly, in long gray coats like his, with gray caps and leather satchels and ink on their fingers, all climbing toward the great doors, all in a hurry, none of them talking. The stairs were so wide that they all fit easily. They flowed upward like a gray river running the wrong way.
Corin joined them.
At the top, the great doors were open. They were made of black iron, studded with bolts as big as fists, and each one was as tall as three men and as thick as a man's arm was long. Two guards stood on either side of them in the black uniforms of the Ministry Watch, with silver buttons and tall black helmets, and long rods of polished black wood in their hands with silver caps on the ends. They did not look at the clerks streaming past them. They looked straight ahead, at nothing.
Inside, there was the Hall.
The Hall
Corin had been in it every working day for years. He did not remember it. And every morning, it took his breath away.
It was one room. One single room, so big that its far end was lost in a blue haze, like the far end of a valley on a misty morning. The ceiling was so high that he could not see it clearly. It was somewhere up there, in the dimness, a great curved vault of black stone ribs, like the inside of a whale. From it hung chains. Long iron chains, hundreds of feet long, and on the end of each chain, a lamp. Not a small one, like the streetlamps. A great cluster of gemstones as big as a cartwheel, a dozen stones in an iron frame, all blazing with cold blue light. There were fifty of them, perhaps more, hanging in long rows down the length of the Hall, and their light fell on everything below like the light of a winter moon.
And below them, there were desks.
Thousands of desks. Rows and rows and rows of them, all exactly the same, all made of dark wood, all with a tall wooden back and a sloping top and a little shelf for ink. They filled the whole floor of the Hall, from wall to wall, from the doors to the blue haze at the far end, in perfect straight lines like the furrows of a plowed field. And at every desk, a clerk. Sitting very straight, with a pen in hand, writing.
The sound was the strangest thing. Ten thousand pens, scratching on ten thousand sheets of paper, all at once. It was a soft sound, and a huge one. It filled the Hall from floor to ceiling. It sounded like rain on a roof. Like insects in summer grass. Like a great sea of sand being poured slowly from one place to another.
Scritch. Scritch. Scritch.
And over the scratching, another sound. A hiss. A thump. A whoosh. Corin looked up.
Above the desks, at the height of a tall man's raised hand, there were pipes. Brass pipes, as thick as a man's arm, polished and gleaming in the blue light. They ran in long straight lines above every row of desks, and then turned, and climbed the walls, and crossed the Hall in great curving loops and bridges, and disappeared into holes in the ceiling. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Like the veins of a giant. And every few seconds, somewhere in the Hall, one of them would whoosh, and thump, and a little brass cylinder would drop out of a hatch onto a desk, and the clerk at that desk would open it without looking up and take out a roll of paper and begin to copy it.
The pneumatic post. Messages flying through the pipes on blasts of air, from one end of the Ministry to the other, from one end of the city to the other, faster than any runner.
Corin stood just inside the great doors for a moment, as he always did, and let the scratching wash over him.
Then he turned left, away from the desks, toward a small plain door in the wall. It was painted gray, like the stone, so that you almost could not see it. Above it, a small brass plate said: LOWER LEVELS. AUTHORIZED STAFF ONLY.
He opened it, and went down.
The Third Basement
The stairs went down a long way.
They were stone, spiral, very narrow, lit by small gemstone lamps in cages every twenty steps. With every turn of the spiral, the air got colder, and damper, and the scratching of the pens above faded away, until there was nothing to hear but his own boots on the stone and the drip of water somewhere in the walls.
The First Basement was the Copying Hall, where the clerks who were not good enough for the Great Hall copied out tax records by hand. He could hear their pens through the door as he passed. The Second Basement was Storage, and smelled of mouse droppings and mildew. The Third Basement had a door of black iron with no handle and a small square window of thick, cloudy glass, and a brass plate:
HARVESTING SECTION. DANGER. DO NOT ENTER WITHOUT A HARVESTER PRESENT.
Corin knocked.
There was a long pause. Then a scuffling sound, and a small cough, and a bolt sliding back, and another bolt, and the door opened about three inches, and an eye looked out at him through the gap. A pale brown eye, very wide, behind a round lens of thick glass.
"Oh," said the eye. "Oh, thank the saints. It's you. Is it you?"
"I think so," said Corin.
The door swung open the rest of the way.
Toby
The young man in the doorway was perhaps twenty-two, and he looked like a frightened rabbit that had been dressed up as a clerk.
He was thin and narrow-shouldered, with a long neck and big ears and a pointed nose. His hair was sandy brown and very short, and stuck up at the back in a tuft that no amount of water could flatten. He wore the gray coat and gray cap of a junior clerk, both too big for him, and a pair of round wire spectacles with lenses so thick they made his eyes look twice their real size. There was ink on his fingers, ink on his cuffs, ink on his chin, and a smear of ink on the end of his pointed nose, as if he had rubbed it while thinking. He was holding a brass cylinder from the pneumatic post in both hands, clutched against his chest like a hot water bottle.
"You're late," he said.
"Everyone says that," said Corin.
"Because you are. Every day. It's the seventh bell and a quarter. You're supposed to be here at the seventh bell." The young man looked at him anxiously through his thick spectacles. "Do you know who I am?"
Corin looked at the second page of the Ledger in his mind. Your clerk is Toby. He is young and nervous. He is on your side.
"Toby," he said.
Toby's whole thin face lit up. It was a startling thing to see. As if someone had opened a shutter in a dark room.
"That's right! That's me. Toby. Junior Clerk, Harvesting Section, Third Basement." He beamed. Then the beam faded a little. "Did you read it?"
"Yes."
"I thought so. You never remember on your own." He sighed. "I keep hoping."
He stood aside to let Corin in.
The Harvesting Section was one long, low room, cut out of the rock under the hill. The ceiling was curved and close, so that Corin had to duck under the iron lamp-cages that hung from it. The walls were bare stone, damp and dark, with little white crusts of salt where the water had seeped through. And along both walls, from floor to ceiling, there were shelves of jars.
Thousands of them. Glass jars with brass lids, the same as the ones on the shelf in his room, but many more, in every size. Most of them were empty. Some were not. In some, there was a faint blue-white mist, turning slowly behind the glass, like breath on a cold window. Each of those jars had a paper label, written in small careful handwriting. A name. A date. A word. Unstable. Volatile. Stable. Do not open.
At the far end of the room, under a single large gemstone lamp, there were two desks. One was huge and old and scarred and covered in a landslide of papers, folders, books, empty jars, dirty teacups, and a half-eaten apple. The other was small and neat and tidy, with every pen in a straight line and every paper squared at the corners, and a little potted plant on one corner that was very clearly dying.
"Yours is the big one," said Toby. "Obviously."
"Obviously," said Corin.
He went and sat down at the big desk. The chair creaked under him. It fit him exactly, the way an old shoe fits. He put the two coats over the back of it and laid the long wooden case across the papers in front of him, and the papers slid, and a teacup fell off the edge and broke on the stone floor.
Toby winced. "I'll get it."
"Leave it."
"It was the last cup. They've cut the crockery allowance again. Did you know that? Again. That's three times this year. First they cut the paper allowance, then the ink allowance, then the candle allowance, and now cups. Next they'll cut the chairs. We'll be sitting on the floor. Filing reports on the floor, in the dark, with no ink, and drinking our tea out of our hands." Toby crouched down and began picking up the pieces of the cup. "And they still want everything in triplicate."
Corin found that he was smiling a little. He did not know why. There was something comforting about Toby's complaining. Like the sound of rain on a roof when you are inside and dry.
"What's in the tube?" he said.
Toby stopped picking up pieces. He looked down at the brass cylinder, still tucked under one arm. And his face changed. All the fussy, anxious, rabbit-like busyness went out of it, and what was left was very young and very pale.
"It came twenty minutes ago," he said. "Red seal. Urgent. From the Watch." He swallowed. His big ears had gone pink. "Inspector. It's Pell."
"Who's Pell?"
"Pell Dorran." Toby stood up. He held out the brass cylinder. His hand was shaking. "He works upstairs. In the First Basement. A copying scribe. Grade Three. He's—we used to—" He stopped. Started again. "We have our lunch together. Most days. On the steps by the boiler room, because it's warm. He's got a terrible laugh. Like a donkey. He laughs at everything. He showed me how to sharpen a nib properly, with a little knife, when I first came, because nobody else would. He—"
He stopped again.
"They found him this morning," he said, very quietly. "In the Ink Quarter. In an alley. He's dead."
The gemstone lamp hummed overhead. Somewhere in the walls, water dripped.
Corin took the cylinder from Toby's shaking hand. It was cold. There was a twist of red wax on one end, already broken. He unscrewed the cap and tipped out the roll of paper inside and smoothed it flat on the landslide of his desk.
It was a printed form, with blanks filled in by hand, in the hurried scrawl of a Watch sergeant.
CITY WATCH. INK QUARTER STATION. REPORT OF DEATH.
Name of deceased: DORRAN, PELL. Scribe, Grade Three, Ministry of Records.
Place: Gutter Lane, behind the Inkwell printing house, off Needle Street.
Found by: a rag-picker, name of Wick, at the fifth bell.
Cause of death: And here the sergeant had stopped filling in the blank and had written instead, in larger letters, pressing hard: SKULL BROKEN. BUT THAT'S NOT IT. HIS HEAD IS EMPTY. NOTHING IN IT. CLEAN AS A SLATE. NEVER SEEN THE LIKE.
And at the bottom, in the space for Action required, underlined twice:
SEND A HARVESTER.
The Kit
Corin read the form three times. Then he rolled it up and put it back in its cylinder and screwed the cap on, slowly.
"Clean as a slate," he said.
"That's what it says." Toby's voice was thin. "What does it mean? It can't mean—it can't mean he's a Slate. Pell's not a Slate. Pell was fine yesterday. He was fine. He was laughing about the pigeon pie."
"It doesn't mean he was a Slate." Corin stood up. "A Slate is someone who's sold too much. Bit by bit. Over years. Their mind wears down like a step wears down. But they're alive. Their heads still work. There's just nothing in them." He opened the long wooden case on his desk. "This is different. Never seen the like. Somebody went into his head and took everything out of it all at once. Fast. And then broke his skull so nobody would look too closely at what was missing."
Toby stared at him. "How do you know that?"
Corin paused with his hand on the lid of the case. He did not know how he knew. The words had come out of his mouth by themselves, smooth and sure, like a lesson learned so long ago that it had worn a groove in him.
"I just do," he said.
He opened the case.
Inside, on dark blue velvet that had faded almost gray along the folds, there was a rod.
It was silver. Slim, about as long as his forearm, and no thicker than a pencil at its narrow end, swelling to the thickness of his thumb at the other, where it fit his hand. Along its tip, a pattern had been carved, a spiral of fine lines winding round and round the silver like the thread of a screw, or the inside of a seashell. The silver had gone dark in the grooves of the spiral, almost black, the way old silver does. But the rest of it was bright, polished smooth by years of a hand holding it.
Near the thick end, just above where his thumb would rest, there was a word cut into the metal. It had been worn very smooth, but in the light of the lamp he could just read it.
MALAKOR.
Corin did not know what the word meant. He looked at it for a moment, the way he had looked at the empty space on the plaque on Lantern Bridge. Then he picked up the rod.
It was cold. Colder than the room. A cold that went straight into the bones of his hand and up his wrist. And as it did, something happened. Very faint. So faint that Corin was not sure, afterward, that it had happened at all.
A whisper.
Not in his ears. Somewhere behind his eyes. A dry, thin, rustling whisper, like old paper being turned in an empty room. No words. Just a feeling of something waking up, and stretching, and turning its attention toward him with a lazy, mocking interest.
Then it was gone.
Corin looked down at the rod in his hand. It was just a rod. Silver. Cold.
"Inspector?" said Toby. "Are you all right? You've gone a funny color."
"I'm fine." He put the rod in the inside pocket of his coat, the one on the right, opposite the Ledger. It fit there exactly, in a narrow pocket that must have been sewn for it. "Jars."
Toby jumped. "Jars. Yes. How many?"
"Three. Clean ones. Small."
Toby scurried along the shelves, reaching up, standing on his toes, muttering. He came back with three small glass jars with brass lids, each no taller than a man's hand. He had wiped them with his sleeve. He held them out, and Corin put them, one by one, into the deep pockets of his coat, where they clinked softly against each other.
Then he took a pair of gloves from a drawer of the big desk. Thin gloves, of soft gray leather, very old, with the fingertips worn shiny. And a small notebook with a pencil tied to it on a string. And a little brass case, round and flat, like a snuffbox. He opened it. Inside, packed in cotton wool, there were six small glass beads, each one filled with something faintly blue. He looked at them for a long moment. Then he closed the case and put it in his waistcoat pocket.
"What are those?" said Toby.
"I don't know," said Corin honestly. "But I keep them in the drawer next to the gloves, so I suppose I take them."
He put on his coat. The old gray one. He looked at the black one, the good one, still hanging over the back of his chair, and decided to leave it there. He would only lose it again.
"Right," he said. "Come on."
Toby went white.
No
"No," said Toby.
"No?"
"No. No, no. I don't do field work. I'm a clerk. Junior clerk. It says so on my door." He pointed at the black iron door, which said nothing of the kind. "I file things. I copy things. I write things in triplicate. I don't go to alleys. I don't look at—I don't—" He swallowed hard. "I don't stand next to you when you open the jars."
"Why not?"
"Because you change." Toby's voice cracked. "When you breathe it in. The Ash. Your face goes wrong. Your voice goes wrong. Last time, you spoke in a woman's voice for an hour and cried about a dog called Biscuit. The time before that, you couldn't remember how to use a spoon and you were very rude to me in a language I didn't know. And the time before that—" He stopped. He looked at the floor. "The time before that, you didn't know who you were for most of a day. You sat on the floor in the corner over there and you kept saying a name. Somebody else's name. Over and over. And you looked at me like you'd never seen me in your life. And I had to read you the first page of your book eleven times before you stopped."
The room was very quiet.
Corin looked at the corner Toby had pointed to. A corner like any other. Damp stone. A little crust of white salt. Nothing there at all.
He did not remember it. He did not remember any of it.
"Eleven times," he said.
"Eleven." Toby pushed his spectacles up his nose with one inky finger. His eyes were very bright behind the thick lenses. "I don't want to do that again, Inspector. Please. I'm not brave. I'm not like you."
Corin looked at the thin, frightened young man for a long moment.
Then he said, quietly, "Pell was your friend."
Toby's face crumpled. Just for a second. Then he pressed his lips together hard, and it un-crumpled, and he nodded.
"Then you should be there," said Corin. "Not for me. For him. Someone who knew him should be there. Someone who can tell me if anything is wrong. If anything is missing. If anything is there that shouldn't be." He paused. "I won't know. I never knew him. And I won't remember him tomorrow. You will."
Toby looked at him. Then at the black iron door. Then down at his own inky hands.
"You won't open the jar," he said, "while I'm standing next to you."
"I'll tell you before I do. You can stand at the end of the alley."
"With my back turned."
"With your back turned."
Toby let out a breath that seemed to go on for a very long time. Then he took off his cap, and smoothed down the tuft of hair at the back of his head with his hand, which did not work, and put the cap on again.
"I'll get my coat," he said.
