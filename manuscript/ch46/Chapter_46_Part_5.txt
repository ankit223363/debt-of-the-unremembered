Chapter 46: Rowan Writes Back, Part 5 of 5: Going Down
Going Down
They did not say anything for a while after that.
There was nothing that needed saying. They stood by the wall of the little stone room with their hands together, his on hers, and the cold came in through the round green window, and the pigeons muttered, and a feather came down, and another. And Lyra felt her brother's hand get warmer, very slowly, the way a stone gets warmer when you hold it, until it was nearly the same as hers.
Then Rowan took his hand away.
He did it gently. He looked at it for a moment, the way he had looked at it before. Then he put it in the pocket of his gray coat.
"I have to go," he said. "The fourth bell."
"I know."
"If I'm late, the sergeant writes it down. And the Inquisitor reads everything the sergeant writes." He looked at the little brass dial by the window. At twenty past four. Something moved in his face that was nearly a smile, and was not. "I can't even tell how late I am. In here."
"That was the point," said Lyra. "She said. A place where it's always the same time."
"Yes."
He did not go.
He stood by the ladder with his hand in his pocket and looked at her. At her hair. At her face. At her red hands. As if he were learning them. As if he were writing them down somewhere, inside, the way Corin wrote things down in the Ledger, so that he could find them again later in the dark.
"What are you going to do?" said Lyra.
It was the question she had promised herself she would not ask. It came out anyway.
Rowan did not answer at once.
"Today," he said, "I'm going to go back down the hill, and take the plate back to the chophouse, and go up the Ministry Steps, and sit on my bench outside his door, and be on time for the fourth bell." He paused. "And when he comes out, and gives me a barley sugar, and asks me if I had a pleasant walk, I'm going to say yes, sir. And fold the wrapper. Small."
"And tomorrow?"
"I don't know." He looked at her steadily. "That's the truth, Lyra. You said you'd stopped lying. So I'll stop too. I don't know what I'll do tomorrow. I don't know what I'll do if he asks me kindly. I don't know what I'll do if he asks me where I went today, and looks at me over his spectacles, the way he does, until you feel like a window." He swallowed. "I won't tell him about the tower. Not today. Not because of you. Because of the clock. Because she brought us here."
Lyra nodded. She could not speak.
"And I won't come looking for you," said Rowan. "So don't send me where you are. Don't send me anything. Not for a while. If I don't know, he can't read it in me." He looked at the floor. "I've watched him read people, Lyra. In the rooms along the Long Office. With his bare hand. I've stood at the door. I know what it looks like when he finds something."
"Ro—"
"And don't come to the pump again. Not you. Not the girl with the feet." His voice went hard for a moment. "They write down everyone at the pump. There's a boy in a gray cap who stands at the corner of Gallows Lane every morning and writes them all down. I've seen him. I don't know whose he is. I think I know."
Lyra went cold.
"A boy in a gray cap," she said. "About twelve."
Rowan looked up.
"Yes."
"He followed Oren on Monday. And me, yesterday. To Comb Street."
Rowan's face went very still.
"Then he's following everybody," he said slowly. "Everybody who goes near anybody." He looked at the hole in the floor, at the top of the ladder. "I came up the hill the long way. Round by the Shambles. I looked. I didn't see him."
"Neither did we, yesterday. Not till Comb Street."
They looked at each other.
Then Rowan put his hand into the pocket of his coat again. Not the pocket he had put it in before. The inside one, on the left. Over his chest. And Lyra thought for a moment that he was going to take out the old letter, the cream one with the blood on it, and give it back to her. And her heart stopped.
He did not take out the letter.
He took out something much smaller, and held it out to her on his palm.
A square of folded waxed paper. White. The size of a fingernail. So tight, and so hard, and so sharp at every corner, that it looked as if it had been carved.
"Eleven," he said. "I did it last night. For the first time. All the way." He held it out. "I was going to show him. This morning. I didn't."
Lyra looked at it.
"Give it to Father," said Rowan. "When you see him. Put it in his hand. Don't tell him it's from me. He won't understand. He won't know who I am." His voice was very quiet. "Just put it in his hand. And see if he does anything."
Lyra took it.
It weighed nothing. It was warm from his chest.
"He'll know," she said.
"You don't know that."
"No," said Lyra. "I don't. But I'll tell you if he does." She closed her fingers over it. "I'll find a way. Not the pump. Not anything he can read. But I'll find a way."
Rowan nodded once.
Then he turned, and sat down on the edge of the square hole in the boards, with his legs hanging down into the dark, and put his hands on the top of the ladder. The way he used to sit on the top of the garden wall at Crown Walk, with his legs hanging over the lane, before he jumped. She had always told him not to. He had always jumped anyway.
He looked up at her.
"Lyra," he said.
"Yes?"
"There's a girl in the Office. An Inquisitor. Halloway." His face was strange. "She hums. When she copies. Three notes. Over and over. The whole Long Office stopped yesterday to listen to her, and she didn't know she was doing it." He hesitated. "I knew the notes. The first three. I didn't know how I knew them. Then I went to bed and lay with the lamp out and I heard the rest. In my head. Going down and round and home."
He looked at her.
"It's hers," he said. "Isn't it. Mother's. The one she sang on the stairs."
Lyra stood very still by the stopped clock.
She had four memories of her mother left. She counted them at night like coins. The brushing. The rain on Crown Walk. The empty chair. And the fourth.
The song that goes up and hangs.
"Yes," she said. "It's hers."
Rowan looked at her a moment longer.
Then he went down the ladder, quickly, the way he had come up, and she heard his boots hit the boards of the floor below, and then the stone of the stair, and then the quick light even footsteps going down and round and round the pillar in the middle, like the inside of a shell, fainter and fainter, until she could not hear them at all.
And then, a long way down, the groan of the black oak door.
And then nothing.
Lyra stood alone in the little stone room at the top of the tower, beside the great black clock that did not go, and listened to the nothing for a long time.
The pigeons came back, one by one, and settled on the beams above the bell, muttering. A feather came down. The green light from the round window lay on the dusty boards in a wavering oval, and the boards were marked now, all over, with the prints of two pairs of boots. Hers, small, worn down at the heels, borrowed. And his, longer, narrower, with the sharp square heels that the Academy gave its cadets, going from the ladder to the wall, and from the wall to the ladder, and nowhere else. He had not crossed the room. She had crossed it to him.
She looked at the little brass dial by the window.
Twenty past four.
She thought that she would like to come back here. One day. When it was over, whatever it was going to be. She would like to climb the hundred and four steps and the two ladders and stand in this room by the stopped clock, where it was always the same time, and not be afraid of anything. She did not know if that day would come. She did not know if any of them would be alive to see it.
She put her hand flat on the cold iron of the clock frame, the way Corin put his hand flat on the Ledger. As if it were a dog that needed to know she was there.
Then she turned toward the ladder.
The Boy Among the Yews
She did not go down at once.
She went to the round green window instead, and wiped the frost off the inside of the thick old glass with the side of her hand, and put her eye to it.
It was like looking through the bottom of a bottle. Everything was green, and bent, and wavering, as if the whole world outside were under water. But she could see. The roof of the church below her, white, with the snow lying in long smooth drifts along the leads. The graveyard beyond it, going down the hill in its crooked rows. The bent black yews. And the path, trodden gray, winding down between the stones toward the lych-gate at the bottom and the street beyond.
And on the path, walking fast, a tall thin figure in a gray coat with the collar turned up.
Rowan.
He did not look back. She watched him go down the path between the graves with his hands in his pockets and his head down, quickly, the way the cadets were taught to walk, not running, never running. Past the leaning stones. Past the fallen ones, the long white humps in the snow like sleeping animals. Past the yew trees, bent nearly to the ground under their load of snow.
She watched him all the way to the lych-gate. He went under its little roof, and out into the street, and turned left, toward the Shambles, the long way round. And the corner of a house took him, and he was gone.
She let out her breath. It made a cloud on the green glass, and she wiped it away.
And saw the boy.
He was at the bottom of the graveyard. Not on the path. Off it, to the right, among the small mounds by the iron railings, the ones with no stones at all, only wooden crosses and sticks tied together with string. He was standing very still behind one of the yews, the last one, the one nearest the railings, so close to its trunk that from the path you would not have seen him at all. From the path you would have seen a yew tree, bent under snow, and a shadow.
From the top of the tower, through the green glass, you could see a boy.
Small. About twelve. In a coat too big for him and a gray cap pulled down to his ears. Standing behind the yew with his back to the trunk, and his face turned, not toward the lych-gate where Rowan had gone, but up the hill.
Up the path. Up past the graves. To the church.
To the low door half sunk in the ground below the windows, with three stone steps going down to it, and a lamp hanging over it on an iron hook, lit in the daylight.
The crypt door.
Lyra did not move.
The boy stood behind the yew for a long time. She could not see his face, not properly, not through the thick green glass at that distance. Only the pale smudge of it, under the cap, turned up toward the church. And then she saw him take something out of his coat. Something small. A book. And something smaller, a pencil. And he wrote. Quickly. Bent over the book, with his back to the yew, in the snow.
Then he put them away.
Then he looked up once more at the crypt door.
And then, without any hurry at all, he stepped out from behind the yew, and climbed over the iron railings at the bottom of the graveyard where they were low and bent, and dropped down into the lane on the other side, and walked away. Not toward the Shambles, where Rowan had gone. The other way. Down the hill. Toward the river. Toward the bridges, and the Hill beyond them, and the Ministry of Records on the shoulder of the Hill, gray and flat and enormous, with the long pale line of its steps going up to the bronze doors.
Lyra turned away from the window.
She went down the ladder, and through the clock room, past the great black iron frame with its wheels that did not turn, and down the second ladder, and onto the stone stair. And round and round the pillar in the middle, a hundred and four steps, in the gray stripes of light from the slits, holding the black greasy rope. She did not count them. For the first time in a long time, she did not count.
She put her shoulder to the black oak door at the bottom and it groaned open, and she came out into the porch, into the cold, into the snow, and went round the side of the church on the trodden path to the crypt door.
Oren was standing in it.
He had not moved. He was standing exactly where Corin had told him to stand, in the low doorway at the top of the three stone steps, with his arms folded across the front of Terrence's coat and the lamp on its hook over his head. His face was gray with cold. There was snow on his shoulders and in his beard, and he had not brushed it off.
He looked at her as she came round the corner of the church. He looked at her face.
"I saw him," he said.
Lyra stopped.
"The boy," said Oren. "Behind the last yew. By the railings." He did not unfold his arms. "I saw him come in over the railings, a quarter of an hour after the cadet came up the path. I saw him stand there. I saw him write."
"And you didn't move."
"You told me not to move." Oren's voice was flat. "Not for anything. Not if I heard you shout. Not if I heard you cry." He looked down the hill at the yews. "I thought about it. I thought about going down there and taking him by the collar. A boy of twelve. In the snow. In a graveyard." He was quiet for a moment. "And then I thought, if I do that, he'll shout. And the cadet will hear him from the gate and turn round. And see me. And then it won't matter what the boy wrote, because the cadet will know too."
Lyra did not say anything.
"So I stood here," said Oren. "And let him write." He looked at her. "Was that wrong?"
Lyra looked down the hill. At the last yew, bent under its snow by the railings. At the place behind it where a boy had stood, and the marks of his boots, small, going away over the railings into the lane.
"I don't know," she said.
She went past him, down the three stone steps, into the crypt. Into the warm brown light, and the smell of pears, and the long low hall between the pillars, and the children lying on their pallets, not looking at her. Fifty-one of them. Polly, watching the lamp. Kit Flynn, turning his spoon. Bet, asleep by the bench in the side vault with her head on her bundle.
Corin was sitting up on his stone bench under the skulls of the old monks. He had the Ledger open on his knee. He looked up as she came in under the arch, and saw her face, and closed the book.
"He came," he said.
"He came."
"And?"
Lyra sat down on the floor by the bench, with her back to the wall, where Juno had sat that morning. She opened her hand. The little white square lay on her palm. Eleven folds. Warm still, a little, from her own fist.
"He answered," she said. "Once."
Corin looked at the wrapper on her palm for a long time.
"And?" he said again, more gently.
Lyra closed her hand.
"And somebody followed him here," she said. "A boy in a gray cap. He stood behind a yew tree and looked at that door and wrote something down in a book. And now he's walking down the hill toward the Ministry with it in his pocket." She looked at the floor. "So the Inquisitor will know where we are by the fourth bell. Or he'll know where something is. A crypt, under a church, with a lamp lit over the door in the daylight. And fifty-one children in it who can't be moved."
The crypt was very quiet. Out in the main hall, Mrs. Doyle was saying There's my lamb to somebody who did not answer.
Corin put his gray hand flat on the Ledger.
"Then we have till the fourth bell," he said. "Or tomorrow. Or Midwinter. We don't know which." He looked at her. "What time does Hesper want you?"
Lyra looked up.
"The fourth bell," she said.
"The gown."
"The gown. For Midwinter. For the Duke's arm." She heard her own voice, very steady. "I can't go to the Masquerade in a gown I swim in, Corin. The whole Hill will look. It's the only way in at the front door."
Corin did not say anything for a long moment.
"Then Oren goes with you," he said. "Again. All the way. In and out." He looked past her, at the crypt door, where Oren still stood in the snow at the top of the steps with his arms folded. "And Gideon goes up the hill to the Steps and watches the road. And Juno, when she's back from the rope-walk, goes to the step women. All of them. Every door." His hand pressed down on the Ledger. "And I sit here on a stone bench under a lot of dead monks with a leg that won't bend, and wait, because there's nothing else I can do."
Lyra looked at him.
She did not say that was cruel, the way she had said yesterday. She did not say anything. She reached up, and put her closed hand, with Rowan's eleven-fold wrapper inside it, on top of his gray one, on top of the Ledger.
And held it there.
And somewhere above them, through the stone and the earth and the floor of the church, through the tower and the clock room and the stopped iron wheels, old deaf Amos at the bottom of the bell rope heard the Ministry's bell across the river, and pulled.
And the great green bell of Saint Bride's spoke, once, and twice, and three times.
The third bell after noon.
One bell to Hesper's.
