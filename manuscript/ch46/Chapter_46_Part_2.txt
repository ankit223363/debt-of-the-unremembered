Chapter 46: Rowan Writes Back, Part 2 of 5: Wick
Wick
Lyra went out into the main hall, and stood by the far brazier, and held her hands over the coals until they hurt.
Wick sent to the Health today. I couldn't stop it. I'm sorry.
She had put the spoon in his hand. On Wednesday morning, in the dark, at the fourth bell, in the empty third arch, with the brazier dying. She had scraped the last of the cold soup into his tin bowl, with the barley in it, and put it down beside his piles of paper, and put the spoon in his hand, and closed his fingers round it. And he had looked up at her. Just for a moment. His faded eyes on her face, the way they rested on nobody else's.
And then she had walked away up the arches to the fire, and not looked round.
And at first light, she had stood at the top of the Steps with the doctor's bag in her hand and watched two men carry him out of the third arch like a sack. And she had heard the sound he made. And she had walked away again, up the hill, behind the barrow, because Gant had looked up at her through the snow, and a child had coughed, and the doctor had said now.
She had not gone back for him. Nobody had. Mother Grue had said she would ask for him back. Mother Grue had said she had done that before.
Now he was at the Health.
She did not know what the Health did with old men who were no use to anybody. She knew what it did with children. She was standing in the middle of what it did with children, in the smell of pears, with fifty-one of them lying on their pallets round her in the brown warm light, not looking at her.
She held her hands over the coals.
"Don't," said a voice at her feet. "You'll blister. And then I'll have to dress them, and I've only got so much lint."
The doctor had woken up.
He was sitting where he had been sleeping, on the floor with his back against the pillar, and he was looking up at her through his crooked spectacles, which he had put on crooked. He had one boot on and one boot off. His face was gray with tiredness. He looked as if he had not slept at all, only stopped moving for a while.
Lyra took her hands away from the coals. They were red, and stinging.
"Wick," she said.
She told him. It did not take long.
Silas listened with his head back against the pillar and his eyes shut. When she had finished he did not open them for a while.
"The Health," he said at last. "Did she say where?"
"No. Just the Health."
"Then it's the house past the tanneries." Silas opened his eyes. "Out on the east road, beyond the last of the yards, where the river bends. A big brick house in a wall, with a garden that nobody walks in. It used to be a workhouse, a long time ago. Then a fever hospital. Now it's the Office of Public Health's House of Rest for the Simple and the Aged." He said the name the way you say the name of a ship that sank. "There's a sign on the gate. Very nicely painted. Gold on blue."
"Have you been inside?"
"I've been to the gate." Silas reached for his other boot. "Four times. Once a year, for four years. To ask about patients of mine who had been taken there by the Health, and not come out. Old Peg Rudd. Mr. Dobbs, who used to sell matches on the corner of Fish Street. A man called Lamb, who had been a soldier and talked to himself." He pulled on the boot. "Each time a very polite young man came to the gate and said that the patient was resting comfortably and could not be disturbed. Each time he gave me a sweet. A peppermint. From a paper bag." He did up the laces, slowly. "I never ate them."
Lyra did not say anything.
"And the Friday cart," said Silas, "goes out of that gate. Every Friday. Before dawn. With its plum-colored scarf on the driver and its crates under a cloth. I've seen it. I've stood in the dark by the tannery wall and watched it go. Down the east road toward the river, and the Lower Docks, and the coal chute in Cinder Lane." He stood up, with a crack from his knees, and put one hand on the pillar to steady himself. "I used to wonder what was in the crates. Then Mr. Vane came back from the Bourse on Sunday night with a bag full of little blue-sealed vials and a printed label that said Care of the Dispensary, the Hill. And I stopped wondering."
"Lull," said Lyra.
"Lull. Grayroot. Boiled down." Silas looked at the children on their pallets. "A sweet a day. To loosen them. So that when the glass goes on, it all comes out at once, easy, like a tooth that's been rocked. And when it's all out, and there's nothing left but a body that eats and sleeps and taps two on its knee—" He stopped. "Then I suppose they keep him. Somewhere. In the garden nobody walks in. Until he forgets how to swallow, like Ned."
The brazier ticked between them.
"Wick's got nothing left to take," said Lyra. "Wren said. She said he was emptied to the walls. Years ago."
"Then they'll keep him anyway," said Silas. "Because he's in the books now. And the Health is very careful with its books." He looked at her over his spectacles. "I'm sorry, girl. I am. But there's nothing to be done for him today. Not by you. Not by me. If I go to that gate again, they'll give me a peppermint and write my name down, and the next cart that comes to Saint Bride's will be for me."
Lyra looked at her red hands.
"Mother Grue said she'd ask for him back," she said. "She said she'd done it before."
"Then let her ask." Silas put his hand on her shoulder, briefly, the way he had put it on her arm at the top of the Steps. "She has her ways. Older than mine. And the Health doesn't know her face."
He went off down the hall between the pallets, a tall stooped heron of a man with his coat hanging off him, to start his rounds.
Lyra stood by the brazier.
Then she went back into the side vault, where Corin was sitting with the Ledger shut on his knee and Juno on the floor beside him, and she held out her hand for the pencil.
Corin gave it to her without asking.
She opened the Ledger at the back. At the page that said, in his square careful capitals, THOSE I OWE. There were a great many names on it now. Some in his hand. Some in hers. Old Jory Hobday. Bramble. Ned. The Hollow brothers, Big Gideon and Saul. Coker. I never said thank you.
She wrote at the bottom, in her own hand, fierce and slanting hard to the right:
Wick. I put the spoon in his hand and walked away. Twice.
She looked at it.
Then she gave the pencil back.
Juno, on the floor, had been watching her write. She did not say anything about it. She put her cap back on her head, and pulled it down, and got up off the floor onto the sides of her feet, slowly.
"The rope-walk," she said. "Lascar Mary's. On the Saltmarsh. That's where Mother Grue's gone?"
"Yes."
"I'll take it. The word. About Wick. After the pump." Juno looked at Lyra. "It's on my way. Nearly."
It was not on her way. It was three miles out along the marsh road in the snow, and three miles back, on feet that she could only walk on the sides of.
"Juno," said Lyra.
"I know," said Juno. "Slower." She went to the arch of the vault. "I'll go slower. But I'll go."
Gideon and Juno
She did not get as far as the door of the crypt.
Gideon was sitting on the bottom of the three stone steps, just inside it, with his back against the cold wall and his wooden boat in his lap. He had been there since dawn. Corin had sent him there, to watch the door, because Gideon could sit still for longer than anyone Lyra had ever known, and not get bored, and not fall asleep, and not look away. He sat with his bitten arm in its sling against his chest and his good hand turning the little boat over and over, and his flat gray eyes on the gap under the door, where the snow-light came in.
He looked up as Juno came down the hall on the sides of her feet.
She stopped in front of him.
They looked at each other. Lyra, coming behind with the empty jug, stopped too, by the last pillar, and watched. She did not know why. Something in the way they were looking at each other. Two children of about the same size, the same thinness, the same red cold hands. One in a cap, one with a sling. Both from the Lower Docks. Both with their boots too big or too small.
"You're the boy from the arches," said Juno. "Gideon. Gran told me. The one who took the step women's warning to Mother Grue."
"Yes."
"I'm Juno."
"I know." Gideon turned the boat over. "You walked fourteen miles on your feet to the barge. In the snow. Lyra said. Over twenty-three bridges." He looked down at her boots. "You walk on the sides now."
"It hurts less."
"I've got no big toes," said Gideon. "The doctor took them. Sunday. They went black." He said it the way you would say I've got a new hat. "I walk on the outsides too. A bit. You get used to it."
Juno looked at him.
Then she sat down on the step beside him. Just like that. Without asking. With a small sound through her teeth as her feet left the floor.
"What's that?" she said. She was looking at the boat.
"A boat."
"I can see it's a boat. What kind?"
Gideon looked at it for a long time, the way he looked at things, as if he were weighing them.
"A barge," he said. "I was making a barge. Like the black one Lyra told me about. With a hay room." He turned it over. "It came out wrong. It's too pointed at the front. Barges are flat at the front. I didn't know till it was done."
"Hessa's is flat at the front."
"I know. That's what's wrong with it." Gideon held it out to her. "You can have it."
Juno looked at the boat on his palm. A little thing, the size of a hand, cut from a scrap of gray wood with a small knife, with a splinter for a mast. Too pointed at the front.
"Why?"
"Because I'll make another one," said Gideon. "A right one. And I don't need two." He went on holding it out. "And you're going to the Saltmarsh, after the pump. To Lascar Mary's. Mother Grue's there. And the others from the arches. Abel, and the Pinches, and Nell, and Tobin." He paused. "Give it to Tobin. He's eight. He hasn't got anything. Mother Grue gave him a spoon once and somebody stole it."
Juno took the boat.
She turned it over in her red hands. She looked at it for a long time. Then she put it inside her coat, carefully, in the pocket where Lyra's letter had been.
"All right," she said.
They sat on the step together, side by side, with their backs to the cold wall, and looked at the gap under the door. Neither of them said anything for a while. Lyra stood by the pillar with her jug and did not move.
"Lyra says," said Juno at last, not looking at him, "that they took your feelings out. At the Bourse. When you were little."
"Yes."
"All of them?"
"Nearly." Gideon thought about it. "Fane said all. In the book. Gideon H. All feelings. Over four winters. But Corin says I shivered once. In my sleep. On Sunday. He wrote it down." He looked at his good hand. "I don't remember shivering. But he wrote it."
Juno did not say anything.
"It's not bad," said Gideon. "Before you say. Everyone thinks it's bad. It isn't. It's just quiet. When the rat bit my arm, it hurt, but I didn't mind it. When the doctor took my toes, it hurt, but I didn't mind that either. When I saw my father in the Bourse, carrying crates, with his face all gray, and he didn't know me—" He stopped. He turned his good hand over, and looked at the palm. "I didn't mind that either. I knew I ought to. I knew it was the kind of thing you mind. So I sat and thought about what minding would be like. For a long time. And I couldn't get it."
The snow-light lay on the floor under the door.
"I mind things," said Juno. "All the time. I mind everything." She pulled her cap down. "I minded when my dad died and they put a line through his name in the parish book, like he was a sum that had come out wrong. I minded when Gran's Terrence came home from the Ministry and didn't know her. I mind my feet. I mind Lyra's hair." She sniffed. "Minding's horrible. I'd give you some of mine if I could. I've got too much."
Gideon looked at her.
And Lyra, by the pillar, saw something happen in his face. Not much. Almost nothing. The flat gray eyes rested on Juno's face for a moment longer than they rested on anything. The way Wick's eyes had rested on hers, once, in the arches, by the brazier. As if there was still somebody there to look at.
Then it was gone.
"You can't give feelings," said Gideon. "Fane can take them. But nobody can give them." He turned back to the gap under the door. "I asked Corin. He said nobody knows how."
"Well," said Juno. "Somebody should find out."
She got up off the step, slowly, with the small sound through her teeth again. She stood for a moment on the sides of her feet, with her hand on the cold wall. "It's a long way to the pump," she said, "on these." Then she went up the three stone steps, and opened the low door, and the slab of white light came in, and the snow, and the cold. And she went out into it.
Gideon watched the door close behind her.
Then he took his little knife out of his sling, where he kept it, and picked up a new scrap of gray wood from the floor beside him, and began, very carefully, to carve a barge that was flat at the front.
Lyra went back to her pallets.
The Tenth Bell
At the tenth bell, the bells of the city came down over the roofs one after another, and Lyra stopped what she was doing and stood still and listened to them.
She was halfway down the main hall of the crypt, with a jug of water in one hand and a cup in the other. She had been going from pallet to pallet since Juno left, holding the cup to mouths. Some of the mouths opened. Most did not. When they did not, you wet a cloth and pressed it to the lips and let the water run in at the corners, a little at a time, and waited, and watched the throat to see if it moved.
The first bell was Saint Clement's, down by the river. Deep and slow. Then the Ministry, high on the Hill, thin and sharp. Then all the others, tumbling.
Ten.
Moll here tomorrow, tenth bell.
She stood in the middle of the crypt with the jug and the cup and thought of a man she had never met.
She knew about him. Of course she did. Everyone in the Ledger was someone she knew about. Sergeant Ebenezer Moll. Watch house, Rope Street. Commonwealth Foot, twelve years. Bad leg, from a wagon. Taps fours and twos when he thinks. He was the one who had read the drum calls on the barge, at the long table, by Hessa's lamp, on Monday. Four is Witness. Two is Hold. Nine is Fold. Three is Home. He was the one who had come out to the barge under a load of coal on Abner Cole's cart, and gone back the same way. He was the one who had sat with Mrs. Marta in the Watch house kitchen at four in the morning a week ago, and made a list of every door in Corin's Ledger, and sent the step women to knock on them.
And on Monday, at the tenth bell, he had sat across a green leather desk from Silas Kross, with his papers from the Commonwealth Foot. And Kross had read them, and tapped, while he read. Four, two, nine, three. On the green leather, with one finger.
And Moll's own finger, under the table, had answered. Once.
Heard.
And Kross had folded the papers, and kept them, and said, Do come back on Thursday, Sergeant. Same hour. And do bring your memory with you.
It was Thursday.
It was the same hour.
Lyra put the jug down on the floor, carefully, so that it would not spill. She put the cup beside it. Then she went to the low door at the end of the crypt, and opened it, and went up the three stone steps into the snow, and stood at the top, in the graveyard, among the leaning stones and the bent black yews, and looked down.
You could see a long way from Saint Bride's. That was the thing about it. It stood at the top of its own hill, and the city fell away from it on every side, roof below roof, chimney below chimney, all white this morning, all smoking, down to the river. And across the river, the other hill. The Hill. With the Crescent along the top of it in a long white curve, and the great houses, and the dome of the Archon's palace sitting above everything like an egg in a cup.
And below the Crescent, on the shoulder of the Hill, gray and flat and enormous, the Ministry of Records.
She could see the Ministry Steps. A long pale line going up the front of it, from Cooper's Rise to the bronze doors. Too far to see people on it. Too far to see anything but the line, pale in the snow, and the dark square of the doors at the top, like a mouth.
She stood and looked at it for a long time.
Somewhere on those steps, now, or a little before now, a man with a bad leg had climbed up. Slowly. One step at a time. The way Corin would climb stairs now, for the rest of his life, sideways, holding the rail. A man of about forty-five, in his Watch sergeant's coat, with his papers in his pocket. Going up the hundred and twelve steps toward the bronze doors and the words over them that Corin had told her about. WHAT IS WRITTEN IS KEPT.
And at the top, waiting for him, a cadet of sixteen at the door, standing very straight. Her brother. Sent to watch how an honest man lies.
And inside, at the green leather desk, the Inquisitor. With a girl in white beside him, taking the notes, in her small square upright hand.
Do bring your memory with you.
Lyra did not know what would happen in that room. She did not know if Moll would come out of it. She did not know if, when he came out, he would still know his own name, or Mrs. Marta's, or the four drum calls, or the face of the boy on the tailboard of the last blue wagon, tapping all night.
There was nothing she could do about it. Nothing at all. She could not go there. She could not send anyone. She could not even send a warning, because the warning was already there, sitting in the room, in white, with a pen.
She stood in the graveyard in the snow until the cold came up through her boots and into her bones and she could not feel her feet. Until the bells of the city had long since finished striking, and the smoke from all the chimneys had drifted, and the dark square of the doors at the top of the Ministry Steps had not changed at all.
Then she went back down the three steps into the crypt, and picked up the jug and the cup, and went on.
She did not tell Corin what she had been doing. He did not ask. But when she came past the arch of the side vault, half an hour later, with the jug nearly empty, he was sitting on his bench under the skulls with the Ledger open on his knee, and he was not reading it.
He was looking at his hand. His left hand. The gray one. Lying flat on the open page.
And the first finger of it was lifting, and falling, on the paper. Very small. Over and over.
Four. Then two. Then nine. Then three.
And then a pause.
And then, once.
He did not know he was doing it. She could see that. His eyes were on nothing.
Lyra stood in the arch with the jug, and watched his finger tap on the page, and did not say anything, and after a while she went on.
