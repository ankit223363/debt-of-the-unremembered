Chapter 17: Slag-Fever, Part 2 of 5: Where the Slag Goes
Where the Slag Goes
The tea was black and very strong and tasted of the tin it had been boiled in. Doctor Silas made it in a dented kettle on the stove at the end of the crypt, and poured it into four mugs that did not match, and put a lump of brown sugar in each one without asking, and gave them out. Then he sat down on an upturned crate by the stove, with his long legs folded up and his elbows on his knees and his own mug held in both thin hands, and looked at the fire through the open door of the stove for a while without saying anything.
Lyra sat on a stool beside him. Juno sat on the floor with her back against the warm brick of the wall. Corin stood. He could not sit. He stood by the nearest pillar with the hot mug in his gloved hands and looked down the long low crypt, at the two rows of trestle beds, at the flushed faces and the bright eyes and the moving lips, and listened to the murmuring.

"You know what slag is," said Doctor Silas at last.
"The bits that are left," said Corin. "In the intake glasses. At the Bank." He did not need the Ledger. He had cleared four glasses that very morning, or yesterday, or the day before, it did not matter. His hands knew. "When people sell at the glass, the stone takes most of it. But not all. Every time, a little gets left behind. In the glass. Bits. Pieces of people. Nobody's." He swallowed. "And after a month the glass goes cloudy, and they can't read through it anymore, and they send it down to the cold room. And I clear it."
"With your rod."
"With my rod. I draw the slag out, and it goes into the silver. And at the end of the day I empty the rod into a slag-jar, the way you'd empty a bucket, and the glass goes back upstairs, clean." He stopped. "Mostly."
"Mostly," agreed Doctor Silas. "And the glasses you can't clear? The ones too far gone? Too thick with it?"
Corin thought about it. About a rack on the left by the door, in the cold blue light. About a man in a dark green coat a size too small, at a ledger stand by the stairs.
"They go out," he said slowly. "On a cart. Briggs signs for them."
"And the slag-jars? When they're full?"
"They go out too."
"Where?"
Corin did not know.
He stood by the pillar with the mug going cold in his hands, and he realized, slowly, the way you realize you have been walking for a long time in the wrong direction, that he had never once asked. Not once. Not in his Ledger, not anywhere. A week of clearing glasses, since the Archon's paper came down and said he was to be kept busy and out of the way. And before him, the Ledger said, for years, a man with a bucket and a cloth, who had gone a bit odd in the end and started crying at nothing. Glass after glass. Jar after jar, sealed and set on the shelf by the door for the carter. And he had never once wondered where the carter took them.
"Here," said Doctor Silas.
He said it very quietly.
"They come here. Every night. To the Flats. The mud flats, out there, below the chapel, where the stream comes out of the Cut into the river." He nodded at the low black door, and the river beyond it. "The Bank pays a carter. Old Moggs, with the piebald horse. He loads up at the Lantern Quay at midnight, all the cloudy glasses that can't be cleared and all the slag-jars that are full, and he drives along the river road with his lamp out, and he comes down the track to the Flats, and he backs his cart up to the pits, and he tips it in." He drank his tea. "The jars break when they hit the bottom. The glasses break too. And Moggs takes a shovel and breaks them up some more, so they won't stand out. And then he shovels a bit of mud on top. Not much. Just enough. And he goes home."
"Pits," said Lyra.
"Pits." Doctor Silas looked at her. "Dug in the mud. Six of them now. Each as big as this crypt. The Ministry was supposed to bury it, Miss Thorne. That's the law. Slag's to be carried out of the city to the old quarry at Marl Hill, twenty miles off, and put down a shaft, and the shaft capped with lead. That's what the law says." His mouth twisted. "But that costs money. And the Flats are close, and nobody important lives in the Cut, and the tide comes in over the mud twice a day and covers everything. So the Bank pays Moggs a silver a night, and Moggs tips it in the pits, and the Ministry writes in its books slag carried to Marl Hill, and everybody's happy."
"And the children," said Corin.
Doctor Silas looked down the long crypt. At the trestle beds. At the little girl with the two thin braids, still sitting up against the wall with her bright glassy eyes, saying in an old man's voice: —two bad knees and a cough—
"There's glass in the pits," he said. "Good glass. Thick glass. From the intake. The best glass in the city, made to hold a memory. Broken, yes. But in big pieces, some of it. And a glazier on Lye Street will give you a farthing a pound for broken glass, to melt down." He was quiet a moment. "A farthing a pound. Do you know how much that is, to a child in the Cut? That's bread. That's a candle. That's a night's rent on a doss-house floor."
Nobody said anything.
"So they go down to the Flats," said Doctor Silas. "Every night. When the tide's out. When Moggs has gone home. With their lamps and their sacks and their bare hands. And they dig in the pits for glass." He turned his mug round in his long fingers. "And they cut themselves. Of course they cut themselves. It's broken glass in the dark. And the slag gets in. Through the cuts. Through their mouths, when they lick the blood off their fingers. Up their noses, when they kick up the dust." His voice did not change at all. "And a day later, they start to burn."
The child on the bed nearest the stove, a boy of about nine with a shock of red hair, sat up suddenly and said in a high clear woman's voice: "Mind the step, Mr. Fenwick, it's loose, I've told the landlord twice." And lay down again, and turned over, and was still.
Doctor Silas did not look round.
"It's not a fever like other fevers," he said. "I've seen every fever there is. Twenty years at the Ministry hospital. Six more down here. Marsh fever, ship fever, scarlet fever, the sweats, the spotted fever, the bloody flux. They all burn the body. This burns something else." He tapped the side of his own head, once, with one finger. "The slag gets into the blood, and the blood carries it up, and it gets into the mind. Pieces of other people. Hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe. Little bits, all broken up, all mixed together. A woman's wedding. A man's knees. A soldier counting his steps. And the child's own mind doesn't know what to do with them. It's like pouring a sack of somebody else's buttons into your sewing box." He drank his tea. "So it fights. It burns. It tries to push them out. And while it's fighting, the bits come up to the top, and talk."
"Do they get better?" said Juno.
She said it very small. From the floor by the wall, with her knees drawn up and her arms round them and her sharp sooty face turned toward the beds.
Doctor Silas looked at her.
"Most of them," he said. "Most of them. If they're strong, and they've eaten, and they're kept warm. A week. Ten days. The fever breaks, and the voices stop, and they sit up and ask for bread. And they don't remember any of it." He paused. "And they go back down to the Flats the next night, because the glazier still pays a farthing a pound."
"And the ones who don't?"
The doctor did not answer at once.
"Some of them," he said slowly, "don't push it out. The fever breaks, the same. The voices stop, the same. They sit up and ask for bread. But they ask for it in the wrong voice." He looked into the stove. "Or they've forgotten their mother's name, and remember somebody else's. Or they wake up and think they're forty years old and a docker with two bad knees, and they can't understand why their hands are so small." His voice was very low. "There was a girl in the summer. Seven years old. Called Dot. She woke up on the eleventh day, and her fever was gone, and she sat up in that bed, there, by the third pillar, and she looked at me, and she said, Doctor, where's my husband?" He was quiet for a long moment. "She still asks. Every day. Her mother brings her to the door sometimes, and she stands there in her little pinafore and looks at all the men going past in the street, and waits for one of them to be him."
Lyra had put down her mug. She was sitting very straight on her stool, with her hands in her lap, looking at the beds.
"And the Ministry knows," she said. "About this. About the pits."
Doctor Silas laughed. A short, dry, tired sound, like a twig snapping.
"The Ministry knows," he said. "Because I told them."
He set down his mug on the floor by the crate. He sat up a little, on his crate, and for a moment, in the red light of the stove, he looked like a different man. Straighter. Younger. A man in a good gray coat, with a badge on it.
"Six years ago," he said. "When it started. When it was only one or two children a month, and I was still a Ministry doctor, at the hospital on the Hill, with a badge on my coat and a room of my own and a man to polish my boots. I came down here one Sunday to see an old patient, and I saw a child in the street talking in a sailor's voice, and I followed her home. And I found the pits." He looked at the dark patch on his coat, where the badge had been. "And I went back up the Hill, and I sat down at my desk, and I wrote a report. Eleven pages. With drawings. On the Matter of a Fever Among the Children of the Cut, and Its Cause. And I signed it, and I sent it up the pipe to the Office of the Archon." He was quiet a moment. "And three days later, a clerk came to my room, with a man in a gray coat behind him. And the clerk said, very politely, that my report had been read, with great interest, and that there was no such illness as slag-fever, and that I was clearly overtired, and that the Ministry was very grateful for my long service, and would I please hand him my badge."
He touched the dark patch on his coat, very lightly, with one finger.
"And the man in gray took it off me with a little knife," he said. "Right there. In my own room. And put it in his pocket."
The crypt was quiet except for the murmuring.
"Six years ago," said Corin slowly, "there were one or two a month."
"Six years ago, one or two a month," said Doctor Silas. "And then it stayed like that. For years. One, two, three. Never more than a handful. Because Moggs only came once a week, then, on a Saturday night, with half a cart."
He looked at Corin. And his gray eyes behind the crooked spectacles were very sharp.
"And then, in the summer," he said, "Moggs started coming every night. With a full cart. Piled up. Every single night, since the end of June. Glasses and jars and glasses and jars. More slag than I've ever seen. More than the intake could make in a year." He shook his head slowly. "I asked him. Once. Where it was all coming from. And he looked over his shoulder, the way people do in this city, and he said, It's not all from the Quay, Doctor. Half of it comes down from the Hill. In sealed crates. Old stock, they say. And then he said, It's like somebody up there's clearing out a cellar."
Corin did not move.
He was thinking of a cold dry room under the Hill. A long way down. A lift on a chain that went down in the dark so long your ears hurt. Iron doors with numbers on them. And a scorched corner of blue paper, folded small behind the marbled lining of his Ledger.

The Flats
Moggs came at a quarter to one.
Doctor Silas knew he would. He said Moggs was the only thing in the Cut you could set a clock by. So at half past twelve he put on a second coat over his gray one, and a scarf, and a pair of old fingerless gloves, and took down a lantern from a hook by the door and did not light it, and looked at Corin.
"You'll want to see," he said. It was not a question.
Lyra stood up off her stool.
"No," said Doctor Silas, without looking at her. "Not you, Miss Thorne. Every watchman in the city has your face in his head tonight, and half the Cut can read a broadsheet. You'll stay here, below ground, with the children, and the bolt on the door." He wound the scarf round his neck. "And the little sweep will stay with you. And if anyone knocks who isn't Captain Vance's knock, or mine, you'll go through that door at the back, into the old bone-room, and pull the shelf across, and wait."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment. Then she sat down again.
Juno did not argue either. That surprised Corin more than anything. She sat on the floor by the stove with her knees up and her arms round them and looked at him with her bright eyes, and said only, "Don't fall in."
"I won't."
"You will," said Juno. "But try not to."
The cold outside was so great that it seemed to have a sound. A thin high singing, just at the edge of hearing, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass. The moon had come up while they were in the crypt. It hung low over the river, enormous, a little lopsided, the color of old bone, and its light lay on everything, and turned everything to silver and black. The ruined chapel. The frozen weeds. The steep bank going down. And below the bank, stretching away to the edge of the black water, the Flats.
Corin stopped at the top of the bank and looked down.
He had expected mud. Brown mud, wet and stinking, the way the Ledger said the river mud was in summer. It was not brown. It was not wet. The cold had frozen it, the whole wide stretch of it, from the foot of the bank to the river's edge, a quarter of a mile or more, into a great flat crust, gray-white with frost, crazed all over with cracks like the glaze on an old plate. The moonlight lay on it, and it glittered. Here and there, where the stream from the Cut came out under the chapel and wandered across the Flats to the river, there was a dark line of ice, black and shining, curving away. And beyond it, the river, black and slow and steaming, with its plates of ice turning in the current, and the moon's long broken road lying across it.
And in the middle of the Flats, there were the pits.
He saw them at once. He could not have missed them. There were six, the doctor had said, and there were six. Great round hollows in the frozen mud, each one as wide across as the crypt was long, with low banks of dug earth heaped up round their edges like the rims of bowls. And they glowed.
Not brightly. Not like the lamps on the bridge, or the windows of the Bourse. But they glowed. A faint, cold, wavering blue, coming up out of the bottom of each one like the light at the bottom of a well, so that each pit was a round shallow bowl of pale blue light lying on the dark gray-white of the Flats, like a moon fallen in the mud. Six of them. Six fallen moons.
And in the blue light, there were lamps.
Small ones. Yellow ones. Candle-lanterns and rush-lights in jars, and once, a single tallow dip held in a bare hand. Moving. Bobbing. Down in the pits, and along the rims of them, and between them on the frozen mud. Dozens of them. Small and yellow in the cold blue glow, like fireflies.
And under every lamp, a shape. A small dark shape. Bent over. Digging.
"Saints," said Corin. He did not know he had said it.
"They come out the moment the tide's gone," said Doctor Silas beside him, very quietly. "They wait on the bank, up there by the chapel wall, in the dark, all of them, for Moggs to come and go. And then they come down." He started down the bank, picking his way carefully, with the unlit lantern swinging in his hand. "Come on. Quietly. And keep your hands in your pockets, harvester. Don't touch anything."
They went down onto the Flats.
The frozen mud crunched under their boots like stale bread. In places the crust was thin, and gave, and their feet went through into the cold black ooze underneath, and came out again with a sucking sound and a smell of rot. They walked carefully, in the moonlight, toward the nearest pit.
As they got closer, Corin began to feel it.
It started in the rod. In the long thin silver rod in the pocket of his coat, against his right side. It went cold. Not the ordinary cold of the night. A deep, aching, inside cold, the cold of the slag room, the cold of glass that has held too much for too long. And then the rod began, very faintly, to hum. He could feel it through his coat. A thin, high, trembling hum, like a struck wire.
Careful, said Malakor. His voice was sharp and low. Careful, boy. Keep it in your pocket. Keep your hand off it. It knows what this is. It wants it.
"What is it?"
He said it aloud. Doctor Silas looked at him sideways, but did not ask.
Slag, said Malakor. Tons of it. More than I have ever felt in one place in all my long death. Pieces of ten thousand people, all broken, all thrown together in the mud. A pause. A harvester's rod is made to take up what is left behind. That is all it is for. And there is more left behind in those pits than in every graveyard in this city. The thin voice went thinner. If you put that rod into one of those pits, it would try to take all of it. And it would bring all of it to you.
Corin took his hand away from his side. He put it deep in his other pocket. And kept it there.
The nearest pit was the fourth. The doctor said so. He had given them numbers.
They stood on its rim, in the heaped and frozen earth, and looked down into it.
It was full of glass.
The bottom of the pit, a man's height below them, was a great shallow bowl of broken glass, mixed with frozen mud and gray-glittering dust, and the glass was glowing. Every piece of it. From inside. Shards as big as dinner plates and splinters as small as pins, all heaped together, all faintly, coldly, wavering blue, so that the whole pit seemed to be breathing light. Here and there among the glass there were other things. The broken necks of slag-jars, with their wax seals still on. The iron frame of an intake glass, bent and rusted. And once, near the middle, a whole jar, unbroken, lying on its side in the mud with its label still on it, and its seal intact, and its gray contents faintly shining.
And there were children in it.
Six or seven of them, down in the bottom of the pit, in the blue light, among the glass. The oldest might have been eleven. The youngest was a tiny thing in a coat made of a sack, with her feet wrapped in rags, sitting in the broken glass with a rush-light in a jar beside her, picking through the shards one by one with her bare fingers and putting the biggest pieces in a sack. None of them spoke. None of them looked up. They dug and picked and dug, in silence, with their breath going up white in the cold blue glow, and the only sound was the clink and scrape of glass.
One of them, a boy, had a cut across the back of his hand. Corin could see it from the rim. Dark and wet in the blue light. The boy looked at it, and put it to his mouth, and sucked it, and went on digging.
Corin's stomach turned over.
"Doctor—"
"I know," said Doctor Silas. "I know. I come down every night. I tell them. I beg them. I've given them gloves. I've given them bread, to stay away. They take the bread, and eat it, and come back the next night, because bread's for tonight and the glazier's for tomorrow." His voice was very tired. "I can't stop them, harvester. Nobody can. You'd have to stop them being poor."
The cart came at a quarter to one.
Corin heard it before he saw it. A slow creak and rumble, coming along the river road above the Flats, from the direction of the Lantern Quay. Then a light. One lamp. Very low, very dim, swinging under the axle where nobody could see it from the road. And then, coming down the long rutted track from the river road onto the Flats, slowly, carefully, a cart. A big flat open cart, piled high, pulled by a horse.
The horse was piebald. Black and white, in great blotches, like a map. It came down the track with its head low and its breath smoking, very slowly, picking its way on the frozen ruts. And on the seat of the cart, hunched up in a coat and a blanket and a hat pulled down so low that you could not see his face at all, sat a man.
The children in the pits did not run. They did not scatter. They simply stopped digging. All of them, all at once, in every pit, as if somebody had blown a whistle. They climbed out, quickly and quietly, with their sacks and their lamps, and went away across the Flats in little groups toward the chapel bank, and crouched down at the foot of it in the dark, and waited. Their lamps went out, one by one.
In a minute, the Flats were empty. There was only the cart, and the horse, and the moon, and the six blue pits.
Doctor Silas put his hand on Corin's arm and drew him down, behind the frozen rim of the fourth pit, out of the moonlight.
The cart came across the Flats. It went past them, so close that Corin could hear the horse breathing and the creak of the harness and the man on the seat muttering to himself, and smell the horse, and the wet sacking, and something else, strong and sharp, coming off the load. The slag-room smell. Cold glass and thunder.
It went on to the sixth pit, the far one, near the river's edge. And stopped. And the man climbed down off the seat, slowly, stiffly, like an old man, and went round to the back of the cart and began to unload.
Corin watched.
Glasses first. Great clouded sheets of intake glass, as big as tabletops, gone milky from inside, that the man slid off the back of the cart one by one and let fall into the pit with a crash and a splintering that rang out over the frozen Flats like a bell. Then jars. Slag-jars, dozens of them, in crates. He tipped the crates over the edge and the jars went rolling and clinking down into the blue glow and broke. Then he took a shovel from the side of the cart and climbed down into the pit and began to smash them. Steadily. Without hurrying. Crunch. Crunch. Crunch.
And then the other crates.
They were at the bottom of the load. Under everything else. Wooden crates, square and heavy, bigger than the jar-crates, and they were nailed shut. The man had to lever them off the back of the cart with the end of his shovel, one at a time, grunting. Six of them. He did not open them. He pushed each one to the edge of the pit, and over, and it went down and landed with a heavy dead thump, and split, and from the split came a spill of gray-glittering dust, like flour from a torn sack, and a glow. A deeper glow. Not the pale wavering blue of the rest. A dark, heavy, steady blue, almost violet, like the blue at the bottom of a flame.
The rod in Corin's pocket jerked.
Not hummed. Jerked. As if something had pulled on it, hard, from the inside. He gasped, and clamped his hand down on it through his coat, and held it.
No, said Malakor. Sharp as a blade. No. Not that. Hold it, boy. Hold it.
"What is it?" said Corin through his teeth.
Old, said Malakor. Very old. Older than the rest. That is not slag from the intake. That is not from this year or the last or the ten before that. His voice was very strange. That has been down in the dark for a long, long time. Somewhere very deep. Somewhere nobody was meant to open again.
The man with the shovel climbed out of the pit. He threw a few shovelfuls of frozen mud down onto the glowing heap, not many, just enough. He climbed back up onto the seat of the cart. He shook the reins, and the piebald horse turned slowly, and the cart went away across the Flats and up the long rutted track to the river road, creaking, with its one dim lamp swinging under the axle, and was gone.
At the foot of the chapel bank, in the dark, the first rush-light flickered back into life.
Then another. And another.
Corin did not wait for the children. He got up from behind the rim of the fourth pit, and walked across the frozen Flats in the moonlight, toward the sixth, with Doctor Silas hurrying behind him saying harvester, harvester, wait, and stood on the rim and looked down at the six split crates in the bottom.
One of them had landed face up.
There was a stamp on its lid. Burned into the wood, black, the way they burned marks into barrels at the docks. He could see it clearly in the moonlight, and in the dark violet glow coming up out of the split.
An eye. Closed.
And under the eye, two letters. Burned deep.
