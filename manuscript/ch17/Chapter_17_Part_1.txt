Chapter 17: Slag-Fever, Part 1 of 5: Over the Wall
Over the Wall
The hay store was black as the inside of a boot.
They went through it by feel. Juno first, with one hand on the hay on the left and the other held out in front of her. Then Lyra. Then Corin, with his hand on Lyra's shoulder, because he could see nothing at all, not the hay, not the floor, not his own hand in front of his face. The great golden blocks of hay that had looked like cliffs in the daylight were only a smell now, dusty and sweet and warm, pressing in on both sides of the narrow passage. Somewhere in the dark, a rat ran across his boot and was gone.
Behind them, through the wooden walls, he could hear the Watch.
The hammering on the gate had stopped. That was worse. Now there were voices in the yard. Rough ones, loud ones, half a dozen of them, and boots on the cobbles, and the clatter of somebody falling over the stone trough under the snow and swearing. Lantern light went past a crack in the wall beside his face, yellow, swinging, and was gone. A voice said, very close, just the other side of the planks, "Up the stairs. Up the stairs, you two. And mind the old soldier, the Sergeant says he's a big one."
And then another voice. Deeper. Slow. Like a cart going over gravel.
"You'll mind your boots on my lord's floor."
Oren.
Lyra's shoulder, under Corin's hand, went hard as wood. She did not stop walking.
The little door at the end of the hay store was frozen shut. Juno put her shoulder against it, and then her whole body, and it gave with a crack like a shot that made all three of them freeze. They stood very still in the dark, not breathing, listening.
Nothing. The voices in the yard went on. Nobody came.
They went out into the night.
The cold hit them like a slap. It was much colder than it had been at sunset. The sky had cleared completely, and the stars were out, hard and bright and white, thousands of them, and there was no moon yet, and the air was so still and so cold that it hurt to breathe. The slush of the afternoon had frozen. Every puddle was a sheet of black glass. Every rut in the mud was a ridge of iron. The snow on top of the high brick wall at the end of the passage, that had been soft and wet when they climbed it yesterday, was a hard white crust now, like the sugar on a cake.
Juno went up the wall first. Lyra went up after her, quick and neat, the way she went up everything, and sat on the top for a moment against the stars, and reached down a hand.
Corin took it.
He did not climb the wall so much as get dragged up it, scraping, with his long gray coat catching on every brick and the frozen crust cracking under his knees. At the top he lay flat on his stomach on the ice for a moment, gasping, with one leg on each side. Lyra was already over. He heard her land, softly, on the far side. Then he rolled, and fell, and landed on his back in a snowdrift at the bottom with all the breath knocked out of him.
Juno's face looked down at him, upside down, against the stars.
She did not say nobody falls off walls except you. She did not say anything. She held out her hand, and pulled him up, and they ran.
The dye-yard was different at night.
In the daylight it had been a strange, almost pretty place, full of colors, with the steam going up from the warm vats and the snow turning red and blue and yellow where it fell into them. At night it was a maze of black shapes. The great round vats stood up in their rows like the stumps of enormous trees, and the boards over them were white with frost, and between them the paths were black ice. Most of the vats had gone cold. But a few were still warm underneath, and from these a thin pale steam was still rising, very slowly, into the starlight, and hanging in the still air in ghostly columns, like people standing quite still among the vats, watching.
Corin did not look at them.
They ran between the vats, slipping, catching at the cold wet wood to keep their feet. Lyra ran with one hand on her sword under her coat to keep it from swinging. Juno ran ahead, small and quick, never looking back. Behind them, over the high wall, the voices in Pruett's yard had got louder. Somebody was shouting. And then, suddenly, very clear, a man's voice from much nearer, from the top of the wall itself, as if somebody had climbed up to look:
"Footprints! Sergeant! There's footprints on the wall!"
They ran faster.
At the end of the dye-yard there was the broken fence, and the gap in it, and Juno went through the gap like a cat through a cat-flap. Lyra went through it sideways. Corin got his coat caught on a nail and tore it and did not stop. And then they were in the alley behind the workshops, black and narrow and stinking of the dye works, with frozen puddles of red and blue along the bottom of it like spilled jewels.
"Left," said Juno, without stopping. "Then down. Then the steps. Then the Cut."
"They'll follow the footprints," said Lyra. She was breathing hard.
"They'll follow them to the dye-yard," said Juno. "And then they'll lose them on the ice. And then they'll stand about arguing." She sniffed. "Watchmen always stand about arguing. Gran says it's the only exercise they get."
They went down the long flight of steps cut into the hill, with the iron rail so cold that it took the skin off Corin's fingers through his gloves. They went along the bottom of the hill, by the backs of tall dark houses, where nobody had lit a lamp. They went across a little square where an old stone fountain stood frozen in the middle, with its water caught in midair in long glassy ropes, like a beard of ice, and its basin full of a solid sheet of gray that a dog was standing on, licking.
And then they were in the Cut.
Corin knew it from the Ledger. He did not know it from his feet. His feet had never brought him here on their own. The Cut, the Ledger said, in a page from years ago, in a steadier version of his own hand. Where the city goes when it has nowhere else to go. Tanneries and lye-works and soap-boilers and doss-houses. Nobody asks your name. Nobody wants to know it.
It was a deep crooked gully that ran down between the hills to the river, where a stream had once been, long ago, before the city built over it. The stream was still there. Corin could hear it, under the cobbles, under the houses, running in the dark somewhere underneath them, gurgling. And on both sides of it, all the way down, crammed into the gully so close that they leaned on each other and over each other and sometimes into each other, there were houses. Old ones. Black ones. Made of wood and patched with tin and stuffed with rags. Stairs went up the outsides of them, wooden stairs, rickety, at every angle, and walkways went from one to the next, and ropes, and planks, so that the whole of the Cut was a sort of great black tangled web hanging in the dark between the two hills.
There were lights in it. Not blue ones. Nobody in the Cut could afford a gemstone lamp. Yellow ones. Candles and rush-lights and tallow dips and the red glow of stoves through cracks in the walls, hundreds of them, small and dim, scattered up and down the dark web like sparks.
And it smelled. Even in the cold. Even with everything frozen. Lye, sharp and bitter, that caught at the back of the throat. Wet ash. Old fat. Smoke. Too many people too close together for too long.
"Nobody'll look here," said Juno. "Not tonight. Watchmen don't come in the Cut after dark. Not unless there's six of them." She looked up and down the dark crooked lane. "But we can't stop. Not in the street. Somebody'll see a Ministry coat and a lady with a sword and sell it by morning." She looked at Corin. "Where, Mister V?"
Corin did not know.
He stood in the frozen lane in the Cut with his torn coat and his skinned fingers and his breath going up white in the starlight, and he did not know. Not Tallow Street. The Watch would be at Mrs. Marta's door within the hour, if they had any sense at all. Not Vespera's. Not after today. Not Bram's. Not the Ministry. Not Hook Lane, or Quill Row, or anywhere his feet had ever walked him, because anywhere his feet knew, other people knew too.
"Doctor Silas," said Lyra.
Corin turned.
She was standing a little way off, against the black wall of a house, with her coat buttoned to her chin and her breath coming in quick white puffs. Her face was very pale in the starlight. Her eyes were very steady.
And at the name, something in Corin had gone cold and tight and small. Silas. A pale man on a counter, swinging his foot. Barley sugar. A wrapper folded smaller and smaller and smaller.
Lyra saw his face.
"Not him," she said. "Not Kross. Another Silas. A doctor. A street doctor." She pushed herself away from the wall. "He has a cellar, down at the bottom of the Cut. Near the river. Under an old chapel. Oren goes to him." Her mouth moved, very slightly. "When Oren fights. He fights, sometimes, for money. In yards. At night. Duels. It's not allowed." She paused. "Doctor Silas sews him up afterward, and doesn't ask, and doesn't tell. He's the only doctor in the city Oren trusts." Another pause. "He's the only doctor in the city who'd take a Thorne girl in tonight and not run straight up the Hill to the Watch with her."
"Do you know where?" said Corin.
"I went once. With Oren. Last winter. When he came home with his arm open from the elbow to the wrist and wouldn't let me send for anyone." Lyra looked down the dark crooked lane, toward the river, toward the bottom of the Cut, where the yellow sparks thinned out and the black web of houses gave way to something flatter and darker. "I held the lamp."
She started walking.
Juno looked at Corin. Corin looked at Juno.
Then they followed her down into the dark.
The Chapel in the Cut
At the bottom of the Cut, where the gully opened out onto the river, there was a chapel with no roof.
It stood on its own, on a little rise of ground above the mud flats, with its back to the black web of the Cut and its face to the river. It was small and old and made of gray stone, much older than anything round it, older than the tanneries and the doss-houses and the soap-boilers, as if it had been standing here first, alone, on a green bank by a clear stream, before any of them came. It had a pointed door and two pointed windows and a little stone tower at one end where a bell had once hung. The bell was gone. The tower was open to the sky, and the stars showed through its empty arches like eyes. The roof was gone too. Only the tops of the walls were left, ragged, with weeds growing out of them, frozen stiff now and white with frost, like gray hair standing on end.
The snow lay inside the chapel as well as outside. You could see it through the empty windows, lying smooth and white on the stone floor, where the people had once sat.
"Saint Bride's," said Lyra. "Oren says the stream was holy once. People came from all over the city to drink from it. Before the tanneries."
She did not go in at the pointed door. She went round the side of the chapel, along the wall, through the frozen weeds that crackled under their boots like paper, to the back. To the side that faced the river. Where the ground dropped away steeply toward the mud, and there was a flight of stone steps going down into the dark under the chapel itself.
At the bottom of the steps there was a door. Low, and heavy, and black, with iron studs. And from under it, and round the edges of it, there came a thin line of yellow light. And a smell. Sharp and clean and strong. Vinegar, and something else, something that bit at the inside of Corin's nose like the cold had.
And a sound. Very faint. Coming through the thick black wood.
A child's voice, talking. Not crying. Talking. Quickly, steadily, in a high thin voice, without stopping, the way a child talks in its sleep.
Lyra knocked. Three times, slow. Then twice quick.
"Oren's knock," she said, very low.
The child's voice behind the door went on talking. Nothing else happened for a long time.
Then a bolt went back. And the door opened, the width of a hand, and a face looked out.
It was a long face. Long and thin and gray, gray with tiredness rather than age, with deep hollows under the cheekbones and deep lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth, and two or three days of gray stubble on the jaw. The nose was long too, and thin, and a little crooked, as if it had been broken once and set by somebody in a hurry. On it, there was a pair of small round spectacles in a wire frame. One arm of the frame had snapped, and been mended with a twist of black thread, so that the spectacles sat a little crooked. Behind them, the eyes were gray and red-rimmed and very sharp.
The eyes went to Lyra. To her short black hair. To her face.
"Ah," said the man. His voice was dry and quiet and rather hoarse, like the voice of a man who has been talking softly to sick people for a very long time. "Captain Vance's young lady. Who held the lamp." He paused. "I wondered if you'd come."
"Doctor Silas," said Lyra.
"Miss Thorne." The gray eyes moved past her. To Juno. To Corin. To Corin's long gray coat, torn at the hem, with the frost on its shoulders. They stayed there. "And a harvester. In a Ministry coat. On my step. At ten o'clock at night." He let out a long breath through his crooked nose. "Of course. Of course you've brought a harvester. Why wouldn't you."
"He's a friend," said Lyra.
"I'm sure he is." Doctor Silas did not open the door any wider. "I read the broadsheet, Miss Thorne. This evening. The fishmonger on the corner wrapped my herring in it. The doctor was murdered. I saw her face." His mouth twisted. "I had a good long look at it while I was eating my supper. And then I thought, she'll come here. She'll come here, because there's nowhere else, and because the Captain told her once that I don't ask questions." He looked at her over the top of his crooked spectacles. "And I was right. And here you are."
Lyra did not answer. She stood on the bottom step, in the cold, with her hand on the hilt of her sword under her coat, and looked at him.
Behind the doctor, in the yellow light, the child's voice went on talking. —and the boats came in at night, all with their lamps lit, and my father was on the first one, and he had a red cap—
"Did you?" said Doctor Silas.
Lyra's chin came up.
"Did I what?"
"Kill Matthias Aske." His voice was perfectly flat. "On the landing under the Lantern Bridge. Two years ago. With the hilt of a sword." He did not take his eyes off her. "I'd like to know. Before I let you in. I don't ask many questions, Miss Thorne. That's true. But I'll ask that one."
"No," said Lyra.
She said it very quietly. She did not say anything else. She did not explain, or argue, or swear, or cry. She stood on the step with her chin up and her gray eyes on his face and said no, once, and waited.
Doctor Silas looked at her for a long time.
Then he looked at Corin.
"And you, harvester," he said. "Do you know who did?"
"Yes," said Corin.
The doctor's gray eyes narrowed behind the crooked spectacles.
"How?"
"I've seen it," said Corin. "In Ash. A dead man's. Someone who was there."
There was a silence. The child behind the door had stopped talking. Somewhere further in, another child coughed, a thick, wet, tearing cough that went on and on.
"He was my teacher," said Doctor Silas.
He said it quite quietly. Not to them. To the dark over their heads, perhaps. To the frozen stars.
"Matthias Aske. When I was a boy at the Ministry hospital, learning to set bones. He taught me how to stitch a wound so it wouldn't pull. He taught me how to tell a fever that would break from one that wouldn't. He taught me that a doctor's first duty is to the patient, and his second is to the patient, and there isn't a third." He was quiet a moment. "And then they told me he'd gone to his sister's in the country. And six months later, a clerk I know at the Ministry told me he'd come out of the river under the Lantern Bridge, nine days before a black carriage went up the Hill to the Thorne house. And that his file had been closed. Misadventure."
He looked down at Lyra.
"He didn't have a sister either," he said.
And he opened the door.
The cellar under Saint Bride's had been a crypt, once.
You could see that. It was long and low and vaulted, with a ceiling of rough stone arches held up on short thick pillars, and in the walls between the pillars there were deep stone shelves, long and narrow, the length of a man. The dead had lain on those shelves, a long time ago, in their winding sheets. The dead were gone now. The shelves were full of other things. Bottles. Jars. Rolls of bandage. Folded blankets. Bunches of dried herbs, hanging. A row of tin basins. A box of candles. A human skull, very old and very brown, with a candle stuck in the top of it, burning, so that the light came out through the empty eyes.
And down the middle of the crypt, between the pillars, in two long rows, there were beds.
They were not proper beds. They were trestles, the kind that coffins were laid on in a church, with planks across them and straw pallets on the planks and blankets on the straw. There were sixteen of them. Eight down each side. And there was a child on every one.
Corin stopped just inside the door.
They were all young. The oldest might have been twelve. The youngest could not have been more than four. They lay on the straw pallets under the gray blankets, some still, some tossing, some sitting up with their knees under their chins, and every one of them was flushed. Red in the face, the deep hot red of fever, with bright glassy eyes and cracked lips. The air in the crypt was warm, because there was a stove at the far end, roaring. But it was a sick warm. Thick, and close, and smelling of vinegar and sweat and the bitter clean smell that bit the nose, and under it all, very faint, something else. Something Corin knew.
The smell of the slag room.
Cold glass. Dust. Thunder that had not come yet.
And they were talking.
Not all of them. But most. Softly, steadily, in the high thin voices of children, every one of them saying something different, all at once, so that the whole long crypt was full of a low murmuring, like the sound of a crowd heard through a wall. And as Corin stood by the door and listened, he began to hear the words. And they were not children's words.
—sixpence a yard, and not a farthing less, and you can tell your master—
—the boats came in at night, all with their lamps lit—
—my wedding day, it rained, and Tom said rain on a wedding is good luck, and I said—
—left, left, left, right, left—
—Mother, Mother, the house is on fire, the house is—
A girl of about six, on the nearest bed, with her hair in two thin braids and her face scarlet with fever, was sitting up against the wall, looking straight at him with her bright glassy eyes. And she was saying, very clearly, in a voice that was not hers at all, a deep slow tired voice, a man's voice, an old man's:
"—forty years on the docks, and what've I got to show for it, two bad knees and a cough and a daughter who won't speak to me—"
Corin could not move.
"Slag-fever," said Doctor Silas beside him.
He had shut the door, and bolted it, and come to stand at Corin's shoulder, with his long thin hands in the pockets of an old coat. The coat had been a good one once. Dark gray, long, with a high collar, the kind the Ministry gave to its doctors. On the left breast, where a badge should have been, there was a patch of cloth that was darker than the rest, the shape of a small shield, where a badge had been taken off.
"Sixteen of them," said the doctor. "Tonight. Last week, eleven. The week before, seven." He looked down the long rows of beds, at the flushed faces and the bright eyes and the moving lips. "Every week, more. Since the summer." His voice was very quiet. "And every one of them is talking in somebody else's voice, harvester. Somebody they never met. About a life they never had."
He turned his head and looked at Corin. At his gray coat, and his gloves, and the long thin shape of the rod in its pocket.
"You work the slag," he said. "Don't you. At the Lantern Quay. In the cold room under the intake hall. Clearing the glasses."
Corin did not answer.
"I can smell it on you," said Doctor Silas. "The same as I smell it on them." He took his hands out of his pockets. They were long and thin and very clean, and they were quite steady. "Sit down, harvester. Miss Thorne. Little one. There's tea. There's always tea." He jerked his head at the stove at the far end of the crypt. "And then I'll tell you where it goes. All that slag you clear. Every morning. Glass after glass after glass." He paused. "Because I think you ought to know."
