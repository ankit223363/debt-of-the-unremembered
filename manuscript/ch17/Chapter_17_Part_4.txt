Chapter 17: Slag-Fever, Part 4 of 5: Four in the Morning
Four in the Morning
Pip ate two slices of bread and dripping and half a cold potato and a lump of brown sugar out of the doctor's tea tin, and then she lay down on the straw with her hand curled under her cheek, and went to sleep. A real sleep. Quiet and deep and easy, with her mouth a little open and her breath going slow. The gray had gone out of her face. It was only pale now. A thin, dirty, ordinary child's face, asleep.
The doctor stood over her for a long time, looking down.
Then he went to the shelf at the back of the crypt and came back with a jar. A thick brown medicine jar with a wide mouth and a cork, the kind that ointment came in. He held it out to Corin without a word.
Corin was sitting on the floor, with his back against the end of Pip's trestle. He had not been able to get up. He had tried twice, and his legs had folded under him like wet paper, and Lyra had said sit, for Saints' sake, sit, and he had sat. He was still shaking. Not as badly now. A deep, slow, steady shiver, that came and went in waves, like the shivering of a man who has been in cold water for a long time and come out.
The rod lay across his knees. It was still glowing. Deep violet, from end to end, dark and steady and cold. The frost had spread from it, onto the cloth of his trousers, in thin white feathers.
He took the jar. He took the cork out with his teeth. He held the mouth of the jar to the tip of the rod, the way he did every evening in the slag room with the day's crumbs, and he tipped the rod, and let it go.
It came out slowly. Not like dust. Not like slag. Like honey. Thick and heavy and dark, a deep glowing violet, sliding out of the carved spiral tip of the rod and down into the brown jar in a long slow thread, and settling in the bottom, and filling it. A quarter. A half. More. It did not stop for a long time. And as it went, the rod got lighter in his hand, and warmer, and the violet went out of the silver a little at a time, from the handle toward the tip, like a lamp being turned down.
When it was done, the jar was nearly full. It glowed in his hands. Deep violet, dark and slow and steady. And it hummed. Very faintly. A single long low note, like someone singing with their mouth shut, a long way off.
He pushed the cork back in. He sat with the jar in his lap and looked at it.
"What will you do with it?" said Doctor Silas.
"Keep it."
The doctor looked at him over his crooked spectacles.
"It's the only one," said Corin. He did not know how to say it better. "Isn't it. That memory. That night. The man in the plain coat in the field, and the gold coming out of his hands, and the people shouting." He swallowed. "Somebody cut his name out of it. Out of the stone, and out of the bridge, and out of an old man's head on a hill. Somebody went to a great deal of trouble." He looked at the violet glow. "And then they put what was left in a crate, and nailed it shut, and buried it so deep that nobody would ever find it. And then, this summer, somebody dug it up, and put it on a cart, and paid Moggs a silver to tip it in the mud." He held the jar a little tighter. "So that it would break. And get trodden in. And rot. And be gone."
The doctor said nothing.
"Somebody wants it gone," said Corin. "So I'm keeping it."
He asked the doctor for a pencil. The doctor gave him one, from his coat pocket, a stub worn down almost to nothing. And on a scrap of paper torn from the margin of an old broadsheet, Corin wrote a label, and tied it round the neck of the jar with a piece of bandage.
PIP. THE SIXTH PIT. THE KING'S SKY.
The doctor went to sleep at half past three.
He did not mean to. He sat down on his upturned crate by the stove to rest his eyes for a moment, with his long legs stretched out and his chin on his chest, and he did not get up again. His crooked spectacles slid slowly down his crooked nose. His breathing went slow and heavy. After a while, he began to snore, very quietly, like a kettle a long way off.
Juno was asleep already. She had curled up on the floor by the warm brick wall of the stove, with her cap over her face and her knees under her chin, like a cat. Twice she muttered something in her sleep, and once she laughed.
The children on the trestle beds murmured on, in their strangers' voices, softer now. All with their lamps lit. Mind the step.
Lyra had not slept. She had pulled her stool round to the stove, near where Corin sat on the floor against Pip's bed, and she sat on it with her elbows on her knees and her chin in her hands, looking at the fire through the open door of the stove. The red light lay on her face. Her short black hair stuck up at the back. Her filthy brown coat was unbuttoned, and he could see the bootlace at her throat, and the little hard shape of the blue stone under her collar.
She did not say anything for a long time.
Then: "It was an oak."
Corin looked up at her.
"In the drawing," said Lyra. "The tree. In the middle of the valley. It was an oak." She did not look away from the fire. "I know oak leaves. There's a rubbing in my pocketbook. I took it from the door frame in the old nursery at Crown Walk, with a bit of charcoal, on Monday. Where somebody gouged out a name, a long time ago. The third name. Above mine." Her voice was very quiet. "There was a W. And next to it, somebody had drawn a little leaf. Very small. With a pencil."
She was quiet a moment.
"It was an oak leaf too," she said. "The same shape. Exactly. Little rounded fingers."
Corin did not say anything. He did not know what to say. He sat on the stone floor with the jar of violet in his lap and looked at her face in the firelight.
"And the buttons," said Lyra. "Faber's buttons. Pell's button. The button woman's. All oak leaves. From the village with the big oak, past the end of the maps." She looked at him at last. Her gray eyes were very bright. "You were born in a village with a big oak. Weren't you. It's on the first page. You were born in Oakhaven."
"Yes."
"So am I going mad," said Lyra, "or is it all the same tree?"
Corin looked down at the jar.
"I don't know," he said. "The drawing on the wall—the man in the field, the gold—Malakor says it's old. Very old. Older than anything. Older than me, or you, or my village, or yours." He turned the jar slowly in his hands. "And I don't remember Oakhaven. Not anything. Not the tree, not the river, not the schoolhouse. Only the name. Only what's written." He was quiet. "So I don't know if it's the same tree. I don't know if it's the same valley. I don't know if there's only one oak in the world, or a thousand."
"But you think."
"I think," said Corin slowly, "that somebody's been cutting names out of things for a very long time. And I think they missed some leaves."
The stove ticked. The doctor snored. Somewhere outside, very far away, the river groaned as the tide turned and the ice began to shift.
"Lyra," said Corin.
She waited.
"There are two ways," he said. "To stop Monday. Two that I know. I should have told you before. In the long room. But there wasn't time."
And he told her.
He told her about the dome. About the woman in violet by the fire, under the glass, with her necklace of colored lights. About Lord Pembrook, and his debts, and his port. A mistake on a paper. A wrong date. A missing seal. Three months. Four. Until the spring. Until she came of age, and was the nearest family, and Cressida was not.
"For what?" said Lyra.
"For my Ledgers," said Corin. "The old ones. One to six."
Lyra went very still on her stool.
He told her about Bram. About a strongbox under a rag rug, and a pressed lily, and a scrap of black lace. About six years of his life in a big black untidy hand that went through the paper. About tide tables. They are not only his.
And he told her about Toby. About fifteen thin cheap forms in the back cover of a book of regulations, with the Archon's own seal printed on the bottom of every one, by mistake. About a file on the second floor, in the Wards room, east side, in a locked cabinet by the door. 2—7—14—6. About a thin pale boy with ink on his fingers who had said, I'm not brave. I'm not like you. But I would.
And about an old man with a broken nose, leaning across a workbench at midnight. If it goes wrong, it won't be you they come for first. It'll be him.
When he had finished, Lyra did not say anything for a long time.
She sat on her stool and looked at the fire. Her hands, in her lap, had closed round each other, the knuckles white.
"No," she said at last.
Corin waited.
"No to the first," said Lyra. "No to Vivienne. No to the dome." She did not look at him. "Six years of you, for three months of him? So that she can read them, and know everything you ever saw, and hold it over you for the rest of your life? No." Her mouth moved. "Father wouldn't want that. If he could want anything. He'd say it was a bad trade. He was very good at trades, before. He'd say you never give away a thing you can't get back, to buy a thing you'll only have to buy again."
"And Toby?"
Lyra was quiet for longer.
"No," she said. "Not yet." She swallowed. "Not if it lands on him. Not if they'll go down to his desk and take him away and empty him out, the way they did Faber's eye, the way they did Tam's mother, because he was kind to a Thorne girl. I won't. I won't have another one, Corin. Not another one, because of us." Her voice cracked, just once, and came back hard. "There's been Bertram. There's been the doctor. There's been Hester, on the Salt Stairs, with her feet in the river. There'll be the red-haired girl, crying for her mam." She looked at him at last. "Not Toby. Not unless there's nothing else. Not unless it's the very last thing, on Monday morning, with the carriage in the lane."
Corin looked at her for a long moment.
"Then what?" he said.
Lyra looked back at the fire.
"Then we make the truth louder," she said.
She said it quite quietly. As if she were only just working it out, as she said it.
"That's what she's done, isn't it. My aunt. She's taken a lie and made it so loud that the truth can't be heard over it. A girl with clean arms, in the Watch house. A broadsheet on every corner. My poor girl." Her jaw was tight. "So we need something louder. Something nobody can argue with. Not a harvester's word. Not a jar of Ash. Not a Thorne girl who climbs roofs." She was thinking hard. Corin could see it. "Hester. Hester was there. In the white room. She saw the screen and the needle man and the other girls. She heard my aunt's voice. And Vespera has the ink. All of it. In a jar on a shelf, with Hollis's memories in it."
"Who'd believe Hester? She's a laundry girl."
"So is Agnes Fell," said Lyra. "And they believed her."
Corin opened his mouth. Then shut it again.
"And somebody in that white room," said Lyra, "put a little northern tree in the last line of a lie. On purpose. Malakor said so. Somebody who wants me not hanged." She looked at him sideways. "That's a friend, Corin. Or at least it's somebody who isn't on her side. Somewhere. In her own house."
The stove ticked. The fire was going down. Lyra reached out, without looking, and took a lump of coal from the scuttle and put it in, and the flames came up round it, red and gold, and lit her face.
"Two days," she said. "Saturday. Sunday. And then Monday at noon." She shut the stove door. "I don't know how yet. But I'd rather lose fighting with the truth than win with the dome's help or Toby's neck."
She sat back on her stool.
And then, very quietly, so quietly that Corin almost did not hear it over the doctor's snoring and the children's murmuring and the tick of the stove:
"Thank you. For telling me. All of it."
"I should have told you before."
"Yes," said Lyra. "You should." Something that was almost a smile moved the corner of her mouth, and went. "Write it down. So you remember you owe me."
The Black Bag
Doctor Silas woke at six, all at once, the way men wake who have spent their lives being woken by somebody knocking.
He sat up on his crate with a jerk, and his crooked spectacles fell off his nose into his lap, and he looked round the crypt with wide startled gray eyes as if he did not know where he was. Then he saw the beds. And his face settled. He put his spectacles back on, and got up, stiffly, and went down the rows one by one, touching foreheads, lifting wrists, listening to chests. He did not say anything. He did it the way Corin read his Ledger in the morning. Every day. The same. To find out what was still true.
When he got to Pip, he stood for a long time.
She was still asleep. Curled on her side, with her hand under her cheek. Her face was pale and quiet. The doctor laid two fingers on her forehead, and held them there, and took them away, and stood looking down at her.
Then he turned round, and looked at Corin.
Corin had not slept either. He had sat on the floor against the end of Pip's bed all night, with the brown jar in his lap, glowing violet, and the rod across his knees. Lyra had sat on her stool by the stove. They had not talked much after four o'clock. Once, she had got up and put more coal on. Once, he had taken out the Ledger and started to write, and found that his hand would not stop shaking long enough to make the letters, and put it away again.
"Your hands," said Doctor Silas.
Corin looked down at them. They were lying on the jar. They were shaking. Not much. A small, steady, fine tremble, like the shiver of a leaf when there is no wind.
"It'll pass," he said.
"It won't," said the doctor. "Not on its own. Not after what you did." He went to the shelf, and came back with a small brown bottle, and pulled out the cork with his teeth, and held it out. "Two swallows. Not three. It tastes like a wet dog and it'll make you sleepy by noon. But it'll stop the shaking."
Corin took the bottle. He looked at it.
"I sell it," said Doctor Silas. "Up and down the Cut, a ha'penny a bottle, to burners whose hands have gone. Old soldiers. Dock mages. Women who light the lamps on the bridge and have burned the color out of their eyes doing it." His mouth twisted. "It's the only thing I make that people will pay for. The Ministry says it's not medicine. The Ministry says nobody's hands shake from burning."
Corin drank two swallows. It tasted like a wet dog.
The doctor watched him. Then he took the bottle back, and corked it, and put it in Corin's coat pocket.
"Keep it," he said. "You'll need it again. People like you always do."
He did not go back to the beds.
He stood in front of Corin, with his long thin hands hanging at his sides, and looked down at him, and at Lyra on her stool by the stove, and at the brown jar glowing in Corin's lap. And his long gray tired face was working at something. Corin could see it. The way Oren's face worked when he was reaching for a word.
"You said you'd seen it," said Doctor Silas. "Last night. On the step. You said you'd seen who killed Matthias Aske. In Ash. A dead man's."
"Yes."
"Whose?"
Corin looked at Lyra. Lyra looked back at him. Then she nodded, very slightly.
"Bertram Ashcombe," said Corin. "Steward to Lord Thorne. Twenty-nine years. He was there. On the landing under the bridge. Hiding behind some crates against the wall." He swallowed. "He saw all of it. And then he went home and kept quiet for two years, because he was frightened. And then he died."
Doctor Silas went very still.
"Bertram," he said. "The steward. Old, with a gray face, very neat. A pipe."
"Yes."
"He was there."
"Yes. Behind the crates."
The doctor did not say anything for a long time. Then he turned round, without a word, and went to the far end of the crypt, past the stove, to the low dark doorway in the back wall that led into the old bone-room. He went in. Corin heard him moving about in there, in the dark. Something heavy being dragged. A grunt. The scrape of stone on stone.
When he came out again, he was carrying a bag.
It was a doctor's bag. Black leather, old, worn soft and gray at the corners, with a brass clasp on top and a brass plate under the clasp. The leather had gone dull with dust. The brass had gone green. And the clasp was not just shut. It was tied. With a length of thin red tape, the kind lawyers used, wound round and round the handles and knotted, very tight, and the knot was sealed with a blob of red wax.
The doctor set it down on the end of Pip's bed, very gently, as if it might break.
Corin bent to look at the brass plate.
M. ASKE. PHYSICIAN.
"He came to my rooms," said Doctor Silas. "On the Hill. Two years ago. In the autumn. The evening before he went to his sister in the country."
He sat down on his crate. He did not take his eyes off the bag.
"He'd never come to my rooms before. Not once, in twenty years. He wasn't that kind of man. He'd see you at the hospital, every day, and nod, and correct your stitches, and that was all. And then one wet night in October there was a knock on my door, and there he was. In his coat and hat. With this." He nodded at the bag. "And he looked…" He stopped. "He looked frightened. I'd never seen him frightened. I'd seen him cut a man's leg off by candlelight in a ditch with the man screaming and his hands never shook. And he stood in my doorway with the rain dripping off his hat, and his face was the color of ash."
The stove ticked. Lyra had not moved on her stool.
"He gave me the bag," said the doctor. "He'd tied it up already. Just like that. Tape and wax. And he said, Keep this for me, Silas. Put it somewhere safe. Don't open it. And I said, What's in it? And he said, My books. My casebooks. The household ones. Twenty years of them." He was quiet a moment. "And then he said, If I come back for it, give it to me, and don't ask. And if I don't come back for it—" The doctor stopped again. His long thin hands had closed into fists on his knees. "If I don't come back for it, don't open it till you know why."
"And you didn't," said Lyra.
"And I didn't." Doctor Silas looked at her. "He went away in the rain. And the next week the hospital said he'd gone to his sister. And six months later a clerk I know told me he'd come out of the river. Nine days before a black carriage went up the Hill." His voice was very flat. "And I thought, well, now I know he isn't coming back. But I don't know why. Not really. Misadventure, the file said. And I thought, I'll know why when somebody tells me. When somebody comes who knows." He looked at the bag. "And they took my badge. And I came down here. And I brought it with me, and I put it in the bone-room behind the stone, and every night for two years I've sat by this stove and not opened it."
He looked at Corin.
"And now you've come," he said. "And you know."
He untied the tape himself.
He did it very slowly, with his long thin steady fingers, picking at the knot. He did not break the seal. He worked the knot loose round it, the way Bram had worked the flap of the envelope loose round the black wax, so that the red seal came away whole, still stuck to the tape. Then he unwound the tape. Round and round. And laid it on the blanket. And undid the brass clasp.
The bag opened with a creak, like an old door.
Inside, there were books.
Small ones. A dozen of them, perhaps more, standing upright side by side, packed tight. Thin black notebooks, the kind doctors carried in their coat pockets, bound in cloth gone shiny with handling, each one with a white paper label pasted on its spine, and on each label, in a small neat sloping hand, two years. Year 1 — Year 2. Year 3 — Year 4. All the way along. And on the front of every one, the same three words.
THORNE. CROWN WALK.
Doctor Silas did not take any of them out. He looked at them for a long moment. Then he looked at Lyra.
"These are yours, I think," he said. "More than mine. Your family's. Twenty years of every cough and fever and broken arm in your house." He moved back from the bag. "You should be the one."
Lyra got up off her stool.
She came to the end of the bed. She stood looking down into the open bag, at the row of thin black books, with her hands at her sides. Then she reached in, and took out the last one. The one at the end of the row. The newest.
It was thinner than the others. Only half full. She opened it at the back, at the last pages, where the writing stopped. And she read.
Corin watched her face.
It did not change at first. She read the last page, slowly, her lips moving very slightly. Then she turned back a page, and read that. Then back another. And then her face did change. It went very still. And then very white.
She held the book out to Corin without a word.
He took it. His hands were not shaking now. The doctor's tonic had done that much. He held the thin black book open in the lamplight and read where her finger had been.
The hand was small and neat and sloping, a careful hand, a doctor's hand, used to writing quickly by bad light. But near the end it was not neat. It had begun to slope more steeply, and press harder, and in places the pen had caught and spattered.
It was dated. Two years ago. The middle of October.
Called to Crown Walk at 7. H.L. (His Lordship) tired, poor appetite, will not say why. Pulse quick. Asks me again about the W. book. I tell him it is safe. He says, "Keep it safe, Matthias. Whatever happens. She is asking." I do not ask who.
The next entry. Two days later.
Councillor C. came to my rooms this evening, uninvited. Very charming. Brought flowers. Lilies. Asked whether I still kept my old books on the household. Asked, as if it were nothing, whether I remembered "poor little W." and where she had gone. I said I was a physician, not a postman. She laughed. She said, "Of course, Doctor. Of course." She left the lilies. I have put them out in the yard. The smell is in everything.
And the last.
I will not write down where the child went. I never have. Not in any book. It is in my head, and it will stay there.
B. sent word by the boy. He wishes to speak with me, privately, about H.L. and "what is coming." He says the house is not safe. He says Councillor C. has servants in it now. He asks me to meet him under the Lantern Bridge, at the landing on the riverside, at eleven tomorrow night. He says nobody goes there.
I am taking these books to S. tonight. If B. is right, they should not be in my rooms.
If I am wrong about all of this, I will laugh at myself on Thursday and fetch them back.
M.A.
And under that, nothing. The rest of the pages were blank.
Corin closed the book.
He sat on the floor of the crypt with the thin black book in his hands, and he did not say anything for a long time. Nobody did. The children murmured on the trestle beds. The stove ticked. The doctor sat on his crate with his face in his hands.
At the landing on the riverside, at eleven tomorrow night. He says nobody goes there.
And a little round man, in a dead steward's memory, kneeling on that landing in the light of a low yellow lantern. And an old steward with a gray face, crouched behind a pile of crates against the wall, in the dark, where he had come to meet him. Too late. Watching.
Bertram saw.
Bertram had not been there by chance. Bertram had asked him to come.
"He went to meet Bertram," said Lyra. Very low. Her voice was not steady. "Doctor Aske. That night. He went to the bridge because Bertram asked him to." She put her hand over her mouth. "And she was there first."
"She knew," said Corin. "Somebody told her. The servants. Councillor C. has servants in it now." He looked down at the book. "She knew where Bertram had asked him to go. And she got there first."
"And Bertram came," said Lyra. "And hid. And watched." Her hand dropped. "And never said. For two years. Because he'd sent the doctor there. Because it was his fault."
Forgive me.
On the back of a pawn ticket, in a shaking hand. And on a label on a jar, in Corin's own.
Nobody said anything.
Then Doctor Silas took his hands away from his face.
"The W. book," he said hoarsely. "What's the W. book?"
Lyra looked down at the open black bag. At the row of thin notebooks, with their white labels and their years.
She reached in. Slowly. And ran her finger along the spines, from one end to the other. Year 1 — Year 2. Year 3 — Year 4. All the way along. Every one labeled the same. THORNE. CROWN WALK.
At the very end, behind the last of them, pushed down flat against the leather at the back of the bag, where you would never see it unless you put your hand in and felt, there was one more.
It was smaller than the others. Thinner. Bound not in black cloth but in green. Faded green, the green of old moss, with the corners worn white.
It had no label on its spine.
On its front, there was only one letter. Drawn in ink, by hand, in the same small neat doctor's writing.
W.
And beside the letter, very small, the way you might draw something without thinking, while you waited for someone to answer a question, there was a leaf.
Rounded. With wavy edges. Like a little hand.
