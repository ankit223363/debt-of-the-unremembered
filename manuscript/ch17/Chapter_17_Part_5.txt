Chapter 17: Slag-Fever, Part 5 of 5: The W. Book
The W. Book
Lyra did not open it at once.
She held it in both hands, the small green book, the way she had held the blue stone out to Rook in the white room behind the clock face. As if it might be hot. As if it might break. She stood at the end of Pip's bed, in the lamplight, with the doctor's black bag open in front of her, and looked down at the single letter on the faded green cover, and the little drawn leaf beside it.
"Keep it safe, Matthias," she said. Very quietly. "Whatever happens. She is asking." She looked up. "That was my father. Two days before. He knew. He knew she was asking. And he told the doctor to keep it safe."
"And he did," said Doctor Silas. "And then he gave it to me."
"And you kept it safe," said Lyra. "For two years. Behind a stone." She looked at him. "Thank you."
The doctor did not answer. He was looking at the green book in her hands with a strange look on his long tired face. As if he were not sure, now, whether he wanted to know what was in it after all.
Lyra opened it.
The first page was writing. The same small neat sloping doctor's hand as the black books. Only a few lines. Written carefully, slowly, as if the doctor had sat a long time over each word before he put it down.
She read it aloud. Her voice was very steady.
"W.
"Brought to me at Crown Walk by H.L. himself, at night, by the garden door. No servants. Wrapped in a soldier's coat.
"Female. Aged about two years. Well grown. Well nourished. No marks, no injuries. Has not spoken. Will not let go of H.L.'s coat.
"H.L. says she is to be his, and I am to say nothing, and I am to look after her as I look after his own.
"He says she was registered. He says she is Bright."
Lyra stopped.
She stood quite still with the book open in her hands. The lamplight lay on the page. The children murmured on their beds. Nobody spoke.
Bright.
Corin did not need the Ledger for that one. He knew it from the Ledger already. Two lines, in two places, that he had read that week without knowing what they meant.
Lord Thorne says "Bright" when I hum the three notes. That was one. From a week ago, in a white room by a window.
And another, older, in Ledger Seven, from the night of the Auditor: Malakor says that Hale said "Blazing" for Elara. And "Bright" for me.
Registered.
He did not know what that meant. Not properly. He knew it was something that had happened to him, once, a long time ago, in a schoolhouse, in a village with an oak, before the wagon came. Something with a glass, and a table, and a man with blue fingers. He knew it from the Ledger. He did not remember it at all.
But his hands had gone cold on his knees.
"Registered," said Lyra. She looked up at him. "What does that mean? She was registered. She is Bright." Her voice was not quite steady anymore. "Corin. What does that mean?"
"I don't know," said Corin. "Not all of it." He swallowed. "But I was registered too. And I was Bright."
The crypt was very quiet.
Lyra turned the page.
The next page was writing too. But not the kind they could read.
It was the doctor's hand still. Small and neat and sloping. But the letters were not letters. They were little marks. Hooks and dots and short slanting lines, and loops, and tiny crosses, packed together in rows, page after page, all the way through the little green book to the end. Like the tracks of birds in snow. Like the marks on Rook's drawers before you knew what they said. Here and there, among the marks, there was a number. A date. Third winter. A word that Corin could read. Measles. Mild. Tooth. A fall from the swing, left wrist, sprained, not broken. Height. And then the little marks again, hiding everything else.
"Shorthand," said Doctor Silas. He had come to look over Lyra's shoulder. His voice was hoarse. "His own. He used it for everything he didn't want the nurses reading. I never learned it. Nobody did. He said a doctor's notes should be like a doctor's mouth. Open to the patient, and shut to everyone else." He looked at the rows of tiny hooks. "I could look at that for a year and not get one word."
"Then she doesn't know either," said Lyra.
They looked at her.
"My aunt," said Lyra. "She went to his rooms. She asked for his old books. Poor little W., and where she had gone. She wanted this." She held up the green book. "And even if she'd got it, she couldn't have read it. Not a word." Her mouth moved. "He was cleverer than her. He was cleverer than all of them. I will not write down where the child went. Not in any book. It is in my head." She looked down at the page. "So it's gone. Wherever she went. He took it into the river with him."
She turned the pages slowly. Hooks and dots. Hooks and dots. Fourth winter. Height. Fifth. Height.
And then, near the end, the book fell open on its own. The way Ledger Three had fallen open on Bram's stool. Because there was something between the pages.
A leaf.
Pressed flat. Brown, and dry, and thin as paper, with the light coming through it in the lamplight so that you could see every tiny vein. Small and rounded, with wavy edges. Like a little hand.
An oak leaf.
And under it, on the page, in plain letters, not shorthand. In the doctor's small neat hand. One line. Written much later than the rest, Corin thought. In different ink. A darker one.
She took this from the garden at Crown Walk on the morning she went, and gave it to me, and said, "For when I come home." I said I would keep it. I have.
Nobody said anything.
Lyra stood very still with the green book open in her hands and the brown leaf lying on the page. Her face had gone white to the lips. Corin watched her. He watched her look at the leaf, for a long, long time. And then he watched her lift her free hand, slowly, to her collar. To the bootlace at her throat. And touch it.
Not the stone. The lace. Just above the stone.
"There was an empty chair," she said.
She said it so quietly that he almost did not hear her.
"At Midwinter. When I was little. At the long table at Crown Walk, with all the candles. My mother put out a chair. Every year. At the end, by Father. With a plate, and a cup, and a cracker, like all the others." She did not look up from the leaf. "And nobody ever sat in it. And when I asked, she said it was for someone who couldn't come." Her voice was very low. "I thought it was a game. I thought it was for the Midwinter ghost. Rowan and I used to put a sweet on the plate, for the ghost, when no one was looking." She swallowed. "And in the morning it was always gone. And we thought the ghost had eaten it."
The stove ticked.
"I think it was Mother," said Lyra. "Who ate it. So we wouldn't be sad."
She closed the green book.
She did it gently, the way you close a door on somebody sleeping, with the oak leaf still inside it. She held it against her chest for a moment. Then she put it inside her coat. Not in a pocket. Inside her dress, against her skin, the way Corin carried the Ledger. Next to the folded broadsheet. Next to the blue stone on its bootlace.
She did not ask the doctor if she could. He did not say anything. He stood beside the black bag with his long thin hands at his sides, and looked at her, and nodded, very slightly, as if she had asked and he had said yes.
"The rest," said Lyra. She looked down at the black books in the bag. "Twenty years of every cough in my house." Her mouth moved. "Are they—is there anything else? About her? About my aunt?"
"I'll read them," said Doctor Silas. "Every page. Today. While you're gone." He put his hand on the bag. "If there's anything, I'll find it. I know his hand. I knew it before I knew my own." He paused. "And the last one. With the lilies. And the bridge. B. asks me to meet him at the landing." His gray eyes behind the crooked spectacles were very hard. "That's a dead man's own hand, Miss Thorne. Saying where he was going, and who he was going to meet, and who came to his rooms two nights before, asking questions. That's not Ash. That's not a harvester's word. That's ink on a page, in the hand of a physician of the Ministry hospital, that any clerk in that building could compare with twenty years of his reports." He took a breath. "It doesn't say she did it. But it says she was asking. And it says where he went. And it says who sent for him."
"Bertram," said Lyra.
"Bertram," said the doctor. "Who's in a jar. Who your harvester can read." He looked at Corin. "Who saw."
Lyra looked at Corin.
And Corin saw it in her face. The thing she had said at four o'clock, by the stove, with the firelight on her. We make the truth louder.
Not one thing. Not a harvester's word. Not a jar of Ash. Not a laundry girl. Not a doctor's book.
All of them. Together. One on top of another, like the boards of Bram's tide tables, sewn into one.
"It's not enough," said Corin. "Not yet. Not against a Councillor."
"No," said Lyra. "Not yet." She buttoned her coat to the chin, over the green book and the broadsheet and the blue stone. "But it's more than we had at midnight."
Juno woke up at seven, sat up with her cap falling off her face, looked round the crypt, said "Gran'll kill me," and was gone up the steps before anybody could stop her.
She came back at nine.
She came down the steps at a run, and hammered on the door, Oren's knock, three slow and two quick, and when the doctor let her in she stood in the doorway with her chest heaving and the cold coming off her coat in a cloud, and her sharp face pink and furious, and she talked so fast that she had to say everything twice.
The Watch had been at Pruett's all night. Six of them. Two were still there, on the gate, stamping their feet. They had searched the hay store and the stable and the long room over it and even Patience's stall, and Patience had bitten one of them, and Mr. Pruett had laughed so hard he had to sit down. They had not gone into the white room. Oren had stood in the doorway of it with his arms folded, and they had looked at him, and at the old man in the chair by the window, and they had not gone in.
The Watch had been at Mrs. Marta's too. At midnight. Two of them, on the step, asking for Mister Vane. Mrs. Marta had told them that Mister Vane was a lodger, and lodgers came and went, and she wasn't his mother, and if they wanted to search his room they could pay his rent first. And they had gone up, and searched it, and found nothing but a bed and a crate and a jar of Ash, and come down again very quickly, and Mrs. Marta had made them wipe their boots.
"And Mister V," said Juno. She stopped. She took a breath. Her face had gone from pink to white. "Mister Pruett said. And the captain said. There was a carriage last night. After we went. Late. Gone eleven."
Lyra had gone very still.
"A black one," said Juno. "With the blinds down. And a lady got out. In a fur coat. With a basket." She swallowed. "And the Watch let her through the gate. They took their hats off. And she went up the stairs to the long room with her basket, and she said to the captain, very nice, very kind, that she'd heard what had happened, and she'd come to sit with her poor brother, because he shouldn't be alone on such a terrible night."
Nobody moved.
"And the captain wouldn't let her in the white room," said Juno. "He stood in the door. And she smiled at him. And she said, Of course, Captain. Of course. You're quite right. He's sleeping. And she put the basket down on the table. And she said—" Juno's voice wobbled. "She said, Tell my niece I can make all of this go away. Every bit of it. Tell her I'm waiting. Tell her she only has to ask."
Lyra did not say anything.
"And then she went," said Juno. "And the captain looked in the basket. After." She swallowed again. "There was a cake. And a bottle of wine. And a little cup of chocolate, wrapped up, still warm." A pause. "And a cake of soap. White. In a little china dish."
"Lilies," said Lyra.
It was not a question.
"The captain threw it in the trough," said Juno. "The whole basket. In the trough. And broke the ice to do it."
Saturday
Pip woke at ten and asked for her glass.
She sat up on the straw with the blanket round her shoulders and her short ragged hair sticking up in tufts, and looked round the crypt with her enormous dark eyes, and found Corin, sitting on the floor against the end of her bed with his Ledger open on his knee, and frowned at him.
"You stood on it," she said. "My big piece. In the sixth. The long curvy one. You stood on it and it broke and you picked me up and I never got it." Her small pointed face was very stern. "That was a farthing, that was."
Corin looked at her.
He had been writing for an hour. Everything. The hay store and the wall and the dye-yard. The Cut. Saint Bride's with no roof. The crypt, and the trestles, and the children talking in dead people's voices. Sixpence a yard. All with their lamps lit. Doctor Silas, and his badge, and the dark patch on his coat. The Flats in the moonlight. Six pits like fallen moons. Moggs and his piebald horse. The crates. The closed eye, burned into the wood. IV. A little girl putting her cut hand to her mouth.
The golden dome. The man in the plain coat. Long live King— and the hole where the name had been.
A black bag. A last entry in a dead doctor's book. B. asks me to meet him at the landing. A green book with a leaf on the cover. She is Bright.
He had written it all, and his hand had not shaken once, because of the doctor's wet-dog tonic, and now he was very tired, and the words on the page had begun to swim.
He put the pencil down.
He felt in his coat pocket. There were four coppers there. He took one out, and held it out to Pip, on his gloved palm.
She looked at it. Then at him. Her face did not change at all.
"That's a whole copper," she said. "That's four farthings."
"Yes."
"My piece was only one."
"I know," said Corin. "The other three are for not going back. Today. Or tonight. Or tomorrow." He kept his hand out. "Not to the sixth pit. Not to any of them. Not for three nights."
Pip looked at the copper for a long time. She looked at it the way Bet Mallow had looked at Hester's arms. The way somebody looks at a thing they have been offered before, and know the price of.
"Two nights," she said.
"Three."
"Two and I'll keep Ratty out as well."
"Who's Ratty?"
"My dog." She held out her hand. It was very small and very dirty, and across the palm, healing already, there was a thin pink line. "Two nights. And Ratty. And the doctor gives me bread."
"Done," said Corin. And he put the copper in her hand.
She closed her fingers round it and put it in her mouth, to keep it safe, the way children in the Cut kept everything safe, and lay down again on the straw, and turned her face to the wall. To the drawing. The charcoal hills, and the river, and the little houses, and the great oak in the middle with every leaf drawn, and over everything, from hill to hill, the soft chalky gold.
She looked at it for a while.
"I like it," she said, round the copper. "Who did it?"
"You did."
"No I never." She yawned. "I can't draw. I can't even do my name." And she shut her eyes.
Corin drew it.
He turned to a clean page in the Ledger, and he sat on the cold floor with his back against the trestle, and he copied it, as carefully as he could, with the stub of pencil, into the book. The two hills. The river. The houses. The oak, with its roots like fingers and its branches like a hen's wings. He could not draw every leaf. He drew some. He drew the dome last, a great curved line from hill to hill, and he did not have any gold, so he wrote GOLD inside it, in small letters, over and over, until it was full.
And underneath he wrote what she had said. In the old, heavy, strange voice.
The king hath put the sky over us. And they shall not come in.
And under that:
From the sixth pit. From a crate stamped with a closed eye and IV. Old stock, from the Hill. Somebody is emptying something very old and very deep into the mud below the Cut. Since the summer. Every night. Why?
Ask Briggs who signs for the crates.
He closed the book.
Lyra was going to stay.
She told him so at the foot of the crypt steps, by the low black door, in the gray light coming down from outside. She had taken off her filthy brown coat and rolled up the sleeves of her gray wool dress, and she had a basin of water in her hands and a cloth over her shoulder, and her short black hair was tied back from her face with a strip of bandage.
"Doctor Silas says the Watch never come in a fever ward," she said. "Not ever. They're frightened of catching it. And there are sixteen children in there and one doctor, and he hasn't slept in two days." She shifted the basin on her hip. "I can wring out a cloth. I can hold a cup. I can sit with a child who's talking in a dead woman's voice and hold her hand till she stops." She paused. "I can't thread a needle. But I can do that."
"Lyra—"
"And he'll read the black books," said Lyra. "Every page. Today. He promised. And if there's anything else, anything at all, about my aunt, I want to be there when he finds it." Her gray eyes were very steady. "And I want to be here when Hester comes. If Bet will bring her. If she'll tell it again, to someone who isn't us."
Corin did not argue.
He thought he could have. He thought there were a great many things he could have said, about the Watch, and the broadsheets, and the sixteen children who could sell a Thorne girl's face for a loaf of bread. But he looked at her, in the gray light, with the basin on her hip and her sleeves rolled up and the bandage in her hair, and he did not say any of them.
"And you?" said Lyra.
"The Quay," said Corin. "The slag room. It's Saturday. I'm supposed to be there at noon. If I'm not, someone will notice." He touched the Ledger in his coat. "And Briggs signs for the carts. Every night. The cloudy glasses and the slag-jars. And the crates." He was quiet a moment. "Somebody gives him a paper for them. With a name on it. Somebody up the Hill."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment.
"Be careful," she said. "Your face is on a broadsheet too, you know. Not the picture. But a Ministry harvester. Every watchman in the city knows what a Ministry harvester looks like."
"I'll be careful."
"You won't," said Lyra. "But try."
And she turned, with her basin, and went back into the crypt, into the warm sick lamplight, toward the trestle beds, toward a little boy with red hair who had sat up again and was saying, in a woman's high clear voice, mind the step, Mr. Fenwick, mind the step. And Corin watched her kneel down beside him, and put the basin on the floor, and wring out the cloth, and lay it on his forehead, very gently. And take his small hot hand. And hold it.
Then he went up the steps into the day.
The Flats were gray.
In the daylight, from the top of the bank behind Saint Bride's, they did not look like anything much. A wide flat stretch of gray-brown mud, frozen hard and glittering a little with frost in the weak white sun, going away to the edge of the river. The river was brown and busy again, full of ice and barges. The six pits were only six low round hollows with banks of frozen earth round them, like the marks a giant's thumb might leave in clay. They did not glow. Not that Corin could see. The sun was too bright, or they were too dull, or the glow had only ever been there in the dark, waiting for night, the way the stars are there all day and nobody can see them.
There were children down there already. A few. Small dark shapes, bent over, at the edges of the pits. Picking.
There were tracks across the frozen mud. Long deep ruts, two of them, side by side. Going out from the foot of the rutted track to the river road, across the Flats, to the sixth pit. And back again.
Moggs's cart.
Corin stood on the top of the bank and looked at them for a long time.
Then he looked up. Across the river. To the far bank, and the long gray front of the Tide Hall with its row of pillars, and its tall square tower, and the great pale round face at the top of it.
The hands said a quarter past ten.
Every other clock in the city said nearly eleven.
Old Tide, three quarters of an hour behind the world. Still going. Still not minding.
He turned his collar up, and put his gloved hands in his pockets, and walked away from the chapel with no roof, up the frozen lane into the Cut, toward the river road, and the Lantern Quay. In his coat, against his heart, the Ledger. In his pocket, the doctor's bottle of wet-dog tonic, and three coppers. And in the inside pocket on the right, wrapped in a strip of bandage beside the cold silver rod, a thick brown medicine jar, nearly full, glowing very faintly violet through the cloth, and humming.
One long low note. Like someone singing with their mouth shut, a long way off.
The last of a king's sky.
He did not know whose. He did not know what it meant. He did not know why somebody had gone to so much trouble to cut a name out of the world, and then dig up the last place it was hidden, and tip it in the mud below the Cut for children to cut their hands on.
But he had it now. In a jar. With a label.
And he was not going to let it go.
