Chapter 17: Slag-Fever, Part 3 of 5: Pip
Pip
The children came back down before Corin had finished looking.
He heard them behind him. The crunch of small feet on the frozen mud. The clink of jars with candles in them. Nobody spoke. They came across the Flats in the moonlight in their little silent groups, from the foot of the chapel bank, and went to the pits, and climbed down into them, and began to dig.
Into the sixth pit, too. Where the crates had gone.
"No," said Corin. He turned round. "No. Not that one. Not tonight. Doctor—"
But they were already going past him. Three of them. Four. Climbing down over the frozen rim, with their sacks, into the blue light. Into the darker light, the violet light, coming up out of the six split crates. They did not look at him. They did not look at the doctor. They had heard men in coats say no before, a great many times, and it had never once put bread in their mouths.
The last one down was the smallest.
Corin knew her. He had seen her in the fourth pit. The tiny one, in the coat made of a sack, with her feet in rags. She could not have been more than six. She came to the rim of the sixth pit, and stood there for a moment, in the moonlight, looking down at the violet glow with her rush-light in its jar held up in front of her. Her face was very small and very thin and very pale, with a pointed chin, and enormous dark eyes, and a mouth that was set in a hard straight line, the way an old woman's mouth sets. Her hair was cut off short, raggedly, as if someone had done it with a knife.
She climbed down.
She went straight to the nearest of the split crates, where the gray-glittering dust had spilled out over the glass in a soft heap, glowing. And she knelt down beside it, in the dust, and put her rush-light down, and began to pick through the broken glass round it with her bare fingers.
"Little one," said Doctor Silas. He had come to the rim beside Corin. His voice was very quiet. "Pip. Not there. Come away from that. Come on, now. There's better glass in the third."
The little girl did not look up. She picked up a long curved shard of glass, as long as her own forearm, thick and heavy and glowing faintly. She held it up to the moonlight, turning it, looking at it. A good piece. A farthing's worth, perhaps. Half a farthing.
And its edge, as she turned it, went across the palm of her other hand.
Corin saw it. He saw the blood come, dark in the violet light, in a long thin line. He saw the little girl look down at it, without any expression at all, the way you look at a raindrop on your sleeve. And he saw her lift her hand, and put it to her mouth.
"No—"
He was already going down. Over the frozen rim, sliding, on the heaped earth, into the pit. The broken glass crunched under his boots. The rod in his pocket screamed. Not hummed. Screamed. A high, thin, terrible sound that he felt in his teeth and his skull and the bones of his face, and Malakor's voice inside it, very far away, shouting something he could not hear. He did not stop. He went across the bottom of the pit through the glass and the glowing dust and the violet light, and he got to the little girl, and he took her by the wrist, and pulled her hand away from her mouth.
Too late.
There was blood on her lips. And on her lips, in the blood, very faint, glittering, the gray dust.
She looked up at him. Her enormous dark eyes were quite calm.
"Mister," she said. "You're standing on my glass."
They carried her up the bank between them.
She did not want to come. She fought them, at first, silently, with her small sharp elbows and her rag-wrapped feet, and twisted round in Corin's arms to look back at the pit where her sack was, and her rush-light, and her long curved piece of glass. Then, halfway up the bank, she stopped fighting. All at once. She went quite still against Corin's chest, and he felt her go hot.
Not warm. Hot. Through the sack coat. Through his own coat. As if he were carrying a loaf straight out of the oven.
"Already," said Doctor Silas. He was breathing hard, from the climb. "Already. It's never this fast. Never. It's always a day. A day at least." He looked down at the little face against Corin's coat. "Saints. What was in those crates?"
Corin did not answer. He did not know.
But the rod in his pocket had stopped screaming. That was the strange thing. As soon as they had come up out of the pit, it had gone quiet. Not silent. Very quiet. A low, steady, trembling hum, the way a dog whines at a door that has been shut on something it wants.
And it was pointing at the child.
He could feel it. Through his coat. Through his side. The rod was pressing toward her. Toward the little hot body in his arms. The way a compass needle presses toward the north.
They laid her on the last empty bed in the crypt.
Lyra had the door open before they knocked. She had heard them on the steps. She took one look at the little girl in Corin's arms and stood aside without a word, and Juno pulled a blanket off the shelf, and the doctor took the little girl from Corin and laid her down on the straw pallet and pulled the sack coat off her, and her rag-wrapped feet, and put the blanket over her. She was burning. Her face had gone from pale to red in the time it took to climb the bank, a deep, dark, terrible red, the red of a coal. Her lips were cracked. Her eyes were open, and bright, and staring at the curved stone ceiling over her head.
She was not talking. That was the thing Corin noticed first. Every other child in the crypt was talking. Murmuring. In other people's voices, about other people's lives. Sixpence a yard. All with their lamps lit. But this one was not. She lay on her back on the straw with her lips pressed together, and her bright eyes on the ceiling, and she did not say a word.
Doctor Silas wrung out a cloth in a basin of cold water and laid it on her forehead. It steamed.
"Her name's Pip," he said. Not looking up. "Nobody knows her other name. I don't think she does. She sleeps in a crate behind the soap-boiler's on Lye Street with three other children and a dog. She's been coming down to the Flats every night since the spring. She's very good at it. She finds the best glass." He wrung out the cloth again. "She's never once had the fever. Not in eight months. I used to think she must be lucky."
He laid the cloth back on her forehead.
"She's not lucky," he said quietly. "Nobody in the Cut is lucky."
Pip did not talk for an hour.
She lay on the straw with her eyes open, burning, while the doctor cooled her with cloths and Lyra sat beside her on a stool holding her small hand, which was so hot that Lyra had to keep changing hands, and Juno brought water in a tin cup that Pip would not drink. The other children murmured. The stove roared. Corin stood at the foot of the bed with his hand flat on his coat, over the rod, holding it still, feeling it hum and press and want.
And then, a little after two o'clock, Pip sat up.
She did it all at once, the way the red-haired boy had sat up earlier to say mind the step, Mr. Fenwick. She sat bolt upright on the straw with the blanket falling off her, and her burning face turned toward the wall beside her bed. The bare stone wall of the crypt, between two pillars, where the shelf for the dead had been cut. The doctor had whitewashed the back of the shelf, once, long ago, to make it lighter. It was gray now, and stained, but it was still pale.
Pip looked at it.
Then she reached out, past Lyra, to the stove. To the iron pan at the bottom of the stove where the ashes fell. And before anyone could stop her, she put her small hand into it, and took out a lump of charcoal, black and still warm.
And she began to draw.
Nobody moved to stop her.
They watched. All four of them. Lyra on her stool, with her hand still held out where Pip's hand had been. Juno by the stove, with the tin cup. Doctor Silas, with a wet cloth dripping in his fingers. And Corin at the foot of the bed, with his hand on the rod.
Pip drew fast. Faster than Corin had ever seen anyone draw. Her small black hand went across the pale stone in long sure strokes, without stopping, without lifting, the way Juno went across roofs. Not like a child draws. Not round wobbly suns and stick people. Like someone who has drawn every day of their life, and knows exactly where every line is going before it gets there.
She drew hills.
Two long low hills, one on each side, coming down to meet in the middle, making a valley. A deep, wide, gentle valley, with a river winding along the bottom of it, curling like a ribbon. And fields on both sides of the river, little squares of them, and hedges between the fields, and here and there a tiny house with a tiny chimney. Dozens of houses. Clustered together in the middle of the valley, by the river, round something.
Round a tree.
She drew it last of all the valley things. In the very middle. Bigger than anything else. A great tree, with a thick strong trunk, and roots that went down and out into the ground like fingers, and branches that went up and out and out, spreading, wider than the houses, wider than the river, so that the whole village seemed to be sheltering under it, the way chickens shelter under a hen. And on the branches, leaves. She drew every one. Small, and shaped like little rounded hands, with wavy edges.
Lyra made a small sound.
Corin looked at her. She was staring at the leaves. Her face had gone very white.
"Oak," she said. Hardly aloud. "It's an oak."
And then Pip put down the charcoal.
She looked round. At the stove. At the shelf. At Juno. Her enormous dark eyes, bright with fever, went over everything in the crypt and came to rest on Juno's coat. On the pocket of it. Where something small and yellow was sticking out.
"Yellow," said Pip.
It was the first word she had said since the pit. She said it in her own voice. A small, hoarse, cracked child's voice.
Juno looked down at her pocket. Then, slowly, she took out what was in it. A stub of chalk. Yellow chalk, the soft kind, worn down to the length of a finger. Corin had read about it in his Ledger. Juno marks the roofs. Little yellow marks, on the chimneys, so she knows her way in the dark.
Juno held it out.
Pip took it. She turned back to the wall. And with the yellow chalk, slowly now, carefully, with her tongue between her teeth, she drew one more thing.
A line. A great curving line, starting at the top of one hill, at the very edge of the valley. Going up, and up, and over. A huge, smooth, perfect arch, curving over the whole of the valley, over the fields and the river and the houses and the great oak in the middle, and coming down again on the top of the other hill.
And then she filled it in.
Gold. Soft, pale, chalky gold, rubbed in with the side of her small hand, all over the inside of the arch, until the whole valley lay under it. Like a bowl turned upside down over a table. Like the glass over a clock face. Like a sky.
A dome.
A golden dome, over a village, over a valley, over an oak.
Pip sat back. She looked at it. She let the stub of yellow chalk fall out of her fingers onto the straw.
And then she spoke. But not in her own voice. In a voice that none of them had ever heard. Not a child's. Not a man's or a woman's that Corin could tell. Old. Very old. Slow and deep and strange, with the words coming out in an odd heavy way, as if they were from a long time ago, when people said words differently.
"The king hath put the sky over us," said Pip. "And they shall not come in."
And she fell back on the straw, and shut her eyes, and burned.
The King's Sky
By three o'clock, Pip was dying.
Doctor Silas did not say so. He did not need to. Corin saw it in the way the doctor's long thin hands moved, faster and faster, wringing out the cloths, laying them on her forehead and her wrists and the soles of her small feet, taking them away again when they steamed, wringing them out again. He saw it in the way the doctor's mouth had set, hard and straight, under the gray stubble. He saw it in Pip.
She was not red anymore. She had gone past red. Her face had gone a strange dull color, like the gray-white crust on the Flats, and her lips had gone blue at the edges, and her breath came short and fast and shallow, like the breath of a bird held in the hand. Her eyes were shut. Under the lids they moved, fast, from side to side, as if she were watching something go past very quickly. Her small burning hands lay on the blanket, and every now and then the fingers moved, as if they were still drawing.
On the wall beside her, the golden dome glowed in the lamplight, soft and pale and chalky, over the little charcoal valley and the great charcoal oak.
"It's too much," said Doctor Silas. He said it very low, to nobody. "Whatever was in those crates. It's too much for her. She's six years old and she weighs less than a sack of coal and she's got it in her blood like poison." He wrung the cloth out. His hands were shaking, now, for the first time. "The others, it's crumbs. A wedding. A man's knees. This…" He looked at the wall. "This is a whole loaf. And it's choking her."
Lyra had not let go of Pip's hand. She held it in both of hers, changing them over when the heat got too much, and her face was as white as the whitewash.
"Can you do anything?" she said.
"I can keep her cool," said Doctor Silas. "I can wet her lips. I can sit with her." He laid the cloth on Pip's forehead. It hissed. "That's all I can do, Miss Thorne. That's all any doctor can do, for this. There's no medicine for having too much of somebody else in you."
Nobody said anything.
And Corin, standing at the foot of the bed with his hand pressed flat over the rod in his coat, holding it still, felt it pull.
It had been pulling all night. Ever since the pit. A steady, patient pressure through the cloth, toward the child, the way a compass needle presses north. But now it pulled harder. As if it knew. As if it could feel the little body on the bed going thinner and fainter, and wanted to get there first.
Malakor, he said. Inside.
The rod did not answer at once.
Then: No.
I haven't asked yet.
You were going to. The thin dry voice was very hard. I know what you are going to ask, boy. I have been listening to you think for six years. And the answer is no.
She's dying.
Children die in this city every night. You cannot harvest all of them.
I'm not going to harvest her. She's not dead. Corin pressed his hand harder on the rod. I'm going to harvest what's in her. The slag. The old piece. The thing from the crates. Only that. He looked at Pip's gray face. I do it every day, Malakor. With the glasses. I take the slag out and leave the glass clean. You showed me how.
A glass is not a child.
A child is a glass, said Corin. That's all any of us are. You said so. It's in the Ledger, in my hand, from years ago. He swallowed. Every mind is a glass, and the slag is what other people leave in it.
The rod was silent for a long time.
If you do this, said Malakor at last, very quietly, you will not be taking crumbs. You will be taking the whole of whatever that is. All of it. Through the rod and into you, before it goes into the silver. And it is old, Corin. Older than anything you have ever touched. I do not know what is in it. A pause. I do not know if you will come back out.
Then pull me out, said Corin. If I don't.
The rod was silent.
Then, very dry, very tired: I always do.
He told them what he was going to do.
He did not tell them very well. He did not have the words, and the rod was pulling, and Pip's breath was going shorter and shorter. But Doctor Silas understood at once. His gray eyes went very sharp behind the crooked spectacles, and he looked at the rod in Corin's hand, and at Pip, and at the golden dome on the wall, and he said only, "Can you?"
"I don't know."
"Can it hurt her?"
"I don't think so. I think it can only hurt me."
Doctor Silas looked at him for a long moment. Then he moved aside.
Lyra did not move. She sat on her stool with Pip's hand in both of hers and looked up at Corin. She did not tell him not to. She did not tell him to be careful. Her gray eyes were very steady.
"I'll hold her," she said. "You do it."
Corin knelt down beside the bed.
He took off his right glove. He did not know why. He had never harvested with a bare hand, the Ledger said, not once. Always wear the gloves. Always. But it seemed wrong, somehow, to do this through cotton. As if it would be like talking to her through a wall.
He took the rod out of his coat. It was so cold that it burned his bare palm. It was humming so hard that he could hear it. A thin high trembling song, like a wet finger on a glass, louder and louder.
He turned Pip's small left hand over in the lamplight, very gently. On the palm, there was the cut. A long thin line, dark, crusted with dried blood. And along it, very faint, glittering in the lamplight like frost on a window, a fine gray dust.
He laid the carved spiral tip of the rod against it.
It was not like the glasses.
With the glasses, the slag came slowly. Thread by thread. Like water into a cloth. Crumbs. Corners. A smell of bread, half a face.
This came like a river.
It came up out of the little hot hand all at once, through the rod, into him, so fast and so huge that it knocked the breath out of his body and the sight out of his eyes and the crypt out of the world. He did not see it go. He did not see anything. He was not anywhere. He was falling. Down, and down, through a great roaring dark, with something pouring into him from above like a waterfall, cold and heavy and violet and endless, and no bottom.
And then there was a bottom. And he hit it.
And he was standing on a hill.
It was night. That was the first thing. A warm night, summer, with a soft wind blowing, and the smell of grass. Cut grass. Hay. And somewhere close, the smell of a great many people, sweat and fear and wool and smoke.
He was old. He knew that at once, the way you know things in a dream. His body was old. His knees hurt, and his back hurt, and his hands, when he looked down at them in the darkness, were thick and knotted and spotted, like the roots of a tree. He was holding a staff. A shepherd's staff. And beside him, holding his other hand, very tight, there was a child.
A boy. Small. Six, perhaps. Seven. With a round face and a mop of dark hair. Looking up at him with enormous frightened eyes.
Grandfather, said the boy. In the old, slow, heavy way that words came in this place. Grandfather, they're coming.
And he looked up, and he saw them.
On the far hill. Across the valley. A line of lights. Torches. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Coming over the crest of the hill in a long, slow, burning wave, like fire running along a hedge. And under the torches, the gleam of metal. Spears. Helmets. And a sound coming across the dark valley from them, low and steady and terrible. Drums. And under the drums, voices. Singing. Not a song he knew. A hard song. A marching song.
And below him, in the valley, in the dark, the village.
He knew it. He had lived there all his life. The river, curling along the bottom. The fields. The little houses, with their lamps lit in every window, and their doors open, and people standing in the doorways, hundreds of people, all looking up at the far hill. And in the middle of it all, the tree. The great oak. Older than the village. Older than anyone. Its branches spread out over the houses like a hen's wings over her chicks, black against the stars.
Grandfather.
Hush, he heard himself say. In an old man's voice. Deep and slow. Hush now, little one. Watch. He said he would come. Watch.
And down in the valley, by the great oak, a man walked out into the open.
Corin saw him only from far away. A small figure, alone, in the lamplight from the houses. Not tall. Not in armor. Not on a horse. A man in a plain dark coat, with his head bare, walking out from under the branches of the oak into the open field by the river, all alone, with the whole village watching him from their doorways and the whole of the burning army coming down the far hill toward him.
He stopped in the middle of the field.
He lifted his hands.
And the light came.
It came out of his hands. Out of the man in the plain coat, in the middle of the field, by the river, under the stars. Gold. Pure gold. Not like the blue of the lamps. Not like fire. Like the sun coming up. It went up from his lifted hands in a great column, straight up into the night, brighter and brighter, so bright that the old man on the hill had to put up his knotted hand over his eyes. And at the top of the sky it spread. It opened. Like a flower opening. Like a bowl being turned over. It came down, slowly, on every side, in a great shining curve, over the fields and the houses and the river and the oak. Down, and down, until it touched the top of the near hill, just below where the old man stood with the child. And the top of the far hill, just in front of the burning army.
And closed.
A dome. A great golden dome, over the whole valley, shining, humming, like the inside of a bell.
The torches on the far hill stopped.
The drums stopped.
The army stood on the crest of the hill in the gold light, all those thousands, with their spears and their helmets, and did not move. Could not move. Could not come in.
And in the valley, all at once, from every doorway, the people began to cheer.
It went up into the gold. A roar. A great rising roar of a thousand voices, and the child beside him was jumping up and down, and shouting, and the old man was weeping, the tears running down into his beard, and all round him on the hill, all down in the valley, the people were shouting a name. One name. Over and over. Long live the King. Long live King—
And the name was not there.
Corin heard it go. He heard them shout it. A thousand voices. Long live King— And where the name should have been, there was nothing. Not silence. Not a gap. A cut. A rough, raw, ragged hollow in the sound, the way the stone had been hollow over the pillars of the Tide Hall, where somebody had stood on a ladder with a chisel and hacked a word away until there was nothing left. The people were shouting. Their mouths were moving. Their faces were bright with joy in the golden light. And the name went out of their mouths into the air, and there was a hole in the air where it was.
Somebody had cut it out.
Not out of stone. Not out of a plaque on a bridge.
Out of this. Out of the memory itself. Out of an old man's memory, on a hill, on the night the sky went gold.
Corin reached for it.
He could not help it. It was right there. He could feel the edges of the hole, raw and ragged, and something on the other side of them, very close, a shape, a sound, the beginning of a word, C—
ENOUGH.
Malakor's voice. Not dry. Not thin. A roar, louder than the drums, louder than the cheering, louder than the humming of the gold.
And something took hold of Corin by the back of his mind, the way Lyra had taken hold of his collar on the Tide Hall roof, and pulled.
He was on the floor.
On his back, on the stone floor of the crypt, between two trestle beds, with his bare hand flung out and the rod lying on the stone beside it. The rod was not humming anymore. It was glowing. A deep, dark, steady violet, from end to end, so bright that it lit the underside of the beds. And it was so cold that frost had formed on the stone round it, in a thin white ring.
Faces over him. Lyra. Juno. Doctor Silas. Upside down.
He could not speak. He opened his mouth and nothing came out. His whole body was shaking. His face was wet. He did not know when he had started crying. He did not know when he would stop.
"Corin," said Lyra.
She had his hand. His bare hand. She was holding it in both of hers, the way she had held Pip's, and her hands were warm, and he could feel them, and that was how he knew where he was.
"Corin. You're here. You're back."
"The girl," he said. It came out in a croak. "Pip."
Doctor Silas had turned away. He was bending over the bed. Over the small gray shape on the straw. He put his long thin hand flat on her forehead. He held it there for a long moment.
Then he took it away, and looked at it, as if he could not believe what it had felt.
"Cool," he said. His voice cracked. "She's cool. It's broken. Saints and all their angels, harvester. It's broken."
On the straw, Pip opened her eyes.
They were dark and enormous and quite clear. She looked up at the curved stone ceiling. Then at the doctor. Then, slowly, at the wall beside her, where the charcoal valley lay under its soft chalky dome of gold.
She frowned at it. A small puzzled frown.
"Who drew that?" said Pip, in her own small hoarse voice. "It's good." She yawned. "Is there any bread?"
