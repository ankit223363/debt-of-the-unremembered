Chapter 81: Sola's Map Table, Part 4 of 5: Inside
Inside
Kestrel lifted the lid.
She did it with both hands, kneeling, and it came up easily now, on hinges that none of them had been able to see, and folded back and rested against the iron leg of the table. A breath came up out of the box. Cold, like the breath of the cistern. Dry. Smelling of old oil, and old paper, and something else, faintly, that Lyra could not name at first and then could.
Charcoal. The smell of a burnt stick.
The box was lined with wood. Pale wood, smooth, gone dark at the corners. And it was nearly empty. Lyra had been ready, without knowing it, for gold, or jewels, or stacks of papers, or rods, or jars. There were none of those.
There was a bundle. And under the bundle, there was a sheet of folded paper.
That was all.
The bundle was the size of a big book, a thick one, like the great book on the lectern in Saint Clement's. It was wrapped in oilcloth. Dark brown oilcloth, stiff and shiny, the kind sailors used to keep things dry, folded over and over round whatever was inside and tied with a cord of plaited leather. Lyra had seen oilcloth like that before. Around the bread at Last Thorn. Around the Ledger, in Corin's coat. Around the page Corin had sent south with Garrett, for Gideon.
Kestrel lifted the bundle out and held it on her knees. She did not untie it. She held it out to Corin.
He could not kneel. So Oren took it from her and put it into Corin's hands.
He held it against his chest with both arms. The way you hold a baby. Or a cat that might jump.
"Is that it?" said Lyra. "The journal?"
Corin nodded. He did not speak. His eyes were on the bundle in his arms, and his black fingertips were moving on the oilcloth, very slightly, feeling it. Not feeling it. He could not feel anything with those fingers. But moving on it, as if they could.
And Lyra, still kneeling by the box, reached in with her good hand and took out the paper.
It was folded in four. Rough paper, thick, gray-brown, the cheap kind that grocers wrapped cheese in, with little bits of straw pressed into it that you could see if you held it to the light. It was soft at the folds from being folded and unfolded many times. And it was smudged. Black smudges, on the outside, where somebody's fingers had held it.
She unfolded it. Carefully. On her knee.
It was a drawing.
A child's drawing. She knew that at once. She had drawn like that herself, once, at the nursery table at Crown Walk with Rowan beside her and Miss Penhallow saying gently, Lyra, gently, you'll go through the paper. Charcoal. A stick of burnt wood, pressed too hard, so that the lines were thick and black in some places and broke off in others where the stick had snapped. Smudged all over, where a small hand had rested on it while it drew. A thumbprint, black, at one corner.
It was a tree.
A round tree, a big round blob of a tree on a thick straight trunk, with roots going down into a line that was the ground. Somebody had tried very hard to draw the leaves. There were dozens of little marks all over the round top, little loops and dashes, each one pressed in carefully, the way a child draws when it wants to get something exactly right. And in the middle of the round top, in among the leaves, there was a shape.
Three points, sticking up. A band under them. Drawn again and again, gone over and over, so that it was the blackest thing on the paper.
A crown.
A tree with a crown in its branches.
Lyra's hands went cold on the paper. She looked at it for a long time. Then she made herself look away from the tree, down, to the bottom of the paper. Because there was something else there. In the bottom right-hand corner. Under the roots. Small.
One letter.
A C.
The C
She knew that C.
She did not know how, at first. She only knew that her heart had stopped and started again, hard, and that the back of her neck had gone cold.
Then she knew how.
It was a capital C. A big one, for the size of the paper. And it leaned. It leaned backward, a little, to the left, as if it were being blown by a wind from the right. The curve of it was not round. It was squashed at the bottom, flat, as if the hand that made it had come down too fast and too hard on the lower part of the curve and had to turn sharply to come back up. And at the top, where the curve began, there was a little hook. A tiny turn of the charcoal, back toward the inside of the letter, like a thumb tucked into a fist.
She had seen that C every day for a month.
In the Ledger. In Ledger VIII, the green book, her mother's book, which Corin wrote in every night in his crooked leaning capitals while she read over his shoulder and told him where he had got a letter backward. CORIN. She had seen the C at the start of it every day. She had seen it at the start of CHAPEL, and CROSS, and CANT. She had read it on the white floor of the room in the Spire, in chalk, where he had written her father's name, and HE KEPT THE CHILDREN. She had read it upside down on his own chest, in Vespera's ink, because he had chosen it for the ink to copy.
The same lean. The same squashed bottom. The same little hook at the top.
A child had drawn this. A child with a stick of charcoal, who pressed too hard. And then signed it, at the bottom, under the roots of the tree, with the only letter of his name he knew how to make.
She did not say it.
She did not look up. She held the paper on her knee with her good hand flat on one corner of it to stop it folding up again, and she looked at the C, and she said nothing at all.
But she knew he had seen it.
She knew because when at last she looked up, he was not looking at the journal in his arms. He was looking at the paper on her knee. Over the top of the bundle. From where he stood, he could see it the right way up. The tree, and the crown, and the C.
And his face.
Lyra had seen a great many of Corin's faces. She had known him longer than he had known her, which was a strange thing to think. She had seen him laugh, and weep, and be afraid, and pretend not to be afraid, and walk into a dead woman's helmet and not come out. She had seen him look at her and not know who she was, every morning for a month. She had never seen this face.
It was not surprise. It was not even fear. It was the face of a man who has walked into a room in a strange house and found, on the wall, a picture of himself.
He did not say anything either.
He only looked at the C for a long time. And then, very slowly, he shifted the bundle into the crook of his left arm, and took his right hand off it, and held it up in front of his face. His gray hand. His black fingertips. He looked at it as if it belonged to somebody else.
The hand that wrote in the Ledger. The hand that leaned its letters.
Then he put it back on the bundle.
Nobody else had seen. Or if they had, they did not know what it meant. Kestrel had gone to sit on her heels by the lamp with her eyes on the doorway, the way she always watched a doorway. Vespera had bent to look into the empty box, sniffing at its wooden lining. Oren had his hand on Corin's arm still, and his eyes on Corin's face, and if he saw anything there he thought it was only tiredness.
Only the two of them.
Lyra folded the paper up again. Along its old soft folds. In four. She did it carefully, so that the charcoal would not smudge any more than it already had. Then she held it out to him.
He looked at it in her hand.
"You keep it," he said.
His voice was very quiet.
"It's yours."
"I know." He looked at her. "Keep it anyway. For now. I'll only lose it." Something moved in his face. "I lose things."
She held it a moment longer. Then she put it inside her coat. In the inside pocket, on the left, where she kept nothing else. Where her heart was.
"All right," she said. "For now."
He nodded.
And that was all either of them said about it. All that day, and all that night. Neither of them said the C, or it's yours, or you were here. Neither of them said Oakhaven. The words sat between them in the cold air of Sola's room, like the shapes of people kept by cloth, and they both walked carefully round them and did not touch.
Slowly
They took the journal back to the guard room, because that was where the fire was.
Nobody said that was why. Kestrel picked up the blue lamp and Oren closed the lid of the iron box, gently, with his foot, and it clicked shut again on its own, and they all went back along the passage in the old man's track without talking. Corin went in the middle with the bundle held against his chest. Lyra walked behind him with her hand inside her coat, over the folded paper.
The fire had burned down while they were gone. It was a red glow in the bottom of the brazier now, with one last thorn stick lying across it, black, with a line of gold along its edge. Oren knelt and fed it. Two sticks. Three. It caught again with that dry crackle, and stood up orange, and the little room filled with warm light and moving shadows, and Lyra let her breath go.
She had not known she had been holding it, all the way along the passage.
Corin sat down on the bench under the brown coat. Slowly. He laid the crutch along the floor at his feet. He set the bundle on his knees and put both his gray hands on it and sat looking down at it, and did not untie the cord.
They all sat down. Vespera at the other end of the bench, with a grunt. Kestrel on the floor by the door, cross-legged, with her back against the wall and her face to the passage. Oren on the floor by the fire, on his heels, the way he sat when he was not sure he would be allowed to stay sitting. Lyra on the floor too, near Corin's feet, with her knees pulled up and her bandaged hand in her lap.
The fire talked to itself.
"Well?" said Vespera at last. "Are you going to look at it, harvester, or are you going to sit on it like a hen?"
Corin did not smile. But he began to untie the cord.
It took him a long time. His fingers could not feel the knots. He did it by looking, bending his head close, picking at the leather with his black fingertips, and Lyra had to sit on her hands to stop herself reaching out to help him. She could not help him. She could not touch his hands. Not here. Not in the Eye, where his reach was awake and hungry and might go into her by itself, like a dog slipping its lead.
The knot came loose. He unwound the cord, round and round, and laid it on the bench beside him in a coil. Then he began to unfold the oilcloth.
It had been folded many times. Over and over. He opened it like a man opening a letter that he is afraid of, one fold, then the next, then the next, and with each fold the oilcloth crackled and a little dust came off it and floated in the firelight. And then the last fold came back, and there it was.
A book.
A big book. Thick. Bound in leather that had once been brown and was now nearly black, rubbed pale at the corners and along the spine where hands had held it. No words on the cover. No pressed tree. Nothing. The pages were edged with something that might once have been red and was now the color of a dried rose. And it was swollen. The pages did not lie flat. They stood up a little, like the pages of a book that has been wet and dried again, or that has had too much written in it. Too many things pushed between them.
Corin put his hand flat on the cover.
Lyra watched his face. It did not change. But he sat there with his hand on the book for so long, without moving, that the fire settled and dropped a stick and Oren had to feed it again.
Then he took a breath.
And he began to open it.
And stopped.
Lyra saw it. His hand had lifted the cover, a finger's width. The leather had creaked. And then his hand had stopped, the cover held up a finger's width off the pages, as if somebody had caught him by the wrist.
His eyes had gone inward.
"What?" said Lyra.
Corin did not answer at once. He was listening. His head had gone a little on one side. Then he let the cover down again, gently, the way you let down the lid of a piano.
"He says," Corin said, "not all at once."
He was quiet a moment. Listening.
"He says, Read it the way I wrote it. Slowly. A page at a time. You'll not take it in, else. Nobody could." Corin's mouth twitched. "He says it took him a good few years to write, and he'll not have it gulped down in an evening like a bowl of soup by a boy who can't count past his own fingers."
Vespera snorted.
"That's him," she said. "That's the old goat exactly. I never met him, and I'd know him anywhere."
"He says he heard that," said Corin.
"Good."
Corin was still listening. Lyra could see him listening. And then she saw something else come into his face. Something that made her sit up.
"He says," Corin said. More slowly. "He says, I'm not going anywhere." A pause. A long one. The fire crackled. "Yet."
Nobody said anything.
The word lay there, in the warm room, by the fire. Yet.
Lyra looked at Corin. He was looking at the book on his knees. His hands were flat on its cover again.
"What does he mean, yet?" she said.
"I don't know." Corin did not look up. "He won't say. He's gone quiet." He was silent a moment. "Not gone. Just quiet. He's there. Behind my ear. I can feel him." He touched the place. "Like a stone at the bottom of a pond."
On the Glass, Malakor had been silent for days. The Glass holds all inner voices still, he had said, when they came into the Eye, like a hand holds a bird. And in the Eye he had spoken again. Close and clear. Boy. You came. And Lyra had watched Corin's face when he said that, out on the black glass in the noon glare, and it had been the face of somebody who has been very cold for a long time coming into a warm room.
And now. I'm not going anywhere. Yet.
She did not like it. She did not like it at all.
"One page, then," said Kestrel, from the door. She had not turned her head. She was still watching the passage. "Tonight. After we have eaten. One page, by the fire. That is how stories are told." She did turn her head then, and looked at Corin. "Slowly. With bread in our hands."
Corin nodded.
"After supper," he said. "One page."
He wrapped the oilcloth round the book again, loosely, not tying it, and held it on his knees.
Supper with Heat
There was a pot in the store room. Lyra found it on a shelf at the back, behind the rotten sacks: an iron cooking pot, black and heavy, with three short legs and a handle that swung, big enough to make soup for a barracks. It was full of dust and a dead moth. She tipped them out and scrubbed it with a handful of the gray rag from one of the sacks and rinsed it with water from the cistern, three times, until the water came out clear.
Oren carried it to the guard room for her. He set it on the iron ring at the top of the brazier, over the fire, and it fitted, exactly, as if it had been made for it. It probably had.
They put everything in it.
There was not much. That was the truth of it. They had eaten the last of the Last Thorn bread in the chapel, two days ago, the last hard flat loaves broken into pieces and shared out, and Lyra had watched Kestrel count the pieces. But they had goat. Strips of dried goat meat from the Ashborn packs, dark red and hard as leather, tied in bundles with string. They had a little bag of something Marrit called grain, which was not grain but some small hard pale seed Lyra did not know, that the Ashborn carried for bad days. They had salt, in a twist of cloth, the last of what Hild had given them at Thistle Ford. They had, in the bottom of Oren's pack, wrapped in a stocking, three dried apple rings from Granny Abbe that he had been saving and not told anybody about. And they had water. All the water in the world.
Oren cut the goat into small pieces with his knife. Kestrel put in the seed. Vespera, sitting on the bench with her blue hands in her lap, told everybody they were doing it wrong, and then got up and did the salt herself.
"The apples?" said Lyra.
Oren looked at the three dried rings in his palm.
"Not in the stew," he said. "After." And he wrapped them up again and put them away.
The pot sat over the fire. The water began to steam. Then to tremble. Then, at last, very slowly, to bubble, big lazy bubbles coming up from the bottom and bursting on the top, and the smell came up off it.
Lyra had not known a smell could make you want to cry.
It was only goat, and water, and seed, and salt. It was the sort of thing she would have pushed away at home without tasting it. But it was hot. You could smell that it was hot. The steam came off it and curled up into the warm air of the little room and filled it, and it smelled of meat, and of salt, and of fire, and of every kitchen she had ever stood in on a winter's day. Of the kitchen at Crown Walk, where Mrs. Pegg let her sit on the corner of the big scrubbed table and eat the ends of the bread. Of the bread fires at Last Thorn. Of the stew pot at Saint Bride's, with Gideon's big hand stirring it.
She sat by the brazier with her knees up and breathed it in, and felt the warmth of the fire on her face, and was perfectly happy, for a little while, for no reason at all.
Kestrel brought the guards down in turns.
She did not ask anybody about it. She stood up from her place by the door when the stew had begun to smell, and went up the twelve steps, ringing, and came down again with Oda and Brin. The two who were hurt. Oda with her arm strapped to her chest and her lips pressed tight. Brin with his hand on his ribs, slow on the stair, his big body filling the passage. Kestrel sat them by the fire, Oda on the bench next to Vespera and Brin on the floor with his back to the wall, and they both held their hands out to the brazier the way everybody had, and Brin's round face went soft and wondering in the firelight like a child's.
"Hot," he said, slowly. "It is hot."
"It is," said Kestrel.
"Fire is hot here." Brin thought about it. "I did not know fire was hot."
"You knew," said Oda, without opening her eyes. "You were small. You forgot."
Brin thought about that too. Then he nodded, very gravely, and held his hands closer to the fire.
Vespera looked at Oda's arm. She did it without being asked, the way Lyra had learned she did everything: with a great deal of grumbling and very gentle hands. She undid the strapping and felt the shoulder, slowly, her blue fingers moving over it, and Oda's face went white and then gray, and she did not make a sound. Vespera grunted and strapped it up again, tighter, and higher.
"He did that well, the Captain," she said. "Putting it back. It's in. It'll be sore a long time. Don't lift anything."
"There is nothing to lift," said Oda.
"Then that's easy, isn't it."
Then Vespera looked at Lyra's hand.
"Give it here, girl."
Lyra gave it. Vespera unwound the bandage, the strip of somebody's shirt that had been wrapped round it in the Ridges, and Lyra looked at her own palm in the firelight and wished she had not. The skin was gone from most of it. Torn off by the hilt of her sword when the iron man's knee closed on the blade and she would not let go. It was raw and red and wet and there were little gray specks of grit in it. Vespera bent over it with her nose nearly touching it and sniffed.
"Clean," she said. "More or less. No smell. Good." She took a little tin out of her belt. The green salve. Pine and something bitter. "This'll hurt."
It hurt. Lyra did not make a sound either. She looked at the fire and counted the thorn sticks in the brazier, and got to nine, and Vespera bound the hand up again with a clean strip from somewhere in her coat, and patted it.
"Nine," said Corin.
Lyra looked at him. He was sitting on the bench with the journal on his knees, watching her.
"You were counting," he said. "I saw your lips. You got to nine." He looked at the fire. "I can follow you that far."
"That was the sticks," she said.
"I know." He was quiet a moment. "I was counting them too."
The stew was done when Oren said it was done. He tasted it off the end of his knife, and blew on it, and tasted it again, and said, "That'll do," and lifted the pot off the fire with his coat wrapped round his hands and set it on the stone floor.
They had four cups and one bowl between eleven of them, counting the six up the stair. So they ate in turns. Lyra did not mind. She would have eaten it out of her own two hands. When her turn came the cup was so hot she had to hold it in her sleeve, and the stew burned her tongue, and the goat was tough and stringy and the seeds were hard, and it was the best thing she had ever eaten in her life. Better than the water. She did not know how that was possible. She ate it slowly. She wanted it to last.
Oren filled the bowl and two of the cups, and took them up the stair himself, with the bowl held in both hands and the cups hooked on his fingers by their handles. Lyra heard him go up. Twelve steps. She heard voices at the top. A small high one, going very fast. Oren's deep one, answering. The small one again, faster.
And then Oren laughed.
It came down the stair. A real laugh. A great loud bark of a laugh, the kind that comes up from a man's belly before he can stop it, and it rang off the iron steps and down the passage and into the guard room, and Brin looked up from the fire, startled, and Oda opened her eyes.
"What did she say?" said Lyra, when Oren came down.
Oren was still grinning in his beard. He sat down on the floor by the brazier, on his heels, and shook his head.
"She asked," he said, "if the old general would mind her eating stew on the doorstep. Because her mam always said you don't eat on other people's doorsteps, it's common." He wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. "And I said, the general's dead, lass, she'll not mind. And she said—" He stopped, and laughed again, softly. "She said, Well, she's not going to be less dead if I eat it inside, is she?"
Vespera cackled. Even Kestrel smiled at the passage.
"Did the man eat?" said Corin.
Oren's grin faded a little.
"She fed him," he said. "Same as the water. Held the cup for him. He let her." He looked at the fire. "He'll not come down. I asked him again. He doesn't look at me when I ask. He looks south."
"What's south?" said Lyra.
Nobody knew.
Later Kestrel went up again and sent Oda and Brin back up to the door, and brought down Sef and Marrit, and they ate what was left in the bottom of the pot, scraping it out with their fingers, and sat by the fire. Sef had the stone Corin had given him, the ringed gray stone from the shore, in his fist still. He had not let go of it all day. He sat with it held against his chest and stared into the brazier with the firelight in his eyes, and did not say anything, and Marrit sat close beside him with her shoulder against his. They had carried Arran together, those two, along the false trail and back. Lyra thought that Sef had not stopped carrying him.
And for a while, in the guard room of Sola's bunker, under the black glass of the Eye, it was like home.
Not like the house on Crown Walk. Not like anywhere she had lived. But like the idea of home. The thing the word means, before you learn to use it for a particular place. A fire, and a roof, and something hot in your belly, and people round you who would not let anything happen to you if they could help it. Vespera took her boots off. Lyra watched her do it, and it seemed to her the most important thing that had happened all day. The old woman bent down with a groan and unlaced her cracked boots, slowly, and drew them off, one and then the other, and set them side by side by the brazier, and put her bare feet, small and bony and blue-veined, out toward the fire on the stone floor. And sighed. A long, deep, old sigh, like a cat settling.
Nobody took their boots off on the Glass. You might have to run.
Kestrel did not take hers off. Kestrel sat where she had sat all evening, on the floor by the doorway, with her back against the wall and her legs crossed and her spear beside her. Facing the passage. Facing the way to the stair. Every time she spoke she spoke with her face to the passage, and every time she was quiet she was watching it, and even when she smiled she did not turn away from it.
That was the other thing about home, Lyra thought. Somebody had to watch the door.
She looked at Corin. He was sitting on the bench with the book on his knees, and he was watching Kestrel too, and Lyra saw that he had seen the same thing, and was thinking the same thing, and for a moment, across the warm room, their eyes met.
He did not know her. She knew that. He would never know her. Every morning she was new to him, a stranger with a voice he trusted, and a name written in his book.
But tonight he looked at her across the fire as if he were glad she was there.
She looked away first. She did not want him to see her face.
