Chapter 81: Sola's Map Table, Part 3 of 5: Sola's Map
Sola's Map
The map was under glass.
Lyra saw that when she came close. The whole top of the table was one great sheet of glass, as thick as her thumb, clear as the Eye. And under the glass, laid flat on a bed of wood, there was the map.
It was painted. Or drawn and then painted. On something pale and fine and a little shiny, like the vellum of the old charters in her father's study, the ones in the glass-fronted case that she had not been allowed to touch. It was very big. As big as the table. And it was, she saw, as she bent over it and Kestrel brought the lamp close, the whole of the north.
Not the Glass. The Glass was not there. That was the first thing Lyra understood, and it took her breath away.
It was the valley.
The valley, as it had been. As Corin had seen it from inside Sola on the iron horse. Lyra knew it, because he had told her about it in the long afternoon of the grounding, when she sat by him and read him his own book and he came up the well on her voice, and afterward, in pieces, he had told her what he had seen. The green. The river. The orchards in blossom.
They were all here.
The river first. Painted in blue, a soft faded blue like a summer sky in a picture, winding down the middle of the map from top to bottom, from the north to the south, with little lines drawn along its sides for its banks. Smaller blue lines ran into it from either side, streams, each one with a name written along it in tiny neat letters. The land on either side of the river was painted green. Many greens. Light green for the flat fields along the river, laid out in long strips, each strip a slightly different shade, like the stripes on the horse blanket. Darker green for the hills that rose up on either side of the valley. And here and there, on the lower slopes, little clusters of round green dots, like peas, with a tiny brown stalk drawn under each one.
Trees. Orchards. Lyra put her finger on the glass over one cluster without thinking and saw that each pea had been painted with a dot of white on it, or of pink.
Blossom.
"Spring," she whispered. "She painted it in spring."
Nobody answered her.
There were villages. Little clusters of tiny painted houses, gray, with red or brown roofs, each one no bigger than a grain of rice, each cluster with a name written beside it. She read them. She could still read. That had not gone. Only the writing. She read the names under her breath, one after another, and they were names that meant nothing to her and then, halfway down the map, near the place where the river bent, they were not.
Applegarth.
The biggest of all the villages. A little square drawn in the middle of it. A church, with a tiny tower. The river running past it, just to the east.
She looked up from the map. She could not help it. She looked at the floor of the room. At the stone flags under her feet. And under the stone flags, she thought, and under the dome, and under the black clear glass of the Eye, far, far down, there it is. Applegarth. Its square. Its well. Its gate, standing open. A farm cart with a horse in the middle of a step. People with their faces turned up.
She looked back down at the map. At the tiny painted village with its tiny painted church.
She had to look away from it again for a moment.
There were roads. Brown lines, thin, running along the valley beside the river and up into the side valleys and over the hills. There was one big road, drawn thicker than the others, that came up from the bottom of the map, from the south, and ran straight up the valley to the middle. Lyra followed it down with her eye. At the bottom of the map, where it came in, it crossed a line. A thick black line, drawn right across the valley from one side to the other, in a zigzag. Like a lightning bolt lying on its side.
"The ditch," said Corin.
She looked at him. He was bending over the glass beside her, close, with his gray hands flat on the edge of the table, and his eyes were on the zigzag line.
"That's where they stood," he said. "The men with the pikes. That's where she rode to. At the end." His finger moved, hovering over the glass, not touching. "Down here. All the way down. From the middle."
There were marks along the black zigzag. Little suns. Each one no bigger than Lyra's smallest fingernail, painted in gold, a round gold dot with tiny rays round it like the spokes of a wheel. A row of them, all along the ditch. And more of them scattered about the valley. On hills. In side valleys. Beside villages. Here a single sun, there two or three together.
"Camps," said Oren. He had come round the table at last and was standing at the far side, opposite them, with his hands behind his back. He did not lean on the table. He looked down at it the way he had looked at the oak chair. "Those'll be camps. Her people. Where they were. That's how you'd do it. A mark for each company."
And below the ditch, below the bottom edge of the green valley, where the map went pale and empty, there were other marks.
Arrows. Gray ones.
Lyra had not noticed them at first. They were painted in a thin watery gray, the color of the Ministry's coats, so pale against the pale vellum that they almost were not there. But once you saw one you saw all of them. Dozens. Long thin gray arrows, all pointing the same way. North. Up the map. Toward the ditch. Toward the suns. Some had numbers written beside them in the same tiny neat hand. Some had a little drawing. A tiny lamp on a pole. A tiny drum.
Oren looked at the arrows for a long time. He did not say anything at all.
Lyra went on looking. She could not stop. She had always loved maps. In her father's study there had been a map of the city on the wall by the window, an old one, brown, with the river drawn in blue and the Hill in green and the streets in black, and she had stood in front of it for hours when she was small and walked through the city with her finger. This was like that. She walked the valley with her eyes. Up the river from the ditch. Past the villages. Past Applegarth. Up to the top, to the head of the valley, where Corin said there had been a round green bowl, and a small lake.
There was the bowl. A round green place at the top of the river. And in the middle of it, a little circle painted blue, with a white edge, like a coin.
The lake. The middle. Where Sola had gone down to give her words.
And next to it, a tiny drawing of a round thing, gray, half in the blue. A little dome.
This place. They were standing in it. They were standing in that tiny gray drawing at the top of the map, looking down at it.
Lyra felt her head go strange, the way it does when you stand between two mirrors and see yourself going away forever.
She looked away from the dome. She let her eyes go wider. Out from the valley, to the hills on either side, and beyond the hills, where the map went on into other valleys, smaller, narrower, drawn with less care. There were fewer names there. Fewer houses. The paint was thinner. As if whoever had drawn the map had cared most about the valley in the middle and only filled in the rest because it was there.
And there, far up in the top left corner of the map, in a narrow valley among dark green hills, all by itself, there was one more village.
It was small. Five or six tiny houses, no more. A thread of blue running past them. No road going in, or only the faintest brown line, like a hair. It was so far from everything else, and so small, that Lyra would have missed it if the lamp had not been held just where it was. It was not marked with a sun. Every other place where Sola's people were had a gold sun beside it. This one did not.
It was marked with a tree.
A tiny tree, painted in dark green, as small as everything else on the map, with a round top. And in the round top, in its branches, a little shape in gold.
A crown.
Lyra stopped breathing.
She knew that tree. She had known it her whole life. It was carved in the stone over the door of the house on Crown Walk, worn soft by a hundred winters. It was on the silver at dinner, on the handle of every spoon. It was cut into the black oak of the vault door at Saint Clement's, where her father lay now, and where her mother's box was, too light. It was on the ring her father had worn on his right hand for thirty-one years and had still been wearing in the white room at the Spire when he tapped fourteen on her palm and stopped. It was pressed, faintly, into the soft dark leather of the cover of the green book in Corin's coat. Her mother's book.
A tree with a crown in its branches.
The Thorne tree. Hers.
And beside it, in the same tiny neat hand as all the other names, there was a word.
She did not need to read it. She read it anyway. Her lips moved on it without any sound.
Oakhaven.
She did not look up. She did not look at Corin. She kept her eyes on the little tree, and the gold crown in it, and the tiny word, and she held herself very still, the way you hold yourself still on a frozen pond when you have heard the ice crack.
But she knew he had seen it.
She knew because, beside her, he had stopped breathing too.
His gray hands were still flat on the edge of the table. She could see them at the edge of her eye. And she saw the left one, slowly, without his seeming to know it, lift from the edge of the glass and go up to the side of his head. To the place behind his left ear. Where the hair grew thin. Where there was nothing now, Vespera said. Where something had been once, and the lamp had taken it.
He touched the place. Rubbed it, once, with his black fingertips. The way you rub at a stain.
Then he put his hand back down on the table.
Neither of them said anything. Not one word.
Across the table, Oren was still looking at the gray arrows. Kestrel was holding the lamp and looking at Oren. Vespera was looking at nothing, at the wall, with her mouth pressed thin. If any of them had seen, they did not show it.
And Lyra did not say it. She did not say that is my family's mark, or why is it there, or that is your village, Corin, it says so in your book. She did not say any of the things that came crowding up into her mouth. Because she did not know what any of them meant. And because, she thought, standing very still with her eyes on the tiny gold crown, he did not need her to say it. He had seen it. He had seen her see it. That was all.
That was enough, for now.
She made her eyes move. Away from the corner. Back along the dark green hills, slowly, and down into the valley, and back to the little blue lake at the top with its little gray dome.
Beside her, Corin breathed again. A long breath, let out slowly, through his nose.
"It's a good map," he said. His voice was quite ordinary. "She was careful."
"Yes," said Lyra. "She was."
And behind his ear, she knew, the old man was listening. And saying nothing too.
The Box
Under her table, Malakor had said, at the top of the stair. Where she kept everything that mattered.
It was Kestrel who found it, because Kestrel was the one who went down on her knees.
The rest of them were still standing round the map. Lyra did not know how long they had stood there. Long enough for the square of daylight from the roof to slide a little way across the glass, from the bend of the river toward Applegarth. Long enough for her legs to ache. Then Kestrel set the blue lamp down on the floor, and folded herself down beside it, and put her head on one side and looked under the table, into the dark between its iron legs.
"There," she said.
Lyra crouched down beside her. Her knees cracked.
Under the table it was black. The lamp, on the floor, threw its cold blue light in under the edge, and Lyra saw the iron legs, square and thick, going away in two rows into the dark. Dust on the floor between them, thick and smooth, unbroken. And in the middle, under the very center of the table, right under the place where the daylight lay on the glass above, there was something square.
An iron box.
It was not big. Smaller than the trunk Lyra had taken to her aunt's house in the country every summer when she was a child. About the size, she thought, of the box Bertram kept the good silver in. It sat on the floor in the dust. And from each of its four corners an iron chain ran away to an iron ring set into the stone of the floor, so that the box sat in the middle of the four chains like a spider in the middle of its web.
"She chained it down," said Lyra.
"So nobody could carry it off," said Kestrel. "Or so it would not move, if the ground shook."
Oren grunted. He was standing behind them, still not leaning on anything. "Or both. She'd have thought of both."
Corin could not crouch. His stiff leg would not let him. He stood with his weight on the crutch and his free hand on the edge of the table and looked at the box past their heads, and Lyra saw his face in the blue light from below, and it was very white.
"Can you get it out?" he said.
Kestrel lay down flat on her front on the floor, in the dust, and wriggled in under the table on her elbows the way they had all wriggled through the short way under the fallen Ridge. Lyra heard the chains clink. She heard Kestrel's breath. Then a scrape, and a grunt, and Kestrel came backing out again with gray dust all down the front of her hide coat and on her chin.
"No," she said. "The chains are short. And they go into the stone. Deep. It will come as far as the edge of the table. No farther."
"Then we open it there," said Lyra.
It took the two of them. Kestrel went back under and pushed, and Lyra lay down on her side in the dust with her bandaged hand held up out of the way and pulled with her good one, and the box came scraping and grinding across the stone floor toward them, slowly, with the chains clanking behind it, until the chains were tight and it would not come any farther. It stopped just at the edge of the table. Half under. Half out, in the blue lamplight, where they could see it.
Lyra sat up and looked at it.
It was plain. That was the first thing. Plain iron, gone dark and rough with age, the color of an old cooking pot. No carving. No sun, no hands. The corners were bound with thicker strips of iron, riveted on. There were two iron handles, one on each side, rusted stiff. And there was a lid. You could see the line of it, running all the way round the box, a hand's width below the top, thin and straight and very tight. So tight that you could not have got a knife blade into it.
That was all.
She looked at the front of the box, and then the sides, and then, leaning over it, the back, as far as the chains would let her. She ran her good hand along the seam of the lid. She felt for a catch. A hasp. A hole.
There was nothing.
"There's no keyhole," she said.
Oren crouched at last. Slowly. With one hand on the table edge and his face tight with his back. He looked at the box for a long moment. Then he put out his thick forefinger and ran it all the way along the seam of the lid, the way she had, and round the corner, and along the side.
"No hole for a key," he said. "No hasp. No pin. Nothing to—" He stopped. His mouth moved, as if he had bitten on something that was not there. "Nothing to fasten it with. Nothing to make it shut." He frowned at the box. "It's just shut."
Lyra knew what had happened. She had watched it happen to him six times on the Glass. A word reached for, and the hand closing on air. Lock. He had burned that one a long time ago, for something he would not talk about. A lock is a thing on a door that keeps people out, he had said to her once, on the Drove Road, slowly, as if reading it off a slate. I know what it is. I just can't call it anything.
She did not say the word for him. You did not. He had asked her not to.
"It's shut," she agreed.
Vespera came and bent over them, holding on to the table with one blue hand. She looked at the box with her sharp old eyes. She sniffed at it.
"That's not a smith's work," she said. "Not only a smith's." She put out one finger and touched the lid, very lightly, and took the finger away at once, and looked at the tip of it. "Cold," she said. "Colder than it should be. Colder than the iron round it."
"What does that mean?" said Lyra.
"It means somebody put a word on it, girl. Like I put words on skin. Only the other way round. Mine are for keeping a thing in. That's for keeping a thing out." Vespera straightened up, slowly, with a hand in the small of her back. "It'll not open for a crowbar. Don't try. You'd only break the bar."
They all looked at Corin.
He had not moved. He was standing exactly where he had been, with his weight on the crutch and his hand on the table, looking down at the box. And his face had gone still. Not blank. Still. The way a pond goes still when the wind drops.
"He says," said Corin. He stopped.
Lyra waited.
"He says it opens to his word." Corin's voice was strange. Slow. As if he were listening to something a long way off and repeating it as it came. "He says he put it on himself. Sixty years ago. Before the light. He says—" He stopped again. He shut his eyes. "He says, Let me, boy. Just this once. Let me have your mouth."
"Corin," said Lyra.
She did not know what she meant by it. A warning. A question. His name, the way she had said it on the Glass when he was down in the well and could not find his way up.
He opened his eyes and looked at her. He knew her voice. She saw him know it.
"It's all right," he said. "I think it's all right. He's asking." He looked down at the box again. "He's never asked before. He always just—" He made a small gesture with his gray hand. "Comes in."
Nobody said anything.
Then Corin nodded. Not to them. To himself. To the old man behind his ear.
He let go of the table. He put both hands on the top of the crutch, the way an old man puts his hands on his stick. He looked at the iron box on the floor.
And he opened his mouth.
Lyra did not know, afterward, what she had expected. A word. A spell. Something like the words the Ashborn said over their bone charms in the dark, soft and low and sing-song. Something in Corin's own voice.
It was not his voice.
That was the first thing, and the worst. It came out of Corin's mouth, out of his throat, with his lips moving round it. But it was not his. It was older. Much older. Dry and cracked and deep, like a door that has not been opened in years being dragged open over a stone floor. A voice with grit in it. A voice that had been shouting at people for a hundred years and had never once been sorry. And it said a word.
One word. Lyra heard it and could not hold on to it. It was long, and it was in no language she knew, and it was full of sounds that did not belong in a mouth. A hiss in the middle of it, and a click, and a long low sound at the end that went down and down like a stone dropped into the cistern. It was the old letters, she thought. The ones in the old books in her father's study, the curling ones on Corin's lowest rib that Vespera had inked and would not explain. That was what those letters sounded like, when somebody said them out loud.
And as the word came out of Corin's mouth, his face changed.
Not much. Lyra could not have said exactly what. His mouth went a different shape round the word. Thinner. Harder at the corners. His eyes narrowed, the way an old man narrows his eyes to see a thing close up. His back, which had been bent over the crutch, straightened a little. And for one breath, standing there in the blue light at the end of Sola's table, he was not Corin.
He was somebody else wearing Corin. The way a coat on a hook keeps the shape of the man who wore it.
Lyra stepped back.
She did not mean to. Her feet did it. One step, back from the table, and her shoulder hit one of the iron chairs and it scraped on the floor.
She had heard Sola's voice come out of him. In the yard at the iron horse, roaring, without words. In the Ridges, when he stood up in front of the iron man as if he had stood like that all his life. And later, Vance, in the dark, so that Oren went white. She had been frightened then. But Sola had come out of him the way a fire comes out of a stove when you open the door. Hot, and sudden, and too big, and then gone again.
This was not like that. This was calm. This was somebody choosing.
This was worse.
The word stopped.
And under the table, the iron box made a sound.
A small sound. A click. Like a latch lifting. And then a long, slow, breathing sound, like a sigh, as the lid of the box came loose from the body of it all the way round, and settled, and was still.
Corin swayed.
His face was his own again. Lyra saw it come back, all at once, the way a face comes back into a window when somebody lights a lamp behind it. He blinked. He put one hand out for the edge of the table and missed it, and Oren caught him by the arm, by the coat sleeve, not the skin, and held him up.
"Steady, lad."
"I'm all right." Corin's voice was his own. Rough. He swallowed. "That was—" He stopped. He looked at Lyra. At where she stood, a step back from the table, against the iron chair. And she saw him see that she had stepped back.
She made herself come forward again. One step. Back to where she had been.
"That was him," she said.
"Yes."
"All of him?"
"No." Corin shook his head. He was holding on to the table now, with both hands. "Only a bit. Only for the word." He was quiet for a moment. "He's sorry. He says it was like putting on a coat that's too small. He says he'll not do it again unless he has to."
"Good," said Lyra.
It came out harder than she meant. Corin looked at her.
"He says," Corin said slowly, "that's fair."
