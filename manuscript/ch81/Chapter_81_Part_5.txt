Chapter 81: Sola's Map Table, Part 5 of 5: Plain
Plain
They ate Granny Abbe's apple rings last of all.
Oren took them out of the stocking and cut each one into pieces with his knife, very carefully, on the flat of his palm, so that there was a piece for everybody in the room and a piece for everybody up the stair. The pieces were very small. Lyra's was no bigger than her thumbnail. It was brown and leathery and sweet and it tasted of the loft over the byre at Thistle Ford, of the apple racks and the straw and the two cows breathing underneath, and of Corin lying on the next pallet in the dark asking her what her voice sounded like when she was happy.
You'll know it, she had said.
She did not know if he had.
She chewed her piece of apple for a long time.
Then Corin took the journal off his knees and held it out to her.
He did not say anything. He only held it out, in both his gray hands, across the space between the bench and where she sat on the floor. And she understood.
He could read. She knew that. He read slowly, with his finger under the line, his lips moving, and he lost his place, and the long words tired him. But he could read. She had heard him read the Ledger to himself, in the mornings, when he thought she was asleep. Reading was not the trouble.
The trouble was that he would not remember it.
Not all of it. He would read a page and the next morning some of it would be there and some would not, the way things were for him now, full of holes. Unless it was written in the Ledger. Unless somebody he trusted read it to him, and he wrote it down.
She took the journal.
It was heavy. Heavier than it looked. She held it on her knees and felt the weight of it go down through her legs into the stone floor. Then she took a breath, and opened the cover.
The smell came up off the pages. Old paper and old ink and something else, faint, sharp. Like the smell of the Harvesting Section that Corin had told her about once, that he said he had smelled every day for years and not noticed. Burnt sugar.
The first page was not a page of writing. It was a page with a few words on it, in the middle, in big letters, the way the first page of a printed book has the name of the book on it and nothing else. And the letters were plain.
She had been afraid they would not be. She had been afraid, all through supper, without saying so, that it would be in the old letters. The curling ones. The ones she could not read, that were cut into the old stones in Saint Clement's and inked on Corin's lowest rib. If it was in those, she would be no use at all. She would have to sit and watch him try.
But it was not. It was in ordinary letters. Big square capitals, pressed hard into the paper with a thick nib, in ink that had gone brown. Not neat. Not crooked like Corin's, either. Straight up and down, every one of them, like soldiers. Like a man who wrote the way he talked.
Lyra read it aloud. Slowly. So that Corin could follow.
"The Book of Malakor," she read. "Called the Grafted."
She stopped. Under that there was a line, drawn straight across the page with a ruler. And under the line, smaller, but in the same square hard capitals:
"Written plain," she read, "so that any fool can read it."
There was a silence.
Then Vespera laughed. A sharp bark of a laugh, like a crow. And Oren, by the fire, made a sound in his beard. And Lyra looked up from the page at Corin, and found that he was smiling. A small smile, a real one, at one corner of his mouth.
"He says," said Corin, "that it was not meant for you. He says he meant him. Linus. Or Corvell. Or whoever came." The smile went a little wider. "He says it turns out it's for both."
"Good," said Lyra.
She looked down at the page again. At the hard square brown letters. So that any fool can read it.
"Good," she said again. "Then I can read it for both of us."
She did not know she was going to say it until she had said it. It came out quite steady. And then she heard what she had said, and felt her face go hot, and looked down at the book so that she would not have to look at anybody.
For both of us.
She could not write. Not one letter. Her father had taken that with him, folded small in his closed hand, into the last room, where he kept the children. She had paid for it with the hand she would have used to unfold it. She could read, and she would never again write her own name.
And Corin could write. Crooked and leaning and with the letters backward. But he could write. And he could not keep what he read.
So she would read. And he would write it down. And between them, she thought, there would be one person who could do both.
She heard a sound and looked up.
Corin had the Ledger out.
He had taken it out of his coat, out of its oilcloth, and opened it on his knee, the green book with the faint pressed tree on its cover. Her mother's book. He had the stub of pencil in his gray fingers, held the way he always held it, in his fist, like a child. And he was writing.
She watched him do it. She could not see the page from where she sat. She could see his face, bent close over it in the firelight. His lips moving. The way he paused at the end of each word and looked at it.
He wrote for a long time. Longer than it would have taken anybody else.
Then he turned the Ledger round, on his knee, so that she could see it the right way up.
She leaned forward and read it.
MONDAY. THE BUNKER.
FIRE THAT IS HOT. WATER.
THE BOX. A BOOK. A DRAWING.
THE C IS MY C.
SHE SAYS SHE WILL READ IT FOR BOTH OF US.
Lyra read it twice. Her eyes stopped on the fourth line. On the C at the start of it, crooked, leaning back, squashed at the bottom, with its little hook at the top. Written by the same hand, she thought, many years older. In the same room.
She did not say anything about that line. She did not ask him what he meant by it. She knew what he meant by it.
"The B in BUNKER is backward," she said.
He turned the book round and looked at it, frowning.
"Is it?"
"The bellies are on the wrong side."
He looked at it for a long moment. Then he rubbed it out with the side of his thumb, which did not work, because it was pencil and his thumb could not feel how hard it was pressing, and only made a gray smear. So he wrote a new B next to the smear, slowly, with its bellies on the right side, and turned it round again for her to see.
"Better," she said.
"It's ugly."
"It's right."
He looked at her over the top of the Ledger.
"That's what you said before," he said slowly. "Isn't it. At Last Thorn. When you found my wrong letters. It's in the book. They're ugly. But they're right."
"Yes."
"I don't remember you saying it."
"I know."
"But I knew you'd say it." He looked down at the page. "Before you did. I knew."
She did not know what to say to that. So she said nothing. She turned the next page of the journal, carefully, and looked at it.
It was full. Close-written, top to bottom, in the hard square capitals, with no spaces between the lines at all. And it began, at the top, I was born on the far side of the Glass—
No, said Corin.
Not Corin. Corin's voice. But the word was not Corin's, and the way he said it was not. Short and sharp. Like a slap on the back of a hand reaching for the sugar.
Lyra looked up.
Corin was sitting very still on the bench. His eyes had gone inward again.
"He says," Corin said, in his own voice, "that's enough for tonight." He was quiet a moment. "He says the name of a thing is a page by itself, and it'll keep us busy till morning. He says go to sleep. He says, Tomorrow. In the light. Not by a fire, when you're all half asleep and soft in the head with stew."
Lyra looked down at the close-written page. At I was born. She wanted, very badly, to read the rest of the line.
She closed the book.
"Tomorrow," she said.
"He says thank you," said Corin. And then, more slowly, as if he could not quite believe it himself: "He says, thank you, girl."
She wrapped the oilcloth round the journal and tied it loosely with the leather cord and put it on the bench beside him.
The Knock
They slept in the guard room, by the brazier, because nobody wanted to sleep among the cots in the long room with the thrown-off blanket. Nobody said so. They just did not go there.
Kestrel and Oren slid the iron door at the top of the stair nearly shut before they lay down. Lyra went up with them to see. It did not close the way a house door closes, on hinges. It slid. It came out of the wall where it had gone in that morning, groaning on old iron, inch by inch, with Oren's shoulder against it and Kestrel pulling from the outside, and the starlight narrowing in the doorway as it came. They left a gap at the end of it, a hand and a half wide, so that it could be opened again from the inside, and so that the air could come and go.
Outside, through the gap, Lyra saw the Eye.
It was night now. The Eye was black. Not the black of the Glass at night, which was black like still sea, with stars in it. Blacker than that. Clearer. It did not hold the stars on its top the way the Glass did. It let them fall in. They went down and down into it, the stars, deeper than seemed possible, so that standing at the top of the stair and looking out through the gap was like standing at the edge of a well that went right through the world and out the other side into another sky.
And the cold came in through the gap. Real cold. The plain cold of a winter night, that had nothing else in it. No hum. No warmth coming up from below. Just cold.
Sef and Marrit would sleep at the top of the stair, Kestrel said, inside the door, on the iron landing, with their spears. Brin and Oda would come down to the fire, because they were hurt, and a hurt person heals better warm.
"And the girl?" said Oren. "And him?"
Kestrel looked out through the gap.
The silent man was sitting where he had sat all day. To the left of the door now, from the inside, with his back against the curve of the dome and his face to the south. He had not moved, as far as Lyra could tell, since noon. His bound hands lay palm up on his knees. In the starlight they looked black.
And Tansy was curled up against his side, asleep, with three blankets over her. Oren had taken them up after supper. Two of the gray army blankets off the cots, and his own coat, the big heavy one he had carried across the Glass, laid over the top. She was a small hump under them, with only the top of her head showing, her hair sticking up.
Oren had told her to come down when she was cold. Lyra had heard him, at the top of the stair. That's an order, mind.
And Tansy had said, from under the blankets, I'm not cold. I've got him. He's like a stove, and then, Goodnight, Captain, and then nothing, because she was asleep.
The man had not used the blanket Oren left for him. It lay folded on the glass beside him where Oren had put it.
"Leave them," said Kestrel. "She chose. He chose." She took her hand off the edge of the door. "We will hear, if anything comes."
Oren looked at the small hump under his coat for a long moment. Then he nodded, and went back down the stair.
Lyra lay down by the brazier between Vespera and the wall. She had a blanket from the long room over her, scratchy and smelling of dust, and her own coat rolled up under her head, and the fire was warm on her face and the stone floor was cold under her hip, and she did not think she would sleep. She thought of too many things. The map. The little tree with the crown in it, in the top corner, all alone. The C. The voice coming out of Corin's mouth that was not his. Yet.
She lay and listened to the others breathing.
Oren, on his back by the door, breathing slow and deep like a bellows. He did not snore. He had told her once that a soldier who snored did not live long. Vespera, beside her, breathing in little quick sighs, and muttering now and then in her sleep, words Lyra could not catch. Brin, against the far wall, breathing carefully, because of his ribs. Oda, quiet. Kestrel, by the doorway, sitting up still with her back to the wall, not lying down at all, so that Lyra could not tell if she was asleep.
And Corin, on the bench, under the brown coat on its hook. He had lain down there on his side with the journal under his head instead of a pillow, and the Ledger inside his shirt, and his gray hands tucked together under his chin, and his stiff leg straight out. Lyra could see his face in the last red glow of the brazier. His eyes were shut. His lips were moving.
Humming. Very softly. Three notes. Low, high, a little lower. And then stopping, short, where the fourth note should have been. And then again. Low, high, a little lower. Stop.
Hask's tune. Old Hask, who had carried Vivienne's death and died of it, smiling, at dawn, and who was in Corin now, a little, like a cat asleep by a fire.
If it stops, she had told him once, you're frightened.
It did not stop.
She began to count his breaths.
She did not decide to. She never decided to. One. Two. Three. She counted them the way she had counted the stairs at home, every day, since she was nine, without knowing why. Fourteen stairs from the hall to the first landing. She had counted them every day until the day she went down them for the last time, and then her father had counted them for her, on her palm, at the end. Fourteen. That's the landing, Father.
Seven. Eight. Nine.
She stopped at nine. She did not know why. Because he could not follow her past nine, perhaps. Because it seemed rude, somehow, to go on counting where he could not come. She started again at one.
At some point she slept.
She knew she had slept because she woke.
She did not know what woke her. She lay still with her eyes open in the dark. The fire had gone down to a dull red eye at the bottom of the brazier, and the room was full of the shapes of sleeping people, black on black, and the air was cold on her face. Nobody had moved. Nothing had changed.
Then she heard it again.
Far away. Up the stair. Through the stone and the iron, faint, but quite clear in the dead silence of the Eye.
Tonk.
A dull sound. Iron, struck with something soft. The side of a fist.
Tonk. Tonk. Tonk.
Four.
She was sitting up. She did not remember sitting up. The blanket had fallen off her shoulders.
A pause. A long one.
Tonk. Tonk.
Two.
Across the room, by the door, Oren was awake. She heard it, the change in his breathing, sudden, from the deep slow bellows to nothing at all. And then a soft sound. Steel. A sword coming out of a sheath, slowly, so as not to ring.
A pause.
Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk.
Nine. She counted it. She could not help counting it. Nine, and she could have counted it in her sleep, because she had. Because she had heard it all her life without knowing what it was. On the arm of her father's chair, in the study, at the end, after he had burned everything. On the edge of the dinner table. On the wall of the stair, as he went up, with one finger. On the back of her own hand, when she sat with him in the evenings and he did not know her. Four. Two. Nine.
A pause.
Tonk. Tonk. Tonk.
Three.
And then nothing.
Lyra sat in the dark with her hands pressed flat on the cold stone floor and her heart going so hard that she could feel it in her teeth.
Oren was on his feet. She saw his shape against the faint gray of the doorway, big and black, with the long line of the sword in his hand. He went out into the passage without a word. She heard his feet on the stone. Soft, quick, for so big a man. Then on the iron. One. The low bell note. Two. Three.
She went after him.
She did not ask herself whether she should. She got up, and her bare feet found the floor, cold, and she went out into the passage and along it in the dark with one hand on the wall, on the whitewash, on the long scorch marks, not caring now whether she touched them. She found the foot of the stair by the bell note of Oren's boots on it above her. She went up. Four. Five. Six. The iron was so cold under her bare feet that it burned. Seven. Eight. Nine.
The gray of the gap came closer. Starlight.
Ten. Eleven.
Oren was at the top. He was standing on the iron landing just inside the door, with Sef and Marrit up on their feet beside him, both holding their spears, both staring at him. He had his sword in his right hand, low. His left hand was on the edge of the iron door, at the gap. And he had stopped.
Twelve.
Lyra came up behind him and looked past his arm, through the gap, out into the night.
The Eye lay black and endless under the falling stars.
And beside the door, outside, on the left, exactly where he had sat all day, the silent man was sitting with his back against the dome.
He had not got up. He had only turned. He was sitting sideways now, a little, with his shoulder against the iron of the door, and his face turned toward the gap. Toward them. His right hand, in its dark wrappings, was still raised, a little way from the iron, closed into a loose fist. As if it had just finished knocking. As if it were waiting to see whether it needed to knock again.
Tansy was still asleep against his other side, under the hump of blankets and Oren's coat. She had not woken. The knocking had not woken her. She had heard it every month for two winters.
The man's eyes, in the starlight, were on Oren's face.
Not looking through him. Not looking south. At him.
Nobody moved. Nobody said anything. The cold came in through the gap round them.
And then Oren did a thing that Lyra did not expect.
He lowered the sword. He let the point of it come down until it rested on the iron of the landing. And he lifted his left hand from the edge of the door, and closed it, and knocked on the inside of the iron with the side of his fist. Softly.
Not four, two, nine, three. Something else. Something Lyra knew.
A quick little run of knocks. A pause. A slower run. And then one more, alone, at the end.
Heard.
Her father's word. The one he had tapped back to her on her palm, in the white room, when she told him she was there. The one the army used to beat out at the end of a march, Oren had told her, to say message received. To say, We are here. We have heard you.
The man's raised fist stayed where it was for a moment.
Then, slowly, it opened. And went down. And lay, palm up, on his knee again, with the other one.
He looked at Oren a moment longer.
Then he turned his face back to the south, and was still.
Oren stood at the gap for a long time, with his sword point on the iron and his hand flat against the inside of the door. Lyra stood behind him in her bare feet and did not feel the cold. Nobody said anything at all.
"He wanted to know," Oren said at last. Very low. Not to anybody. "That's all. He wanted to know if we were still here."
He slid the sword back into its sheath, slowly, so it would not ring.
And Lyra, standing at the top of Sola's twelve steps in the cold, looked out at the man sitting by the door in the starlight with a sleeping child against his side, and understood that he had been knocking on that door every month for longer than anybody knew. Knocking, and waiting. And nobody had ever answered.
And tonight, in the middle of the night, from outside, he had knocked again. Four, two, nine, three. Softly. On a door that had opened at last.
As if to ask whether they were still there.
