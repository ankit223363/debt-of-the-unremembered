Chapter 81: Sola's Map Table, Part 2 of 5: Water
Water
It was Kestrel who said the word. Lyra had been thinking it for an hour and not saying it, the way you do not say the name of a thing you are afraid of losing.
"Water," said Kestrel.
She said it quietly, standing up from the fire. And everybody's face changed.
The fire had made them forget. For a little while. Now Lyra felt her mouth again. Her tongue, thick and dry, stuck to the roof of it. Her lips, cracked in three places, one of the cracks so deep it had bled in the night and dried in a black line. The back of her throat, which felt as though somebody had rubbed it with sand. The last of the water had gone before noon, up on the Eye. Sef had had the last swallow of it, from the last skin, because Kestrel said so. Seven of them had had nothing since the morning. Lyra was one of the seven. So was Corin.
And outside, up the twelve iron steps, sitting in the white noon by the iron door, there were six more. Tansy and the silent man and the four Ashborn on guard, and none of them had had a drop since the morning either.
"There'll be water," said Corin.
He had said it in the chapel too. He'd not send us to a place with no water. Lyra had believed him then, because there was nothing else to do. Now she looked at him across the fire and saw that he did not know. He only hoped. His gray hand had gone back into his pocket.
"Then we look," said Kestrel.
They left Vespera by the fire with the packs, because Vespera said her knees had walked far enough for one day and if there was water anywhere it would not run away from her while she sat. They took the blue lamp and went on along the passage. Past the second doorway, on the right.
It was a bigger room than the first. Longer. And it was full of beds.
Not beds. Cots. Iron cots, narrow, the kind they had in the barracks, Oren said, the kind he had slept on for twenty years. A row of them down each wall, eight on a side, with a narrow way between. Some had thin gray blankets on them still, folded square at the foot. Some had nothing. On one, near the door, there was a blanket that had not been folded. It lay crumpled, half on the cot and half on the floor, as if somebody had thrown it off in a hurry in the night and jumped up and never come back to fold it.
Nobody went in. They looked from the doorway and went on.
The third doorway was on the left, past the guard room. It was low, so low that even Lyra had to duck under its iron frame, and it led down. Three stone steps, worn hollow in the middle. And then a room that smelled different.
Lyra smelled it before she saw it. It was the first thing that had smelled of anything except iron and dust and old smoke since the stair. A cold smell. Damp. Like the cellars on Crown Walk, where the wine was kept in the dark and the walls sweated in summer. Like a well.
"Oh," she said.
The store room was long and low and lined with shelves. Stone shelves, deep, built into the walls. Most of them were empty. On a few there were things: sacks gone gray and flat and rotten, slumped over like sleeping dogs, that had once held grain or flour and now held only dust. A row of clay jars with wooden lids. A coil of rope. A crate with its side stove in. On the back wall, a rack of iron tools, a spade, a pick, a long iron bar.
And along the right wall, a row of water butts.
Big wooden barrels, the kind that stood under the drainpipes behind the stables at home to catch the rain. Six of them, bound with iron hoops gone red with rust. Lyra went to the first one with her heart going hard and looked in.
Dry. Dry as a drum. The staves had shrunk apart as they dried, so that there were cracks between them you could have put a finger through, and the bottom was covered with a fine pale crust like the salt the sea leaves on a rock.
She went to the next. Dry.
The next. Dry. In the bottom of that one there was a small dead thing, a spider or a beetle, curled up, so old and so dry that it had gone the color of paper.
The fourth. The fifth. The sixth. All dry. All of them. She stood with her hands on the rim of the last one and stared into it and felt something in her chest go down, slowly, like a stone dropped into deep water.
Behind her, nobody spoke.
Then Oren sniffed.
Lyra turned round. He was standing in the middle of the floor with his head up and his eyes half shut, the way Bertram stood in the hall at Crown Walk when he thought one of the maids had burned the toast. He sniffed again. Long and slow. He turned his head a little to the left. Then a little more.
Then he went down on his knees.
It was not graceful. His back was still bad from Sola's throw, and he made a noise going down, a grunt, and put one hand flat on the floor to catch himself. But he went down, and bent forward, and put his face close to the stone, near the foot of the back wall, under the rack of tools. And sniffed again.
"Here," he said.
There was dust on the floor there, the same as everywhere. Lyra could not see anything. Oren began to brush it away with the side of his hand. Under the dust there was stone, and then there was not stone. There was iron. A round iron plate, set flat into the floor, as wide across as a cartwheel's hub. It had a ring set into it, lying flat in a groove, an iron ring the size of a bracelet, rusted into place.
"Help me," said Oren.
Kestrel was already beside him. She took the long iron bar off the rack on the wall without being asked, and fitted the end of it through the ring, and Oren took the other end, and between them they heaved. Nothing happened. They heaved again. The ring screamed. A long, thin, grinding shriek of rust that made Lyra's teeth hurt, and then a crack, and the ring came up out of its groove and stood upright.
Oren dropped the bar. He took hold of the ring in both his hands. He set his feet. Lyra saw his face go red and then dark in the blue light, and the cords stand out in his neck, and she thought of his back, and opened her mouth to tell him to stop.
The lid came up.
It came all at once, with a sucking sound, and a breath of cold came up out of the hole underneath it. Real cold. Wet cold. It hit Lyra in the face and she gasped at it. It smelled of stone and of something green, something living, moss or weed, and of the thing that has no smell at all but that you know anyway, the moment it is near. The smell of water.
Oren swung the lid over and let it fall on the floor. It rang like a gong.
They all bent over the hole.
It was round, and lined with stone, and it went straight down. Kestrel held the blue lamp out over it. The light went down the stone sides, which were wet and shining and had little ferns growing out of the cracks between them, pale ferns, almost white, that had never seen the sun. Down and down. And at the bottom of the light, perhaps two men's height below them, there was something black.
Black and flat and perfectly still. And in it, very small, there was the blue lamp, and four faces looking down.
"Saints," whispered Oren. "Saints and all their candles."
Nobody laughed this time.
There was a bucket. Of course there was a bucket. It hung on a hook on the wall beside the tools, a wooden bucket bound with iron, with a long rope coiled on the hook beside it, and Lyra had looked straight at it when she came in and not seen it. Kestrel took it down. The rope was old. She ran it through her hands, slowly, all the way along, feeling for rot, and shook her head at one place and cut that piece out with her knife and knotted the ends together again. Then she tied the bucket on and let it down into the hole, hand under hand.
Lyra heard it touch. A soft slap, far down. Then the rope went heavy and tight in Kestrel's hands.
Kestrel pulled it up.
It came up slowly, knocking against the stone sides, dripping. The drips fell back down the hole with a little hollow sound, one after another, plink, plink, plink, like somebody playing a single note over and over on a very small harp. And then the bucket came up over the edge into the lamplight, and it was full.
Full to the brim. Black in the shadow, and then, as Kestrel set it down on the floor, not black at all. Clear. So clear that you could see the grain of the wood at the bottom, and a little pale fern leaf turning slowly round and round on the top.
Kestrel did not drink.
She knelt by the bucket and put one finger into the water, and took it out, and touched it to her tongue. Then she sat quite still for a moment with her eyes shut.
"Good," she said. "It is good water. Rain water. Old." She opened her eyes. "It came down through the glass, a little at a time, for sixty years, and waited here." She looked up at Corin. "Drink, Ashborn."
It took Lyra a moment to understand who she meant.
Corin did not understand at all, at first. He stood looking down at the bucket, leaning on his crutch, his face blank. Then he understood, and his face changed, and he shook his head.
"You first," he said. "Or Oren. Or—"
"Drink," said Kestrel. "The guest drinks first. That is the rule. And then the one who is most dry." She held out the tin cup from her belt. "You are both."
He took the cup. His gray fingers could not feel it, Lyra knew. He held it the way he held everything now, with his whole hand wrapped round it and his eyes on it, to be sure. He knelt, awkwardly, with his stiff leg out to the side, and dipped the cup into the bucket, and lifted it out full and dripping. And drank.
Lyra watched his throat move. Once. Twice. Three times. Then he stopped, and took the cup away from his mouth, and sat on his heels with his eyes shut and water running down his chin into the cloth round his neck. He did not say anything.
Then he dipped the cup again and held it out. Not to Kestrel. To her.
"Lyra," he said.
She took the cup carefully, so that her fingers would not touch his.
The water was so cold that it hurt her teeth. It hurt all the way down. She felt it go down her throat in a cold line and spread out in her chest, and she had never in her life tasted anything so good. Not the wine at the Bourse. Not the chocolate Bertram used to make at Midwinter with a stick of cinnamon in it. Not anything. It tasted of stone and of nothing and of being alive. She drank it all. She did not stop to breathe. When it was gone she stood holding the empty cup with water running down her own chin, and her eyes were wet, and she did not know if it was the cold.
Then Oren, and then Kestrel. Then Corin again, and Lyra again. Round and round. The cup went from hand to hand and back into the bucket, and Kestrel let the bucket down into the dark again when it was empty, and drew it up full, and they drank again. They drank until their bellies were tight and sloshing and Lyra could feel the water moving inside her when she breathed. They drank more than was sensible. Kestrel, who had made them sip one mouthful at a time for five days, did not say one word.
Oren drank the last cupful of the third bucket and lay down flat on his back on the floor of the store room with his arms out and his eyes shut.
"I'm going to be sick," he said peacefully.
"Don't," said Lyra.
"I won't. I'm only saying." He opened one eye. "I've had worse ideas than this. Not many."
Corin laughed. Lyra heard it. Not Arran's laugh, the fox-bark that came out of him sometimes now like a bird out of a box. His own. Short and surprised.
Up the Stair
They filled every skin they had.
Kestrel would not let anyone go up until it was done. She sat on the floor by the hole with the empty skins in a pile beside her and filled them one by one from the bucket, tipping it slowly so as not to waste any, pressing the air out of each one with her knee before she tied it off. The goatskins went fat and heavy and round, like sleeping animals. When she had filled them all she counted them, touching each one, her lips moving. Then she counted them again.
"Nine," she said. "Full."
"And the hole?" said Lyra.
Kestrel let the bucket down one more time and drew it up and looked at the rope, at how much of it was wet. Then she looked at the stone sides of the hole.
"The water has not gone down," she said. "Not a finger. It is deep, and it is wide down there. Wider than the hole." She coiled the rope over her arm. "There is enough for all of us. For a long time."
Lyra had not known how much she had been afraid until she heard that. Her knees went weak. She sat down on the edge of the nearest empty shelf.
They carried the full skins up to the guard room and left five of them by the fire with Vespera. Vespera had already heard. She was sitting on the bench under the brown coat with her boots still on and her blue hands round a cup Oren had carried up for her, and she did not say anything clever. She only nodded at them, and drank, and nodded again.
Then Lyra and Kestrel and Oren took the rest up the stair. Corin wanted to come. Kestrel told him to sit down by the fire before he fell down, and he sat.
The twelve iron steps rang under them going up. Lyra counted them without meaning to. At the top there was the doorway, the square of it blazing white, and when she came up into it the noon light hit her in the face like a slap and she had to stop and shut her eyes and put her hand over them.
When she could see again, she saw the Eye.
It was the same as it had been an hour ago. Two hours. She did not know. Black and clear and endless, like ice over deep water, stretching away on every side to the far ring of the gray shore. The sky white over it. No hum. In the Eye there was no hum, and the silence was the first thing you noticed, every time, the way you notice when a clock in a room stops.
And beside the doorway, on the right, the silent man sat with his back against the dome, exactly as they had left him. Facing south. His bound hands lay palm up on his knees. The strips of Tansy's gray shirt that wrapped them were dark at the palms, and darker still across the fingers, where the blood had come through and dried and come through again. His eyes were open. He was looking out over the Eye at nothing at all.
And beside him, Tansy, with her knees pulled up and her chin on them.
She saw them. She saw the skins, round and fat and heavy, swinging from Oren's big hands.
She did not say anything. Lyra had never once, in three days, known Tansy not to say anything. She only got up, very slowly, and stood looking at the skins with her mouth a little open.
"Water," said Lyra. "There's water, Tansy. Lots of it. A whole well."
Tansy's chin began to shake.
Oren did not give her a skin. He looked round, and Lyra saw him see that the child could not hold a full skin and drink from it without spilling half, and he did not want her to spill any of it. So he went back down the stair. Lyra heard him go, ringing, twelve steps. She heard him come back, ringing, twelve steps, more slowly. And he came up into the light with the wooden bucket from the store room in his hands, half full and slopping.
He set it down on the black glass in front of Tansy.
Tansy looked at it. Then she went down on her knees, and put both hands on the rim of the bucket, and put her whole face into it.
Not her mouth. Her face. Her whole head, nearly, down into the water, so that her short red-brown hair floated out on the top of it like weed. Lyra heard her drinking. Great gulping swallows. She came up, gasping, water pouring off her chin and her nose and her eyelashes, and took one breath, and went down again.
"Steady," said Oren. "Steady, little one. It'll still be there in a minute."
Tansy came up again. She knelt back on her heels and looked at him with her face streaming.
"I know," she said. "I know it will. I just wanted to be in it."
And she began to laugh, and to cry, both at once, with the water running off her, and she put her wet arms round Oren's knee and held on.
Oren stood very still and let her. After a moment he put his hand on her wet head.
The Ashborn came. Sef first, from the left of the door, where he had been sitting on his heels with his spear across his knees. Then Marrit. Then Oda, with her bound right arm held against her chest and her face gray with pain under its gray line, walking stiffly. And Brin last, slowly, one hand pressed to his left side where his ribs were cracked, his big round face set and patient. Kestrel gave each of them a skin and stood with them while they drank. She did not tell them to sip. She did not say anything at all. She only touched each one, as they drank, on the shoulder or the back of the neck, the way she touched her people. Sef drank half his skin in one go and then sat down hard on the glass with it held against his chest, the stone Corin had given him that morning still clutched in his other fist.
Brin drank slowly, with his eyes shut, wincing at every swallow because his ribs hurt him when he swallowed. When he was done he opened his eyes and looked at Kestrel.
"He said there would be water," Brin said. "The gray one."
"He did," said Kestrel.
"He was right."
"He was lucky."
Brin thought about that, slowly, as he thought about everything. "Is that not the same?"
Kestrel did not answer. But the corner of her mouth moved.
And last of all, the silent man.
He had not moved. He had not turned his head when they came up. He had not looked at the skins, or at the bucket, or at Tansy with her face in the water. He sat with his back to the dome and his bound hands on his knees and looked south.
Tansy saw Lyra looking at him. She wiped her face on her sleeve and got up and went and knelt by him.
"He forgets," she said. "I told you. He forgets about water. You have to give it to him or he'd just sit there till he went all dry and crackly, like a leaf." She held out her hand to Lyra without looking. "The cup."
Lyra gave her the tin cup.
Tansy filled it from the bucket, carefully, to the brim. Then she held it up to the man's mouth with both hands, the way you would hold a cup for a baby or a very old person, and tipped it, just a little.
He drank.
He did not take the cup. He did not lift his hands. He only drank, when the water touched his lips, slowly, the way a horse drinks, and Tansy tipped the cup a little more each time and watched his throat to know when to stop. When the cup was empty she filled it again. And again. Four cups. Five. On the sixth he turned his head away, very slightly, and she stopped at once.
"That's enough, then," she said. "He says."
She wiped his beard with her wet sleeve, where the water had run into it.
And for just a moment, as she did it, the man looked at her. Not south. Not at nothing. At her. At her face, close to his, with the water still dripping off her hair.
Then he looked south again.
Tansy sat back on her heels. She did not seem to have noticed. She looked up at Lyra instead and grinned, a wide wet grin with a gap in it where a tooth was coming.
"I said maybe somebody'd answer this time," she said. "Didn't I? I said. When he knocked. I said we've got lots of people now." She looked at the open doorway behind them. At the iron door, slid back into the wall, with Oren's bloody handprint still dark on the carved sun. "And it opened. And there's water." She said the last word as if it were the name of a saint.
"There's a fire too," said Lyra. "Down there. A real one. Hot."
Tansy's eyes went round. She looked at the stair. Then at the man beside her. Lyra watched her want to go, and watched her decide not to, and it was like watching somebody put down something heavy they had been about to pick up.
"He won't go in," Tansy said. "I asked him. He just looks." She leaned her shoulder against the man's arm. "So I'm stopping. We said we'd keep the door. In case."
Oren crouched down in front of them. It hurt his back to crouch, Lyra could see. He did it anyway.
"You'll come down when you're cold," he said. "Tonight. When the stars come out. You'll come down to the fire and get warm, and I'll bring him something hot, and you can come back up after if you want. That's an order, mind."
Tansy considered him.
"Are you the captain of me?"
"I'm the captain of anyone who's under the age of twelve and stood on my doorstep."
"It's not your doorstep. It's hers." Tansy nodded at the carved sun.
Oren looked at the sun for a long moment. At his own handprint, dark on its worn middle.
"Aye," he said quietly. "It's hers. But she let me in."
And he got up, slowly, with a hand on his knee, and went back down the stair.
The Command Room
The gray thing hanging across the last doorway was a blanket.
An army blanket, Oren said. The same coarse gray wool as the ones folded on the cots, but bigger, and heavier, and nailed up across the top of the doorway by its corners so that it hung down to the floor like a curtain. It had been there so long that the dust lay on the top edge of it in a soft gray ridge, and the wool had gone stiff, and when Kestrel put out her hand and pushed it aside, it did not move like cloth. It moved like a door. It swung back all in one piece, slowly, and a little gray cloud came off it and hung in the lamplight.
They stood in the doorway and looked.
Corin was with them now. He had got up from the fire when he heard them come back down the stair, and Lyra had seen him decide not to be told to sit again, and Kestrel had seen it too, and had said nothing. He stood at Lyra's left with his weight on the crutch. Vespera had come too, slowly, with one blue hand on the wall, and stood behind them. Oren last, with his head bent under the low ceiling, filling up the passage at their backs.
It was a long room. Longer than any of the others. It went back and back into the dark beyond the reach of the blue lamp, and it was low, like all of them, with a ceiling of heavy stone slabs laid across iron beams. And it was wide. Wider than the passage by a long way. The walls were whitewashed and scorched. But here the scorch marks did not run in streaks. They were patches. Round black patches, here and there, on the walls and on the ceiling, as if the fire had come in here in pieces, in balls, and thrown itself about.
And there was light.
Not much. Not from the lamp. From above. Halfway along the room, there was a square hole in the ceiling, no bigger than a window in a cottage, and down through it came a shaft of gray daylight. It came down straight, the way light comes down through the gap in a barn roof, and you could see the dust turning in it, slowly, like tiny fish in a pool. It fell on the floor of the room in a square. Or it would have fallen on the floor. It did not reach the floor.
It fell on the table.
Lyra had never seen a table like it. In the dining room at Crown Walk there had been a long table, oak, black with age and polish, that could sit twenty if you put the leaves in. This was longer. And it was not for eating at. It stood in the middle of the room on thick square iron legs, and it was so long that its far end was lost in the dark, and so wide that you could not have reached across it from one side to the other, not even Oren. And its top was not wood.
It shone.
The gray square of daylight lay on it, in the middle, and where the light lay the top of the table shone like water. Like the Eye. A flat, clear shine, with something under it.
"Her map," said Corin.
He said it very quietly. Lyra did not know if it was him who said it, or the old man behind his ear.
Around the table there were chairs. Iron ones. Plain, square-backed, with seats of iron slats, the kind you might see in a barracks or a schoolroom. There were many of them. Lyra counted them without meaning to. Eight down one side. Eight down the other. And they were not tucked in under the table. They were pushed back. Every one of them. Pushed back from the table at an angle, some a little, some a lot. One had gone so far back that it had hit the wall and stopped there, tilted. Another lay on its side on the floor.
As if the people sitting in them had all stood up at once.
Lyra had seen that once before. At Crown Walk, at a dinner, when a footman had come in and said something low in her father's ear, and her father had stood up so fast that his chair had gone over behind him, and all the guests had stood up too without knowing why, all at once, their chairs scraping back. She had been nine. She had been on the stairs, watching through the banisters, when she should have been in bed. She did not remember what the footman had said.
At the head of the table, at the near end, under the edge of the gray light, there was one more chair.
It was not iron. It was wood. Oak, dark, heavy, with thick arms and a high back carved with something Lyra could not make out in the dimness. It was not pushed back. It stood square to the table, a little way from it, as if whoever had sat in it had stood up slowly, and stepped back, and stopped there, looking down at the map.
And over the back of it, there was a blanket.
Not an army blanket. A horse blanket. Lyra knew it at once, because the grooms at home had thrown blankets just like it over the carriage horses on cold nights in the stable yard. Thick woven wool, in broad stripes of faded red and brown and a color that had perhaps once been yellow. Its edges were bound in leather. It hung over the back of the oak chair, folded once, the way you would hang your coat over a chair when you meant to come back for it.
Nobody went near it.
Kestrel held the lamp up and looked at the oak chair for a long time. Then she lowered the lamp and went round the other way, along the left side of the table, keeping well clear of it. Lyra followed her. Corin followed Lyra.
Oren did not move from the doorway.
Lyra looked back at him. He was standing exactly where he had stopped, with his hand on the frame, and he was looking at the chair. At the horse blanket over its back. And his face was the face he had worn at the cross in Haverstock, when Lyra read him the names of his family one by one off the stone. The face of a man standing in a church.
"Oren?" she said.
He blinked. He took his hand off the frame.
"Aye," he said. His voice was rough. "I'm coming. Don't mind me."
But he did not sit down. Not then and not later. Lyra noticed that. There were sixteen iron chairs round that table, and Oren, whose back hurt him, who had carried two packs across the Glass and sat down whenever he was let, never sat in any of them. He stood. All that day, and all that evening, whenever he was in that room, he stood. As a soldier stands when his officer is in the room.
And nobody, all that day, ever sat in the oak chair. Nobody said not to. Nobody had to.
