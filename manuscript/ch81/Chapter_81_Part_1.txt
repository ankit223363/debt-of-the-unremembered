Chapter 81: Sola's Map Table, Part 1 of 5: Twelve Steps
Twelve Steps
The twelve iron steps rang under Lyra's boots like the inside of a bell, and at the bottom, the dust lay as thick and undisturbed as snow.
She counted them going down. She could not help it. One, under the white square of noon at her back. Two. Three, where the light from the doorway began to thin and Tansy's blue lamp, in Kestrel's hand below her, began to matter more. Four. Five. Each step was a single plate of iron, worn smooth in the middle by feet, rough and scaled with rust at the edges where no feet had gone. Each one gave a little under her weight and sang. Not loudly. A low, hollow note, like a cracked bell a long way off. And the note went down ahead of her into the dark and came back up, smaller, from the walls.
Six. Seven.
The rail was on her right. A round iron bar, cold, set into the stone on iron brackets. She kept her bandaged right hand off it, because her palm was still raw from the hilt of her sword, and held her left hand a finger's width above it instead, the way you hold your hand over a stove to see if it is hot. It was not hot. It was very cold. It had been cold, she thought, for sixty years.
Eight. Nine.
Ahead of her, Corin's back went down. Slowly. One step and then the crutch, one step and then the crutch, his left hand on the rail, his gray fingers wrapped round the iron. She could see his shoulders under his coat. She could see the way he stopped on the ninth step for just a moment, with his head a little on one side, as if he were listening for a number that did not come.
Then he went on. Without it.
Ten. Eleven. Twelve.
And then there were no more steps. There was a floor.
Lyra stood on it. Stone, flat, very hard. She had not known until her feet touched it how much she had wanted something under them that was not glass. She stood still and let her heels feel it. Stone. Real stone, laid by hands, with cracks between the flags.
"Twelve," she said, out loud, before she could stop herself.
Corin turned his head. In the blue lamplight his face was the color of a fish's belly, and his eyes were very dark.
"Twelve?" he said.
"The steps. There were twelve."
He thought about that. She watched him do it. Then he nodded, the way you nod at somebody who has told you a thing in a language you only half speak.
"All right," he said. "Twelve."
Behind her, Oren came down the last of the stair with his hand on his sword hilt and both packs hanging off him like a mule's panniers, and the iron rang under him, deep and hollow, much louder than it had rung for her. He stopped on the bottom step. He did not step off it at once. He stood there with his head bowed under the low ceiling and looked.
Lyra looked too.
They were in a passage. It ran straight ahead of them, away from the foot of the stair, into the dark. It was not wide. Two people could have walked side by side in it, if they did not mind their elbows touching. And it was not high. Oren's head was a hand's width from the ceiling. Corin's, a little more.
The walls had been white once. You could see that. They were made of big square blocks of stone, the joints between them neat and straight, and over the stone somebody had painted whitewash, thick, the way they did in dairies and in the cellars of big houses, to keep things clean and to throw back the light. Most of it was still there. It had gone the color of old teeth, cream and gray, and in places it had come away from the stone in flakes and lay on the floor in little curled white chips.
And all along the walls, on both sides, at the height of a man's shoulder, there were black marks.
Long ones. Scorch marks. They ran along the passage in streaks, the way soot runs up a chimney wall, but sideways. As if something very hot had come down the stair once, a long time ago, and turned the corner at the bottom and rushed away down the passage like water down a drain, licking both walls as it went. The whitewash under the marks had blistered and gone brown. In one place, near Lyra's left hand, there was a mark in the black like the print of a hand, fingers spread. A small hand. Pale, where it had been pressed flat against the wall and the soot had not been able to get under it.
She did not touch it.
There were iron brackets on the walls, too. One every few paces, on alternate sides, at the height of her head. Shaped like a small iron hand held out, palm up, with a ring in it for a lamp. There were no lamps in them. Two of the rings were bent. One had a little black puddle of something under it on the floor, hard and shiny, which might once have been oil.
And on the floor there was the dust.
She had never seen dust like it. In the house on Crown Walk, Bertram had kept the dust in its place with a cloth and a feather brush and a long look, and she had never once seen more of it than would cover a fingertip. In the crypt at Saint Bride's, where they had slept for a month, there had been coal dust and candle soot and the gray flour of old stone, but people had walked through it every day and it never had time to lie down.
This dust had had time. Sixty years of it.
It lay over the stone floor of the passage, gray, soft, perfectly smooth, as thick as the snow on the Drove Road had been the morning they climbed out of the fens. It was so even that it looked like cloth. Like felt. You could have rolled it up. It lay in little drifts against the foot of each wall. It lay on the iron brackets, a soft gray hump on each one, like a cat asleep on a shelf. It lay on the bottom step of the stair, where Oren still stood, and his boot had broken it, and round the edge of his boot it had cracked into tiny gray cliffs.
Nobody had walked here.
That was the first thing she thought. Nobody has walked here since the light came.
Then Kestrel lifted the blue lamp higher, and Lyra saw that she was wrong.
There was a track.
It ran down the middle of the passage, away from the stair, into the dark. Not a wide track. One person's. A line of footprints, one in front of the other, with a narrow path of broken dust between them, as if whoever had made them had walked carefully, in a straight line, putting each foot down just ahead of the last. The prints were not fresh. Dust had come down into them too, over the years, soft, so that their edges were rounded and blurred and they were shallow, like prints in snow after more snow has fallen. But they were there. You could see them. You could see the shape of the heel, and the wider part at the front.
They went in. Lyra looked for a long moment to make sure. They went in, away from the stair. And then, beside them, so close that in places the two lines ran together, there was a second line, coming back.
One person, going in. One person, coming out.
"Someone came," she said. Her voice was very small in the passage. The whitewash did not throw it back. The dust drank it.
"Once," said Kestrel. She was crouching now, with the lamp held low, and her shaved head bent over the prints, and her face was doing the thing it did when she was reading something she did not like. "A long time after the light. Not sixty winters. Less. But long."
"How long?"
Kestrel shook her head. "This one does not know dust. This one knows glass and snow." She touched the edge of one print with the tip of her finger, very gently, the way she would have touched a sleeping child. "Big feet. Boots. A man. He walked in a straight line. He did not stop to look at anything. He knew where he was going." She looked up at Corin. "And he walked out the same way."
Corin did not say anything. He was standing very still beside her with his weight on the crutch. His eyes were on the prints.
And then, behind his face, something moved. Lyra saw it. She had learned to see it on the Glass, these last days, the way you learn to see weather coming across a field. A small turning of the eyes, inward. A listening.
"What does he say?" she said.
Corin blinked.
"He says," Corin said slowly, "that those are his boots." He frowned. "He says he was younger then. And faster. And he had more sense than to come down here with a lame man and a girl who counts out loud."
Oren made a noise in his throat. It was nearly a laugh.
"Tell him," said Oren, "that the girl who counts out loud has kept us all alive across the Glass. Tell him that from me."
Corin's mouth twitched. "He heard you."
"Good."
"He says he knows. He says that's why he puts up with her."
Lyra felt her face go hot. She was glad of the blue light, which made everybody's face the same strange color.
Vespera came down the last step behind Oren, holding her skirts up out of the way with one blue hand, and stood blinking at the passage with her sharp old nose lifted. She sniffed. Once. Twice.
"Iron," she said. "Old candles. Dust." She sniffed a third time, longer. "And something else. Something burnt a long time ago. Not wood." Her mouth went thin. "Don't touch the walls, girl."
"I wasn't going to."
"Good. Don't."
They went along the passage. Kestrel first, with the lamp. Then Corin, because Kestrel would not let him be anywhere but in the middle. Then Lyra, close behind him, near enough to catch him if the crutch slipped on the dust and far enough that she would not touch him by accident. They had agreed that, up on the Eye. No touching. Not here, where his reach was awake again and might go into her by itself, like water finding a crack. She kept her hands in her coat pockets and her eyes on the back of his neck.
Behind her, Vespera. And last, Oren, who had to walk with his head bent.
They walked in the old track. Kestrel did not say to, but she did it, and they all did it after her, putting their feet in the old man's prints. It seemed the right thing. It seemed rude, somehow, to break the dust where nobody had broken it. As if the passage were asleep, and they might wake it.
The floor went down a little. Very gently. Lyra felt it in her knees. The air grew colder, and stiller, and smelled more strongly of iron, and under the iron of something else. A dry smell. Like the inside of an old trunk that has had wool in it.
There were doors. Or there were doorways. The first was on the left, perhaps twenty paces along, a square dark opening in the white wall with an iron frame round it and no door in the frame. The next was on the right, a little farther. And then another. And at the very end of the passage, where the blue light only just reached, a wider one, with something gray hanging across it.
Kestrel stopped at the first doorway on the left and held the lamp in.
The Guard Room
It was a small room. Square. Whitewashed, like the passage, and scorched, like the passage, but less: the black marks ran only a little way in round the frame and then stopped, as if the fire had looked in and not liked what it saw.
There was a bench along the back wall. Plain wood, gone gray, with a long crack down the middle of the seat. There were iron hooks above it, a row of them, and on two of the hooks there were still things hanging. A coat. A strap.
The coat was brown. Or it had been brown. It was a long heavy coat of the sort the drovers wore on the Drove Road, with deep pockets and a cape over the shoulders, and it hung from its hook by the loop at the back of its collar, and its arms hung down at its sides. It had hung like that for so long that it had taken the shape of the hook and the wall. When Lyra looked at it she thought for one sick moment that there was somebody in it. Then she saw that there was nobody. Only the shape of somebody, kept by the cloth.
The strap was leather, black and cracked, with a brass buckle green with age.
On the floor in the middle of the room there was an iron brazier.
It was round, and stood on three iron legs, and came up to Lyra's knee. A big round iron bowl, black, with holes punched in its sides in a pattern like stars, and a ring of iron round the rim for setting a pot on. There was gray ash in the bottom of it, old and fine. And beside it, stacked neatly against the wall under the bench, there was wood.
Thorn wood. Lyra knew it at once. She had carried armfuls of it at Last Thorn for the bread fires and torn her sleeves on it. Pale gray sticks, crooked, knotted, bristling with long thorns gone hard and dark. Somebody had cut it, a long time ago, and brought it across the Glass, and stacked it here against the wall of a room under the ground, and then never burned it.
"Saints and all their candles," said Oren, very softly, behind her.
He pushed past her into the room. He did not ask. He put the packs down on the dusty floor, both of them, one and then the other, and went down on one knee by the woodpile, and picked up a stick of thorn and turned it over in his big hands. He snapped a twig off the end of it. It broke dry and clean, with a sound like a finger click.
"Dry," he said. "Dry as a bone." He looked up at Kestrel. "Will it burn? Here? You said nothing burns on the Glass."
Kestrel stood in the doorway with the lamp. She did not answer at once. Lyra could see her thinking.
"On the Glass, a fire is a poor thing," Kestrel said at last. "It burns, but small, and it does not warm. The Glass drinks the heat out of it. That is why we carry the stones." She looked round the room. At the white walls, the bench, the coat. "But this is not the Glass. This is under the Eye. Here the cold is only cold." She looked down at Oren. "Try. We will see."
Oren did not need to be told twice.
He had his tinder box out of his coat before she had finished speaking. A battered flat tin with a dent in the lid that Lyra had watched him open every night since the fens: a flint in it, and a steel, and a twist of charred cloth, and a little bundle of dry grass he kept topped up from wherever they had been. He knelt by the brazier and broke up thorn twigs in his fingers, small, and laid them in the bowl on top of the old gray ash. Then bigger sticks, crossed over them. He did it the way he did everything with his hands. Without hurry. Without wasting anything. The way he had sharpened his sword every night on the Glass.
Then he struck the steel.
A spark. It jumped and died in the dust.
Another. It fell on the charred cloth and lay there, a tiny red eye. Oren bent his head and blew on it, so gently that his beard hardly moved. The red eye grew. It spread along the edge of the cloth, a thin bright line, and a curl of smoke came up off it, gray and then white, and the smell of it came to Lyra across the room. Smoke. Real smoke. She had not smelled it since the last bread fire at Last Thorn, and she had forgotten, she found, that she loved it.
Oren put the cloth into the grass. He blew again. The grass caught.
And then, all at once, there was a flame.
It was small. It was yellow. It stood up out of the grass, no higher than Lyra's thumb, and wavered, and leaned over toward Oren's breath, and steadied. Then it found the first of the thorn twigs, and climbed it, and the twig crackled. A sharp, dry, happy sound. And then another twig caught, and another, and the flame went up through the crossed sticks the way a cat goes up a tree, quick and sure, and spread, and was not small any longer.
It was orange. It was real.
Lyra had not known how much she had missed the color. On the Glass there had been the light from below, green and gold and then the tea-brown of evening, and the cold blue of Tansy's lamp, and the stars. No orange. Nothing warm-colored at all. Now the fire stood up in the iron bowl and threw orange light all over the white walls of the little room, and on the brown coat on its hook, and on Oren's face, bent close over it, and on his beard, so that his beard was full of gold.
And the heat came.
It came out of the brazier in a wave and hit Lyra in the face and the front of her body, through her coat, through the cloth wound round her neck. She had forgotten that too. On the Glass you were hot in the day, a dry oven heat that came up from under your feet and made the air shake, and you were cold at night, a bitter cold that came down out of the stars. But neither of those was like this. This was the heat of a fire. It came from one place. It had a front and a back. You could hold your hands out to it.
She held her hands out to it.
She did not decide to. Her hands came out of her pockets by themselves and went out toward the brazier, palms forward, both of them, the bandaged one and the bare one. And beside her, Vespera's blue hands came out too. And Kestrel's, with the lamp set down on the bench. And Oren's, big and scarred and already near enough to the flames that the hair on the backs of them should have been singeing. And Corin, last of all, leaning on his crutch in the doorway, slowly held out his left hand, his gray one, toward the fire.
Five pairs of hands. Well, four pairs, and one. Round an iron bowl in a room under the ground.
And somebody laughed.
Lyra did not know who it was at first. Then she knew it was her. It came up out of her chest like a hiccup, a short silly sound, and she could not stop it, and then Vespera was laughing too, a cracked dry cackle like a hen, and Oren was laughing, deep, with his shoulders shaking, and even Kestrel, who almost never laughed, made a sound through her nose and shook her head and smiled at the floor. They stood round the brazier with their hands held out and laughed at nothing. At the fire. At being warm. At being under the ground in a dead woman's house, at the bottom of the world, with their hands held out like children at a hearth on a winter night.
Corin did not laugh. But he smiled. A slow smile, a little crooked, that started at one corner of his mouth. He was looking at his gray hand in the firelight.
"I can feel it," he said.
The laughing stopped. Lyra looked at him.
"The heat," he said. "On my hand. A bit. Not much." He turned the hand over, slowly, and held the back of it to the fire instead. "Not with the fingers. The fingers don't feel anything. But here." He touched his palm with his thumb. "In the middle. Like somebody breathing on it."
Nobody said anything for a moment.
On the Glass, his hands had felt nothing at all. Not the cold, not the heat, not the glass under them when he fell. Lyra had watched him put his left hand flat on the hot glass at noon in the Fold Hills without knowing he had done it, and leave it there until she pulled it away.
"That's good," said Vespera. Her voice had gone gruff. "That's very good, harvester. Keep it there. Not too close."
"How close is too close?"
"Closer than that."
He kept it there.
They stood for a long time. Longer than they needed to. The fire settled and began to burn steadily, with a low sound like somebody talking quietly in the next room, and the thorn sticks glowed red along their edges and dropped little pieces of themselves into the ash. Lyra felt the heat go into her. Into her face first, and her hands, and then deeper. Into the front of her legs through her breeches. Into her chest. She felt the stiffness in her shoulders begin to come undone, a knot at a time, like a rope being unpicked. She had not known it was there. She had carried it all the way from the Edge.
Five days, she thought. Since the snow stopped in its clean line and we stepped off it onto the black. Five days and five nights, and not once warm. Not like this.
She counted the days on her fingers in her head, to be sure. Wednesday, the Edge. Thursday, the Trench. Friday, the iron horse. Saturday, the long day of grounding. Sunday, the chapel and the Ridges. Monday. Today. Six days, if you counted today. Five nights.
She did not say it out loud. But she looked at Corin when she had finished counting, and found that he was looking at her, as if he knew she had been doing it, and was waiting to see if she would tell him the number.
"Five," she said. "Five nights since the Edge."
He nodded. He held up one gray hand, the fingers spread. Five. He looked at it.
"That's a hand," he said.
"Yes."
"Then I can hold that one." He closed the hand slowly into a fist. "Five."
Oren picked up another thorn stick and laid it on the fire, very carefully, as if it were made of glass.
