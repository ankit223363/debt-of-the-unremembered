Chapter 78: Glass Rain, Part 4 of 5: Both Sides
Both Sides
He went back down the step, and back down the chapel, and sat down again beside Lyra, and put the lamp on the flagstones. His hand was still cold. He held it against his chest under his coat. He did not look at the back wall again. Not for a long time.
Across the chapel, Vespera was awake.
She was sitting up on her bench at the back, with a blanket round her shoulders, and Kestrel was sitting on the floor in front of her, cross-legged, with her back very straight. They were talking. Low. In the southern tongue, mostly, so that Corin could follow some of it. Not all. He had lost a lot of the southern tongue, somewhere. Some of it was in the Saltmarsh. Some of it was in the trench. But he could follow some.
He did not mean to listen. He was too tired to do anything else.
"She was a valley girl," Vespera was saying. "Sola. Did you know that? Not a lady. Not a soldier, to begin with. A girl from a farm, from one of the valleys up here. Before all this was glass."
Kestrel nodded. "We know."
"Her people grew apples. And pears. There were orchards all up the valleys, Hask used to say. All the way to the hills. In the spring you could smell them from the Edge." Vespera's voice was soft and dry. "And when the Ministry came up the valleys with their gray coats and their Ash lamps to take what they wanted, she was the one who stood up. A farm girl. And they followed her."
"Until the light," said Kestrel.
"Until the light."
Neither of them said anything for a moment. The storm roared outside.
"Some of them lived," said Vespera. "Did you know that too? Not many. Her people. The valley people. The ones who were far enough back. At the edges. In the hills. When the light came out of her and went over everything and turned it to this." She moved one hand. A small movement. Taking in the chapel, the storm, the whole Glass. "A few of them lived. Burned. Blinded, some. But alive."
"And they could not go home," said Kestrel.
"There was no home. There was this."
"So they stayed."
"So they stayed," said Vespera. "At the Edge. Where the thorns lean away. They stayed, because where else would they go? And the dead were all round them. Out here. In the glass. Thousands. Their own people. Their families. Their children. Lying in the glass under their feet, glowing." She paused. "You can go mad, with that. Living on the edge of a thing like that. With all your dead in it. Looking up at you."
Kestrel did not say anything.
"And there were others," said Vespera. "Did they tell you this part? The ones who brought you up? There were soldiers."
Kestrel's head turned. Just a little.
"Ministry soldiers," said Vespera. "Gray coats. The ones at the back of the legions. The ones far enough away from her when she lit, that the light did not take them either. They stood at the Edge and looked at what they had done. What she had done. What the whole war had done. And some of them did not go back."
"Deserted," said Kestrel. Very quietly.
"Deserted. Threw their coats down at the Edge and would not pick them up. Stayed. With the valley people. The people they had come up the valleys to kill." Vespera laughed, softly, without any happiness in it. "Imagine that. Sitting down at the same fire. The ones who had the farms, and the ones who came to burn them. Both of them looking at the same glass. Both of them with their dead in it."
"They learned to breathe them," said Kestrel.
"They learned to breathe them. Somebody did. Somebody found out how. To take the dead in, a little, and carry them, and give them back. Not to burn them. Not to take them. To carry them. So they would not go mad. So nobody had to drown in it alone." Vespera was quiet a moment. "Hask knew who first. He never told me."
Kestrel sat very still on the flagstones.
Then she reached up and touched the gray line on her own face. Forehead to nose. The way Corin had seen the Ashborn touch it more times than he could count. A small quick touch, two fingers. Like a sign. Like a hand on a door.
"That's us," she said.
Vespera nodded.
"We are what's left," said Kestrel. Slowly. Not to Vespera, Corin thought. To herself. Or to the dark. "Of both sides. The farms and the gray coats. The ones who stood up and the ones who came to put them down." She took her fingers away from the line. "We're what's left of both sides, carrying each other."
The storm roared outside. The colored light did not move.
Corin sat in the gold patch on the floor with Lyra asleep against him and his cold hand inside his coat, and thought about that. Both sides. The farm girl and the gray coats. Sitting down at the same fire. Breathing in each other's dead.
He thought about the trench. The soldiers standing in their rows in the green glass with their pikes, facing north. He had thought they were rebels. Sola's people. He was not sure now. He had not looked at their coats. He had not looked closely enough at anything.
He thought about the badge on the chest of the tall one. The tree with the crown.
He thought about the eighth face.
He did not know what any of it meant. He was too tired. It went round and round in his head with Hask's three notes and the roar of the storm, and he could not make it stop, and he could not make it fit.
After a while he stopped trying. And just listened. To the storm, and the hum under it, and the two women talking low in the southern tongue at the back of the chapel. And to Lyra breathing, against his shoulder.
Kestrel's Count
Later, Kestrel counted.
She did it in the middle of the chapel floor, on a spread hide, the way she did it every evening on the open glass. Everything they had, laid out in rows. Corin watched her from his place by the bench. He had seen her do it before. He did not remember seeing it. But his eyes knew the order, before she got there. Water first. Then food. Then felt. Then everything else.
The water was in the goatskins. She lifted each one and weighed it in her two hands. Bounced it a little. Listened. Laid it down. Full on the left. Half in the middle. Empty on the right.
The right-hand row was longer than the left.
Corin could see that without counting. He did not need numbers for it. He could see the shapes. The fat skins on the left, and the thin flat ones on the right, and there were more thin ones than fat. A lot more.
Kestrel looked at the rows for a long time.
Then she looked up. At the people round her. At Oren by the door. At Vespera on her bench. At Lyra. At Corin.
"Two nights," she said. "Of water. Maybe three, if we are thirsty."
Nobody said anything.
"The storm took a day," said Kestrel. "We sat. We did not walk. But we drank." She touched one of the thin skins with her finger. "And the heat in here was less, but not nothing. And the litter-carrying is thirsty work." She looked at Arran, asleep on his litter. Then away. "So. Two nights."
"And the Eye?" said Oren.
"One night," said Kestrel. "From here. Past the Ridges. If we walk well. If nothing stops us." She sat back on her heels. "And at the Eye, there is her place. Under the ground. Where the old man in you says the box is. Places like that, in the old days, they kept water. In tanks. Under the floor." Her mouth went thin. "Nobody has been inside in sixty years. So there will be water. Or there will not."
Corin looked down at his hands.
Malakor. He had not thought of Malakor all day. Not all the day before. Not since the Edge. The old voice that had come into him and held a door, and said Count, boy, and then gone silent at the first step onto the Glass. Silent ever since. Not gone. Corin could feel him. Somewhere. Far down. The way you feel a stone at the bottom of a pond, without seeing it. Sitting very still. Waiting.
He did not know for what.
"There'll be water," Corin said.
Everybody looked at him.
He did not know why he had said it. It had come out the way the kettle had. The way the hand-signs had, in the shade by the horse. From somewhere underneath.
"How do you know?" said Kestrel.
Corin thought about it.
"I don't," he said honestly. "But he wants us to get there. More than we do, I think. He's waited a long time." He touched his chest. The place below his collarbone, where the first line was. "He'd not send us to a place with no water. Not if it stopped us getting there."
Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. With her sharp dark eyes, in the colored light.
Then she nodded. Once.
"Then we drink a mouthful each tonight," she said. "Not a cup. A mouthful. And tomorrow, at the Eye, we find out."
She began to put the skins away. In order. Full, half, empty. Rolling up the empty ones tight and tying them, and putting them in the bottom of the pack, because you never threw away a thing that could hold water.
Corin watched her do it.
And felt, far down, at the bottom of the pond, the stone that was Malakor shift. Just a little. As if, in its sleep, it had heard its name.
Small Sounds
The afternoon went on. If it was afternoon. He could not tell anymore. The light never changed.
The chapel filled up with small sounds.
He lay back against the fused bench with his eyes half shut and listened to them. It was a thing to do. And it was better than thinking.
There was the storm, of course. Always the storm. The great dull rushing roar on the other side of the stone, like the sea, like a river in flood, going on and on. And under it the hum. You could hear the hum again, in here. Faint and high.
And over them, the small sounds.
Oren, sharpening.
He had his sword across his knees by the door frame, and a stone in his hand, and he was drawing the stone along the edge of the blade. Slowly. From the hilt to the point. Then lifting it, and going back to the hilt, and drawing it again. Shhhk. Shhhk. A long, even, steady sound. Over and over. His eyes were shut. Corin could see that from where he lay. Oren sat by the door in the gray-white light that came in round the square stone frame, with his scarred face very still and his eyes shut and his big hands moving on the blade, and did not open them. As if he did not need to see. As if his hands knew the sword better than his eyes did.
Shhhk. Shhhk.
Corin thought of the notch. There was a notch in that blade, near the point. From the iron, at Last Thorn. He did not remember it. But it was in the book. THE BLADE BIT AND STUCK. Oren was sharpening round it. He could hear the stone catch, every time, at the same place near the end of the stroke, and skip, and go on.
Arran, snoring.
Not loudly. A small, wet, whistling snore, from the litter under the green streak. In and out. Arran had gone to sleep in the middle of sulking about his flute, with his mouth open and one arm flung over his eyes, and slept like a child.
Marrit's ward-words.
Very low. A murmur. More like a hum than words. She was sitting against the left wall with her bone charms in her lap, turning them over one by one in her old fingers, and murmuring to each one as she turned it. The same few words, over and over, in a tongue Corin did not know. A prayer, he thought. Or a list. Or names.
The scratch of Vespera's needle.
She was mending. Something of Arran's. A shirt, he thought, torn in the storm. Sitting on her bench with the cloth close to her eyes in the gold light and a long needle in her fingers, drawing the thread through with a small dry whisper. In. Pull. Out. In. Pull. Out.
And Tansy.
Tansy was talking.
She had woken up. And she was sitting beside the silent man again, under the back wall, with her knees drawn up to her chin and her uncle's coat pulled round her, and she was telling him about the storm.
Telling him. As if he had not been there. As if he had been somewhere else all day, and missed it, and needed to be told.
"...and it came all white, Nobody. All white, from over there, like a wall. Like a whole wall of milk. And everybody had to put cloths on their faces, and Kestrel shouted, and Oda went like this, with her scarf, and you couldn't see anything, Nobody, nothing, not even your hand, I tried, I held my hand right up in front of my face and it wasn't there. And it stung. It stung like bees. Look, I've got cuts, look, here, and here. And we had a rope. Everybody was on the rope. And you were at the front. You led everybody. Did you know that? You were at the front holding my hand and you walked right through it all, like it wasn't even there, and you didn't fall down once. Not once. I fell down twice. And you found the chapel. You found it in all that white, and nobody else could see anything. How did you do that? How did you know where it was?"
The silent man did not answer.
He was sitting as he had sat all day. Legs crossed. Hands on his knees. Face turned up to the back wall. To the eighth figure, at the end of the row. To the smooth place under the crown, where the face had been.
He did not look at Tansy. He did not seem to hear her. He just looked at the smooth place. Steadily. Without blinking. As if he was waiting for a face to come back into it.
"I don't think you even know," said Tansy. Not crossly. Fondly. The way you talk to a dog that has done something clever without meaning to. "I think you just went. Didn't you? You just went where you were going, and the chapel was there." She leaned her head against his arm. "That's all right. I don't mind. I'll tell you about it, so you know."
And she went back to the beginning, and told it all again.
Corin lay against the bench and listened to her tell it. The white wall. The cloths. The rope. The stinging. Like bees. The silent man at the front, walking through it all as if it was a clear day.
And under her voice, Oren's stone on the blade. Shhhk. Shhhk. And Arran's snore. And Marrit's murmur. And Vespera's needle. In. Pull. Out.
And the storm. And the hum.
He found that his eyes had closed. He found that he did not mind. He found that, for the first time since the trench, since the iron horse, since before that, he was not afraid of anything at all. Not right now. Not in here.
It was a very small thing. He knew it would not last. But he lay in the gold light and the small sounds and let it be there, for as long as it would stay.
Oren at the Door
It did not stay very long. Something was pulling at him. He did not know what, at first. Then he did.
Oren.
Oren had not spoken to him all day. Not once. Not since the dawn, since the white. He had sat by the door with his sword and his stone and his eyes shut, and he had not looked across the chapel at Corin, not even when Corin laughed. Corin had felt it. A space. Like a draft from a door left open.
He got up. Crutch, good leg, stiff leg. And went down the chapel to the door.
Oren opened his eyes when Corin's shadow fell across him. He did not say anything. He looked up at Corin for a moment with his gray eyes. Then he moved over, a little, on the flagstones, to make room.
Corin let himself down. Slowly. Beside him. With his back against the wall by the door frame and his stiff leg out across the floor, and the gray-white light of the storm coming in round the edge of the stone and lying across his knees.
For a long time neither of them said anything.
Oren drew the stone along the blade. Shhhk. Lifted it. Drew it again. Shhhk.
"Oren," said Corin.
"Mm."
"Did I do something? Yesterday?"
The stone stopped.
Oren did not look at him. He looked down at the blade across his knees. At the notch near the point. He ran his thumb along the edge of it, slowly, feeling for the place where it caught.
"Why?" he said.
"Because you went white," said Corin. "At the horse. In the shade. I said something, and everybody looked at me, and you went white. And you said it was the heat." He paused. "It wasn't the heat."
Oren was quiet for a long time.
Corin waited. He had learned that much. Or Sola had. He did not know anymore which of the things he knew were his and which were hers. He only knew that with Oren you waited.
"You said my name," said Oren at last.
"Your name?"
"Vance." Oren said it carefully. As if it was heavy. "You looked at me and you said Vance. In her voice."
Corin felt something go cold down his back.
"Her voice?"
"Sola's." Oren still did not look at him. "A woman's. Low. Rough. Like someone who's done a lot of shouting, a long time ago." His thumb went along the blade. "You didn't hear it. I could see you didn't. You blinked and you were you again and you asked me what was wrong."
Corin sat very still.
He did not remember it. Not any of it. He remembered the shade, and the heat, and Oren's face going white. He remembered his hand making shapes in the air, the signs, the four and the two and the fold and the three, and Oren saying those are the calls. He remembered being frightened of his own hand.
He did not remember saying anything.
He looked down at his hands. Lying on his knees. Gray, with the black tips. With the fine red lines of the storm across the backs, scabbing. He had thought they were his. They were not, all the way. Some of the time they were hers. And now his mouth was hers too. Some of the time. Without asking him.
"I'm sorry," he said.
Oren looked at him then.
Turned his head, and looked at him, with his scarred face and his short gray beard and his tired gray eyes, for a long moment.
"Don't be," he said.
"But you—"
"Don't be." Oren put the stone down on the flagstones beside him. Carefully. "The old woman says it'll happen again. Vespera. She says when it does, I should answer."
"Answer?"
"Answer her. Say I'm here. Or something." Oren rubbed his jaw. His fingers rasped on the stubble. "I didn't, yesterday. I didn't say anything. I just sat there like a post and went white. Like a boy." He shook his head slowly. "Sixty years she waited on that horse. And when she finally sees one of hers, he can't even say hello."
Corin did not know what to say to that.
So he did not say anything. He sat beside Oren in the doorway, in the storm light, and looked out at the white.
After a while, Oren picked up the stone again. And the blade. And began to sharpen. Shhhk. Shhhk. But slower now. As if he was not really thinking about it.
"If it happens," said Corin. "Again. When I'm—when she's—" He stopped. Tried again. "Will you tell me? After?"
Oren's stone stopped.
"Tell you what?"
"What she said. What I said. In her voice." Corin looked down at his hands. "I want to know. I don't want there to be things I said that I don't know about. Not to you." He swallowed. "Rule one. No lies. Not even the kind ones."
Oren looked at him.
Then, slowly, he nodded.
"All right," he said. "I'll tell you."
"Every time."
"Every time."
Corin let out a breath. He had not known he was holding it.
They sat together by the door for a long time after that. Neither of them talking. Oren sharpening, slow. Corin watching the white. And it was better. The draft had gone. Whatever had been open between them was shut again. Not all the way. But enough.
At last Oren put the stone away in his pocket, and laid the sword across his knees, and leaned his head back against the stone of the door frame, and shut his eyes.
"Go and sit with your girl," he said. Without opening them. "She keeps looking over here to see if you've fallen in a hole."
Corin looked back up the chapel. Lyra had woken. She was sitting against the bench in the gold light, watching them. She looked away quickly when he looked.
He got up. Crutch. Good leg. Stiff leg.
"Oren," he said, standing.
"What?"
"Thank you. For the pack. Last night. For carrying it."
Oren did not open his eyes.
"It was nothing," he said.
And Corin, who remembered him saying exactly that last night, in exactly that voice, with two packs on his bad back, knew that it had not been nothing then either. And let him say it.
