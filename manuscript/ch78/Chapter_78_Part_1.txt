Chapter 78: Glass Rain, Part 1 of 5: White on the Edge
White on the Edge
The glass rain came out of the north as a white wall, and Tansy, who had talked for two days without stopping, stopped.
Corin noticed that before he noticed the wall.
He had been walking all night. He did not remember much of it. The tap of the crutch. The swing of the stiff leg. The good foot. The tap. Over and over, in the dark, until the tap was the only thing in the world and he was not a man walking anymore but just the sound of a stick on glass. Hask's three notes, going round in his head, keeping time. The silent man somewhere on his left, a tall shape at the edge of his eye. Lyra's voice now and then, low, at his right side. All right? All right. And the cold. The cold most of all. Coming up through the felt and the boot and the sole of his foot and into the bones of his good leg, so that by the end of the night he could not feel it, and walked on it anyway, like walking on a block of wood.
And all night, at the front of the line, Tansy talking.
She talked to the lamp. She talked to the silent man. She talked to Arran on his litter, and to the bearers, and to the stars. She talked about goats, and about a bird she had seen once with a red head, and about the shrines in the Ridges where the water was, and about the time she had found a whole pear, a real one, dried up and brown and hard as wood, lying on the glass for no reason at all, and had kept it for a week before she ate it. She talked about the way the Glass sounded in the rain. She talked about her uncle's boots, which she was wearing. Her voice went on and on in the dark, small and high and busy, like a bird in a hedge. Corin had stopped hearing the words after the first hour. But he heard the voice. It was like the tap of his crutch. It was a thing that was there, that meant the night was going on, that meant they were all still walking.
And then, just as the sky in the east went gray, it stopped.
In the middle of a word. Corin heard it stop. He lifted his head.
The line had stopped too. All of it, without anybody saying. The litter-bearers had stopped, and set Arran down on the glass. Kestrel had stopped at the front, with her hand up. Lyra had stopped beside him. Everybody was standing still in the gray light, and looking north.
Corin looked north.
At first he did not see anything. Only the Glass, going away flat and dark under a sky that was just beginning to go pale. And the long low line where the two of them met, at the edge of the world.
Then he saw that the line was wrong.
It was not sharp. It should have been sharp. Every morning on the Glass, the edge of the world had been as sharp as a knife, a clean hard line between the dark ground and the pale sky. This morning it was soft. Blurred. As if somebody had taken a thumb and rubbed along it.
And above the blur, low down along the whole of the north, there was a band of white.
Not cloud. Cloud was gray, or it had edges. This was white. A flat dull white, like milk spilled along the bottom of the sky. Thin, at first. A finger's width, held out at arm's length. Then, while he watched, a little thicker. Rising. Very slowly. The way water rises in a jug.
And the wind changed.
Corin felt it on his face. There had been no wind all night. The Glass at night was always still. Now, all at once, there was a breath of air coming down out of the north, against his cheek. Not cold. That was the strange thing. It should have been cold, coming at dawn across the night glass. But it was warm. Warm and dry. And it smelled.
It smelled of hot dust. Like a road in summer. Like the inside of a baker's oven, when the door is first opened and the heat comes out at you. Dry and hot and scratchy at the back of the nose, so that he wanted to sneeze.
He looked at Tansy.
She was standing at the front of the line beside the silent man, holding his hand. She had the clay lamp in her other hand, with its small blue flame. She was not talking. She was looking at the white band along the north with her mouth shut tight and her eyes very wide.
That frightened him more than anything. More than the white. More than the wind. Tansy not talking.
Everybody else had seen it too. He saw them all looking at her. Kestrel. Arran, up on one elbow on his litter. Oren, at the back. All of them looking at the small thin girl in her uncle's coat at the front of the line, and waiting for her to say something. And she did not.
Then she did.
One word. Two. Very quietly. Not in her busy bird's voice. In a flat, small, frightened voice he had never heard her use.
"Glass rain," said Tansy.
Glass Rain
After that, everybody moved at once.
Kestrel shouted. Not one word this time. A whole string of them, in her own tongue, fast and sharp, like stones thrown. And the Ashborn were moving before she had finished. Corin saw Oda tear the scarf off her neck and wind it round her face, up over her nose, so that only his eyes showed. He saw Brin do the same. He saw Marrit, old as she was, pull her hood down to her eyebrows and tie it under her chin with a jerk. He saw Sef drop the back of the litter and come running down the line with his hands full of strips of cloth, pushing one into every hand he passed.
One came into Corin's.
He stood holding it. He did not know what to do with it. His hands did not want to work. They were cold and slow and stupid from the night, and the gray of them looked very gray in the dawn light, and the black tips of his fingers were dark as ink against the pale cloth.
Lyra took it out of his hands.
She did not say anything. She just took it, and shook it out, and wound it round his face. Over his mouth. Over his nose. Round the back of his head, twice, and tucked the end in hard. Her fingers were quick and cold against his ears. Then she pulled his hat down over his eyebrows, as far as it would go. Then she did her own.
"Eyes," she said. Muffled, through the cloth. "Keep them shut if you can. Slits if you can't."
He nodded.
The white was coming.
He could see it now. Not a band anymore. A wall. A great flat white wall standing up out of the north, from the ground to halfway up the sky, as wide as the world. Its top was soft and boiling, like steam over a pot. Its bottom was hard and flat, flat on the glass. And it was moving. Toward them. Not fast, it seemed. It seemed slow, the way a big thing seems slow from far away. But it was getting bigger very fast. Every time he blinked it was bigger.
And the wind came ahead of it.
Warm. Then hot. Then a pushing, steady, hot wind full of the smell of dust, leaning on him, so that he had to lean back into it on his crutch to stand up. He felt his coat flap round his legs. He felt the cloth on his face press flat against his mouth when he breathed in.
And under the wind, a sound.
He had heard the hum of the Glass for days now. He had stopped hearing it, almost. Now he heard something else, coming up under it, the way thunder comes up under rain. A hiss. A long, low, rising hiss. Like a wave on a beach of stones, drawing back. Like a whole crowd of people all whispering at once. Getting louder.
The white hit them.
It was not like rain. He had thought it would be like rain. It was not like anything. One moment he could see the line of people in front of him, and Tansy's blue flame bobbing at the front, and the litter, and the pale sky. The next there was nothing. White. All of it. Everywhere. Up and down and on every side. As if somebody had thrown a sheet over the world.
And the noise. The hiss became a roar, all at once, so loud that it went into his chest and his teeth and his skull and stayed there. He could not hear the hum anymore. He could not hear the tap of his crutch. He could not hear his own breath. He could not hear anything but the roar.
And then he felt it.
Grit. Fine, hard, sharp grit, driven sideways by the wind, hitting him. Hitting every part of him at once. His coat. His hat. The cloth over his face. His hands.
His hands. He had not covered his hands.
It stung. At first that was all. Tiny stings, more than he could count, like being slapped with a fistful of sand, over and over. Then it was more than stinging. He felt it go through the skin. Not deep. Not much. But it went through. He looked down, through the slit of his eyes, through the white, and saw the backs of his hands, and there were fine red lines on them. Too many to count. Tiny. Like the scratches of a cat. Running crossways over the gray skin and the black tips. Beading with small bright drops.
Glass. It was glass. The wind had come across the Glass all night from somewhere far to the north and picked up the top of it, the finest top of it, the tiny sharp grains and splinters that the cold and the heat had cracked off over all the long years, and it was throwing them.
Glass rain.
He pushed his hands inside his coat. Under his arms. The left one up against the crutch handle, the right one into his armpit. The grit hit his sleeves instead. He felt it hitting the back of his neck, above his collar. He felt it on his ears, round the edge of the cloth. He felt it finding every gap.
Somebody was pulling his sleeve.
Lyra. He could not see her. He could only feel her hand. But he knew it was Lyra. The grip. The pull. Two short tugs, and then one long.
This way.
He went where she pulled.
The Rope
She pulled him into a crowd.
That was what it felt like. He could not see it. But he could feel it. Bodies. Pressing round him on every side in the white. Shoulders. Elbows. Somebody's back against his chest. Somebody's pack hitting his arm. All of them crowded together in the roar, close, the way sheep crowd together in a gale.
And hands. Hands at his waist.
Somebody was tying something round him. He felt it go round. Rough. Thick. A rope. Pulled tight round his middle, over his coat, just above his belt, and knotted at his side. Hard. Twice. He felt the knot jerk. Then the hands went away, and the rope went on from him, to someone else. To his left. And from his right, another stretch of it, going to someone else again.
He understood.
They were tying everybody together. Waist to waist. A long line of people on one rope. So that nobody could get lost. So that if you let go, if you fell, if the wind knocked you down and the white swallowed you, you would still be on the rope, and the others would still be on the other end of it, and they would feel you go, and stop, and pull you back.
He put his hand on the rope where it left his waist. Going right. He followed it with his fingers, a little way. It went to another waist. He felt the coat there. Rough. Short. A coat he did not know. He could not have said whose it was.
Then a hand came back along the rope and found his and squeezed it, and he knew the hand. Lyra. Of course. It was her coat, then, and he did not know it. He did not remember what coat she wore. He had lost that, somewhere. In the trench, perhaps. With his name. And not got it back.
It did not matter. He knew her hand.
The rope jerked at his waist. Forward. Once. Twice.
They were moving.
They walked into the white.
He did not know how. He could not see anything. Not his own feet. Not his crutch. Not the hand he held up in front of his face, when he tried, just to see. Nothing. White. Only white, and the roar, and the grit hitting him, and the rope at his waist pulling him forward, steady, steady, a little to the left, steady.
He walked. Crutch, swing, step. Crutch, swing, step. He could not hear the tap. He could only feel it. The jolt up his arm when the end of the crutch hit the glass. He walked with his eyes shut because there was nothing to see if he opened them, and they filled with grit when he did, and stung, and ran.
Somebody at the front was leading.
He could feel that, through the rope. The rope did not wander. It did not stop and start, the way a rope stops and starts when the man at the front is lost and feeling his way. It pulled steady. Forward and a little left. Forward. Forward and a little right. Forward. As if whoever was at the front could see.
It was the silent man.
Corin did not know how he knew. He could not see him. He could not see anything. But he knew. He could feel it, somehow, through the rope and the white. The long thin shape at the front of the line, walking into the storm with his head up and his wild beard blowing, sure-footed, steady, as if it were a clear day. As if he could see straight through the white to whatever was on the other side of it. With Tansy's small hand in his.
He did not hurry. He did not stop. He walked as he always walked, at the same even pace, his long legs eating up the glass. And the rope followed him. And everybody on the rope followed the rope.
Corin did not know how long they walked. An hour. More. Time went strange in the white. There was no sun to go by. There was no sky. There was only the roar and the grit and the pull of the rope and the jolt of the crutch, over and over, until he could not tell whether he had been walking for an hour or a day or his whole life.
His good leg began to shake.
He felt it start. Down in the thigh. A small tremble, like a cord pulled too tight. He kept walking. It got worse. His knee wanted to fold. His foot did not want to come down flat. He set his teeth and kept walking, and the rope pulled him, and he went, and the leg shook and shook.
And then the rope stopped.
Not slowed. Stopped. All at once. He walked into the back of whoever was in front of him, and felt them stagger, and felt a hand catch his arm. Lyra's.
They stood in the white, in the roar, all of them, pressed together.
And then, very faintly, through the cloth on his face and the grit in his eyes, Corin saw something.
A darker place. In the white. A little way ahead. Square. Not a shadow. Not a shape of the storm. A square dark place, standing up out of the glass. With straight edges.
Nothing on the Glass had straight edges. Nothing but the Sun Road.
The rope jerked. Forward. Hard. Toward the square.
The Chapel
It grew out of the white as they came toward it.
First the square. Dark. Flat-topped. Then, as they came nearer, the square got edges, and the edges got corners, and the dark got shapes in it. And Corin saw, through the slit of his stinging eyes, that it was a building.
A building. Out here. In the middle of the Glass, where there was nothing, where there had been nothing for days.
It was small. Not much bigger than a cottage. Made of stone. Gray stone, square blocks of it, the kind you saw in the old churches in the city, set one on top of another in neat rows. He could see the rows. Some of them.
But the rest of it had melted.
That was the only word for it. He had seen candles melt. He had seen wax run down the side of a candle in a draft and pool at the bottom and go hard again in long lumpy ridges. That was what this building looked like. A stone candle that had stood too near a fire.
The roof was the worst. It had been pointed once. He could see that. A tall pointed roof, the kind a chapel has, with a little stone cross on the top. The cross was still there, nearly. A lump. Leaning. But the roof under it had sagged. Gone soft and slumped down in the middle, like a tent with a broken pole, and run down over the tops of the walls on both sides in thick gray folds, and hardened that way. Hanging. Frozen in the act of falling.
The walls had run too. Not as much. The top half of them had slumped and bulged, so that they leaned out, here and there, as if the chapel was breathing out. The bottom half was straighter. And all over them, the walls, the stone, there was glass.
Glass that had run. In streaks. Long thick streaks of it, down the walls from the top to the bottom, the way paint runs if you put too much on the brush. And it was colored. That was the strangest thing. Not the dark smoky glass of the ground. Colored. Red streaks. Green ones. Gold. Blue. Running down the gray stone side by side in long wavering stripes, and pooling at the bottom in colored puddles that had gone hard.
The windows, Corin thought. Somewhere in the white roar, a part of him that still thought clearly thought it. The windows. A chapel has colored windows. They melted. They ran down the walls.
And in the middle of the front wall, square and straight and whole, as if nothing had ever happened to it at all, there was a door frame.
Just the frame. The door itself was gone. But the frame was there. Four big square stones, two standing up and one across the top and one across the bottom, clean-edged, perfect. Not melted at all. As if whoever built it had made it of something that would not run.
The silent man walked through it.
Corin felt the rope go slack at the front, and then tighten again, pulling him on. He saw, very dimly, the shapes in front of him going through the square frame one by one. Into the dark beyond. Into the inside.
And then it was his turn.
He came up to the door frame. He put his hand out and touched the stone of it as he went by. It was rough. And warm. And it did not have any glass on it at all.
He stepped through.
And the roar stopped.
Not stopped. But it went. All at once. Like a door slamming. Like putting your head under water in a bath, so that the noise of the house goes far away and blurred and small. One moment the white roar was in his ears and his chest and his skull. The next it was outside. Behind him. Muffled. A great dull rushing, on the other side of the stone, like the sea heard from inside a house on a cliff.
And in here it was quiet.
So quiet that he could hear his own breath. Rasping through the cloth over his mouth. He could hear the others breathing round him. Somebody coughing. Somebody, Arran, on his litter, laughing, weakly, the way a man laughs when he has been very frightened and now is not.
And under it all, coming back, faint, the hum.
Corin pulled the cloth down off his face.
The air in here was still. It smelled of dust and old stone and something else, very faint. Something sweet. Like old candles. Like the inside of a church on a weekday when nobody is there.
He opened his eyes.
And for a moment, he thought the storm had followed him in.
Because the light in here was colored.
Inside
The light came through the walls.
Not through windows. There were no windows anymore. They had all run. But the glass that had run down the walls outside had run down the inside too, in the same long streaks, and in some places it had run right through, where the stone was thin, so that the wall was not stone at all anymore but glass. Colored glass. Thick and wavy and full of bubbles, like the bottom of an old bottle.
And the white storm outside was shining through it.
So the inside of the chapel was full of colored light. Dim. Soft. Moving, a little, as the storm moved outside. Green, mostly. A deep sea green, from the long green streaks on the left wall. And gold, from the right. And here and there, low down near the floor, patches of red, like spilled wine. And blue, high up, in the sagging roof, where a round window had been once and had melted into a great blue drip, hanging down from the ceiling like an icicle made of sky.
It lay on everything. On the floor. On the stone walls. On the faces of the people standing in the doorway, pulling the cloths off their mouths, blinking. Lyra's face was green on one side and gold on the other. Oren's was red to the chin. Tansy, standing in the middle of the floor with the clay lamp in her hand, was blue all over, like a little drowned girl, and her blue flame had gone invisible in the blue light.
Corin stood in the doorway and looked round.
It was one room. One long low room, longer than it was wide. The floor was flagstones, big and square and worn smooth in the middle, as if a great many feet had walked up and down it for a great many years. Some of the flags had cracked. Some had lifted, at the edges. Grit had blown in through the door and lay in drifts in the corners, pale and fine as flour.
There were benches. Or there had been. Stone benches, low and plain, running along both walls. Most of them had melted at the ends where they met the walls, and fused into them, so that they seemed to grow out of the stone. One, near the back, was still whole. A long stone bench standing by itself, square and solid, with a hollow worn in its seat where people had sat.
At the far end there was a step. One step, up. And above the step, the back wall.
He could not see the back wall properly. Not yet. It was in shadow. The colored light did not reach it. It was only a dark shape, a flat dark space at the end of the room.
He looked at it anyway. He did not know why. Something about it pulled at his eyes.
Then somebody bumped into him from behind, and the rope at his waist tugged, and he remembered it was there.
They untied the rope. It took a long time. The knots had pulled tight in the storm and the grit had got into them, and everyone's fingers were cold and stiff and cut. Kestrel did it in the end, going down the line with her knife, cutting each knot, so that they each stood there afterward with a little loop of rope still round their waist like a belt, and had to work that loose too.
The litter came in last. Brin and Oda and Sef and Marrit, carrying it through the door frame sideways because it was too wide to go straight. Arran was lying on it with his eyes shut and his face very white under the gray line and the red of the light. His foot in its wrappings was dark with grit. But he opened his eyes when they set him down on the flagstones, and looked up at the sagging roof and the great blue drip, and said, faintly, in the southern tongue:
"Oh. Pretty."
And Kestrel, kneeling beside him, put her hand over her eyes and laughed. Or cried. Corin could not tell which. It might have been both.
Vespera knelt down by the litter and began to unwrap the foot.
And Corin looked down at his own hands.
They were bleeding. Not much. The fine red lines across the backs of them had all beaded up and run together, here and there, into small smears. They looked like the backs of hands that have been in a fight with a cat. Under the blood, the skin was gray. The black tips of his fingers were black. The lines did not hurt, much. They stung. That was all.
He turned them over. The palms were clean. He had kept them inside his coat.
Lyra's hand came and took his left one. She turned it to the green light and looked at the cuts. Her own face had cuts on it. Small ones. On her forehead, above where the cloth had been. On her cheekbones. One on the side of her nose. Fine red lines, like the lines on his hands.
"You should have covered them," she said.
"I know."
"Vespera will have something."
"I know."
She did not let go of his hand. She stood holding it, turned up to the light, and looked at the cuts for a long time. As if they were writing she was trying to read.
Then she let go, and went to find Vespera's bag.
Corin stood by the doorway. Behind him, the storm roared. Outside the stone frame, the world was white. Nothing in it. Nothing at all. As if the whole Glass had been rubbed out.
He turned his back on it. And went in.
