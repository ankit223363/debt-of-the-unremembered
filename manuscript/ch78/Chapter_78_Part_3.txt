Chapter 78: Glass Rain, Part 3 of 5: Ledger Eight
Ledger Eight
He buttoned his shirt. Slowly. His fingers were stupid on the buttons. And he did not ask about the last line, the one at the very bottom of his ribs, in the old curling letters. He had felt it there. Lower down than the others, along the bone, where the skin was thin. He had seen it, too, out of the corner of his eye, when he bent to look. A line of shapes that were not capitals. Loops and hooks and long tails, running into each other, like a vine.
He did not ask. He thought Lyra would not know. And he thought, without knowing why, that if he asked Vespera she would only look at him with her pale eyes and say later.
Instead he asked for the book.
Lyra gave it to him. She had been carrying it inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth. She unwrapped it and put it in his hands.
It was heavier than he remembered. He did not remember, really. That was the thing. He knew it was his. He knew it the way he knew his own hands. But he did not remember the weight of it, or the feel of the cover. Smooth, old, dark leather, gone soft at the corners. With a pattern pressed into it, very faint, worn almost away. A tree. With something in its branches.
Tansy brought the lamp.
She did not wait to be asked. She saw him with the book and came across the chapel with her clay lamp in both hands, the small blue flame bending as she walked, and set it down on the flagstones beside him. Carefully. Where it would not tip.
"There," she said. "Now you can see."
The flame was very small. A little blue tongue on the end of a twisted twig, sitting in a pool of something that was not oil and did not smell. Its light was cold. Blue-white. It made the gold light round them look warm, and the green look deep. But it lit the page.
He opened the book.
LEDGER VIII.
His own hand. He knew it at once. The big square capitals, each one standing by itself, not joined. Pressed hard into the paper, so that he could feel them from the back of the page with his fingertips.
BEGUN AT SAINT BRIDE'S, THE NIGHT AURELIAN THORNE DIED. IN HIS WIFE'S BOOK. GIVEN ME BY HIS DAUGHTER.
He read it twice. He looked at Lyra.
She was sitting very still beside him, with her knees up. Watching the page, not him.
His daughter. That was her. He knew that. He had read it on her face, the night he woke. He had read it in the way she held his hand in the storm. But here it was, in his own writing, from a night he did not remember, in a place he did not remember. Saint Bride's. A church. A crypt. He had a feeling of a crypt. Low round arches, like the inside of a loaf. And a smell of tea.
He turned the page.
READ THIS FIRST.
Under it, the next line.
LYRA THORNE. PARTNER. RULE ONE: NO LIES BETWEEN US. NOT EVEN THE KIND ONES.
He stopped there for a long time.
Then the next.
I DO NOT REMEMBER HER. SHE READ THIS PAGE TO ME. SHE IS COMING AFTER.
He looked up again. She was still watching the page.
"You read it to me," he said.
"Yes."
"Before. In the crypt."
"Yes."
"And now you've read it to me again. Off my own chest."
She did not answer that. She did not need to.
He read on.
He read slowly. It was hard. Some of the words he knew at once, the way you know a face in a crowd. Others he had to look at for a long time before they came. And some did not come at all. He read them anyway, as shapes, and went on, and sometimes they came later, two or three pages on, all at once, with a little jolt.
He read about a boat, and a ditch, and a rag doll in a basket with one button eye. THE STITCHES LEAN BACK. I HAVE SEEN STITCHES LIKE THAT. I CANNOT THINK WHERE. He read about a village called Thistle Ford, and a woman with a pipe she did not light. MY FEET WENT LEFT AT THE TWO SISTERS. I DO NOT KNOW WHY. He read about Haverstock, and a cross with names on it. SHE READ OREN HIS FAMILY. HE SAID EVERY ONE AFTER HER.
He looked across the chapel at Oren, sitting by the door with his sword. Oren did not look round.
He read about Last Thorn. THEY CARRY THEIR DEAD TOGETHER SO NOBODY DROWNS. He read a page he had written for somebody called Gideon, and under it, in his own capitals, very small: SHE FOUND MY WRONG LETTERS. He read about a funeral. SUNDAY. HER FATHER WAS BURIED TODAY. SHE STOOD FACING SOUTH. I STOOD WITH HER.
He stopped there too.
He did not look at her that time. He did not need to. He could feel her beside him. Very still.
HASK TOOK HER DEATH AND DIED. THOSE I OWE: HASK.
Hask. The hum in his head went quiet when he read that. As if somebody had put a hand over a bell.
He read on. Through the turnips, and the V. jar, and the baby, and the needle, and the tree with a crown. THERE WAS SOMETHING BEHIND MY LEFT EAR. THE LAMP TOOK IT. Through the Edge. Through the fire. Through the first night on the Glass, and the trench, and the people in the trench, in shaking letters, written at a rest, by starlight. Through the girl who talked to Nobody. THIS WAY IF YOURE NICE.
And then the writing changed.
It was not his anymore. Not his big square capitals. Capitals still, but smaller. Thin and even and very careful, each one exactly the same height as the one before, like letters cut on a stone. The writing of somebody who had learned to write properly, at a desk, a long time ago, and had not had much use for it since.
Vespera's.
She had written the last pages. While he was gone. While he was lying on the glass by the iron horse with no name and no words, roaring. She had written down what happened. THE IRON HORSE. HE WENT IN AND DID NOT COME BACK OUT. And then a gap. And then: GROUNDING. I INKED HIM. HIS GIRL CHOSE THE WORDS.
And then, last of all, in the old careful hand, smaller than the rest:
SHE READ HIM THE WHOLE BOOK, OUT LOUD, ALL AFTERNOON. HE CAME BACK UP THE WELL ON HER VOICE.
Corin read that line three times.
Then he closed the book. And sat with it on his knees, in the blue light and the gold, and did not say anything at all for a long time.
One True Thing
The storm roared. The colored light did not change. Across the chapel, Vespera was asleep on her bench, sitting up, with her chin on her chest. The Ashborn were talking in their own tongue, low, in a huddle. Tansy had gone back to the silent man by the step, and was sitting beside him, talking to him in a whisper.
Corin sat with the closed book on his knees.
"Lyra," he said.
She turned her head.
He did not know how to ask it. He had been thinking about it while he read, all through the last pages, and he still did not know. So he asked it the plainest way he could.
"Tell me something," he said. "One thing. One true thing. About you." He put his hand flat on the cover of the book. "Not in here."
She looked at the book. Then at him.
"Why?"
He thought about that.
"Because everything in here," he said slowly, "I wrote. Or Vespera wrote. Or you read to me and I wrote. It's all things I saw. Or things I was told. It's all me, looking at you." He looked down at his gray hand on the dark leather. "I want one thing that's just you. That I didn't write down. That I didn't see. That you give me."
She was quiet for a long time.
He thought she was not going to answer. She sat with her knees up and her arms round them and looked at the far wall, at the long gold streak of melted glass running down it, and he watched the light move on her face and waited.
Then she said, "All right."
She did not look at him. She kept looking at the gold streak. And she began.
"There was a day," she said. "In the winter. In the city. Before all this. A Friday, I think. We were walking somewhere. I don't remember where. Somewhere cold. It doesn't matter." She stopped. Went on. "And you were in front of me. Walking. And I was watching you."
"Watching me?"
"Watching you walk." She almost smiled. "You have a way of walking. You don't know you do it. You stick your head forward. Like this." She showed him. Pushed her chin out, her neck out, stiffly. "And your legs are too long. And you take these great long steps, and your knees go up, and your elbows go out, and your coat flaps. And you look about you. All the time. As if you're looking for something in a ditch."
Corin did not say anything. He was listening so hard it hurt.
"And I said it before I could stop myself," said Lyra. "Out loud. To your back. I said, You walk like a heron."
The small line on his ribs. Under his shirt. He felt it. Hot. As if it had heard.
"And you didn't even turn round," said Lyra. "You didn't stop. You didn't look at me. You just went on walking, with your head stuck out and your elbows going, and you said, quite calmly, over your shoulder, as if you'd been waiting all day to say it—"
She stopped.
And turned her head. And looked at him.
And he knew it. Before she said it. He did not remember it. He did not remember the day, or the street, or the cold, or her voice saying heron to his back. None of it. It was all gone. Burned, or taken, or lost in the trench with his name.
But he knew what he had said.
It came up out of him the way the word had come in the heap, in the dark, the night he woke. Out of nowhere. Out of the bottom of the well. A thing he had always known and never known he knew.
"And you talk like a kettle," said Corin.
Lyra's face went still.
And Corin laughed.
He did not mean to. It came up out of him the same way the words had. All at once. A real laugh. Not the small tired breath he had been laughing with since the trench. A laugh from all the way down. It shook him. It shook the book on his knees. It went up into the sagging roof of the chapel and came back off the stone, and the Ashborn stopped talking in their huddle and turned round to look, and Tansy looked up from beside the silent man with her mouth open.
He could not stop. He did not know why it was so funny. It was not funny. It was a stupid thing. A heron. A kettle. Two people being rude to each other on a cold street. But it was the funniest thing he had ever heard. Because it was true. Because she did. She talked like a kettle. She talked in short sharp bursts, and then went quiet, and then burst out again, hissing and spitting, when something made her angry. Like a kettle on a stove. And he walked like a heron. And somebody had written it on his ribs so he would never forget.
He laughed until his chest hurt. Until the new ink hurt. Until his eyes ran.
And Lyra did not laugh.
He saw that, through the laughing. Through the wet in his eyes. She sat beside him and watched him laugh, and her face was very still, and very white in the gold light, and she did not laugh at all.
It'll Grow
The laugh went out of him slowly.
It did not stop all at once. It ran down, the way a clock runs down. Smaller. And smaller. Until it was only a breath, and a shake in his shoulders, and the wet on his face. And then it was gone, and he was sitting in the gold light with the book on his knees and his chest aching, looking at her.
She was looking back at him. Still. White.
"What?" he said. "What's wrong?"
"Nothing."
"You didn't laugh."
"No."
"Why not?"
She did not answer. She looked at him with that still white face for a long moment more. And then she looked down, at her own knees, at her hands round them. And her hands were holding on very tight.
He did not understand. He had told her the right thing. He knew he had. He had seen it in her face, the moment before he said it. She had been waiting for it. Hoping for it. And he had said it, and it had been right, and he had laughed. And she had not.
He looked at her. Trying to read her. The way he had tried to read the words on his chest. Upside down. Backward.
And as he looked, his eyes went to her hair.
It was short. Very short. He had noticed that before, of course. He had seen it every day since he woke. Dark and short and sticking up a little at the back, where she had slept on it, and flat on top with sweat and dust from the storm. Cut close to her head, all round. Not cut by anyone who knew how to cut hair. Uneven. Longer on one side than the other. Ragged at the edges, as if it had been done quickly, with a knife.
She had not always had it short. He knew that. He did not remember it long. He did not remember her any other way than this. But he knew. The way he had known the kettle. Down at the bottom of the well, something knew that her hair had been long, once. Long and dark and heavy. And that something had happened to it.
And before he knew he was going to say anything, he heard himself say it. Gently. The way you would say a thing to somebody who was hurt.
"It'll grow," said Corin.
Lyra's head came up.
She looked at him. And her face did a thing he had never seen any face do. It broke. Not all at once. Slowly. Like ice breaking on a pond, from the middle out. The stillness cracked, and the whiteness cracked, and something came up through the cracks that he did not have a name for.
Then she turned away.
She turned her whole body away from him. On the flagstones. Round. To face the wall. The fused stone bench, and the long gold streak of melted glass running down the wall above it. She turned her back to him and sat with her face to the wall and her shoulders up round her ears and her arms wrapped round her knees, and did not make a sound.
Corin sat and looked at her back.
He did not know what he had done.
He went back over it. It'll grow. That was all he had said. He did not know where it came from. It had just been there, in his mouth, the way kettle had been there. A thing to say about hair. A kind thing. A true thing. Hair grows. That was the thing about it. It was not like the other things.
It came back.
And as he thought that, he heard it. Not in his own voice. In hers. Hair grows. That's the thing about it. It isn't like the other things. It comes back. From a long way off. From a doorway somewhere, with a cold draft coming through it, and somebody standing in it with their back to him, holding a pair of boots by the laces.
He did not remember it. He did not remember the doorway, or the boots, or the place. But he heard it. Her voice. Saying it. To him.
They were her words.
She had said them to him, once. In some cold room, after something had happened. After the hair. And he had kept them. Somewhere. Down in the bottom of himself, under everything that had burned and everything that had been taken, he had kept them. Without knowing. And now he had given them back to her. As if they were his own.
He sat very still.
He did not touch her. He thought about it. He thought about putting his hand on her back, between her shoulders, where they were drawn up so tight. He did not. He did not know if she would want it. He did not know anything.
So he sat. And waited. And after a while he opened the book again, on his knees, and looked at the last page, at the old careful capitals. HE CAME BACK UP THE WELL ON HER VOICE.
And waited.
After a long time, she turned back round. Her face was dry. Quite dry. But the skin round her eyes was red, and her mouth was set hard, the way it was set when she was holding something heavy and did not want anybody to see it was heavy.
She did not say anything about it. Neither did he.
She only reached out and took his left hand, the gray one, the cut one, and held it. Hard. In both of hers. And sat beside him like that, with her shoulder against his, facing the colored light, and did not let go.
The Mural
Later, when Lyra had gone to sleep against his shoulder, he looked at the back wall.
He had been looking at it, on and off, all day. He could not help it. Every time his eyes moved round the chapel, they went there. To the flat dark space above the one step at the far end, where the colored light did not reach. It pulled at him. The way a word you cannot quite remember pulls at you.
The silent man was still sitting under it. He had not moved all day. Except to eat, once, when Tansy held bread to his mouth. He sat on the flagstones below the step with his long legs crossed and his hands on his knees and his face turned up to the wall. Looking at it. As if he was waiting for it to say something.
Corin eased Lyra's head off his shoulder. Gently. Onto his rolled coat. She murmured, and did not wake.
He picked up Tansy's lamp.
Tansy had left it beside him when she went back to the silent man. The little clay bowl with its twisted twig and its small cold flame. He held it in his right hand. He got his crutch under his left arm. He got up, the clerk's way, slowly, grunting, with the stiff leg swung out. Nobody looked round. The chapel was quiet, except for the storm outside and the soft sounds of people sleeping.
He walked up the chapel. Past the Ashborn, asleep in their huddle against the left wall. Past Arran on his litter in the green light. Past Vespera on her bench. To the step.
The silent man did not look at him. Tansy, asleep now against the man's side with her mouth open, did not stir.
Corin stepped up the step. Carefully. Crutch first. Then the good leg. Then the stiff one, swung up after.
And held up the lamp.
The blue light went up the back wall.
The wall had run, like all the others. The glass from above had come down it in a sheet. Not in streaks, here. A sheet. Thick and wavy and clear, like water frozen in the act of pouring down a rock. It covered the whole wall from the top to the bottom, a hand thick, more, with bubbles in it, and ripples, and here and there a long thin streak of color, gold or green, running down through it.
And under the sheet of glass, on the stone, there was a painting.
He saw it slowly. The way you see a face in a dark window. First nothing. Then a shape. Then shapes. Then, all at once, what the shapes were.
Figures. Painted on the stone, under the glass. Standing in a row. Side by side, all the way across the wall, from one corner to the other. Tall. Taller than a man. Each one standing very straight, very still, with its hands folded in front of it. In long robes. Old robes, the kind you saw in the oldest pictures in the oldest churches. Deep red. Deep blue. Gold at the hems, and at the cuffs. The paint had faded and the glass had blurred it, but he could still see the colors. Under the water of the glass.
And on their heads, crowns.
Every one of them. Thin gold crowns, painted with care, each point picked out. Not big crowns. Not the great heavy crowns of kings in picture books. Small ones. Simple. Like circles of gold leaves.
He counted them. He could count them. There were not too many. He went along the row, slowly, with the lamp, and counted on his fingers, the way a child does.
One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven.
Eight.
Eight crowned figures, in a row, under the glass.
He looked at their faces.
The glass had blurred them. Of course it had. The glass had blurred everything. But the faces were there. He could see them. The first had a long face with a beard. The second, a round one, a woman's, he thought, with her hair under the crown. The third was young. The fourth was old. Each one different. Each one blurred and soft and swimming under the thick glass, like faces seen through rain on a window. But there. Eyes. Noses. Mouths. You could see that each one had been a person once. That whoever painted them had painted real faces. People they knew.
Seven faces. Blurred, but there.
And the eighth.
The eighth figure stood at the end of the row, in the far right corner. Like the others. Tall and straight in a long robe, a deep green one, with the gold at the hems. The hands folded. The thin gold crown on the head.
And under the crown, where the face should be, there was nothing.
Not blurred. Not faded. Nothing. Smooth. The paint there had melted, and run, and gone flat and pale and blank, as if somebody had taken a thumb and wiped it. All of it. The eyes and the nose and the mouth. Just that one face. Just that one. As if the heat that had come through this chapel long ago, on the day of the light, or the glass that had come down over the wall, had reached out of all the eight and chosen that one, and rubbed it out.
The others were all still there. Only the eighth was gone.
Corin stood on the step with the lamp held up and looked at the smooth place where a face had been.
And without thinking, without deciding, without knowing he was going to do it at all, he put his hand on it.
His left hand. The gray one. He let go of the crutch handle, so that the crutch leaned against him, and reached up, and laid his gray palm flat on the cold thick glass, over the smooth blank place, the way you would lay your hand on somebody's face in the dark to know who they were.
The glass was cool. Smooth. A little damp.
He stood like that for a moment. With his hand on the eighth face.
Then he took it away.
Quickly. As if it had burned. Though it had not. Though it had only been cool. He took it away and held it against his chest, and stood there with his heart going hard, and did not know why.
Below him, on the flagstones, the silent man had turned his head.
He was not looking at the wall anymore. He was looking at Corin. At Corin's hand, pressed against his chest. With his pale, still, deep-well eyes.
And then he turned back. And looked up at the wall again. At the eighth figure. At the smooth place under the crown.
And went on looking.
