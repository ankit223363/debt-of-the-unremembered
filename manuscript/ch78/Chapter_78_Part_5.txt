Chapter 78: Glass Rain, Part 5 of 5: Counting in Her Sleep
Counting in Her Sleep
He went back up the chapel and sat down beside Lyra. She had looked away when he came. Now she looked back. And did not ask what he and Oren had said. And he did not tell her. Not yet.
She was tired. He could see it. More tired than he was, perhaps. She had been awake for most of two days and a night, walking, and carrying the litter, and watching him, always watching him. He could see the watching in her face. Even now. Even sitting still.
"Sleep," he said.
"I'm not tired."
"You are."
She did not argue. That was how he knew how tired she was. She leaned her head back against the stone of the bench, and shut her eyes, and was asleep before he could have counted to three.
He did not sleep. He sat beside her and watched her instead.
He had not had the chance before. Not really. Since the trench, since the waking, she had always been watching him. Never the other way. And he found, now that he could, that he wanted to look. To learn her. The way he was learning everything else. Again.
She slept with her mouth a little open. With her head tipped back and a little to one side, toward him. Her short hair stuck out against the stone. There was a line between her eyebrows that did not go away, even asleep. A small one. Like a crease in a page that has been folded too many times.
Her hands were in her lap. Folded. Holding on to each other.
And after a while, her lips began to move.
Not words. Not at first. Just moving. Then, very faintly, a sound. A whisper. He leaned closer to hear.
"...four. Five. Six..."
She was counting.
In her sleep. Very softly. Barely a breath.
"...seven. Eight. Nine..."
He knew that. He did not know how he knew it. But he knew it the way he knew the kettle. She counts when she's frightened. Somewhere in him, under everything, that was written down. Not on his skin. Deeper.
She counted on. Past nine. He could not follow her there. The numbers went into the place where numbers did not mean anything to him anymore, and became just sounds, just shapes of breath. But he listened to them anyway. The rhythm of them. Steady. Going on. Like footsteps. Like the tap of a crutch on glass.
What was she frightened of? Here? Now? In her sleep, in the quiet, with the storm outside and the walls round them?
He thought he knew.
Him.
Not of him. For him. Frightened that she would wake up and he would be gone again. That the eyes would be wrong again. That he would look at her the way he had looked at her by the iron horse, as if she were a noise. That he would go down the well, and she would read and read and read, and this time he would not come up.
He put his hand out.
He did it slowly. He did not want to wake her. He put his left hand, the gray one, the cut one, very gently on top of her folded hands in her lap. Just laid it there. Not holding. Just there.
The counting stopped.
In the middle. Between one number and the next. Her lips went still.
And her hands, under his, came apart. Slowly. And one of them turned over, palm up, under his palm. And closed round his fingers. Tight. The way it had closed round them in the storm, on the rope.
She did not wake. Her breathing went slow and deep. The line between her eyebrows went, a little. Not all the way. A little.
Corin sat beside her with his hand in hers and did not move for a long time.
He thought: I will remember this.
And then he thought: No. I might not. Things go.
And then he thought of the book. On his knees. With the pencil in its spine.
And he thought: Then I will write it down.
Writing It Down
Before the light went, he wrote.
He did not decide to. His hand decided. He woke from a doze against the bench with the book still on his knees and the pencil already in his fingers, as if his hand had gone looking for it while he slept. The stub of pencil from the spine. Very short now. Worn down nearly to nothing, so that he had to hold it right at the end, pinched between his thumb and his first two fingers, with the gray of his fingertips pressed white round it.
He opened the book to the last page. Past Vespera's careful capitals. HE CAME BACK UP THE WELL ON HER VOICE. To the space under them.
And sat looking at the empty paper.
He did not know what to put. He knew he should put something. That was what the book was for. That was what it had always been for. Every day, every place, everything that happened, everything he lost and everything he kept. Written down. So it was somewhere. So it was not only in him, where it could burn.
But he did not know how to start.
So he started the way the book started every day. With where he was.
THE CHAPEL.
His hand was shaky. The letters came out big and leaning, not square the way they used to be. He could see that, looking back through the pages. His old capitals marched across the paper like soldiers, straight and even and pressed hard. These ones staggered. Like men at the end of a long march, leaning on each other.
He did not mind. They were his.
THE STORM.
He stopped. Thought about the storm. The white wall. The grit. The fine red lines on his hands, scabbing now, dark against the gray. The rope. The silent man at the front. He thought about writing all of it. He did not. It would take too long, and the pencil was too short, and he did not think the storm was the thing that mattered.
He thought about what mattered.
He looked at Lyra.
She was asleep. She had gone to sleep again sitting up, against the bench beside him, with her head tipped back against the stone and her mouth a little open. Her short dark hair was sticking up at the back. There was a smear of grit on her cheek, and a fine red cut across the bridge of her nose. In the gold light she looked very young. Younger than he had ever seen her. Though he could not remember having seen her any other way.
He looked down at the book. And wrote.
SHE TOLD ME ABOUT THE KETTLE.
He stopped. Read it. Went on.
I LAUGHED.
And then, after a long time, because it was true, and because the first rule on the first page said no lies between us, not even the kind ones:
SHE DID NOT.
He sat and looked at the three lines. At the shaky leaning capitals. SHE TOLD ME ABOUT THE KETTLE. I LAUGHED. SHE DID NOT.
It was not enough. He knew it was not enough. But he did not know what else to say about it. He did not understand it well enough. Why he had laughed. Why she had not. Why it had made her face go white and still like that. Why, when he said the other thing, her whole face had broken.
He thought about the other thing.
He wrote it down too. Slowly.
I SAID IT'LL GROW. SHE TURNED AWAY.
He stopped again. He looked at it. He thought about the voice he had heard, afterward. Her voice. In a doorway, somewhere, a long time ago. Hair grows. That's the thing about it. Saying it to him. First.
He wrote the last line. Very small. Smaller than the others. At the bottom.
I THINK SHE SAID IT TO ME FIRST.
Then he put the pencil back in the spine, and closed the book, and sat with it on his knees.
Across the chapel, the silent man was still sitting under the back wall. Still looking up at the smooth face. And Tansy had gone to sleep against him, with her mouth open and her uncle's coat over her like a tent.
Oren had stopped sharpening. He was sitting by the door with his sword across his knees and his eyes open now, looking out at the white.
The white was not as white.
Corin saw that. He sat up a little. Looked past Oren, through the square stone frame of the door. At the storm.
It was going gray. Not dark gray. Light. The light of evening, coming through it. And it was thinner. He could see, very faintly, through it, something like a line. Low down. Where the ground should be.
And the roar had changed.
It was not as loud. He had not noticed it getting less. But it had. It was not a roar anymore. It was a rushing. Like wind in trees. Like the sea a long way off.
Oren turned his head and looked back at him across the chapel.
Neither of them said anything. But Corin saw Oren's hand close on the hilt of his sword.
Ready
Kestrel had seen it too. The thinning. She was on her feet before Corin could say anything, going round the chapel, touching people on the shoulder. Not loudly. Not shouting, the way she had shouted in the white. Quietly. One touch. One word. Ready.
And they got ready.
They did it without talking. Corin watched them. The Ashborn rolling their blankets tight and strapping them to their packs. Sef and Brin checking the knots on the litter poles. Marrit putting her bone charms away one by one into a little leather bag, and tying the neck, and hanging it round her own neck under her coat. Oda tying her scarf back on, ready, over her mouth and nose, though the storm was nearly gone.
Vespera packing her pots. Each one into its own fold of cloth, into its own place in the bag, the way she had wiped her needles at the iron horse. Not hurrying. She never hurried. But not slow, either. Then kneeling by Arran's litter and wrapping his foot again in clean linen, over the honey, tight, from the toes to the ankle, while he lay with his eyes shut and his jaw set and did not make a sound.
Corin put the book away.
He wrapped it in its oilcloth, the way Lyra had done, folding the corners in. He put it inside his coat. On the left. Against his chest. Over the black words he could not read. He felt the weight of it settle there.
Then he woke Lyra.
He did not have to do much. He only took his hand out of hers. And she woke at once. All at once. Her eyes open and her hand already going to her sword, before she knew where she was. The way Oren woke. The way, he thought, Sola had woken.
She looked at him.
"What?"
"The storm," said Corin. "It's going."
She listened. He saw her hear it. The rushing, gone small. And under it, more and more, the hum.
She was up. Sword buckled. Pack on. Hand held down to him, to help him up, before he had even got the crutch under his arm.
He took it. He let her pull him up.
And they stood together in the middle of the chapel, in the colored light that was going dim now as the white outside went gray, with everybody round them ready and waiting and nobody saying anything. All of them listening. To the storm going. To the hum coming back.
At the far end, under the back wall, the silent man still sat with Tansy asleep against him. He had not moved. He was still looking up at the eighth face.
But his hands, on his knees, had closed.
Scoured
At dusk, the wind dropped.
It did not stop all at once. It went down the way a fire goes down. Slowly, and then quickly, and then not at all. The rushing became a hiss. The hiss became a whisper. The whisper became a long sigh, like a breath let out. And then there was nothing. Only the hum.
The silence after it was so big that it rang.
Everybody went still. Corin saw them do it. All round the chapel, people with their packs on their backs, stopping where they stood. Looking at each other. Arran on his litter, blinking up at the roof. Marrit with her hand on the little bag at her throat. Vespera by the bench, with her own bag half-buckled. Tansy, awake now, standing by the silent man with her lamp. All of them listening to the silence the way you listen to a noise.
Kestrel reached inside her coat, without a word, and took out the bone flute, and dropped it on Arran's chest. He grinned up at her and hung it back round his neck on its cord. "The storm stopped," he said. "You said." And she said, "We did," and almost smiled.
Kestrel got up and went to the door.
Oren was already standing there, in the square stone frame, with his sword in his hand. He moved aside to let her pass. And she stepped out.
Corin got up. Crutch. Good leg. Stiff leg. Lyra was up beside him, awake now, rubbing her eyes. They went to the door together, and stood in it beside Oren, and looked out.
And the Glass had been made new.
That was what it looked like. As if somebody had taken the whole of it and scrubbed it. The storm had come over it all day and taken off the top. The dust. The grit. Everything that had lain on it. And what was left was bright. So bright. Even in the dusk, even with the sun going down red in the west and the sky going purple overhead, the glass shone. It shone like the inside of a shell. Like water in a bowl. Clean and dark and gleaming, every swell and hollow of it picked out sharp, going away and away to the edge of the world. And the last of the storm hanging low along the south, a long thin smear of white, going away.
It was beautiful. Corin had not thought the Glass could be beautiful. It was.
And it was empty.
He saw that next. Looking down at the ground in front of the door. At the glass round the chapel walls. At the place where they had come in that morning, on the rope, out of the white.
There was nothing there.
No prints. Not one. Not theirs. Not the deep round dents of the crutch, or the drag of the stiff foot, or the soft felted marks of the Ashborn, or the two long lines of the litter poles. Nothing. The storm had taken them all. Scoured them away. As if nobody had ever walked here. As if nobody had ever come.
Kestrel stood a few paces out from the door, on the bright glass, and looked round her. Slowly. All the way round. North. East. South. West. And back.
Then she came back to the door.
"Good," she said.
Oren looked at her.
"Every track is gone," said Kestrel. "Ours. Theirs. Everybody's." She looked south. "They cannot follow us now. Not by the ground. There is nothing to follow."
She was quiet a moment.
"And bad," she said.
"Bad?" said Lyra.
Kestrel did not answer at once. She was still looking south. At the long thin smear of white going away along the edge of the world.
"All day," she said slowly, "the storm was loud. So loud that we could not hear each other speak. So loud that we could not hear the hum." She turned her head and looked at Lyra. "So loud that we could not hear anything coming."
Nobody said anything.
Corin looked south. At the bright, empty, scoured glass. At the dusk coming down over it.
And he listened.
The hum. Only the hum. High and thin and steady, the way it had been since the Edge. Nothing else. No wind now. No storm. No voices. Behind him, in the chapel, nobody was speaking. Everybody was listening, the way he was.
The hum.
The hum.
And something else.
Close
Tick.
Corin heard it. Very faint. So faint that at first he thought it was in his own head. The way you hear your own heart, sometimes, in a quiet room at night.
Tick.
Not his heart. Too even. Too hard. Too much like metal.
Tick. Tick.
He turned his head. Slowly. Listening. Trying to find where it came from.
It did not come from the south.
That was the first thing. That was what made his stomach go cold and drop. He had been listening south. They had all been listening south. That was where the iron was. Behind them. Back the way they had come, a day behind, two days, frozen every night on the open glass and walking every morning on their trail.
This did not come from the south.
It came from the west. From his left. From the side where the sun was going down. Out of the low red light, out of the last long thin smear of storm-white still hanging there along the edge of the world, between the chapel and the sun.
And it was not far.
Tick. Tick. Tick.
Close. It was close. Very close. Not a day away. Not an hour. Close enough that he could hear it over the hum without straining. Close enough that it was getting louder while he listened.
And it was not one.
He heard that next. Under the first ticking, a little out of step with it, a second. Like two clocks in two rooms. Like two hearts. Tick-tick. Tick-tick. Two of them.
Two iron men. Walking. At the speed of a man. Out of the white.
They had walked through the storm.
He understood it all at once. The way you understand a thing in a nightmare, all of it, without anybody telling you. They had walked through the storm. All day. While the party sat in the chapel and ate cold bread and listened to Arran's flute and Tansy's talk and the small sounds. While the white roared and hid everything. The iron men had not stopped for the storm. Why would they? The grit could not cut them. The white could not blind them. They did not need to see. They only needed to walk. Where the word said. And the storm had hidden the sound of them coming, all day, under its roar, so that nobody heard. Nobody heard anything.
And now the storm was gone. And the sound was here.
And they were close.
Kestrel had heard it too. He saw her head snap round. West. He saw her face.
Oren had heard it. He was already turning in the doorway, with the sword coming up.
Lyra had heard it. Her hand was on his arm. Tight. Tighter than the rope.
Behind them, in the chapel, somebody said something in the southern tongue. Sharp. Afraid. And then there was a scramble of movement, the litter being snatched up off the floor, Arran saying what, what, the poles knocking on the stone.
Corin looked west.
The last of the storm lay along the edge of the world in a thin white smear, with the red sun sinking into it. And in the smear, low down, against the red, there were two shapes.
Dark. Tall. Thin. Too tall.
Walking.
Coming out of the white the way the chapel had come out of it that morning. First shapes. Then edges. Then what the edges were. Two tall gray figures with their collars up and their blank heads turned toward the chapel, and their long arms hanging, and their great shovel hands. Walking straight across the scoured bright glass. Not fast. Not slow. Never faster. Never slower.
Toward the door.
And the silent man was already on his feet.
Corin did not see him get up. He never did. He was just there. Beside Corin in the door frame, tall and thin, with his wild beard and his long hands. He had come down the whole length of the chapel from the back wall, from the step, from the eighth face, without a sound. And he was standing in the doorway with Tansy's hand already in his, looking west, at the two shapes, with his still deep-well eyes.
Tansy was looking up at him.
Then she looked west. At the shapes. At the sun, sinking. At the sky, going from red to purple overhead. Measuring it. The way Corin had seen Kestrel measure the water. The way he had seen Oren measure a room.
The sun was a finger above the edge of the world. Less.
She looked north. At something Corin could not see. Something out there in the dusk, on the bright glass, past the chapel. A long low line. Darker than the glass. Like a wall. Like a row of walls.
She tugged the silent man's hand. Hard.
And she said it. Not in her flat, small, frightened voice from the morning. In her other voice. Her bird's voice. High and quick and certain, as if she had known all day that this was coming and had only been waiting for the moment to say it.
"Run," said Tansy. "The Ridges."
