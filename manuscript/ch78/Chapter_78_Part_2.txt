Chapter 78: Glass Rain, Part 2 of 5: A Day Indoors
A Day Indoors
The storm did not stop.
Corin had thought it would. Storms stopped. In the city, a storm came over the roofs and rained on everything for an hour and went away, and the gutters ran, and then it was over. This did not go away. It roared outside the chapel walls all morning, and when the morning was gone it roared on, and the colored light on the walls never changed, never brightened, never dimmed. Green and gold and red and blue. Steady. Like the light in a dream.
They settled.
There was nothing else to do. Kestrel had said it, standing in the middle of the floor with her arms folded, when the rope was all untied: We stay. Until it stops. Nobody argued. Nobody wanted to go back out into that.
So they settled into the chapel the way people settle into a room they have to share for a long time. Each finding a place. Each making it theirs, a little.
Vespera took the whole bench at the back, the one that had not melted. She sat on one end of it with her bag open and her pots in a row beside her, like a woman at a market stall, and called people over one by one to have their cuts seen to. Arran first. Then the others. She had a salve in a small round tin that smelled of pine and something bitter, and she dabbed it on every red line on every face and every hand, and it stung worse than the glass, and nobody complained.
She did Corin's hands last. Without speaking. Turning each one over in her own brown spotted hands, holding it close to her eyes in the gold light, dabbing each cut. When she was done she held the left one a moment longer and looked at the gray. At the place, a hand's width below his shoulder, less a finger, where his rolled sleeve showed the line. The edge. Where the gray stopped and the pale skin began.
She did not say anything about it. She let go.
The Ashborn took the left side of the chapel. Kestrel and Arran and Sef and the others. They put their packs against the wall and sat with their backs to them, close together, the way they sat in the shades on the open glass. Arran's litter was laid along the wall under a long green streak, so that he lay in green light like a man at the bottom of a pond, and seemed to like it.
Oren took the door.
Of course he did. Corin did not even need to see him do it. He sat down just inside the square stone frame, on the flagstones, with his back against the wall and his sword across his knees and his face to the white. He sat very straight. His back still hurt him. Corin could tell by the way he sat.
Tansy went everywhere. She could not sit still. She went round the whole chapel touching the walls, the benches, the streaks of colored glass, the cracks in the floor, as if she was learning it by heart. She had never been inside, she said. She had seen it more times than she could count from outside, from far off, a dark square on the glass, but she had never come in. She had been afraid to. She did not say why.
The silent man sat by the step. At the far end. Near the back wall. He had gone there as soon as the rope was cut, without anybody telling him, and folded himself down on the flagstones with his long legs crossed and his long hands on his knees, and sat. Facing the back wall. Looking up at it. At the dark flat space there that the colored light did not reach.
Corin sat with Lyra in the middle. On the floor, against a fused bench, in a patch of gold.
At noon, or what they thought was noon, they ate.
Cold bread. The last of the hard round loaves from Last Thorn, gone so dry that you had to soak each bite in your mouth for a long time before you could chew it. A strip each of the black dried goat. A mouthful of water from the skin, passed round, each person taking exactly one swallow and passing it on, with everybody watching.
Corin chewed his bread and watched the colored light.
Arran played his three notes.
He had his flute out on the litter, the little bone one he wore on a cord round his neck, yellow with age, with holes worn smooth by his fingers, and he lay on his back in the green light and played. The three notes. Low, high, a little lower. Stop. Over and over. Softly at first. Then not so softly. The notes went up into the sagging roof and came back off the curved stone, round and full, so that they seemed to come from everywhere at once.
Low. High. A little lower. Stop.
Low. High. A little lower. Stop.
Corin found that his foot was tapping. In time. And that Hask's tune in his head had gone quiet, to listen.
Low. High. A little lower. Stop.
The others listened too, at first. Tansy stopped going round the walls and sat down to listen. Marrit's lips moved, as if she knew words to it. Even the silent man, by the step, turned his head a little.
Then Arran played it again. And again. And again.
And after a while Corin saw Kestrel's face change. She was sitting against the wall beside the litter with her eyes shut. And every time the three notes came round and stopped, her mouth went a little thinner.
On the ninth time, or something near it, she opened her eyes.
She reached over without a word and took the flute out of her brother's hands.
Arran looked up at her. Hurt. Innocent.
"I was playing that," he said.
"You were," said Kestrel. "And now we are keeping it." She put the flute inside her coat. "Until the storm stops."
"That's not fair."
"No," Kestrel agreed. And shut her eyes again.
Arran lay back on his litter in the green light and sulked. Loudly. For a long time. And Tansy laughed at him. And then Lyra laughed, beside Corin, quietly, into her bread. And Corin found that he was laughing too.
Outside, the storm roared on.
Arran's Foot
When Arran had finished sulking, he called Corin over.
He did it by throwing a crust at him. A small hard end of bread, from the litter, across the chapel. It hit Corin on the shoulder and bounced off onto the flagstones. Corin looked round. Arran was grinning at him from the green light, and crooking one finger.
Corin went. Crutch, swing, step. He let himself down beside the litter, slowly, with his stiff leg out, and sat with his back against the wall under the long green streak, so that the green light lay on both of them.
Vespera had unwrapped Arran's foot. It lay on a folded hide at the end of the litter, bare, propped up on a rolled coat. Corin looked at it, and then made himself keep looking.
The sole was raw. Not skin anymore. A long patch of it, from the heel nearly to the toes, red and wet and shiny, with white ragged edges where the blisters had burst and torn. Vespera had put something on it, a thick yellow paste that smelled of honey, and it glistened in the green light. Round the edges of the raw place the foot was swollen up tight, the skin stretched and shiny, the toes fat.
"Beautiful, isn't it?" said Arran.
"It looks like it hurts."
"Oh, it does." Arran wriggled his toes, very slightly, and winced. "It hurts like nothing has ever hurt. But Vespera says it won't rot. She says the honey will keep the rot out. And when the new skin comes, it'll be tough as a goat's hoof, and I'll be able to walk on the hot glass in bare feet and laugh at everybody." He grinned. "So it's worth it, really."
Corin did not believe that. But he did not say so.
"Why did you call me?" he said.
Arran's grin went a little smaller. He looked up at the sagging roof. At the great blue drip hanging down from where the round window had been.
"You don't remember Last Thorn," he said. "Do you?"
Corin thought about it. He reached for it. Down. And found pieces. A slope of thorns. A fire. The smell of burnt honey. Goats. A flute, playing a silly tune, and children laughing. And then nothing. Smoke. And the Ledger, telling him the rest. THEY CARRY THEIR DEAD TOGETHER SO NOBODY DROWNS.
"Some," he said. "Not much."
Arran nodded. As if he had expected that.
"You made a promise there," he said. "To us. To Kestrel. And to me." He was not grinning at all now. "Before we came out. You said, if one of us goes, out here, on the Glass, you'll take your share. Like the rest of us. You won't stand back and let us carry it without you." He looked at Corin. "Do you remember that?"
Corin did not. He reached for it and there was nothing there. Not even a piece. Just smoke.
But he did not need to remember it. He could see in Arran's face that it was true. And he could feel, somewhere very far down, the shape of it. The way you feel the shape of a key in your pocket without taking it out.
"I don't remember," he said. "But I'll do it."
Arran looked at him for a long moment.
Then the grin came back. Slowly. Not the big foolish one. A smaller one. A real one.
"Good," he said. "I only wanted to know. In case."
"In case what?"
"In case nothing." Arran waved a hand. "In case my beautiful foot falls off and I die of shame. In case I talk my sister to death and she kills me. The Glass is the Glass." He shrugged. "My grandmother always said, you make your promises before you need them. Not after. After is too late."
He lay back on his litter and looked up at the roof again.
"Anyway," he said, "I'd be very good to carry. I'm light. I'm cheerful. I know a great many songs. I'd be no trouble at all." He turned his head and looked at Corin sideways. "You'd hardly notice I was there. Except you'd start laughing at things that aren't funny. And wanting to play the flute badly. And looking at goats with love."
Corin laughed. He could not help it.
"That's not so bad," he said.
"That's what I keep telling Kestrel."
They sat for a while in the green light, saying nothing. Arran's foot glistened on its folded hide. Across the chapel, Tansy was going round the walls again, touching the colored streaks. The storm roared.
"Vane," said Arran.
"What?"
"The old man. Hask." Arran did not look at him. "He's in you. A bit of him. Isn't he? You hum his tune."
Corin nodded.
"What's it like?"
Corin thought about it. He thought about the three notes going round and round in his head on the long night march. About the way they had gone quiet, to listen, when Arran played his flute.
"Like company," he said. "Like somebody sitting in the corner of the room. Not talking. Just there. Doing something with their hands. And you're glad they're there, even when you forget they are."
Arran was quiet for a long time.
"He used to sleep in the goat pen," he said at last. "Did you know that? The old fool. He said the goats were better company than people. He said goats never asked you how you were." He smiled at the roof. "I used to take him his supper out there. When I was small. And he'd tell me stories. With his hands in the goats' wool. About the valleys, before. About the orchards."
"I know," said Corin. He did not know how he knew. "Apples. And pears. You could smell them from the Edge."
Arran turned his head and stared at him.
Then he laughed. A short, startled bark of a laugh, like a fox. And put his arm over his eyes.
"Oh, that's strange," he said, from under his arm. "That's very strange, Vane. That's his voice. You said it just like him."
Corin did not say anything. He sat against the wall in the green light and felt Hask's three notes stir in the back of his head. Pleased. Like an old man in a corner who has heard his name.
"Don't mind me," said Arran, still under his arm. His voice was thick. "It's the foot. It makes me soft."
"I know," said Corin.
And he stayed there, beside the litter, until Arran fell asleep.
Why Tansy Never Came In
Tansy found him there, still sitting by the litter.
She came round the end of it on her way along the wall, touching the streaks of colored glass as she went, one after another, the way a child runs a stick along railings. Green. Green. Gold. And then she saw him, and stopped, and sat down beside him on the flagstones without asking, with her knees up and her uncle's coat spread out round her like a tent.
"He's asleep," she said, looking at Arran.
"Yes."
"He talks too much." She said it with great seriousness. "Even more than me. Kestrel says so."
Corin did not say anything to that. He did not think it was a thing he could win.
Tansy looked round the chapel. At the colored light. At the sagging roof and the great blue drip. At the benches growing out of the walls.
"I never came in here," she said. "Before."
"You said."
"Two winters. I've seen it all the time. From the Ridges, you can see it, on a clear day. A little dark square. And I came close, once. Right up to the door." She pointed at the square stone frame at the far end, where Oren sat. "I stood right there. And I looked in. And I didn't come in."
"Why not?"
Tansy did not answer at once. She picked at a loose thread on the cuff of her coat. Pulled it. Wound it round her finger.
"Because of the wall," she said.
Corin looked down the chapel. At the far end. At the step, and the flat dark space above it, where the colored light did not reach. Where the silent man sat, looking up.
"That wall?"
Tansy nodded.
"I could see it from the door," she said. "Not properly. It was dark. But I could see there were people on it. Standing in a row. Tall ones. And I thought they were real. I thought they were real people, standing at the back in the dark, looking at me. Waiting for me to come in." She wound the thread tighter round her finger. "So I ran away."
"They're paintings," said Corin.
"I know that now." Tansy looked at him sideways. "I'm not stupid. I know now. But I didn't then. I was only little then." She considered. "Littler."
She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in a lower voice:
"And one of them didn't have a face."
Corin went still.
"I saw that," said Tansy. "Even from the door. Even in the dark. They all had faces except one. The one at the end. That one was just smooth. Like an egg." She shivered, inside the big coat. "That's the one I didn't like. That's the one I ran away from. Because I thought, if it hasn't got a face, how does it see? And if it can't see, why is it looking at me?"
Corin did not know what to say.
He looked down the chapel. At the silent man, sitting under the wall. Looking up. Not at the seven. At the end. At the smooth one.
"Nobody likes it," said Tansy. "The smooth one. He's been looking at it all day." She nodded down the chapel at the silent man. "I asked him why. He didn't say."
"He never says."
"No," said Tansy. "But this time he didn't say different." She frowned, trying to find the words. "Usually when he doesn't say, it's like nothing. Like a wall. Like asking a rock. But this time, when I asked about the smooth one, it was like—" She stopped. Thought. "It was like he was trying to remember. And couldn't. And it hurt."
She unwound the thread from her finger. Let it go.
"I don't like it when he hurts," she said simply.
Then she got up, and brushed her coat down, and went back along the wall, touching the streaks. Green. Gold. Gold. All the way to the end. To the step. And sat down beside the silent man, close, under the wall of crowned figures, and leaned against his arm.
And he let her.
Corin sat by Arran's litter and watched the two of them. The small girl and the long thin man. Sitting together in the dark at the far end of the chapel. Under the seven faces and the one that was gone.
Upside Down
In the afternoon, Corin opened his shirt.
He had been wanting to all day. Since the dawn. Since before that, perhaps. Since the night at the iron horse, when he had come up out of the dark and found Lyra's face a hand from his, and his own name in his mouth like a stone he had picked up off the road. He had known, then, that something was written on him. He could feel it. Not the ink. He could not feel the ink. He could feel the skin where it had gone in, tight and tender and hot, in long lines across his chest and down his ribs. Like the feeling of a sunburn. Like a hand pressed flat on him that never lifted.
He had touched it, in the night, on the march. Through his shirt. Running his fingers along the lines without seeing them.
Now, in the quiet of the chapel, in the gold light, he unbuttoned his shirt and pulled it open and looked down.
There were words on him.
Black ones. Big square capitals, in a hand that was not his, marching across his chest in rows. Below his left collarbone. Across the middle. Down the left side, over his ribs. The skin round them was still red and a little puffed, and there were small dark crusts along some of the lines where blood had come up and dried.
He could see them. He could not read them.
They were upside down.
Of course they were. Whoever wrote them had stood in front of him and written them the right way up for somebody looking at him. For somebody standing where they stood. Not for him. Not for a man looking down at his own chest from above. From up here, every letter was on its head. Every word ran backward. The rows ran from right to left, and the letters had their tops at the bottom, and he could see the shapes of them but not what the shapes were.
He bent his head further. Squinted. Tried to turn them round in his mind. The first big line, below his collarbone. The letters were large. He could see a round one. And a straight one with a little tail. And another round one.
He could not do it. His mind would not turn them. It kept trying, and they kept slipping, like trying to read a sign in a mirror, and after a while his head began to ache.
He looked up.
Lyra was watching him. She was sitting beside him against the bench, with her knees up and her arms round them, in the gold light. She had been watching him the whole time. He could see that in her face.
"I can't read them," he said. "They're the wrong way up."
She looked at him a moment longer. Then she shifted round on the flagstones, so that she was in front of him. Kneeling. Facing him. Where whoever had written them had knelt.
"I'll read them," she said.
And she did.
She read them slowly, one at a time. She did not touch them. She held her finger just above each line, close but not touching, and moved it along under the words as she read, the way you show a child where you are on a page. Her voice was quiet and steady. The colored light lay across her face.
"Your name is Corin Vane."
He had known that one. He had known it since the night. But it was different, hearing it. Hearing it read off his own body, in her voice. It went into him differently. Lower down.
"You are twenty-eight."
Twenty-eight. That was a number. A big one. He could see that it was a number. He could hear her say it. But it did not mean anything to him. It was a sound, like a word in a language he did not speak. He let it go past.
"You were born in Oakhaven."
Oakhaven. He knew that word. He did not know why. Something moved, very far down, when she said it. Like a fish turning in deep water. Then it was still again.
"You used to fear the dark."
She paused. Moved her finger down.
"Not anymore."
He looked at her.
"Is that true?"
"I don't know," said Lyra. "You tell me."
He thought about it. He thought about the nights on the Glass. The cold, and the stars above and below, and the heap. The dark under the hides. He had not been afraid in the dark under the hides. He had been afraid of many things, out here. But not that.
"I think it is," he said. "Now."
She nodded. Moved her finger again. And her voice changed. Just a little. Went a little lower, and a little more careful.
"Lyra Thorne. Partner."
He looked down at it. At the place where her finger was. He could not read it. But he knew it was there now. Her name. On him. In black.
"No lies between you," said Lyra.
And stopped.
Neither of them said anything for a while.
Then she moved her finger down. Down the left side. Over his ribs. To a small line, smaller than the others, below his heart. She held her finger over it. Her face did something he could not read either.
"You walk like a heron," she said.
He looked down at the small line. At the place on his ribs.
And something happened in his chest. Not under the ink. Deeper. He did not know what it was. It was not a memory. He did not remember anything. It was more like the feeling just before a memory. Like a smell from a kitchen you have not been in for years, that comes round a corner at you in the street and makes you stop.
He walked like a heron.
Somebody had thought that was important enough to write on him. On his body. Forever. Among his name and his age and the town he was born in.
"Who chose that one?" he said.
Lyra did not look away.
"I did," she said.
