Chapter 9: Madame Hesper's Needle, Part 2 of 5: The Needle and Thimble
The Needle and Thimble
The shop was at the narrow end of Market Row, where the street bent like an elbow and the stalls gave way to proper doors with proper glass.
It was not a grand shop. The front was painted a dark plum color that had gone brown at the edges where the rain had worked on it for many years. Above the door hung a wooden sign in the shape of a thimble, with a long silver needle painted through it, and under the needle were the words THE NEEDLE AND THIMBLE in gold letters. Some of the gold had flaked off. The second E in NEEDLE was mostly gone, so that from a little way off the sign seemed to say THE NEEDL AND THIMBLE, as if whoever painted it had run out of patience at the last moment.
The window was full. That was the first thing Corin noticed. Most shop windows on Market Row showed one or two good things in a clean space, so that you would look at them and want them. This window showed everything at once. There were three dress forms standing close together like women waiting for a carriage, each one wearing half a garment held on with pins. There was a hat with a brown feather. There was a man's waistcoat in yellow silk with no buttons yet, only small chalk crosses where the buttons would go. There was a roll of blue ribbon that had come loose and spilled down over a stack of folded shirts. There was a china cat with one ear chipped off. There was a card, tucked in the corner of the glass, that said in neat black ink: Mending. Turning. Letting out. Taking in. No work refused. No questions asked.
Corin read the last line twice.
"That's her," Lyra said, shaking water off her umbrella. "That card has been in that window since before I could read. My mother used to say it was the most honest sign in the whole city."
"Why?"
"Because everyone in this city has questions they don't want asked," Lyra said. "And Madame Hesper is the only one who ever put it in writing that she wouldn't."
Juno stood a little way back on the wet cobbles, holding her grandfather's brown coat closed at the neck with one hand. The coat came down past her knees. The sleeves had been rolled four times and still swallowed her fingers. Rain dripped from the brim of her cap onto her nose, and every time a drop fell she blew it away with her bottom lip pushed out.
"I'm not coming in," Juno said.
"You're soaked," said Lyra.
"The coat's soaked. I'm fine. The coat's got to go back dry, so the coat ought to come in. But I'm not." She looked at the plum door as if it might bite her. "Shops like that got eyes. Somebody measures you, they know you. Grandpa Terrence always said. Don't let a tailor or a doctor put their hands on you unless you're dying or getting married."
"I am not getting married," said Corin.
"Then you better be dying," said Juno, "because you look like it."
Lyra laughed, a short, surprised sound, and then pressed her lips together as if she were cross with herself for laughing. "Come in, Juno. She won't measure you. She won't even look at you if you don't want her to. And she has a stove."
Juno thought about the stove for a while. Corin watched her think. She had a way of tilting her head, as if she were listening to a far-off bell and counting the strokes. At last she sniffed, wiped her nose on Grandpa Terrence's sleeve, remembered that the coat had to go back clean, looked at the sleeve in horror, and marched toward the door.
"Only for the coat," she said.
A bell rang when Lyra pushed the door open. It was not a little shop bell. It was a deep, heavy bell, like something taken off a church or a ship, hung on a curl of iron above the frame. It made one long bonnng that went on and on and seemed to shake the floorboards. Corin felt it in his teeth.
Then the warmth hit him.
It was a damp, thick warmth, the warmth of a laundry or a kitchen. A big black iron stove stood against the left-hand wall, with a pipe going up into the ceiling and a kettle on top that whispered to itself. On a rack beside the stove, three heavy irons sat heating, their handles wrapped in rags. Steam rose in slow curls from a length of gray wool that hung over a wooden horse near the window, drying. The steam smelled of wet sheep and soap. Under that there was another smell, sweeter and sharper. Corin breathed it in and tried to name it.
"Lavender," said Lyra, seeing his face. "She puts it in the drawers so the moths won't come. Everything she makes smells of it. When I was small I thought that was what grown-up ladies smelled like. Lavender and starch."
The shop was long and thin, deeper than it was wide, like a hallway that had decided to become a room. On the right-hand wall, from the floor to the ceiling, there were shelves, and on the shelves were bolts of cloth lying on their sides with their ends showing. Corin had never seen so many colors in one place. There were blacks that were nearly blue and blacks that were nearly brown. There were greens of every kind: the green of moss, the green of old bottles, the green of new leaves with the sun behind them. There were reds that made him think of fire and reds that made him think of meat. There was a whole shelf of whites, and none of the whites were the same. Some were cold, like snow. Some were warm, like milk. One was gray-white, like the Ash that lay in the bottom of his Ledger pocket after a hard harvest.
He looked away from that one.
In the middle of the room stood a long table, scarred and scratched and polished by many years of elbows. On the table lay a pair of shears as long as Corin's forearm, a pincushion shaped like a tomato and bristling with pins, three spools of thread, a lump of yellow chalk, a measuring tape coiled like a sleeping snake, and a cup of tea that had gone cold and grown a skin.
At the back of the shop was a curtain made of dark red velvet, faded pink along its folds. Behind it, somebody was moving. Corin heard the creak of a floorboard, the rattle of a drawer, and a voice.
"If that is a customer, the shop is closed," said the voice. "If that is the rain, it may come in and sit by the stove. If that is Mrs. Pellow about her son's trousers, they are not ready, they will not be ready until Tuesday, and if he grows another inch before then I shall send him to a carpenter."
The voice was dry and thin and sharp, like a pair of good scissors. It was the voice of a woman who had been giving orders for a long time and did not expect to stop.
"It's me, Madame," Lyra called. "It's Lyra."
The curtain went still.
Then it was pushed aside, and Madame Hesper came out.
She was tall. That was the first thing. She was taller than Lyra and nearly as tall as Corin, but she was so thin that she seemed even taller, like a heron standing in a reed bed. She wore a black dress buttoned up to her chin, very plain, with no lace and no trimming, but it fit her so perfectly that it looked like it had grown on her. Over the dress was a long apron of brown linen with deep pockets across the front, and the pockets bulged with things: a second tape measure, a packet of needles, a little pair of scissors shaped like a stork, a stub of pencil, a folded paper of pins.
Her face was long and narrow, with high cheekbones and a long, straight nose. Her skin was pale and fine and covered with small lines, like paper that had been folded and unfolded many times. Her hair was iron gray, pulled back very tight and twisted into a knot at the back of her head, and there were two long pins stuck through the knot in a cross. She might have been sixty-five. She might have been seventy. She had the kind of face that would look the same at eighty as it had at fifty.
On a thin silver chain around her neck hung a pair of round spectacles. She did not wear them. She held them in one hand and tapped them against her other palm as she looked at the three wet people dripping on her floor.
She had pins in her mouth. Four of them, held in the corner of her lips, sticking out like the whiskers of a cat. She spoke around them without seeming to notice they were there.
"Lyra Thorne," said Madame Hesper. "You are wet through. You are not wearing the blue wool I turned for you in the spring. You are wearing your second-best brown, which has a tear in the left cuff that you have mended yourself, very badly, with the wrong color thread."
Lyra put her left hand behind her back.
"And you have brought a child and a man," Madame Hesper went on. "The child is wearing a coat that belongs to a man who has not worn it in eight years. The man is wearing a coat that belongs to the government." She took the pins out of her mouth one at a time and pushed them into the tomato pincushion. "Which of them am I to shout at first?"
"Neither, please," said Lyra. "Madame, this is Mr. Vane. And this is Juno."
"I know who Juno is," said Madame Hesper. "She steals apples from Mr. Grout's cart on the corner every Thursday and he pretends not to see because his wife tells him to. Good afternoon, Juno. Sit by the stove. Take off that coat and hang it on the hook by the pipe or it will smell of wet dog until the end of time."
Juno stared at her. Her mouth opened. Nothing came out.
"Terrence's coat," Madame Hesper said, a little more quietly. "I put the lining in that coat. Twenty-two years ago. Brown sateen. He wanted red and I told him red was for soldiers and fools. He said he had been both. I gave him brown anyway." She looked at Juno, and something in her long, stern face moved, just slightly, like a curtain when a door opens in another room. "He is a good man, your grandfather. Whatever they did to him, he paid on time, every time, and that does not wash out. Tell Marta I asked after him. Hang it up."
Juno hung it up. She did it very carefully, smoothing the shoulders and turning the collar so it would dry flat. Then she sat on the little stool by the stove, as close to the heat as she could get without catching fire, and put her hands out to it, and did not say one word.
Corin had never seen Juno lost for words before. He wrote it in his head, to put in the Ledger later. Juno met someone she could not talk back to.
Then Madame Hesper turned to him.
She put on her spectacles. She did it slowly, hooking them over one ear and then the other, settling them on the bridge of her long nose. Behind the glass her eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and very, very sharp. She did not look at his face. She looked at the rest of him. She looked at his boots, which were black and old and had been resoled twice. She looked at the hem of his trousers, which was wet and spattered with mud. She looked at his gray coat, the Ministry coat, with its high collar and its row of plain dark buttons, and she looked at it the way a doctor looks at a wound.
"Ministry of Records," she said.
"Yes, Madame."
"Third Basement. Harvesting."
Corin blinked. "How do you know that?"
"The buttons," said Madame Hesper. "The Ministry orders its coats from Gallow and Sons on Tanner Street. They have done so for thirty years. The upper floors get horn buttons. The clerks get brass. The Third Basement gets iron, painted black, because the Third Basement works in places where brass would be stolen and horn would be broken." She reached out with one long finger and tapped the third button down, very lightly. It made a small dull click. "Iron. Painted. The paint is chipped on this one, and under the chip the iron has rusted, which means you have been out in weather like this many times and nobody has ever looked after this coat. Gallow and Sons do not look after anything once it has left the shop. I would not let them sew a button on a sack."
She took the spectacles off again and let them fall on their chain.
"So," she said. "Lyra Thorne brings a Harvester into my shop on a wet Saturday. That is either very good news or very bad news, and I am too old to wait and see which. Tell me what you want, child, and tell me quickly, before the irons get too hot and scorch my wool."
Lyra took a breath. Corin watched her gather herself the way he had seen her do it at the stable, and again on the Hill steps: shoulders back, chin up, hands folded in front of her. Her mother's way, he guessed, though he did not know. It was a lady's way of standing. It made her look older than twenty, and it made her look tired.
"I need a coat for him," Lyra said. "Not a good coat. A servant's coat. A footman's coat. Something that will let him stand behind me in a drawing room in the High Quarter, and walk two steps behind me up Crown Walk, and nobody will look at him twice."
Madame Hesper said nothing for a long moment. The kettle on the stove whispered. The steam rose from the gray wool by the window. Somewhere outside, a cart went past, its wheels hissing in the wet.
"Crown Walk," Madame Hesper said at last.
"Yes."
"You are going back to the house."
"To the street," said Lyra. "To the house, if I can. And to my aunt's."
Madame Hesper's face did not change. But her hand, the one holding the spectacles, closed very tightly around them, so tightly that her knuckles went white as chalk.
"Sit down," she said. "Both of you. I will put the kettle on properly. This is not a conversation for standing up."
She turned and went to the stove, and Corin saw that the hand which had closed on the spectacles was shaking, just a little, as she lifted the kettle. She held it steady with the other hand. She did not spill a drop.
Corin sat down at the long scarred table, on a stool with three legs that rocked when he moved. Lyra sat across from him. Between them lay the great shears, open like a mouth.
Remember this, he told himself. The sign that says no questions. The bell like a church. The lavender. The way her hand shook when Lyra said Crown Walk.
He did not know yet why it mattered. But he had learned, in the years he could not remember and the weeks he could, that the things which made a person's hand shake were almost always the things that mattered most.
What a Body Tells a Tailor
The tea came in cups that did not match. Lyra's was white with a gold rim, very fine, so thin that Corin could see the shadow of the tea through the side of it. His own was thick brown clay with a chip in the handle. Juno's, by the stove, was a jam jar.
"I have one good cup," said Madame Hesper, "and it is for the only person in this room who has ever paid me in coin." She put it in front of Lyra. "Your mother bought me that cup. Do not drop it."
Lyra held it in both hands as if it were a bird.
Madame Hesper did not sit. She stood at the end of the long table with her own cup, which was plain white and had no handle at all, and she drank from it in small, quick sips, the way a bird drinks. She looked at Corin over the top of it.
"Stand up," she said.
Corin stood up. The three-legged stool rocked and nearly fell, and he caught it.
"Take off the coat."
He hesitated.
It was a small thing. A moment. But Madame Hesper saw it, and Corin saw her see it. Her pale eyes went to his chest, to the left side, where the Ledger sat in the inside pocket over his heart, and then down and to the right, where the long pocket held the rod, and then, last of all, to a place just above his right hip, inside the coat, where the black cloth square was sewn and the three stones lay together, small and hard and warm.
She could not see any of those things. The coat was buttoned. But she looked at each place as if she could.
"You do not take off your coat in front of people," she said. It was not a question.
"Not often."
"Because of what is in it."
"Yes."
Madame Hesper put down her cup. "Mr. Vane," she said, "I have been making clothes for fifty-one years. I started when I was fourteen, the same age as that child by my stove, sweeping pins off the floor of a shop on Silk Street for a woman who hit my knuckles with a ruler every time I dropped one. I have made wedding dresses and burial clothes. I have made uniforms for men who went to the war and I have let out the same uniforms for the same men when they came home fat and sad. I have sewn gold into the hems of ladies who were running away from their husbands, and I have sewn letters into the linings of gentlemen who were running toward things they should not have been. I have never once told anybody what was inside a coat that I had my hands on." She held out one long, thin hand, palm up. "Take off the coat. Put it on the table. I will not touch the pockets. I give you my word, and my word is the only thing I own that is not for sale."
Corin looked at Lyra.
Lyra nodded, very slightly.
He unbuttoned the gray coat. The iron buttons were stiff and cold under his fingers. He slid it off his shoulders and folded it in half, carefully, so the inside pockets were against each other and nothing could fall out, and he laid it on the long table next to the open shears. It looked smaller lying there than it had felt on him. It looked like an old gray animal that had lain down to sleep.
Under the coat he wore a white shirt that was not very white anymore, and a dark waistcoat with two buttons missing, and a pair of brown braces holding up his trousers. He felt suddenly cold, even with the stove going. He felt as if he had taken off a layer of skin.
Madame Hesper did not touch the coat. She did not even look at it. She looked at him.
She walked around him once, slowly, the way a farmer walks around a horse he is thinking of buying. Her black skirt whispered on the floorboards. She did not speak. She stopped behind him, and he felt her looking at the back of his neck, and his shoulders, and the line of his spine. Then she came round to the front again, and took the measuring tape out of her apron pocket, and let it uncoil from her hand like a long yellow ribbon.
"Arms out," she said. "To the side. Like a scarecrow."
Corin put his arms out.
She measured him. She was fast. The tape went around his chest, and around his waist, and from the back of his neck down to his wrist, and from his shoulder to his shoulder, and she did not write anything down. She just said the numbers out loud, under her breath, in a low steady voice, the way Juno counted roof tiles. "Thirty-eight. Thirty-one. Twenty-four and a half. Seventeen." Her fingers were cool and dry and very light. They touched him only where they had to and no more.
And as she measured, she talked. Not to him, exactly. More to the tape. More to the air.
"You stand on your left foot," she said. "Most men stand on their right. You put your weight on your left because your right hand wants to be free. Always. Even now, with your arms out, your right hand is a little higher than your left, and the fingers are a little bent, as if they are ready to close on something."
Corin looked at his right hand. She was right. He made the fingers straight.
"No," said Madame Hesper. "Don't do that. Don't change it. I am not telling you to fix it. I am telling you what I see, so that when I make your coat, I make it for the man you are and not the man you are pretending to be." She ran the tape from his armpit to his hip on the right side. "There is a long pocket on this side of your coat. Inside. It goes from here," she touched his ribs, "to here," she touched just above his hip, "and whatever is in it is heavy at the top and light at the bottom, because your coat has stretched in a wedge shape and pulled the seam. A stick, or a rod. Something you carry with the thick end up, so you can pull it out fast."
Corin said nothing. In the coat on the table, in the long right pocket, the rod lay quiet. He thought he felt it listening.
She is very good, said a voice in his head, dry and old and amused. Malakor. It was the first time the rod had spoken all day. I knew a man in Marrowmere who could tell you what a person had for breakfast from the creases in his gloves. He was not as good as this woman.
Be quiet, Corin thought.
I am always quiet. It is you who are loud. Your heart is going like a drum.
Madame Hesper moved the tape to his left side. She measured from his collarbone down to the bottom of his ribs, and as she did it she paused, just for a breath, over the place where the Ledger usually lay.
"Here," she said, "you have worn a weight against your heart for a long time. A book. Not a big book, but thick, and square at the corners, and you have carried it every day for years. Look." She lifted the edge of his waistcoat a little, without touching the shirt beneath, and pointed. "The waistcoat is worn thin in a square. Exactly a square. The size of a book you could hold in one hand. A man does not carry a book against his heart every day for years unless he cannot live without it."
"I can't," said Corin. The words came out before he had decided to say them.
Madame Hesper looked up at him. Her eyes were very close now, very pale behind the hanging spectacles.
"No," she said quietly. "I don't suppose you can."
She went on measuring. Down his arm. Around his wrist. Around the top of his leg, quickly, without any fuss. She did it the way a nurse would do it, as if a body were only a body and nothing to blush about.
Then she stopped. Her hand, holding the tape, hovered over a place on his right side, low, just above the hip, on the inside of where the coat would be. She did not touch it. There was nothing there now; the black cloth square was sewn into the coat, and the coat was on the table. But she looked at the place on his body where it would have rested, and she frowned.
"Here," she said. "Something small. Something you keep very close. The shirt is a different color here, Mr. Vane. Do you know that? It is a little brown, like a scorch. As if something has been warm against you, not hot enough to burn, but warm, for many days."
Corin looked down. He had never noticed. But she was right. On his shirt, just above the right hip, there was a faint brown patch the size of a coin, like the mark an iron leaves when you hold it too long in one place.
The stones. The three stones in the black square, marked U.W. They had been warm for days now. Warmer since the house on the Hill. Warmer since Lord Thorne had tapped his four numbers on the wall.
"Things that are warm when they should be cold," said Madame Hesper, "are things to be careful of. My mother used to say that about bread and men. I say it about pockets." She coiled the tape back into her hand with a quick, neat twist. "I am finished. Put your arms down before they fall off."
Corin put his arms down. They ached. He had not known how long he had been holding them out.
By the stove, Juno was staring at Madame Hesper with her mouth a little open, as if she had just watched a street magician pull a rabbit out of a drain.
"How'd you do that?" Juno said. "How'd you know all that? About the rod and the book and the warm thing? You never even touched his pockets."
Madame Hesper turned her pale eyes on her.
"I did not know anything," she said. "I read it. A body is a book, child. Everything a person carries, everything they are afraid of, everything they love, it all pulls on the cloth. It stretches the seams. It wears the elbows thin. It makes one shoulder lower than the other. You only have to learn the letters." She put the tape back in her apron. "Your grandfather carried his keys on a great iron ring on his left hip for forty years, every night, and when he came to me for that coat his left shoulder was a quarter of an inch lower than his right, from the weight of them. I cut the coat to fit the shoulder. Now you wear his last key on a string, on the same side." She looked at Juno's chest, where under her shirt the brass key stamped NIGHT DOOR. III hung on its string. "And your left shoulder, child, is already starting to drop."
Juno put her hand flat over the key and held it there.
Nobody said anything for a while.
Then Lyra said, very gently, "She did my mother's clothes, Juno. All of them. For twenty years. My mother used to say Madame Hesper knew her better than her own mirror did."
"Your mother," said Madame Hesper, "was a very bad liar and a very good customer. She paid on the first of every month and she never once asked me to make her look thinner than she was." She picked up the cold cup of tea with the skin on it, looked at it, and set it down again. "Sit, Mr. Vane. Put your coat back on, if it makes you feel safer. You look like a peeled egg."
Corin put the coat back on. He did it quickly. As soon as the weight of it settled on his shoulders, as soon as he felt the Ledger against his heart and the rod along his ribs and the small warm square at his hip, he felt himself again. Or as close to himself as he ever got.
He sat down on the rocking stool.
"You've measured me," he said. "But you haven't said if you'll make the coat."
Madame Hesper looked at him for a long time.
"I am deciding," she said, "what kind of servant you are going to be."
"Is there more than one kind?"
"There are a hundred kinds," she said. "There is the footman who stands at the door and opens it. There is the footman who carries the trays. There is the man who walks behind the lady's chair in the street. There is the man who holds the horses. There is the valet who knows his master's secrets and the coachman who knows where his master goes at night. They all wear different coats, and the people of the High Quarter can tell every one of them apart at fifty paces, the way you can tell a sparrow from a pigeon." She tapped her spectacles against her palm. "If I put you in the wrong coat, they will know. Not because you look like a Harvester. Because you look like the wrong kind of servant. And a wrong servant is the one thing a High Quarter lady will always notice, because she has spent her whole life training her eyes to see nothing else."
"Then what kind should I be?" said Corin.
Madame Hesper did not answer at once. She looked at Lyra. Something passed between them, something old and quiet, the kind of look two people give each other when they have both been at the same funeral.
"There is a trunk," said Madame Hesper slowly, "upstairs. In my back room. I have not opened it in two years."
Lyra's cup rattled on its saucer. She put it down.
"Madame," she said. "You kept them?"
"I was paid for them," said Madame Hesper. "I was never paid to throw them away."
She turned and walked to the red velvet curtain at the back of the shop. She pulled it aside. Behind it was a narrow staircase, very steep, almost a ladder, going up into darkness.
"Come," she said to Corin, without turning round. "You are going to carry it down. I am sixty-five years old and I do not lift trunks for Harvesters."
