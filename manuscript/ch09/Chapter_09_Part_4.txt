Chapter 9: Madame Hesper's Needle, Part 4 of 5: Two Steps Behind
Two Steps Behind
"Now," said Madame Hesper, "you will learn how to stand."
Corin was wearing the green coat. Or half of it. She had put it on him inside out first, to pin the waist, and then the right way round, to pin the sleeves, and now it hung on him with forty or fifty pins sticking out of it in every direction, so that he felt like a hedgehog that had been dressed for a funeral. Every time he moved his arms, something pricked him. The collar was stiff and high and pushed his chin up. The tails flapped against the back of his knees. The brass buttons were cold where they touched his shirt through the open front.
He did not have his own coat on. His own coat lay folded on the table beside the lamp, with Juno sitting next to it, one hand resting on it, guarding it. He had asked her to. She had looked at him strangely, and then nodded, and put her hand on it as if it were a sleeping dog that might wake and run off.
"I know how to stand," Corin said.
"You know how to stand like a Harvester," said Madame Hesper. "Feet apart. Weight on the left. Right hand ready. Shoulders forward, like a man walking into the wind. You stand as if you are about to take something from somebody." She walked around him, tugging at the coat here and there. "A footman does not stand like that. A footman stands as if he has already given everything away."
"How does he stand, then?"
"Lyra," said Madame Hesper. "Show him."
Lyra got up from her stool. She looked uncertain. "I was never a footman, Madame."
"No. But you grew up with six of them. You watched them every day of your life until you were eighteen. Show him how Abel stood."
Lyra looked at the floor for a moment. Then she went and stood by the wall near the dress forms, and she changed.
Corin did not see exactly how she did it. It was not one thing. It was a lot of small things all at once. She put her feet together, heels touching, toes a little apart. She put her hands behind her back, the right hand holding the left wrist. She straightened her back, but not proudly. More the way a broom stands when you lean it against a wall: upright, but only because the wall is holding it there. She lifted her chin a little. And her eyes went flat. That was the strangest part. Her eyes went to a spot on the far wall, just above the door, and stayed there, and they stopped seeing anything. They were open, but they were empty, like windows in a house where nobody is home.
She stood like that, perfectly still.
Juno whistled softly. "She's gone," Juno said. "She's like a lamp post."
"That," said Madame Hesper, "is a footman."
Lyra let her hands fall and turned back into herself. She looked embarrassed. "Abel could stand like that for three hours," she said. "At my mother's dinners. Three hours by the sideboard, and he never moved, and you forgot he was there. And then my father would lift one finger, just one, like this," she lifted her finger an inch off the edge of her teacup, "and Abel would be beside him with the wine before he had even put the finger down."
"So he was watching," said Corin.
"He was always watching," said Lyra. "That's the trick of it. The eyes go flat, but the ears don't. And the eyes aren't really flat either. They're only pretending."
Corin thought about that.
Then he went and stood by the wall where she had stood. He put his heels together. He put his hands behind his back, the right hand holding the left wrist, and the pins in the sleeves stabbed him, and he did not move. He straightened his back. He lifted his chin. And he found a spot on the far wall, above the door, where a brown water stain looked a little like a fish, and he looked at it, and he tried to make his eyes go empty.
It was harder than it looked. His eyes did not want to go empty. They wanted to look at everything. They wanted to look at Madame Hesper's hands, and at Lyra's face, and at the steam rising from the gray wool by the window, and at the door, and at the window, and at the street outside, because his eyes were a Harvester's eyes, and a Harvester's eyes were always looking for the thing that was wrong. They had been trained that way for so long that he could not remember a time when they had not.
You are trying too hard, said Malakor. The rod was in the gray coat, on the table, under Juno's hand. But its voice came to him clear as ever, from across the room. You are trying to see nothing. That is not how it works. You must see everything, and let none of it show.
How?
Pretend you are in the Third Basement. Pretend you are at the Ash tables, sorting. You look at a hundred memories a day. You do not let any of them touch you. You see them, and you put them in a jar, and you close the lid, and your face does not move. You have done this every day for years. You simply do not remember doing it.
Corin thought about the Ash tables. He did not remember them, not really. But his body did. Something in his shoulders loosened. Something in his face went still. He looked at the brown fish on the wall above the door, and he let the rest of the room come to him from the edges of his eyes, without turning his head, the way you hear rain without listening for it.
"Better," said Madame Hesper. "Much better." She sounded surprised. "Now say Yes, my lady."
"Yes, my lady."
"No. You said it like a question. As if you were asking her if she was a lady. Say it as if it does not matter what she has asked you to do. As if she has asked you to carry her parcel, or jump in the river, and both are the same to you."
"Yes, my lady."
"Too low. You sound like a gravedigger. Lighter. Faster. It is not a sentence. It is a door closing quietly."
"Yes, my lady."
Madame Hesper tilted her head. "Again."
"Yes, my lady."
"Again."
"Yes, my lady."
By the table, Juno had started to giggle. She had both hands over her mouth and her shoulders were shaking. "He sounds like a parrot," she said through her fingers. "A sad parrot. A parrot what's been left out in the rain."
"Then you do better," said Madame Hesper, without turning around.
Juno stopped giggling. She stood up from the table, still keeping one hand on Corin's gray coat, and she put her heels together and her free hand behind her back, and she lifted her small sharp chin, and she said, "Yes, my lady," in a voice so sweet and so empty and so perfectly bored that Lyra laughed out loud.
"Where did you learn that?" said Lyra.
"The Pike boys' mam does a lady's washing up on the Hill," said Juno. "She does the voice when she comes home. She does it at the dinner table to make us laugh. Yes, my lady. No, my lady. Shall I fetch your other gloves, my lady, the ones you threw at me this morning?" She sat back down and put both hands on Corin's coat again. "Easy."
Corin tried again. He tried to sound like Juno doing the Pike boys' mam doing a High Quarter servant. "Yes, my lady."
"There," said Madame Hesper. "That will do. Now walk."
This was the hardest part. Lyra went to the door of the shop, and turned, and walked slowly up the long thin room toward the curtain at the back, the way a lady walks: not fast, not slow, with her head up and her hands folded loosely in front of her. Corin was to follow her. Two steps behind. Not one step, because one step was too close, and people would think he was her brother or her husband. Not three steps, because three was too far, and people would think he was not with her at all. Two steps, exactly, and a little to the left, so that her right hand was free to take things from people and his right hand was free to carry things for her.
He got it wrong. He got it wrong over and over. He walked too fast and nearly stepped on her heel. He walked too slowly and fell behind. He walked straight behind her instead of to the left. He walked like a Harvester, with his shoulders forward and his eyes going everywhere. Every time, Madame Hesper said "No," in her sharp dry voice, and every time, Lyra turned round at the curtain and walked back to the door and started again.
"Think of it as a dance," said Lyra, the sixth time. "You do not lead. You follow. You do not watch my feet. You watch my shoulders. When my shoulders move, you move."
"I don't know how to dance," said Corin.
"How do you know you don't?"
He opened his mouth to answer and found he had nothing to say. He did not know if he could dance. He did not know if he had ever danced. Perhaps once, long ago, before the Ledger, before the Ministry, he had danced at a wedding in Oakhaven, or at a fair, or in a kitchen with his younger sister standing on his feet. He did not know. There was no way to know. The page for that was gone.
"I don't know," he said.
Lyra looked at him for a moment. Then she turned and started walking again, toward the curtain, slower this time.
And this time he watched her shoulders, and only her shoulders. When her left shoulder dipped a little as she stepped, he stepped. When her right shoulder dipped, he stepped again. He kept two steps behind and a little to the left. He let his eyes go to a spot just above her head, not quite on her and not quite away from her. He kept his hands behind his back, the right holding the left wrist, and the pins stabbed him, and he did not care.
They reached the curtain. Lyra stopped. He stopped.
They turned. They walked back.
They reached the door. They stopped.
Madame Hesper said nothing at all.
"Was that right?" said Corin.
"A footman does not ask if he was right," said Madame Hesper. "A footman waits to be told." But the corner of her thin mouth had moved, just slightly, upward. "It was right."
They did it ten more times. Then twenty. The light outside the window went from gray to a darker gray, and the rain kept falling, and the steam rose from the wool, and the irons cooled on their rack and Madame Hesper put them back on the stove to heat again. Juno fell half asleep by the table with her head on her arms and one hand still on Corin's gray coat.
At last Madame Hesper said, "Enough. Now we test it."
"Test it how?"
"Outside," she said. "On Market Row. In the street. Where people are." She took an old umbrella from a stand by the door. It was black and very large, with a carved wooden handle in the shape of a duck's head. "Lyra will walk to the corner and back. You will walk behind her. You will carry this over her head. If anyone in the street looks at your face, the test is failed and we start again."
"In this coat? With the pins?"
"Put your own coat over it," said Madame Hesper. "No. Wait." She frowned. "That is a better test. Wear your own coat. Your gray Ministry coat. And walk behind her the way a footman walks."
"Why?"
"Because I want to see something," said Madame Hesper.
So Corin took off the green coat, very carefully, with Madame Hesper pulling out the worst of the pins first, and he put on his own gray coat again. He felt the weight of it settle. The Ledger over his heart. The rod along his ribs. The warm square at his hip. He took the duck-handled umbrella. He followed Lyra out into the rain.
Market Row was busy, even in the wet. The stalls had their canvas roofs pulled down low, and water ran off the edges in silver strings. People hurried between them with their collars turned up and their heads down. There was a woman selling hot chestnuts from a brazier, and a man selling umbrellas from a cart, and a boy selling newspapers who was shouting something about the price of Ash on the Lantern Quay exchange. A cart full of cabbages had lost a wheel and lay tipped in the gutter, and two men were arguing over it while the cabbages rolled slowly away down the hill.
Lyra walked through all of this as if it were a garden. Head up. Hands folded. Not fast, not slow.
Corin walked two steps behind her and a little to the left, holding the great black umbrella over her head, so that the rain ran off its edge and fell on him instead of on her. He kept his eyes on the spot just above her hat. He kept his face still.
And nobody looked at him.
Not one person. The chestnut woman looked at Lyra and called out, "Hot chestnuts, miss, warm your hands!" The umbrella man looked at Lyra's umbrella and shook his head sadly, because she already had one. The newspaper boy looked at Lyra and held up a paper and shouted. A constable in a black cape, standing under a shop awning, looked at Lyra, touched his hat to her, and looked away. The two men arguing over the cabbages both stopped arguing for a moment to watch Lyra walk past, and then went back to arguing.
None of them looked at the man in the gray coat holding the umbrella. It was as if he were part of the umbrella. As if the umbrella had grown a pair of legs to carry itself.
They reached the corner. They turned. They walked back.
Outside the plum-colored door of the Needle and Thimble, Madame Hesper was standing in the doorway with her arms folded. Juno was beside her, awake now, peering round her skirts.
"Well?" said Lyra, when they reached the step.
"Nobody looked at him," said Juno. "Not one. Not even the copper."
"No," said Madame Hesper. "I did not think they would." She stood aside to let them in, and as Corin passed her she said, quietly, so that only he could hear: "You see, Mr. Vane? It is not the green coat that makes you invisible. You are invisible already. Nobody in this city looks at a gray coat. They have been taught not to. They are afraid that if they look, the gray coat might look back." She shut the door behind him. The heavy bell went bonnng. "The green coat is only to tell the High Quarter which kind of invisible you are."
Corin stood dripping in the warm, lavender-smelling shop and thought about that for a long time.
He thought about all the people on Market Row who had looked at Lyra and not at him. He thought about the Ministry, and the gray coats, and the way doors closed quietly when he came down a corridor and people stopped talking when he came into a room. He thought about how easy it would be, for someone who knew this, to walk into any house in the city in a gray coat and walk out again with anything at all, and nobody would remember his face.
He thought: That is how they did it. Whoever came for Lord Thorne in the carriage. They did not need to hide. They only needed to wear the right coat.
And then he thought, more slowly, and with a cold feeling in his stomach: And that is how I have done it too. For years. Every day. In every house I ever walked into.
He did not write that one in the Ledger. He did not need to. He knew, somehow, that it was the kind of thing he would find out again tomorrow, whether he wrote it down or not.
The Card from Crown Walk
When they were inside again, and the umbrella was dripping in its stand, and Madame Hesper had gone back to pinning the green coat on the table under the lamp, Lyra sat down on her stool and took something out of the pocket of her second-best brown.
She held it in her lap for a while without saying anything. Corin saw her thumb moving on it, back and forth, the way people rub a coin for luck.
"I have to show you this," she said at last. "I was going to show you on the way here. And then I couldn't. And then we were here, and I still couldn't." She held it out across the table to him. "It came this morning. Early. Before I sent Juno to you with my note."
It was a card.
Corin took it by the edges, the way he had been trained to take anything that might matter, so that his fingers would not leave marks or take any away. It was about the size of his palm, thick and stiff, and the color of cream. Not white. Cream. The color of the top of the milk. The edges had been cut with a little wave in them, like the edge of a pie crust, and they were painted with a thin line of silver.
He turned it to the lamp and read it.
At the top, in printed letters, small and fine:
Councillor Cressida Thorne At Home
And under that, in handwriting:
Monday evening, from the eighth bell. Number 11, Crown Walk.
And under that, smaller, in the same hand:
My dearest Lyra. How very long it has been. Of course you must come. I have thought of you so often and never known how to begin. Bring whomever you need; there is always room. I am so glad you wrote.
And at the bottom, one letter. A single C.
The ink was violet.
And the C was not like other C's. It was round and full, rounder than most, curving round to the left and down and up again like an ordinary C. But at the top, where the pen had first touched the card, it did not begin plainly. It began with a little curl, turned sharply down into a point, like a hook. Like a fishhook. Like the hook a fisherman ties on the end of his line, with a tiny barb at the tip so that whatever is caught on it cannot slip off.
Corin had seen that C before. He did not remember seeing it. But he knew where it was written down.
He set the card very carefully on the table, face up, in the circle of lamplight. Then he took the Ledger out of his inside pocket, and opened it from the back. Inside the back cover, where he kept the loose things, there was a folded letter from Lyra, and a gray slip with a red stamp, and a cream envelope that smelled faintly of flowers. Mrs. Hobb's envelope. The one he had taken from Lord Thorne's sickroom, the one that had come with the lily soap, from a friend.
He laid the envelope next to the card.
The same cream. The same thick, soft paper. On the back of the envelope there was a seal of white wax, broken now, but still showing a shape pressed into it: a small flower with long, curling petals. A lily.
Then he turned through the Ledger to a page from earlier in the week, a page in his own handwriting, and found what he was looking for. He had copied it from the Benevolent Fund book, in the Ministry, line for line, the way he copied everything. Private arrangement made. C. And under it, he had drawn the C, as carefully as he could. A round curve, with a hook on top.
He put the open Ledger beside the card and the envelope.
Three things in a row on the scarred table. A card. An envelope. A page.
Nobody said anything.
Juno got up from her stool by the stove, came over, and looked. She looked for a long time, with her lips moving a little, the way she did when she was reading something hard.
"That's the same," she said. "The C. On the card and in your book. That's the same."
"It looks the same," said Corin.
"It is the same. Look at the hook. Look at the little point. You couldn't do that by accident."
"A lot of people make their C's with a curl," said Corin. He said it slowly, not because he believed it, but because somebody had to say it. "Clerks. Teachers. It's taught in some schools. I have seen hundreds of C's."
You do not remember seeing hundreds of C's, said Malakor dryly.
It's still true.
It is still true, the rod agreed. But that is not a schoolroom C. That is a C that somebody practiced, many times, alone, until it was hers.
Lyra had not looked at the three things on the table. She was looking at Corin.
"I know," she said quietly. "I saw it as soon as I opened the card. I sat on my bed above the stable with it in my hand and I thought, that is the C from the Benevolent Fund. That is the C who made a private arrangement. I thought about burning it. I thought about not coming today." She folded her hands in her lap, very tightly. "And then I thought, she is my father's sister. She is the only family I have left in this city except Rowan, and I don't even know where Rowan is. And I thought, maybe there is a reason. Maybe it is not what it looks like."
"Maybe it isn't," said Corin.
"Do you believe that?"
He did not answer. He looked at the violet ink.
Behind them, the pins had stopped going into the green coat.
Corin turned. Madame Hesper was standing very still at the table, with a pin halfway to her mouth, and her pale eyes were fixed on the cream card in the lamplight. She had put her spectacles on. He had not seen her do it.
"Lilies," said Madame Hesper.
"Madame?"
"That card. It smells of lilies. I can smell it from here." She put the pin down. She did not stick it in the tomato. She just laid it on the table, as if she had forgotten what pins were for. "She always wore lilies. Even as a girl. Fourteen years old, she came into this shop with her mother, old Lady Agatha, to be measured for her first long dress, and she was already wearing lilies. Lily water on her wrists and behind her ears. Too much of it. It filled the whole shop. It got into my cloth and I could not get it out for a month." Her thin mouth tightened. "I made every dress that girl wore until she was twenty-two. Then she stopped coming."
"Why?" said Lyra.
Madame Hesper was quiet for a moment. Then she took off her spectacles and let them fall on their chain.
"Because of the quarrel," she said. "Because I was there."
Lyra's hands came apart in her lap.
"You were there?" she said. "Nobody ever told me what it was about. Nobody. Not my mother, not my father, not Bertram. I asked and asked. They all said it was before I was born, and it was old, and it did not matter."
"It was before you were born," said Madame Hesper. "By about four months." She looked at Lyra, and her long face softened just a little. "Your mother was carrying you. I was at the house to let out her gowns. She stood in the little dressing room off the long gallery, in front of the long mirror, and I was on my knees with my mouth full of pins, pinning her hem, because she had grown and the hem had come up. And through the door, in the gallery, I heard them."
"Heard who?"
"Your father. And his sister." Madame Hesper's voice became flat and careful, the voice of somebody reading back words they have kept in a drawer for a long time and taken out only rarely. "I did not see them. The door was half shut. I heard only voices. Your father's voice, very low. And Miss Cressida's voice, very high, like a kettle when it starts to scream."
"What did they say?"
Madame Hesper looked down at the green coat on the table.
"I do not know what they were talking about," she said. "I want to be honest with you, child. I heard words. I did not understand them. I have thought about them for twenty years and I still do not understand them." She took a breath. "Miss Cressida said: You cannot keep them hidden forever, Aurelian. Somebody will come asking. And when they come, I will not lie for you. And your father said: Then don't lie. But don't come back. And she said: You will owe me for this. All of you. Every single one. And then there was the sound of something breaking. A vase, I think. A big one. And she came out of the gallery past the dressing-room door, very fast, in a white dress, and the smell of lilies came in after her, so strong that your mother put her hand over her mouth. And Miss Cressida saw me on my knees with my pins, and she stopped."
The shop was very quiet. The rain whispered at the window.
"She looked at me," said Madame Hesper. "For a long time. And she said: Madame Hesper. I didn't know you were here. Very sweet. Very polite. And then she said: You didn't hear anything, did you?"
"And what did you say?" said Corin.
"I said, Hear what, Miss Cressida?" Madame Hesper's mouth twisted. "The same thing I said to your mother, Lyra, years later, about her list. It is the only thing a woman in my trade can ever say." She picked up the pin again, looked at it, and pushed it into the tomato. "She never came to my shop again. She had her clothes made after that by Lasalle, on the Hill. French cuts, white silk. Very expensive. Very cold."
Lyra said, so quietly that Corin almost did not hear it: "White silk. And a smell I did not like."
"What's that?" said Juno.
"It's what I remember of her," said Lyra. "The only thing. I was five. A Midwinter dinner. My father let her come, that once, I don't know why. I remember a great deal of white silk, and a smell I didn't like, and that's all." She looked down at the card on the table. "Lilies. It was lilies. I didn't know the word for the smell, then. I only knew it made me want to go and stand in the garden."
Corin looked from Lyra to Madame Hesper to the card. Then he looked at the envelope from Mrs. Hobb, with its white wax lily.
You cannot keep them hidden forever, Aurelian.
Keep who hidden?
You will owe me for this. All of you. Every single one.
And years later, a list, in violet ink, folded small, in a secret pocket in a traveling dress. Those We Owe.
Two women who wrote in violet ink. One who owed. One who said she was owed.
He wrote it all in the Ledger, the whole quarrel, word for word, as Madame Hesper had said it. When he had finished, his hand ached.
Then he looked at the card again. Bring whomever you need; there is always room.
"You're going," he said to Lyra. It was not a question.
"Yes."
"Even though she might be the C."
"Because she might be the C," said Lyra. She lifted her chin, and for a moment she looked like the portrait of her mother in the narrow room above the stable, the one with the silver half-mask brooch. "If my aunt made a private arrangement when my father's money stopped, then I want to look at her face while I eat her cake. I want to see if she can look back." She took a breath. "And I want somebody standing behind my chair who knows how to read a room. Somebody nobody will look at."
She looked at the green coat on the table, half pinned, glowing in the lamplight like a forest.
"That's why I needed the coat," she said. "Not just for the house. For her. I can walk into the empty house with a key. But I can't walk into my aunt's drawing room with a Harvester. Not in a gray coat. Everybody there will know what a gray coat means." She looked at him. "But everybody in the High Quarter knows what Thorne green means too. It means the old family. It means the ones who were here first. If I walk into Number 11 Crown Walk with a footman in Thorne green two steps behind me, every lady in that room will know I'm telling them something. I'm telling them the Thornes aren't finished."
Corin looked at the coat. Abel's coat. The man who went everywhere Lady Thorne went, and went to the coast with her, and did not come back.
"And Monday afternoon?" he said. "Before the At Home?"
"Number 3," said Lyra. "Our house. It is on the same crescent as hers, a few doors along. I want to go inside, before I go to her. I want to see it first." Her voice wavered and then steadied. "The auction people never changed the locks on the garden door. Bertram told me that, the night before he left. He said, Miss Lyra, the garden door on the mews side, the key is under the third stone of the step, and they will never think to look. I've never used it. I've never dared." She looked at the card. "But if I'm going to walk into her house, I want to walk into my own first."
Madame Hesper put down her pins.
"Then the coat must be ready by Monday noon," she said. "Not Monday evening. Noon." She looked at the green coat, at the pins, at the lamp. "I will sew all night tonight. And all day tomorrow. The Lord of the Seventh Day may forgive me for working on a Sunday. If he does not, I will make him a waistcoat and we will discuss it."
"Madame, you don't have to—"
"I know I do not have to," said Madame Hesper. "That is exactly why I am going to."
