Chapter 9: Madame Hesper's Needle, Part 1 of 5: Saturday
Debt of the Unremembered. Arc 1: The Siphon's Ledger.
Saturday
The page fell open by itself.
That was the strange thing. He had read the first page, and the second, and the pages in other people's hands, the way his own hand told him to. He had turned past them to the long crowded pages that came after. And somewhere near the front, after Lyra's neat slanting line and before the first of the crowded ones, the stiff new book had simply fallen open in his hands, as if it had been opened there many times already. As if its spine had already learned the place.
There was only one word on the page. In the middle. In capitals. So big that it filled the line from edge to edge, and pressed so hard that the pencil had gone right through the paper on the last letter, leaving a small torn hole.
ELARA.
He looked at it for a long time.
Then he read what was written under it. Slowly. All of it. A woman from a bank. Short dark hair, cut straight. A gold front tooth over a gap. A glass that went white. A pool, and dragonflies, and a small hot hand holding his, and a voice. Cor, look, it's so pretty. A word an old man had said a long time ago, in a schoolhouse, with an oak tree outside. Blazing.
And at the bottom, by itself, in a hand that had been shaking:
Is she?
He sat on the edge of his narrow bed in the gray light with the book open on his knees and did not move for a long time. He did not know her. He did not know what she looked like. He did not know anything about her except what was written on this page, by a man who had been him, the night before, and who had been very frightened, and very tired, and very sure of something he could not say.
He turned to the front. To the inside of the cover.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
The ache came. Under his ribs. The thumb, pressing. It did not go away this time. It stayed, the whole time he sat there, quiet and patient and sore, like a bruise you keep forgetting is there until you touch it.
He did not know what to do with it. So he did what the first page told him. He got up. He put on his boots first, and then his coat. He ate the stew outside his door, crouching in the doorway, and it was hot and salty and slightly burned on the bottom. He read the notes on his door frame, and one of them was new and said OWES 1 SILVER. NEXT FRIDAY. And under it, HE IS A FOOL. A GOOD ONE. And he did not know what it meant, and it made him smile anyway.
When he came out into Tallow Street, it was raining.
Not hard. A thin gray steady rain, the kind that does not seem to fall so much as hang in the air, so that you walk through it the way you walk through mist, and come out the other side wet without knowing how. The gemstone lamps were still burning, blue and cold on their tall black necks, and the rain made a soft blue haze round each of them, like breath round a mouth on a winter morning. The cobbles shone. The gutters ran. Somewhere a shutter banged in the wind.
And a voice above him said, "It's raining."
He looked up.
There was a girl on the gutter four stories up. Small and thin and dressed like a boy, in a coat much too big for her, with the hood of a sack pulled over her cap against the rain. A brass key hung on a string round her neck. She was sitting with her knees pulled up and her arms round them, and she looked wet, and cold, and extremely cross.
"Juno," she said, before he could open the book. "Mrs. Marta's granddaughter. I'm your runner. I caught you over the Cut. That's once." She glared at him through the rain. "And it's raining. So it's one copper today, not two. And I'm not happy about it."
He opened the book anyway. He found her line, at the bottom of the second page. She tells you once. He looked up at her.
"I know," he said. "I read it."
Juno sniffed. It was a very wet sniff. But under the sack hood, he thought, she looked a little less cross.
"Message," she said. "From the posh lady. Linden Walk. Over the horse." She dug inside her coat and came out with a small folded square of paper, damp at the corners, and held it up. "She gave it me last night. At dark. She said, give it to him the moment he comes out, and not before, because he won't know what it means until he's read his book." A pause. "She's very bossy, your posh lady."
She leaned out over the edge of the gutter, far out, so far that his stomach turned over, and let the folded paper go. It fluttered down through the rain, four stories, turning over and over like a leaf, and he caught it.
He unfolded it. The paper was thick and good, the kind that had once been expensive and had been kept a long time in a drawer. The writing was the neat clear slanting hand from the third page of his Ledger. The ink had run a little at one corner where the rain had touched it.
C.
Rule five. I went to Halloran and Dunn yesterday, on Market Row, as I said I would. The auctioneers. I have their lot book open in front of me as I write this. I paid a clerk a silver to let me sit in the back room with it for an hour, and I have copied what matters.
It is not what I thought.
Meet me at noon. Market Row, at the bottom of the Hill steps, outside a shop called the Needle and Thimble. There is a sign with a gold thimble on it. You cannot miss it.
Wear your oldest clothes. Not the Ministry coat. If you must bring the rod, bring it under something.
I will explain when you come.
L.
P.S. Your Juno ate the last of Oren's apples. He is not speaking to her.
Corin read it twice. Then he looked up at the gutter.
Juno was watching him read, with her chin on her knees and the rain dripping off the end of her nose.
"Did you eat the last of Oren's apples?" he said.
"He offered," said Juno, with enormous dignity. "Sort of. He didn't say no. He just looked at me. He looks at everybody like that." She wiped her nose on her wet sleeve. "Are you going? To the posh lady? At noon?"
"Yes."
"Then you'll want a different coat. She said." Juno looked down at his long gray Ministry wool with deep disapproval. "You've only got the one. I've seen your room."
He had not thought of that. He looked down at the coat. At the brass buttons, with the open book and the closed eye. Not the Ministry coat.
"Gran's got one," said Juno. "In the trunk. Under her bed. Grandpa's old one. From before. Brown. Too big for anybody." She paused. "She won't give it you."
"No."
"I could get it for you," said Juno thoughtfully. "Without asking. It's only borrowing." She paused again. "Two coppers. On account of the rain, and on account of if she catches me she'll skin me."
"Juno."
"What?"
"I've got seven coppers. Until next Friday. And I owe your gran a silver."
Juno looked at him for a long moment through the rain. Then she sighed. It was her grandmother's sigh exactly, long and heavy and theatrical, meant to be heard from the other end of a street.
"Fine," she said. "On account. Like Mister Holt. You can owe me." And she was gone, over the ridge of the roof and away, before he could say anything else.
She came back a quarter of an hour later, down the drainpipe this time, with a great dark bundle tucked under one arm and a look on her face of deep, wicked satisfaction. She dropped onto the cobbles beside him and held the bundle out.
It was a coat. Brown wool, very old, very heavy, smelling of camphor and dust and, very faintly, of pipe smoke. It was enormous. When Corin put it on over his own clothes, the sleeves came down past his fingertips and the hem came down to his ankles, and it hung off his shoulders like a tent off its poles.
Juno looked at him critically.
"You look like a scarecrow," she said. "A poor one."
"Good."
"I suppose." She reached up, without asking, and turned the collar up round his ears, and stood back again. Her face changed a little. Just for a moment. Something went across it, quick and young and not quite steady, like a small cloud going across the sun.
"He used to turn it up like that," she said. "Grandpa. When it rained. On the roof. When he showed me the stars." She stopped. Then she said, very gruffly, "Bring it back. Dry. Or I'll never run for you again."
"I'll bring it back."
"Dry."
"Dry."
She nodded, once, short, and did not look at him. And then she said, "Market Row. The bottom of the Hill steps. I'll take you. But not the roofs. Not in the rain. Even I don't do the roofs in the rain."
They went by the streets. He walked with the brown coat flapping round his ankles and the collar up and his own gray Ministry coat folded over his arm under it, with the rod in its long pocket and the Ledger in its place over his heart and the sewn black square, warm, against his ribs. And Juno walked beside him, close, with her sack hood pulled down and her hand on her key.
She did not say much on the way. But once, at the corner of the Salt Stairs, when a cart went past too close and splashed them both, she reached out, without looking, and took a fistful of the brown coat's sleeve, and held on to it, all the way down the steps. As if she were keeping it from blowing away.
Market Row
Market Row ran along the foot of the Hill like a hem along the bottom of a skirt.
It was the place where the two halves of the city met. Above it, going up and up in long white terraces, there were the houses of the High Quarter, with their columns and their iron balconies and their tall clean windows, and above them all, at the very top, the white spires of the Council House against the gray sky. Below it, going down and down in a jumble of roofs and smoke, there was everything else. The Ink Quarter. The Lower Docks. The river. And in between, on Market Row, there were the shops that sold to both.
They were narrow, and old, and very fine. Glovers and hatters and bootmakers. A shop that sold nothing but fans. A shop that sold nothing but ribbons, in a window full of a thousand colors, so bright in the gray rain that it hurt to look at. A chocolate maker, with a smell coming out of his door so rich and sweet and brown that Juno stopped dead on the wet pavement and shut her eyes and breathed it in, and Corin had to take her by the sleeve and pull her on. A perfumer, with tiny glass bottles in his window like jewels. Corin did not look at the perfumer's window. He did not know why. He walked past it fast, with his collar up, and did not breathe until he was by.
The pavements were wide and swept, and the people on them were of two kinds. There were the ones who came down from the Hill in carriages, and stepped out under umbrellas held by footmen, and went into the shops without looking at anyone. And there were the ones who came up from below on foot, in the rain, and stood outside the shops, and looked in at the windows, and did not go in.
Corin, in a dead night porter's coat that came down to his ankles, was the second kind. Nobody looked at him at all.
At the far end of Market Row, where the street widened into a little square, there was a great flight of white stone steps going up into the rain. Wide steps, shallow and smooth, with iron lamps on posts on either side, and at the top of the first flight, a pair of tall iron gates, standing open. Two men in dark uniforms stood beside the gates, under a little stone roof, out of the rain, with short black rods at their belts.
"The Hill steps," said Juno, at his elbow. She said it in the same voice she had used for the Cut. "You can't go up without a reason. A good one. Written down. Or a carriage. Or a coat that says you belong to somebody who belongs up there." She sniffed. "I've never been up. Not even on the roofs. They've got spikes on the roofs up there. And dogs."
And on the corner of the little square, across from the steps, there was a shop with a gold thimble hanging over its door.
It was a small, narrow, very old shop, squeezed between a bootmaker's and a shop that sold umbrellas. It had one bow window, with small panes of thick wavy glass, and in the window there was a dress form. A headless wooden lady, on a stand, wearing half a gown. Only half. One side of it was finished, a deep plum silk, with lace at the shoulder and tiny pearl buttons down the front. The other side was only pinned. Raw edges. Tacking thread. A hundred pins, catching the gray light, sticking out of the bare cloth like the quills of a hedgehog. Over the door, the gold thimble swung slowly on its iron bracket in the wind, and dripped.
And under the bootmaker's awning next door, out of the rain, with the hood of a gray cloak pulled forward over her face, there stood Lyra Thorne.
He did not know her.
He had read her line in the Ledger that morning. She is your partner. You chose it. He had read the long crowded pages after, and found her everywhere in them. A sword at his throat in a dark printer's shop. Embroidery burned to cut a chain. A father in a white room who tapped on the wall. A portrait turned round. A letter that said I have not cried. He knew more about her, he thought, than he knew about himself.
But he did not know her face.
She pushed back her hood a little as they came up. Pale. Dark hair pinned up at the back. Gray eyes, like rain on slate. A small cut on her chin, almost healed. She looked at Juno first, and something softened at the corner of her mouth. Then she looked at Corin. At the enormous brown coat, flapping round his ankles in the wind, and the collar turned up round his ears, and his wet hair.
She looked at him for a long moment.
"Do you know me?" she said.
It was the same plain question she had asked him on the stairs over the stable. He did not know that. But he heard, in her voice, that she had asked it before, and that she would ask it again, and that she had decided not to let it hurt.
"No," he said. "But I read you."
She nodded, as if that were the right answer.
"Then that will have to do," she said. She looked at the coat again. "Where in the world did you get that?"
"It's Grandpa's," said Juno. "I borrowed it. Without asking. He has to bring it back dry."
Lyra looked at the girl. Then back at the coat. And something went across her face that Corin could not read. It was gone at once.
"Then we'll keep it dry," she said. "Come under here. Both of you. Before you drown."
They stood under the bootmaker's awning, the three of them, close together against the wall, with the rain dripping off the edge of the canvas in front of them like a bead curtain. Lyra took a folded sheet of paper from inside her cloak. Not the good thick paper of her letter. Cheap paper, gray, the kind clerks use for copying.
"Halloran and Dunn," she said. "The auctioneers. Three doors down. They sold everything. The silver, the paintings, the chairs, the beds, the carpets, the kitchen pans. They have a lot book. A great fat thing, bound in green, with every lot in it, and the price, and who bought it." Her voice was low and quick and even. "I copied ours."
She unfolded the gray paper. Her neat slanting hand, smaller and faster than in her letter. A list.
"Most of it is what I expected," she said. "The silver went to a dealer on Gilt Street. The paintings went to a dozen different houses on the Hill. People who'd been to our dinners. People who'd danced in our ballroom." Her mouth tightened. "They came to the sale and bought our chairs and went home and sat in them." She ran one finger down the list. "That isn't what matters. These are."
She pointed.
Lot 1. The house and grounds, No. 3 Crown Walk, the Hill. NOT OFFERED. Sold before the auction, by private agreement, to a buyer represented by the Oblivion Bank.
And beside it, in a different hand, the clerk's hand, copied by hers:
Agent for the buyer: Fourth Desk.
Corin did not move.
He did not know why the words Fourth Desk went into him like that. Like a cold hand laid flat on the back of his neck. He looked down at them on the gray paper, in Lyra's small neat hand, and the ache under his ribs, which had never quite gone away all morning, came back hard.
ELARA. Auditor, Fourth Desk, the Oblivion Bank.
Lyra was watching his face.
"You know it," she said. "The Fourth Desk."
"I read it," said Corin. His voice came out strange. "This morning. In my book. On a page by itself."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment. She did not ask. He saw her decide not to ask. Not yet.
"Two years ago," she said, "the Fourth Desk of the Oblivion Bank bought my family's house before anyone else was allowed to bid for it. For a price that isn't written down. And it has stood there ever since, on Crown Walk, with its shutters closed, and nobody living in it." A pause. "I've walked past it. Twice. I couldn't make myself stop."
She moved her finger down the list.
Lot 12. Long-case clock, walnut and brass, twelve-stone movement, made by Lord Edmund Thorne. WITHDRAWN before sale, by order of the vendor's agent, B.
"My grandfather built it," said Lyra quietly. "With his own hands, in his workshop at the top of the house. It took him three years. It has twelve little stones set into its face, round the dial, one for every hour, and they light up, one after another, as the hours go round." She swallowed. "Twelve stones. Eleven are ours. The twelfth is mine. The twelfth is the witness." She did not say where the words came from. Corin knew. They were in his Ledger, copied from a letter that had faded in bad light. "It wasn't sold, Corin. Bertram took it out of the sale. Himself. Before anyone could bid."
"Where did it go?"
"It doesn't say." Her finger moved again. Down, to the very bottom of the list. "But this does."
Proceeds of sale to be paid to the vendor's agent, B., at the following direction:
Care of No. 3, Crown Walk, the Hill.
The rain dripped off the edge of the awning. A carriage went by in the square, its wheels hissing on the wet stones, and a footman on the back of it looked down at them, the three of them pressed against the wall in their old clothes, and looked away.
"The house," said Corin slowly.
"Our house," said Lyra. "Bertram told the auctioneers to send the money from the sale of everything we owned to our own house. The house he'd already sold to the Bank. The house with the shutters closed and nobody in it." Her voice was very steady. Too steady. "And then he told me he was going to his sister's in the country. And he cried in the hall. And he went."
"Has anyone been to the house since?"
"I don't know." She folded the gray paper. "That's what I mean to find out."
She looked up at him. And then past him, across the little square, through the rain, at the great flight of white steps, and the open iron gates at the top of the first flight, and the two men in dark uniform standing under their little stone roof, with their short black rods.
"I can go up those steps any time I like," she said. "I'm a Thorne. They still let a Thorne up the Hill, even a poor one. Even one who lives over a horse." She looked back at him. "You can't. Not in that coat. Not in your Ministry coat either, not without a writ, and we both know nobody will give you a writ to go poking round the old Thorne house. Not now. Not with an eighty-eight C on your file."
Corin did not ask how she knew about the eighty-eight C. He had told her himself, on a handbill, through the bars of a grating. He knew because he had read it.
"So how do I go up?" he said.
Lyra almost smiled.
"The same way every other man in the city goes up the Hill without a writ," she said. "Two steps behind someone who has one. Carrying her umbrella." She turned toward the door with the gold thimble swinging over it. "Come on. Madame Hesper's been waiting since the eleventh bell, and she does not like to be kept waiting, and she likes even less to be kept waiting by a man she has never met who is about to ask her to make him a coat for nothing."
"For nothing?"
"For nothing," said Lyra. "Because that's what she's been doing for me for two years, and she has never once let me thank her for it. And she won't let you either." She reached for the door. "Juno. You'll come in. You're not going to sit on a roof in the rain while I'm inside where it's warm."
"I'm not cold," said Juno, whose lips were blue.
"You are now," said Lyra. "In."
And she opened the door, and a little bell rang over it, high and sweet, and a warm breath came out of the shop, smelling of steam and starch and lavender and new cloth. And they went in, out of the rain.
