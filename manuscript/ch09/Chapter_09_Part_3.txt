Chapter 9: Madame Hesper's Needle, Part 3 of 5: Thorne Green
Thorne Green
The stairs were so steep that Corin had to climb them with his hands as well as his feet. They creaked under him like an old ship. At the top was a low door, and through the door was a room under the roof, so low that he could only stand up straight in the very middle of it, where the roof beams met.
It was a storeroom. It was the kind of room that every old shop has somewhere, a room where things go to wait, and wait, and finally be forgotten. The only light came from one small round window at the far end, gray with rain and dirt, so that everything in the room looked as if it were underwater. There were dress forms up here too, but older ones, with the stuffing coming out of their chests. There were hatboxes stacked in leaning towers. There was a birdcage with no bird in it. There was a rolled-up carpet tied with string, and a child's rocking horse with no tail, and a mirror turned to face the wall. The air smelled of dust and lavender and the sour, papery smell of moths, even though the lavender was meant to keep the moths away.
Madame Hesper came up behind him with a candle in a brass holder. She did not use her hands on the stairs. She just walked up them, straight-backed, as if they were an ordinary staircase in an ordinary house. Corin thought that she had probably been walking up these stairs every day for forty years and her feet knew them better than her eyes did.
"There," she said, and pointed with the candle.
Under the round window, half hidden behind the rolled carpet, there was a trunk.
It was a big trunk, nearly as long as a coffin and about as deep, made of wood covered in dark green leather. The leather had been beautiful once. Now it was cracked and dry and pale along the edges, like the skin on an old man's hands. There were brass corners on it, gone brown, and two brass straps across the lid with buckles. On the front, in the middle, there was a brass plate, and on the plate, worn down nearly smooth by years of hands, there was a crest. Corin bent close to see it.
It was a rose. A single rose, open, with five petals. And wound around the stem of the rose, so tightly that it seemed to be part of it, there was a long branch covered in thorns.
"The Thorne crest," said Madame Hesper behind him. "A rose for the family that married in, two hundred years ago, and a thorn for the family that was already there. That is what Lady Thorne used to tell me. She said the rose was always the one that got the credit, and the thorn was always the one that did the work."
Corin put his hand on the brass plate. It was cold.
"Pull it out," said Madame Hesper. "Into the middle, where you can stand. Carefully. The handles are rotten."
The handles were rotten. The left one came away in his hand at the first pull, and he had to put his arms around the whole end of the trunk and drag it backward, his boots scraping on the boards. It was heavy, but not as heavy as it looked. Whatever was inside was soft. It shifted as he pulled, with a sound like a sigh.
When it was in the middle of the room, under the meeting of the roof beams, Madame Hesper gave him the candle to hold and knelt down in front of it. She knelt slowly, one knee and then the other, and he heard her joints crack. She undid the first buckle. Then the second. The leather straps were so old that they bent stiffly, like dead fingers.
Then she put both hands on the lid and stopped.
She stayed like that for a long moment. Corin did not say anything. The candle flame bent and straightened in a draft from the window. Down below, he could hear the faint sound of Lyra's voice, and then Juno's, and then the clink of a cup.
"Two years," said Madame Hesper. She said it to the trunk. "After the house went. After the auction. The men from Halloran and Dunn came to the house to make their list, and before they got to the servants' hall, Mr. Bertram came to me in the night with this trunk on a handcart. He said, Madame, they are going to sell the liveries by the pound for rags. He said, these coats were made in your shop, and they ought to come home. He paid me one silver to keep them. One silver. For as long as it took. I told him it was not enough and he said it was all he had." She was quiet for a breath. "He was a stiff old stick, Bertram. But he loved that house more than the family did."
Corin's hand tightened on the candle. Bertram. The old steward. The man who had sent Oren away, and let the carriage in, and signed the book for Harvester L. Hale and one guest. The man who had gone to a sister in the country.
"When did you last see him?" Corin asked.
"That night," said Madame Hesper. "With the handcart. In the rain, like this." She looked up at him, and her pale eyes caught the candlelight. "Why?"
"No reason," said Corin.
Madame Hesper looked at him a moment longer. Then she said, "You are almost as bad a liar as Lady Thorne was," and opened the trunk.
The smell came up first. Lavender, very strong, so strong it made his eyes water, and under it the clean, heavy smell of good wool that has been folded away and kept dry. Then the color.
It was green.
Not one green. Many greens, all lying together in folded layers, but all of them were the same family of green, the way all the people in a family have the same nose. It was a deep, dark green, the green of a forest at evening, the green of ivy on a cold wall. Where the candlelight touched it, it shone a little, with a soft glow, like the back of a beetle. Where the shadow lay on it, it was nearly black.
"Thorne green," said Madame Hesper. Her voice had changed. It had gone soft, softer than he had heard it. "I mixed the dye myself. Lady Thorne's mother-in-law, old Lady Agatha, sent back the first six batches. She said they were too blue, or too yellow, or too cheerful. She said a Thorne servant should look like a hedge in winter. Something you could not get through." She reached into the trunk and touched the top coat, very gently, with two fingers. "I got it right on the seventh batch. I was twenty-six years old. Old Lady Agatha looked at it for a whole minute without saying anything, and then she said, That will do, and walked out. It was the nicest thing anyone in that family ever said to me."
She began to lift the coats out, one by one, and lay them over the edge of the trunk.
There were six of them. Six footmen's coats, all in Thorne green, all cut the same way: long, coming down to the back of the knee, with tails that split at the back. They had high, stiff collars that would stand up under the chin. They had wide cuffs that turned back, and the cuffs were faced with a lighter green silk, the color of new leaves. Down the front of each one there was a double row of buttons.
Corin held the candle closer to the buttons.
They were brass. Real brass, not painted iron. They had been polished once, a long time ago, and they had gone dull, but the shape on them was still sharp. On every button, small as a fingernail, there was the rose. And around the rose, the thorn.
"Two hundred and twelve buttons," said Madame Hesper. "I counted them when Bertram brought the trunk. In case the auction men came looking. Every button has a number scratched on the back. The number tells you which footman it belonged to, and which coat, and which place on the coat. Old Lady Agatha did that. She said servants steal buttons."
"Did they?"
"Of course they did. Everybody steals buttons." Madame Hesper lifted out the fourth coat and held it up to the candle, looking at the shoulders. "But not from the Thornes. Nobody ever stole a button from a Thorne. You would not be able to sell it. Every pawnbroker in the city would know where it came from."
She looked at the fourth coat, and then at Corin, and then at the coat again. She shook her head and put it down. She held up the fifth. She looked at Corin. She frowned. She put it down.
She held up the sixth.
This one was a little different. The green was the same, and the buttons were the same, but the cut was plainer. The tails were shorter. The collar did not stand up so high. And there was something on the left sleeve, just above the cuff: a band of darker green, so dark it was almost black, about the width of two fingers, sewn all the way around.
"This one," she said.
"Why that one?"
"Because the other five are for footmen who stand in halls and open doors," said Madame Hesper. "And this one is for the man who walks behind the lady in the street." She touched the dark band on the sleeve. "That band means I belong to her. Not to the house. Not to the master. To her. The man who wore this coat went everywhere Lady Thorne went, two steps behind her, carrying her parcels and her umbrella and her shawl. He waited outside every shop. He stood at the back of every drawing room. Nobody ever spoke to him and nobody ever looked at him, and he knew everything." She held it up against Corin's shoulders, measuring with her eyes. "His name was Abel. He was about your size. A little thicker in the waist. He liked sugar in his tea."
"What happened to him?"
Madame Hesper lowered the coat.
"He went to the coast," she said, "with Lady Thorne. When she went. Lyra was twelve. He went with her to carry her bags and he did not come back either." She folded the coat over her arm. "Nobody ever asked about him. That is what it means to be a servant, Mr. Vane. When you go, nobody asks."
Corin looked at the green coat over her arm. At the dark band on the sleeve. At the rose and thorn on the brass buttons, catching the candle, winking at him like small eyes.
A man who walked two steps behind Lady Thorne. A man who went everywhere she went and knew everything and was never looked at. A man who went to the coast and did not come back.
Abel, he thought, and filed it, the way he filed everything, in the part of his mind that would be gone by tomorrow morning unless he wrote it down.
Lady Thorne did not go to the coast alone, said Malakor, very quietly, from the pocket at his ribs. Ladies who go to die by the sea do not usually take a man to carry their umbrella.
Unless they are not going to die, Corin thought.
Unless, agreed Malakor, they are not going to die.
"You will need to close the trunk," said Madame Hesper. "And carry it back where it was. I will take this one down." She stood up, with the coat over her arm, her knees cracking again. "I will have to take the waist in. I will have to let the sleeves down half an inch, because Abel had short arms and you have long ones. I will have to clean it and press it, and polish the buttons, and the collar wants a new lining because the moths have been at it in spite of everything." She looked down at the coat. "And there is one other thing I will have to do. Something I always did. Something I did for every coat that ever left my shop for that house."
"What?"
But Madame Hesper had already turned toward the stairs, the candle in one hand and the green coat in the other, and she did not answer.
Corin closed the trunk. He did up the stiff old buckles. He dragged it back under the round window, behind the rolled-up carpet, where it had waited for two years and would wait again. The broken handle he laid on top of it, so it would not be lost.
Before he went down, he stood for a moment in the low gray room, under the beams, with the birdcage and the tailless horse and the mirror turned to the wall. He thought about Bertram coming through the rain with a handcart in the middle of the night, to save a trunk of coats from being sold for rags. A stiff old stick who loved the house more than the family did.
A man like that, he thought, does not let a carriage in at night unless somebody makes him.
And a man like that does not go to a sister in the country without saying goodbye to the woman who is keeping his coats.
A Pocket for a List
When Corin came down the steep stairs, Madame Hesper had already laid the green coat out on the long table, flat on its back, with its arms spread wide like a man lying in the snow. She had pushed the shears and the tomato pincushion and the cold tea to one end. She had lit a lamp, an oil lamp with a green glass shade, and pulled it close, so that a circle of warm light fell on the coat and everything else in the shop went dim around it.
Lyra had come to stand at the table. She was looking down at the coat with an odd face. Not sad, exactly. More the face of somebody who has opened a book and found a pressed flower inside that they had forgotten putting there.
"Abel's," Lyra said, when Corin came up beside her. "That's Abel's. I know it by the band." She touched the dark green band on the sleeve. "He used to let me ride on his shoulders in the garden. He smelled of boot polish and peppermints. He used to call me Miss Small." She took her hand away. "I had forgotten that. I had forgotten him. How could I forget him?"
"You were twelve," said Madame Hesper, without looking up. "Twelve-year-olds forget servants. That is what servants are for. To be forgotten, so that the people they serve can remember more important things." She said it plainly, without any bitterness, the way she might have said that bread was for eating. "Juno. Come here. You will hold the lamp."
Juno got up from her stool by the stove, slowly, as if she still did not quite trust any of this. She came and took the lamp and held it up, high, the way she must have held lanterns on the rooftops at night. The circle of light swung and then steadied.
Madame Hesper took the small stork-shaped scissors out of her apron. She turned the green coat over so that the inside showed. The lining was a pale green silk, faded almost to gray, and stained yellow under the arms. She found a seam on the left side, on the inside, just under the place where the arm met the body. She snipped one stitch. Then another. Then she slid the point of the scissors along the seam, so carefully and so quickly that the thread seemed to fall apart by itself, and the lining opened like a mouth.
"Look," she said.
They looked.
Inside, between the lining and the green wool, there was a pocket. It was not a big pocket. It was flat and narrow, about as long as Corin's hand and about as wide as three fingers. It was made of the same pale green silk as the lining, so that if you did not know it was there, you would never see it. It had no button and no flap. It was just a little slot, a hidden sleeve, sewn so that the opening faced up and toward the heart.
There was nothing in it.
"Every coat," said Madame Hesper. "Every coat that ever left my shop for the Thorne house had one of these. The footmen's coats. The coachman's coat. The steward's black coat, for Bertram. Lord Thorne's coats, all of them, his hunting coat and his evening coat and his old brown coat for the garden. And every gown I ever made for Lady Thorne." She ran one long finger along the edge of the little pocket. "In the gowns, I put it in the bodice, here, under the arm, where a lady's hand can go without anyone noticing. Lady Thorne asked me to. The very first gown I made for her, the year she married, she stood where you are standing, Mr. Vane, in her shift, and she said: Madame Hesper, I want a pocket. A small one. A secret one. One that nobody will ever find."
"What for?" said Juno.
Madame Hesper looked up at her over the lamp.
"I asked her that," she said. "She said: For a list."
"What list?"
"I did not ask," said Madame Hesper. "My sign says no questions. I meant it then and I mean it now." She looked back down at the coat. "She said that every person in her house should carry one. A small list, close to the heart, of the things they must not forget. She said she had been told to do it by somebody she trusted, when she was a girl. She said, In this city, Madame, people lose things. They lose their gloves and their keys and their hats. And sometimes they lose much more than that. If you have a list, and it is close to your heart, then even if you lose everything else, you will still have the list."
Corin put his hand, without thinking, flat over his own heart. Over the Ledger.
Nobody said anything. The lamp hissed softly in Juno's hand.
"Did you ever see one?" Corin said at last. "A list? In one of the pockets?"
Madame Hesper was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice was lower.
"Once," she said.
She put down the stork scissors. She folded her hands together on the green coat, one long thin hand over the other.
"It was the last gown I ever made for her," she said. "A traveling dress. Dark blue, very plain, for the journey to the coast. The doctor had told her the sea air would be good for her chest. That is what we were all told." She paused. "She came in for the last fitting. Here, in this shop, on a wet day like this one. She stood on that box," she nodded at a low wooden box by the dress forms, "and I knelt and pinned the hem. And when she lifted her arm for me to check the seam under it, the little pocket was already full. She had already put her list in it, before the gown was even finished. And the seam was only pinned, not sewn, and when she lifted her arm, the paper fell out."
She stopped.
"It fell on the floor," said Madame Hesper. "Just there." She pointed at a place on the bare boards near the box, worn pale by many years of feet. "It fell open. It was only one sheet, folded small, the way she always folded things. Very small, into a little square. I did not mean to look. But it was right there by my knee, open, and before I could look away I saw the top of it."
"What did it say?" said Lyra. Her voice was very quiet.
Madame Hesper looked at her.
"At the very top," she said, "in her own hand, in violet ink, it said: Those We Owe."
The words seemed to hang in the warm damp air of the shop.
Those We Owe.
Corin felt the three stones grow warm against his hip, through the coat, through the shirt. Not hot. Just warm. The way a cat is warm when it climbs into your lap.
"And under that?" he said.
"Names," said Madame Hesper. "A long list of names. Very small writing. I saw only the first few before she bent and picked it up." She shut her eyes, the way people do when they are trying to see something that is far away. "I remember the first. It was a name I did not know. Something short. And next to each name there was a mark. A small mark, like a little cross, or a tick. Some had the mark and some did not." She opened her eyes again. "She picked it up very quickly. She folded it back into its little square and put it in the pocket and held her arm down tight against her side. And she looked at me. She did not look angry. She looked frightened. And she said: You did not see that, Madame."
"And you said?"
"I said, See what, my lady? And she laughed. And then she cried a little. And then she said, Thank you. And she said one more thing." Madame Hesper looked down at her own hands. "She said: Keep sewing pockets. Whatever happens to us. Whoever comes to you. Keep sewing pockets, Madame, and one day somebody will need one."
She picked up her needle. It was already threaded, with a long green thread that matched the lining.
"So I keep sewing them," she said. "I sew them into everything. Into Mrs. Pellow's son's trousers. Into Terrence's brown coat." She glanced at Juno, and Juno's hand flew to her grandfather's coat drying on the hook. "Into every one of Lyra's coats, since she was twelve. Nobody asks me to. Nobody knows they are there, most of them. But I sew them."
"There's one in Grandpa's coat?" Juno whispered.
"Inside left, under the arm. Look when you get home. There may be something in it, or there may not. That is his business, and your grandmother's, and not mine."
Juno stared at the brown coat on its hook as if it had just spoken to her.
Lyra had gone pale. She put her hand up under her own left arm, inside her second-best brown, feeling along the seam.
Her fingers stopped.
"It's here," she said. "There's a pocket. I never knew." She pulled her hand out. It was empty. "There's nothing in it."
"No," said Madame Hesper gently. "There would not be. You never knew to fill it."
She bent over the green coat, and began to sew the lining closed again around the hidden pocket. Her needle went in and out, in and out, very fast, and each stitch was so small that Corin could hardly see it. She sewed the way other people breathe, without thinking about it, without stopping.
"This one will be yours, Mr. Vane," she said, not looking up. "When the coat is finished, the pocket will be yours. What you put in it is your own affair." Her needle flashed in the lamplight. "But I would put something in it, if I were you. A man who carries a book over his heart every day for years is a man who is afraid of losing things." She bit off the thread. "And a man who is afraid of losing things should not keep everything in one place."
Corin looked at the little green pocket. At the place on the floor by the box where a paper had fallen open, years ago, with Those We Owe written at the top in violet ink.
Violet ink.
He thought of Mrs. Hobb, Lord Thorne's nurse, and her lily soap, and the cream envelopes sealed with white wax, written in violet ink, from a friend.
He thought of the Benevolent Fund book, and the fishhook C.
The same color. The same ink. Lady Thorne, writing a list of people she owed. And somebody else, years later, writing to her husband's nurse in the same color.
Two women, he thought. Two women who used violet ink.
Or one.
Write it down, said Malakor softly.
Corin took out the Ledger. He opened it to the day's page, under the heading he had written that morning, and with the stub of pencil from his pocket he wrote, in his small, careful, slanting hand:
Lady Thorne asked for a secret pocket in every coat, "for a list." Her own list was headed THOSE WE OWE, in violet ink. Names, with marks next to some. "Keep sewing pockets. One day somebody will need one." The "friend" who writes to Mrs. Hobb uses violet ink too. ASK: who taught Lady Thorne to keep a list?
He looked at the last line for a long time. Then he underlined it twice.
