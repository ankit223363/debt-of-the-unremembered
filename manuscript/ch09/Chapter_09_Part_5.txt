Chapter 9: Madame Hesper's Needle, Part 5 of 5: The Pattern Book
The Pattern Book
The light had gone out of the window by then. It was not night yet, but it was the dark end of a wet afternoon, when the lamps come on in the shops one by one and the street outside turns the color of a bruise. Madame Hesper lit a second lamp and hung it from a hook in the ceiling, so that the whole long table was in light.
Then she went to a cupboard under the shelves of cloth, unlocked it with a small key from her apron, and took out a book.
It was a big book. Bigger than the Ledger by three or four times. It was bound in brown leather that had gone soft and dark with handling, like the leather of an old saddle, and its corners were rubbed round. The pages were thick and a little yellow. Some of them stuck out at the edges, where bits of cloth had been pinned or pasted in.
She laid it on the table next to the green coat and opened it.
"My pattern book," she said. "One of them. There are nine. This is the seventh. It goes from the year I turned forty to the year I turned sixty." She turned the pages slowly. "Every customer has a page. Sometimes many pages. I write down their measurements every time I make them something, because people change. They grow and shrink and stoop and straighten. And I write down what I made, and what it cost, and what cloth I used, and I pin in a scrap of the cloth so I can match it later."
Corin looked at the pages as they turned. Each one had a name at the top, written in Madame Hesper's sharp, upright hand. Under the name there were columns of numbers, and little drawings of dresses and coats and sleeves, quick and clever, done with only a few lines. And here and there, pinned to the page with a dressmaker's pin gone rusty, there was a tiny square of cloth. Red velvet. Gray tweed. Yellow silk. A scrap of black lace like a spider's web.
It was like a Ledger, he thought. A Ledger of bodies. A whole city's worth of people, written down a few numbers at a time.
Madame Hesper stopped turning.
"There," she said.
At the top of the page, it said: Lady Thorne.
That was all. No first name. Just Lady Thorne, the way the servants must have said it, and the shopkeepers, and everybody in the city except her husband.
Under it there was a little drawing. A woman, standing, very straight, seen from the front. Only a few lines, but Corin knew her at once. He knew her from the portrait in Lyra's narrow room: the long neck, the curl by the left ear, the way the head was held, as if she were listening to something far off. Madame Hesper had caught it with six strokes of a pencil.
And under the drawing, the numbers. Many years of numbers. Line after line, one for each time she had been measured. Bust. Waist. Hip. Shoulder. Arm. Each line had a date beside it, and next to each date, a few words about what had been made.
Wedding gown. Ivory silk. 12 yds. Pocket under left arm, as asked.
Riding habit. Bottle-green. Pocket.
Evening gown, gray. Let out at waist. (She is expecting. Do not mention.)
Lyra had come to stand beside Corin. She was reading over his shoulder. When she came to that line, she made a small sound in her throat.
"That's me," she said. "Do not mention. That's me."
"It was the year of the quarrel," said Madame Hesper. "The gray gown. I let it out twice more before you came."
They read on. Gowns and coats and capes. A gown for a ball at the Archon's palace. A black gown for a funeral, Old Lady Agatha, d. A blue dress, the one in the portrait: Blue wool crepe, for the painter. Crimson thread for the embroidery in the picture, she brought her own. And then, on the next page, the lines began to get shorter, and further apart, and the last one of all said:
Traveling dress. Dark blue. Plain. For the coast. Pocket under left arm — already filled.
And under that, in the same sharp hand, but smaller, and pressed so hard into the paper that the pencil had nearly torn it:
Chest: no change from last year. No change from the year before. Healthy as a horse. Why the sea?
Corin read the line three times.
Then he looked up at Madame Hesper.
She was looking at the page, not at him. Her long face was very still.
"I measured her the week before she went," said Madame Hesper. "A lady with a weak chest who is being sent to the sea for her health loses her breath on the stairs. She coughs into her handkerchief. She gets thin, here," she touched her own collarbone, "and her chest measure falls, because she cannot fill her lungs. Lady Thorne had walked up my stairs without stopping. She had not coughed once. Her chest was the same as it had been every year since she was twenty." She shut her mouth tight for a moment. "I wrote that line and I never said it out loud. Not to Bertram. Not to anybody. I have no right to say what is wrong with a lady's health. I am a dressmaker. But a tape measure does not lie, Mr. Vane. It cannot. It is only a ribbon with numbers on it."
Lyra had gone white.
"She wasn't ill," she said. Her voice was very small. "They said she was ill. My father said. The doctor said. Everybody said. They said the sea air would help. They said she died there, in the spring, in a house by the water, and they buried her there because it was too far to bring her home." She put her hand flat on the page, over the pencil drawing of the woman with the curl. "She wasn't ill."
"Not in her chest," said Madame Hesper carefully. "That is all a tape measure can tell you. Not in her chest."
Not ill, said Malakor, from the gray coat. And taking a footman. And taking a list of names she owed, folded small, close to her heart. The rod's voice was very quiet. Ladies who are dying do not need lists, Corin. Ladies who are running do.
Corin did not answer it. He turned the page.
The next page was not measurements. It was drawings. Small drawings, many of them, of buttons and clasps and hooks and brooches, each one with a note beside it. It seemed to be a page where Madame Hesper kept the things her customers wore with their clothes, so that she could make the clothes to suit them. A cameo, with a note: Mrs. Langley. Heavy. Reinforce the collar. A row of jet buttons: Lord Pembrook, mourning, will not take them off.
And in the middle of the page, larger than the others, and drawn with much more care, there was a mask.
A little half-mask, the kind people wear at a ball. Covering the eyes and the nose. Two empty holes where the eyes would be. Madame Hesper had drawn it from the front and from the side and from the back. From the back, Corin could see that there was a pin on it, a long one, with a safety catch. And beside the pin there were tiny marks, like scratches, on the inside of the silver. Little lines and curves, very small. Madame Hesper had copied them exactly, the way she copied everything.
Next to the drawing, the note said:
Lady Thorne's brooch. Silver. Marrowmere work — the mask-makers of the canal city. She wears it on every collar. Must make a small loop of silk thread inside every collar to take the pin, so it does not tear the cloth. DO NOT PIN THROUGH THE EYES. She says it is bad luck.
Marks on the back: makers' marks? Ask.
And under that, in a different color, as if she had come back to it later:
Asked. She said: "They are not makers' marks, Madame. They are my name. My real one. The one I was given on the water." Would not say more.
Lyra was staring at the drawing of the little marks on the back of the mask.
"Every girl gets one where she was born," she said slowly. "On the day she is named. That's what Father told me once. When I asked about it. He said, somewhere with a great deal of water." She looked up. "Marrowmere. The canal city. My mother was from Marrowmere."
"She never said so," said Madame Hesper. "Not in so many words. But that is Marrowmere silver. Nobody else makes masks like that. I have seen two others in fifty years. One on an old woman who came in once for a black shawl and paid in coins I had never seen before. And one on a sea captain who wanted his coat let out."
Corin bent close over the drawing of the marks. Little lines. Little curves. Like letters, but not letters he knew. He looked at them for a long time, trying to make them into something. They would not go.
"May I copy this?" he said.
Madame Hesper looked at him. Then she reached into her apron, took out a sheet of thin tracing paper, the kind tailors use for patterns, and laid it over the page. She handed him her pencil.
Corin traced the mask. Front, side and back. He traced every tiny mark on the back of the silver, slowly, holding his breath, making his hand go as steady as he could. When he was finished, he folded the tracing paper small, very small, into a little square, the way Lyra's mother had folded her list.
He did not know why he folded it like that. His hands did it by themselves.
Fold it small, he thought. When something is too big to carry.
He put it inside the back cover of the Ledger.
When he looked up, Lyra was turning the pages again, forward, past her mother's pages, through the rest of the book. She was looking for something. Her fingers went fast. Then they stopped.
Miss Lyra Thorne.
It was a short page, near the end of the book, and it went on, Corin guessed, into the next one. Under the name there was a drawing of a little girl, perhaps four or five years old, standing with her hands behind her back and her chin stuck out. Under that, measurements in tiny numbers. Then a few lines:
First party dress. White. Sash pink. Hates the sash.
Second party dress. Blue. No sash. (She won.)
Embroidery apron, small. Her mother is teaching her to sew. Pocket in the apron, under the left arm, as her mother asks. "For her list, when she is old enough."
Lyra put her finger on that line and kept it there.
"I don't remember any of this," she said. "The party dresses. The sash. The apron." Her voice shook. "I burned it. In the Inkwell. The day my mother taught me to sew. I burned it to save his life, and now I don't have it, and I'll never have it again." She was not crying. She was holding herself very still so as not to. "And here it is. In your book. You wrote it down."
Madame Hesper looked at her for a long moment.
Then she did something Corin had not seen her do all day. She sat down. She pulled out the stool next to Lyra and lowered herself onto it, slowly, and she put one long thin hand over Lyra's hand on the page.
"I was there," said Madame Hesper. "The day she taught you. Did you know that? She asked me to come up to the house and bring a little hoop and some linen, because she wanted you to start on something that was properly made. The room at the top of the house, over the rose garden. It was summer. The light came in gold. Your mother had on the blue dress. You were six and you had your tongue out of the corner of your mouth the whole time, like this." She did it. Her tongue came out of the corner of her stern thin mouth, and it was so strange and so unexpected that Juno, by the stove, let out a snort of laughter and then clapped her hand over her face. "And your mother held your hand on the needle, and she said, In, through, pull. In, through, pull. Every great thing is only patience."
Lyra's lips moved. In, through, pull.
"And you got the thread in a knot," said Madame Hesper, "a terrible knot, a bird's nest, and you threw the hoop on the floor and said you would never sew again as long as you lived. And your mother picked it up and gave it back to you and said, Then fold it small, darling. When something is too big, fold it small and put it somewhere safe, and carry it anyway. And try again tomorrow." Madame Hesper's grip tightened on Lyra's hand. "And you tried again the next day. And the day after that. And you have been sewing ever since. You sell your handkerchiefs in the market on Fridays. I have seen them. They are good. Better than good."
Lyra was crying now. Quietly, without any sound, the tears running straight down her face and dropping onto the page of the pattern book, onto the line that said For her list, when she is old enough.
"I can't remember it," she whispered. "You're telling me, and I can't remember it."
"No," said Madame Hesper. "But now you know it. Which is not the same. But it is not nothing."
Corin looked away. He looked at the lamp, at the green coat, at the steam rising from the wool. He thought about the Ledger in his pocket. About his whole life, written down in his own hand, read every morning by a stranger who had to take his word for it.
Now you know it. Which is not the same. But it is not nothing.
He thought that might be the truest thing anybody had ever said to him. He would write it down later. He would put it on the first page, if there was room.
After a while, Lyra wiped her face on her sleeve, the sleeve with the badly mended cuff, and sat up straight.
"How much do I owe you?" she said. "For the coat. And for my blue wool in the spring, and the brown, and the gray last winter. You never send me a bill. I give you coppers when I have them and you put them in your apron and never count them." Her chin came up. "I want to know. I want to pay it."
Madame Hesper let go of Lyra's hand. She turned back to the page with the little girl on it, to the bottom, where in very small writing there was one more line that Corin had not seen.
Paid in advance, by her mother, for coats and mending until her twenty-first year. Twelve gold. Left in my keeping the week she went to the coast. "Do not tell her until she asks. And if she never asks, never tell her."
Lyra read it. She read it twice.
"You asked," said Madame Hesper simply. "So I am telling you. Your coppers are in a jar on the shelf behind the curtain, every one. I was going to give them back to you on your birthday." She closed the pattern book, gently, and laid her hand flat on its cover. "Your mother knew, child. She knew she was not coming back. Whatever she was going to the sea for, she knew. And she made sure that whatever happened, somebody in this city would keep you warm."
The lamp hissed. The rain fell. Down at the far end of the long room, the stove ticked as it cooled.
Lyra said nothing at all for a long time.
Then she said, "My twenty-first year. My birthday is in the spring." She looked at the closed book. "What happens then?"
"Then," said Madame Hesper, "the money runs out. And you will have to pay me like everybody else." She stood up, very stiff, and reached for her pins. "Or your mother will come back and pay me herself. I have not decided which I would like better."
Those We Owe
They left the Needle and Thimble a little after the sixth bell.
Madame Hesper did not come to the door with them. She was already bent over the green coat again, under the hanging lamp, with a fresh mouthful of pins and her spectacles on, and she only lifted one long hand without looking up when the heavy bell above the door went bonnng. Her last words to them were said around the pins.
"Monday. Noon. The back door, in the alley, not the front. If you come to the front in a gray coat on a Monday, Mrs. Pellow will see you and tell the whole of Market Row I am being arrested." A pin went in. "And Mr. Vane."
"Madame?"
"Eat something. You are a quarter of an inch thinner in the waist than you were when you came in. I do not know how a man does that in one afternoon, but you have done it, and I will not have my coat hanging off you like washing."
Outside, the rain had thinned to a fine drizzle, the kind that does not fall so much as hang in the air and wait for you to walk into it. The lamps of Market Row were lit, and each one had a soft round halo of mist around it, like a dandelion clock. The stalls were closing. Men were tying down canvas and stacking crates and shouting to each other across the wet street. The chestnut woman was shoveling the last of her coals into a bucket, and the smell of them, burnt and sweet, followed them all the way to the corner.
Juno walked between Corin and Lyra. She was wearing Grandpa Terrence's brown coat again. It was dry. Madame Hesper had taken it off the hook by the stovepipe and shaken it out and run a hot iron over the shoulders before she gave it back, without saying a word, and now it was warm and smelled faintly of lavender instead of wet dog. Juno kept stroking the front of it as she walked, as if she were not sure it was real.
She also kept putting her hand, very quickly, inside the left side of it, under the arm, and taking it out again.
"Did you look?" said Lyra gently.
"No," said Juno.
"Is there a pocket?"
Juno was quiet for a few steps. "Yes."
"Is there anything in it?"
Juno did not answer. She walked faster. Her small sharp face, under the cap, was closed up tight like a fist.
"It's Gran's business," she said at last. "That's what the lady said. His business and Gran's. So I'm giving it to Gran. And if Gran wants to tell me what it says, she'll tell me. And if she don't, she don't." She sniffed. "She tells you once."
Lyra looked at Corin over Juno's head. Corin did not say anything. He thought that Juno had just done something that most grown men he could imagine would not have managed, and that she had done it as if it were nothing.
At the bottom of the Hill steps, where Linden Walk went off to the left toward the livery stable and the stone pineapple on the gate, Lyra stopped.
"This is me," she said.
"I know," said Corin.
She stood for a moment under the dripping branches of a plane tree, holding her umbrella, the duck-handled one, which Madame Hesper had told her to keep until Monday. In the lamplight her face looked tired and young and very determined, all at the same time.
"Thank you for today," she said.
"I didn't do anything."
"You stood still for four hours while an old woman stuck pins in you, and you let her tell you that you were invisible, and you didn't argue." Lyra almost smiled. "I think that's something."
She hesitated.
"Corin," she said. "What she said. About my mother. Healthy as a horse."
"Yes."
"If she wasn't ill. If she wasn't going to the sea to get well." Lyra's hand tightened on the duck's head. "Then where was she going?"
"I don't know," said Corin. "Somewhere with a great deal of water."
Lyra looked at him for a long time. Then she nodded, once, the way her mother must have nodded at Madame Hesper when she said thank you, and turned, and walked away down Linden Walk toward the stable and the smell of horses and her father humming at the window.
Corin watched her until the dark took her.
Then he and Juno went on, down the long wet slope toward the river and Tallow Street.
They did not talk much. Juno was thinking about the pocket. Corin was thinking about everything. The steam rising off the gray wool. The tape measure, uncoiling. A man who is afraid of losing things should not keep everything in one place. A list, falling open on a bare wooden floor. Those We Owe.
Tallow Street was dark and narrow and smelled of the river, as always. Number 14 leaned against its neighbors like a tired man leaning on two friends. There was a light in Mrs. Marta's window on the ground floor, and the shadow of somebody moving behind the curtain.
And on the front step, sitting hunched up with his knees under his chin and a sack over his head against the drizzle, there was Toby.
He jumped up when he saw them, and the sack fell off, and his thin pale face came out from under it, wet and pink and very pleased with itself.
"Inspector!" said Toby. "I've been here since five. Mrs. Marta wouldn't let me in. She said I looked like a drowned mouse and she wasn't having drowned mice in the hall. Then she gave me a bun." He held up half a bun, slightly squashed. "I saved you some."
"Toby," said Corin. "What are you doing here? It's Saturday."
"I know! That's why!" Toby glanced up and down the dark lane, as if the Ministry itself might be hiding in a doorway, and dropped his voice to a loud, excited whisper. "I did it. I asked Faber. About the button. The oak-leaf button. Like I said I would."
Corin felt the three stones grow warm against his hip.
"Inside," he said. "Not on the step."
They went in. Mrs. Marta's door was open a crack, and her sharp gray eye looked out at them through it, and she said, "Juno. In. Now. Your supper's cold and it's your own fault," and then, to Corin, "You owe me one silver, Mr. Vane. I have not forgotten, even if you have." And then, more quietly, looking at the brown coat on Juno's shoulders: "Is that dry?"
"Dry as a bone, Gran," said Juno. "Drier. The lady ironed it."
Mrs. Marta's eye went very still in the crack of the door.
"What lady?"
"Madame Hesper. On Market Row. She said she put the lining in. She said to tell you she asked after him." Juno took a breath. "And she said there's a pocket. Inside. Under the arm. And I haven't looked in it, Gran. I haven't. I'm giving it to you."
There was a long silence behind the door.
Then the door opened wide, and Mrs. Marta stood there, small and gray and bent, in her black shawl, with a wooden spoon in her hand. She looked at Juno. She looked at the coat. She put out her free hand, very slowly, and Juno took off the coat and put it into her arms.
Mrs. Marta held it against her chest. She did not look in the pocket. She just held it.
"Twenty-two years," she said, to nobody. "Brown sateen. He wanted red."
Then she went back inside with the coat and Juno, and shut the door.
Corin and Toby stood alone in the dark hall at the bottom of the narrow, crooked stairs.
"What was that about?" Toby whispered.
"I don't know," said Corin. "Tell me about Faber."
They sat on the bottom step, side by side, in the dark, because the hall lamp had not been lit and Corin did not want to go up and Toby did not want to go home. Toby talked very fast, the way he always did when he was excited, with his hands going up and down like birds.
Faber's shop was on Hook Lane, Toby said, right next door to Vespera's, the little clock-mender's with the cracked window and the bench full of springs and wheels. Toby had walked past it twice before he was brave enough to go in. Then he had gone in with his collar turned up and his hands in his pockets, the way he thought a real investigator would, and asked Faber if he had any buttons for sale. Brass ones. With leaves on.
"And he was so pleased, Inspector! Nobody ever asks Faber about anything but clocks. He went all red. He got out a tin from under the bench, and there were three of them in it, all the same, little brass buttons with an oak leaf on. He said they came off a soldier's coat. An old one, from the war. He said he got them in trade at the Sunday rag market under the Lantern Bridge, for mending a woman's watch. An old woman with a tray of buttons. She cuts them off old coats and sells them." Toby leaned closer. "And I asked him where she got the coats. And he said he asked her that too, because the buttons were so fine. And she told him."
"What did she say?"
"She said they came off the coats of men from the village with the big oak," said Toby. "Up past the end of the maps. That's what she said, Faber said. Those exact words. And she said a cart comes down from there twice a year, with whatever they can sell, and she buys the coats off it."
Corin sat very still on the dark step.
Toby put his hand flat over the inside pocket on the left of his coat. Even in the dark, Corin knew what was in there. A red sock, darned at the heel with blue thread. And a folded receipt, stamped PAID IN FULL. Pell Dorran's debt, paid for a twelve-year-old girl called Nan, who was practicing her letters at a window in a village that was not on any map.
"Pell's village, Inspector," Toby whispered. "Nan's village. A cart comes down from there. Twice a year. And the button woman knows the cart." His voice shook. "I paid the debt. But she doesn't know. Nobody's told her. She's still waiting for a letter from him, and he's never going to write it, and nobody's ever going to tell her why." He took a breath. "If we find the cart, we could send her a letter. A real one. And the receipt. So she knows nobody's coming for her."
Corin looked at the small dark shape of Toby beside him.
"The button woman might not know anything," he said.
"She might," said Toby.
"And she might not want to talk to a man in a gray coat."
"That's why I'm coming," said Toby. "Nobody's ever scared of me."
No questions asked, Corin thought. The card in Madame Hesper's window. A whole city of people who did not ask, and an old woman under a bridge who had answered, once, because a clock-mender had fixed her watch.
"Will you come?" said Toby. "Tomorrow? I can't go by myself. Mother says the Lantern Bridge is full of thieves. But if I'm with an Inspector—"
"I'm not an Inspector, Toby. I'm a Senior Investigator who's been sent to sit in the Intake Hall and stamp forms."
"You're my Inspector," said Toby, as if that settled it.
Corin found that he did not have an answer to that.
"The eighth bell," he said at last. "Tomorrow morning. Under the bridge. Bring the receipt."
Toby made a small, delighted noise, like a kettle. Then he gave Corin the squashed half of the bun, and patted his inside pocket once more to be sure the sock and the receipt were still there, and pulled the sack over his head again, and went out into the drizzle, and Corin heard his quick light footsteps going away down Hook Lane until the sound of the river swallowed them.
Corin went up the stairs.
They were the same stairs as always. Narrow and crooked and dark, and they creaked in the same places. He went past the second-floor landing, and Old Terrence's chair by the round dirty window was empty, because Old Terrence was downstairs, in the warm, with his wife and his granddaughter and his brown coat. He went past the fourth-floor landing and the scratched words on the wall that he could not read in the dark. He went up and up, to the top of the house, to his own small room under the roof.
He lit the candle. He sat on the bed. He ate the half bun, slowly, because Madame Hesper had told him to, and because it was the only food he had had all day.
Then he took out the Ledger, and he wrote.
He wrote everything. The shop. The bell like a church. The lavender. The tape measure and what it had told her: the rod, the book, the warm square at his hip, the brown mark on his shirt. The trunk in the attic. Thorne green. Abel, who walked two steps behind Lady Thorne and went to the coast and did not come back. Bertram and the handcart. The secret pocket. Those We Owe. The quarrel, word for word. You cannot keep them hidden forever, Aurelian. The card with the hooked C. The pattern book. Healthy as a horse. Why the sea? The mask. The marks on the back of the mask: my name, my real one, the one I was given on the water. Twelve gold, left for Lyra's coats. Do not tell her until she asks. Juno and the brown coat. Mrs. Marta holding it. Toby on the step. Faber's oak-leaf buttons. The button woman under the Lantern Bridge. A cart that comes down twice a year from the village with the big oak, up past the end of the maps.
When he had finished, it was very late, and the candle had burned down to a stub, and his hand ached all the way up to the elbow.
He sat and looked at the last page.
A man who is afraid of losing things, Madame Hesper had said, should not keep everything in one place.
He was afraid of losing things. He lost everything, every single night. The Ledger was the only thing that stood between him and the dark. Seven volumes of it. Six of them locked in Bram's strongbox. One of them here, in his hand. If anyone ever took this one, or burned it, or if he dropped it in the river by accident, he would wake up one morning and not know his own name, and there would be nothing to tell him.
He had known that, he supposed, for years. He had probably thought it a hundred times. And forgotten it a hundred times.
But nobody had ever told him what to do about it.
He tore a page from the very back of the Ledger. A small one, a half page, that had nothing on it. He smoothed it flat on his knee. And at the top, in his small careful slanting hand, he wrote:
THOSE I OWE
He looked at the words for a long time. Then he began to write names under them. Slowly. One at a time. Thinking about each one before he wrote it down.
Lyra Thorne. She burned the day her mother taught her to sew, to save my life.
Juno. She carries messages over the roofs and never asks for more than a copper. She told me once.
Toby. He calls me Inspector.
Vespera.
He stopped there and thought about Vespera for a while, and then he wrote nothing after her name, because what he owed Vespera was too long to fit.
Captain Oren Vance. He burned "lilies" to make a shield.
Mrs. Marta. One silver. And more than that.
Pell Dorran.
Nan Dorran. Twelve. She does not know she is safe yet.
And then, last, after a long time, with the pencil not quite steady:
Elara.
He looked at the name.
On the first page of the Ledger, the page that said READ THIS FIRST, there was another Elara. You had a younger sister named Elara. And in the Fourth Desk of the Oblivion Bank, behind a glass desk, there was a woman with short dark hair and a gold tooth who read people through their palms and gave away one day of her own life every time she did it, and her name was Elara too.
He did not know if they were the same. He did not know if they were different. He did not know which one he owed, or how much, or for what.
He wrote, very small, beside the name: (which one?)
And then, smaller still: (both?)
He read the list over. Nine names. It was not very long. He thought that Lady Thorne's list had been much longer. He thought that a person who lived as long as she had, and knew as many people, and owed as much, would need a very long list indeed.
But it was a beginning.
He did not put next to any of the names the little marks that Madame Hesper had seen on Lady Thorne's list, the crosses and the ticks. He did not know yet what the marks had meant. Perhaps a tick meant paid. Perhaps a cross meant lost. Perhaps it was the other way round. He would find out, he thought, one day. And when he did, he would know how to mark his own.
He folded the half page small. Very small. In half, and in half again, and in half again, until it was a tight little square no bigger than the end of his thumb.
He did not put it in the Ledger.
Instead, he unbuttoned his shirt at the top, and found the inside of the collar, where the cloth was doubled. With the point of his pencil he worked open a few stitches in the seam, and he slid the little square of paper inside, between the two layers, close against the side of his neck. It was not a proper pocket. It would not last. But on Monday Madame Hesper would give him a coat with a proper pocket in it, a secret one, under the left arm, close to the heart, where nobody would ever find it.
And until then, the list would be here. Not in the book. Somewhere else. In a second place.
So that if he ever lost the book, he would still have the list.
He blew out the candle.
In the dark, lying on his back on the narrow bed, with the rain ticking softly on the roof above him, he hummed. Three notes. The same three notes he always hummed, that he did not know the name of, that he had been humming, the Ledger said, for as long as anybody could remember.
Against his hip, inside the black cloth square, the three stones marked U.W. grew warm. Not hot. Just warm. Warm like a hand.
And from the pocket along his ribs, in the dark, Malakor said, very softly: There is a fourth note, you know.
Corin stopped humming.
What?
Nothing, said the rod. Go to sleep. You have a long day tomorrow, and you will not remember tonight.
But he lay awake for a long time anyway, with his list folded small against his neck, listening to the rain, and trying to hear a note that was not there.
