Chapter 70: Onto the Glass, Part 2 of 5: While They Can't
While They Can't
The ticking stopped a little after that.
Corin did not notice it at first. He noticed the space where it had been. The way you notice a clock has stopped in a room, not when it stops, but some time after, when you look up and the silence has been there for a while without your knowing.
He stopped walking and turned round.
The Edge was a long way behind them now. A pale line in the dark, low down, where the snow lay on the gravel. Above it the long dark slope going up, and the ridge, and over the ridge the faint red smear in the sky where Last Thorn was still burning. Very small. Very far. Like the glow of a pipe in a dark room.
And on the pale line of the Edge, three shapes.
He could not see them well. He could see only that they were there, where there had been nothing before. Three dark tall shapes, standing in a row on the last of the snow, very still. As if they had come down the slope to the very edge of the Glass, and stopped there, and were looking out after them.
They were not moving.
He watched them for a long time. They did not move.
The others had stopped too. He heard their boots go quiet, one by one, in front of him. Then Kestrel's voice, low, from up ahead.
"They have stepped onto it," she said. "Look at the feet."
Corin looked. He could not see feet. He could see only the three shapes against the pale line. But now that she said it, he thought that perhaps they were not standing on the snow. Perhaps they were a little way out from it. A step, or two steps. On the black.
"And now they can't step off it again," said Kestrel. "Not till the sun comes up."
She came walking back toward them, along the loose line of the walkers, with her spear over her shoulder. Her boots knocked on the Glass. She stopped beside Corin and looked back at the Edge with him.
"At night the Glass goes cold on top," she said. "Not cold like snow. Cold like the inside of a stone. The warm stays down deep, and the top goes hard and still. And anything iron that stands on it, the cold gets into. It gets into the joints first. Then the wheels. The little teeth." She held up her hand and closed it, slowly, into a fist. "Then they cannot move. They freeze. They stand where they are, all night, like old dead trees. And in the morning when the sun comes up and the top goes warm, they wake up again. And they walk."
Corin looked at the three shapes. Standing in a row. Not moving.
"How do you know?" said Lyra.
"Because our grandmothers' grandmothers watched the gray men try," said Kestrel. "When the Glass was new. When the war was done, and the gray men came north with their iron things to see what was left. Old Hask told it. They brought iron out onto the Glass in the day, and it worked. And they made camp. And in the night it froze where it stood, and in the morning it would not move for a long time, and some of it never moved again. They left it there. Some of it is still out there." She tipped her head north. "You will see."
She turned back to the north. And looked up. At the sky.
"So this is the rule," she said. "We walk while they can't. We rest while they can. Every night we walk from dark to dawn. Every day we lie down and hide from the sun and wait. And every night they stand still, and we get further away. And every day they walk after us. And if we are quick, and if we are lucky, they never catch up." She looked at Corin. "Can you walk all night, harvester?"
"Yes," said Corin.
He did not know if it was true.
"Good," said Kestrel. "Because we must."
She went back up the line. The others started walking again. Knock. Knock.
Corin looked back at the three shapes on the Edge once more. Then he turned his back on them.
And it was then that he really saw the stars.
He had seen them before, of course. He had seen them lying in the Glass from the Edge, and from the ridge, and from under the last tree. But he had seen them from outside. Now he was in them.
They were above him. All the stars of the north, thick and white and close, more than he had ever seen in the sky over any city. The great pale band of the river of stars going across from one side of the world to the other, like spilled milk. The bright ones and the faint ones and the ones so faint you could only see them if you did not look at them straight.
And they were below him. All the same stars, lying in the black, every one of them. The pale band going across under his feet. The bright ones and the faint ones. The Glass was so smooth and so dark that it held them perfectly. There was no line where the sky stopped and the Glass began. Out at the edges of the world, in every direction, the stars above came down and met the stars below, and it was all one. One great round bowl of stars, and the ten of them walking across the middle of it on nothing.
Corin's head swam.
He put out a hand. Not to anyone. Just out. The way you do when you feel you are going to fall. And Lyra was there, and she caught his wrist, and held it.
"Don't look down," she said. Very quietly. "Not for long. It's like looking down from a high place. Your feet start to think they're falling."
"Have you done this before?"
"No." She was looking down herself. He could see the stars in her eyes. "But I've stood on the top of a very tall house at night. On the roof. And looked down at the lamps in the street. It's like that. A bit. You want to go to them."
He did not ask whose house. He thought he might have known once.
She let go of his wrist. They walked on.
He did not look down for long, after that. He looked at the backs in front of him. Kestrel, far ahead, the white fur of her cape pale in the dark. Arran's long thin shape, and his plait swinging. Marrit, slow and heavy and steady. Oda, quick. Sef. Brin, walking like a man on a frozen pond. They went in a loose line, a few steps apart, each one following the one in front. If you looked at the backs, and not at the stars, your feet stayed on the ground.
The warmth came up through his boots. The wind came down from the north, small and dry. It smelled of nothing at all. Not of snow, not of earth, not of smoke. Of nothing. Like the air in a jar that has been sealed for a very long time.
And behind them, very far now, three dark shapes stood on the Edge in a row, and did not move, and did not tick, and waited for the sun.
The First Cairn
Marrit found the first cairn.
Corin had wondered how they would find anything at all, out here. There was nothing to find. There was black glass, and stars, and the wind. No path. No tree. No hill. Nothing that stuck up. Nothing to say this way or not that way. Kestrel walked as if she knew. But how could she know? It was all the same, in every direction, the same flat black shine going out to the edge of the stars.
Then, perhaps two hours out from the Edge, Marrit stopped.
She was walking second in the line, just behind Kestrel, slow and heavy in her furs, leaning on her long pole. She stopped. And put the end of the pole down on the Glass, knock, and stood there with her old gray-lined face turned a little to the left. As if she were listening.
Kestrel stopped too. She did not ask why. She waited.
The line came up behind them and stopped, one by one.
Marrit lifted her pole and pointed. Left, and a little ahead.
Corin looked. He saw nothing.
Then he did.
A small dark lump on the black, a long way off. No bigger than his thumbnail held out at arm's length. Darker than the dark around it, because it did not shine. Everything else out here shone. The Glass held the stars. This did not. It was a hole in the stars, very small, low down. And it had a shape. A rough round shape, a little higher than it was wide, with something on top.
Marrit said something in the round speech. Kestrel answered. Then Marrit turned her pole a little, so that it pointed not quite at the lump but a hand's width to its right. And began to walk that way.
They all followed.
It took a long time to reach it. Things out here were further away than they looked. There was nothing in between to measure by. The lump got bigger very slowly, the way the moon gets bigger when it comes up over a hill. Then all at once it was close, and it was a pile of stones.
A cairn. Higher than Corin's waist. Made of round gray river stones, the kind you would find at the bottom of a stream, piled carefully one on another into a rough fat tower. The stones at the bottom were big, as big as a man's head. The ones at the top were small. And on the very top, there was a flat stone, laid across like a hat, and on the flat stone a smaller one standing up on its end, long and thin, like a finger.
The finger pointed north. A little east of north.
"River stones," said Vespera, behind him. She sounded surprised. "There are no rivers out here."
"There were," said Kestrel. "Before. They carried them. Out from the Edge. A few each time, in their packs. Every walker who came this way, for years and years, carried a stone out and put it on." She put her hand on the top of the cairn, very gently, on the flat hat stone. "Old Hask carried three. When he was young. He told this one which ones."
She did not say which ones. She took her hand away.
Marrit had come up to the cairn on the other side. She did not touch it. She stood and looked at it for a long time, with her pole in her hand. Then she said something to Kestrel. Low and slow. And Kestrel listened, and nodded, and looked at Corin.
"Marrit says she has walked past this one before," said Kestrel. "When she was younger. Out past this one, and the next, and the next, as far as the fourth. Looking for her son. Who went over the Glass one autumn and didn't come back." A pause. "Not to bring him back. To look for him. And to put a stone on, for him, wherever she stopped."
"Did she find him?" said Lyra.
Kestrel asked. Marrit answered. A short answer.
"No," said Kestrel. "She sat at the fourth cairn for three days, and the Glass gave her nothing. Then she walked home." Kestrel's voice did not change. "The Glass keeps things. Sometimes the walkers find them. Sitting, facing north, with their hands in their laps. Very dry. Sometimes they don't." She pointed to the cairn. To one of the middle stones. A gray one with a white line round it, like a belt. "That one she put on, on the way out. For luck. It didn't work."
Nobody said anything.
Marrit bent down. Slowly, because she was old and her furs were heavy. And took something from the pouch at her belt. A stone. A small gray river stone, round and smooth, no bigger than a hen's egg. She held it in her hand for a moment, as if to warm it. Then she reached up and set it on the cairn, on a little ledge between two others, very carefully, so that it would not fall.
She said a name. One word. Corin did not catch it.
Then she straightened up, and took her pole, and stood back.
"For Ennis," said Arran quietly, beside Corin. "Who went back for the goats."
Corin remembered. The old goat-man, on the night of the fire. Who had gone back into the smoke with his stick when the pen broke, and had not come out again. There had been no one to breathe him in. There had been nothing left to breathe.
One by one the Ashborn went up to the cairn. Kestrel. Arran. Oda. Sef. Brin. Each of them had a stone, from a pocket, from a pouch, from inside a fur. Each of them set it on the cairn, and said a name. Different names. Corin did not know any of them. Seven stones went on the cairn. Eight. More than he could feel. Then they were done.
Kestrel looked at the four from the south.
"You do not have to," she said. "You don't have stones. And these are not your dead."
Oren bent down.
Corin did not see what he was doing at first. The big man went down on one knee on the Glass, by the foot of the cairn, and felt about in his coat. Then he took something out of an inside pocket. Small and dark. He held it up to the starlight and looked at it, turning it over. Then he reached out and set it at the foot of the cairn, on the Glass, against the bottom stones.
It was a button. A brass army button, old and worn almost smooth, with a ring on the back where the thread had gone through.
"My sergeant's," said Oren. He did not get up straight away. He stayed on one knee, looking at it. "Kept it thirty years. Don't know why. He'd have laughed at me." He got up. His knee cracked. "There. Now he's somewhere."
Kestrel looked at the button for a long moment.
Then she nodded. Once. And touched two fingers to the gray line on her face, forehead to nose. To Oren.
Oren did not know what to do with that. He went red under his beard. He nodded back, stiff, like a soldier.
They walked on.
Marrit pointed the way. North, and a little east, the way the finger stone pointed. And they followed her out into the stars again. And behind them the cairn got smaller and smaller and then was gone, a small dark hole in the shine, and then nothing.
"How far to the next one?" said Corin.
"One night," said Kestrel. "If we're quick. Two if we're not." She did not look back. "If we miss it, we sit down and wait for light. That's the rule. You do not walk on, if you've lost the stones. You sit. You never walk on."
"Why?"
"Because the ones who walk on," said Kestrel, "are the ones Marrit came out here looking for."
The Voice
The quiet got into all of them, after the cairn.
Nobody talked. There was nothing to say, and the Glass did not want you to say it. It was hard to explain. The quiet out here was not like the quiet of a room, or a field, or a sleeping street. It was bigger than that. It went all the way out to the edge of the stars and all the way down into the black under their feet, and the ten of them were very small in the middle of it, and every sound they made seemed too loud. The knock of a boot. A cough. The slosh of water in a skin. Each one went out into the quiet and did not come back. There was nothing for it to bounce off.
Corin found that he was walking with his shoulders up round his ears.
He made himself put them down.
They came up again.
And in his head, nothing. Still nothing. The warm place with the furs and the low fire, and the dent in the furs, and nobody in it.
He began to talk.
He did not mean to. It came out of him the way water comes out of a cracked jug. Not loud. Under his breath. The way you talk to yourself when you are alone in a house and doing something with your hands.
"One," he said. "Two. Three. Four."
Counting his steps. Out loud, now, since he could not count them inside.
"Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine."
And then the gray place. The blur. He stopped. Started again.
"One. Two."
He was aware of Lyra beside him. She had not moved away. She was walking at his left shoulder, a little behind, where she had been since the Edge. He could hear her breathing. He could hear her listening.
"Three. Four. Five."
He did not want to stop. If he stopped, there would be the quiet again. The big quiet outside and the small dead quiet inside, and nothing between them but his own skin.
"Six. Seven."
"Eight," said Lyra.
He stopped.
Not walking. Counting. His feet went on.
"Nine," said Lyra. Very low. In the same voice he had been using, the voice you use to yourself. "Ten."
He did not feel ten. It went by him, a blur. But he heard it. Her voice, saying it.
"Eleven," said Lyra. "Twelve."
He walked. He listened. He did not try to feel the numbers. He just let her voice say them, one after another, steady, in time with their feet, the way a drummer keeps time on a march. Not looking at him. Looking ahead. Saying the numbers into the dark as if they were the most ordinary thing in the world.
She got to a number that he heard as just a long word. Then she stopped.
"One," she said.
And started again.
"Why did you stop?" he said.
"That's a hundred." She did not look at him. "You count to a hundred and then you start again. It's how my mother taught us. When we couldn't sleep. You count to a hundred, and then you start again, and somewhere in the second hundred you're asleep."
"I'm not trying to sleep."
"I know." A pause. "Two. Three."
He let her count. It was better than the quiet. It was much better. It was a voice, out here, in the big black bowl of stars. A small human voice going on and on, steady, saying ordinary things.
And he found, after a while, that he was not listening to the numbers at all. He was listening to her.
To the voice.
He knew it. He had known it, he thought, before he knew anything else about her. When everything else was gone, when he had lost her face and her name and all the rooms she had been in, there had still been this. A voice saying his name. The rope he had held on to, in Vespera's jar, in the spring night with the rain on the thatch. Corin. Corin. Corin. Three times, and he had heard all three, and let go because it was her.
It was the same voice. Saying numbers now. Saying nothing that mattered. But the same.
"Sixteen. Seventeen."
It was a low voice, for a woman. A little rough at the edges. Not soft. She did not make it soft for him, the way people make their voices soft for the sick. She said the numbers flatly, plainly, like someone counting coins on a counter.
But there was something under it. He could not have said what. A kind of warmth, low down. Like the warmth of the Glass coming up through his boots. Not hot. Not on the top. Deep down. Patient.
And under that, so quiet he had to stop breathing to hear it, the hum.
She was humming. Under the numbers. She did not know it. Or perhaps she did. A small tuneless sound, low in her throat, between one number and the next, like a cat purring in another room.
If it stops, she had said, you're frightened.
It had not stopped.
Out here, on the black glass, in the dark, with three iron things standing frozen behind them and a whole night of nothing ahead, Lyra was not frightened. Or not enough to stop humming.
He held on to that.
He held on to it the way he had held on to her voice in the jar. Not with his hands. With whatever it is in you that holds on.
"Thirty-one. Thirty-two."
"Lyra."
"Thirty-three." She did not stop. "What?"
He did not know what. He had said her name just to say it. To hear it go out of his own mouth into the quiet and not be lost.
"Nothing," he said.
"Thirty-four."
They walked. Up ahead, far ahead, Kestrel's white cape swung in the dark. The finger stone was long behind them. The next cairn was a night away.
And Lyra counted. And hummed. And Corin listened. And the quiet outside stayed big and black and full of stars, but the quiet inside him was not quite so empty anymore. There was a voice in it now. Not the old dry voice. A different one. Low, and rough at the edges, and counting.
It would do.
For now, it would have to do.
