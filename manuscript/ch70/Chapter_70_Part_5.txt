Chapter 70: Onto the Glass, Part 5 of 5: Feet
Feet
Vespera did their feet before the dark came.
She went from shade to shade on her knees, small and bent, with her needle roll and her pots and her bird scissors, and she made every one of them take off the felt and the boots and show her. She did not ask. She just held out her blue hand, palm up, and waited, until whoever it was gave up and put a foot in it.
"Feet are how you die out here," she said, when Oren grumbled. "Not iron. Not thirst. Feet. A blister goes bad, the foot swells, you can't get your boot on, you walk on it anyway, you fall behind. Then you're gone. Give it here."
Oren gave it here.
His feet were big and square and hard as old leather, with thick yellow heels and a long white scar across the top of the left one. Vespera looked at them for a long moment, turning them this way and that in the gold light under the hide.
"Saints," she said. "You could walk across this on those without boots."
"Thirty years of marching," said Oren. He sounded pleased. "Nothing gets through them."
"Something will, one day. Put them away. They smell like a dead goat."
Brin's feet were all right. Brin's shoulder was not. She made him sit up and take the sling off, and she felt all round the joint with her small hard fingers while he looked at the hide over his head and breathed through his nose. It was swollen. It was dark with bruising, purple and green, from the top of the arm up over the shoulder and across toward his neck. But it was in. Oren had put it back at the Edge with his boot in Brin's armpit and one long hard pull, and Brin had screamed once and then said a word in the round speech that made Arran laugh even with the town burning behind them. It had stayed in.
"No spear," said Vespera. "Not for a week. Not even to lean on." She bound it up again, tighter, and pinned the sling with a bone pin. "If anything comes at you, you run. You understand run?"
Brin looked at Arran. Arran said something. Brin laughed and nodded.
"He says he understands run," said Arran. "He says he's very good at it."
Oda would not let her look. She sat at the end of the far shade with her bone spear across her knees and her boots on and her face shut, and when Vespera held out her hand, Oda looked at it and did not move. Vespera held it out for a long time. Oda did not move. In the end, Marrit leaned over and said something to Oda, quite short, quite quiet, and Oda went red under the gray line on her face and pulled her boots off and put her feet out without a word.
They were bad. Both heels were rubbed raw, and there was a blister on the side of the right foot as big as a hen's egg, gone dark and tight.
"How long have you been walking on this?" said Vespera.
Oda did not answer.
"Since the Edge," said Arran. "She would not say. She is like that. She thinks if she says she is hurt, Kestrel will send her back."
"We can't send anyone back," said Kestrel, from the mouth of the shade. She had come down off the swell at last. "There is no back."
Vespera lanced the blister with her finest needle, quick, and pressed the water out of it with a pad of gauze, and Oda did not make a sound. Then she smeared all of it, the blister and the two raw heels, with salve out of a small clay pot. The burnt-honey-and-pine one, the same one she had used on Corin's ear and arm. And bound it, and put the felt back on, wound a different way, so that it did not rub in the same place.
Oda looked at her feet. Then at Vespera. She said one word.
"She says thank you," said Arran.
"She can say it in a week, if she's still walking," said Vespera. But she patted Oda's ankle, once, before she let it go.
Sef's feet were all right. He let her look without a fuss, and put them away again, and went back to sitting on his own at the north end of the last shade, looking out north across the Glass with his chin on his knees. He had not said much all day. Corin had not heard him say anything at all.
"The girl," said Corin to Arran, low. "At the Edge. With the baby. Who held on to him."
Arran looked at Sef's back for a moment.
"His sister," he said. "The baby is hers. Her man died in the hard cold, two winters ago. Sef carried him. Sef has carried her and the baby since." He turned his flute over in his long fingers. "She told him not to come. She told him the baby needs him more than Kestrel does. He told her he will come back." A pause. "That's what she said into his face. She said, You'd better."
Corin looked at Sef. At the singed ends of his hair, gone short and frizzled at the back where the fire had caught it. At the set of his thin shoulders.
He did not say anything. There was nothing to say.
Then it was his own turn.
"Boots," said Vespera, and held out her hand.
He took them off. The felt came away damp and gray. And the right heel was raw. He had known it would be. He had felt it all through the night and the morning, a hot sore spot that came at every step. The skin had gone, in a place the size of a thumbnail, and it was red and wet.
Vespera clicked her tongue.
She did not use the honey-and-pine salve. She reached past it into his own pack, which was lying by his head, and felt about in it, and came out with a small brown pot with a cloth tied over the top. He kept it at the top, where his hand could find it in the dark.
"Goose fat and yarrow," said Vespera. "From the big woman at Thistle Ford. Hild. For your leg. Change it every night, she said." She untied the cloth. It smelled of the byre, and of green things, and of a warm kitchen a long way south. "And have you?"
He had. Most nights, anyway. The wound on his leg was closing at last, a long pink seam with a dark crust at one end, and the hot angry red round it had gone. It still ached with every step. It would for a while yet, Silas had said. A month with the stick. But it was not going bad again.
"Good," said Vespera. "Then it can do for heels too." She dipped two fingers in the fat and spread it over the raw place, gently. It was cold, and then not cold, and then it did not hurt so much. "There. Now you've got a bit of Thistle Ford on your foot. That's more than most people take out of a place."
She tied the cloth back on the pot and put it back in his pack.
Then she sat back on her heels and looked at him. At his face.
"You laughed," she said. "Before. With her. I heard you."
"Yes."
"Good." She began to roll up her needle roll, slowly, tying the strings. "Do it again. Out here, you'll need to." She tucked the roll into her belt. "A laugh is a kind of giving, harvester. The Glass doesn't mind that."
First Camp
The sun went down in the west, and it went down twice, the way it had come up.
Corin watched it from the mouth of the shade with the hide pushed up off his eyes. The sun touching the rim of the world. The other sun, the one in the Glass, rising up to meet it. The two of them touching, and flattening against each other, and running together into one long red bar of fire along the edge of everything. Then the bar getting thinner. And thinner. And going out, like a coal when you blow on it the wrong way.
And the color went out of the Glass, the way it had come in. Gold to red. Red to a dull purple, like a plum. Purple to gray. Gray to the deep ink-blue, sky and Glass both. And then the first stars, in the sky, and in the Glass, at the same moment, pricking out one by one.
The heat went out too. Not at once. Slowly. The Glass gave it back to the sky, all the heat of the long day, and for an hour after sunset the air over it shook in the dark, and you could feel it coming up off the black on your face, like the heat off a loaf just out of the oven.
Then the cold came.
It came down out of the north on the small dry wind. And it came up out of the Glass, which was strange, because the Glass was still warm deep down. Corin could feel it was. But the top of it went cold, as Kestrel had said it would. Cold like the inside of a stone. In the time it took to roll up the felt, the Glass under his hand went from warm to cool. In the time it took to take down the shades and pack the poles, it went from cool to cold.
And far behind them, the ticking stopped.
He did not feel it stop. Kestrel did. She had her hand on the Glass, at the edge of where the shades had been, the last thing before they went. She held it there for a long breath. Then she took it away, and stood up, and nodded.
"Now," she said.
They ate first. Standing up. Bread and fat and the dried brown curls. Four mouthfuls of water each, with Kestrel watching. Then they walked.
They walked all the first part of the night. Corin did not know how long. It was like the first night, and not like it. The same doubled stars. The same knock of boots, the same small dry wind. But his legs were heavy now in a way they had not been. The muscles at the backs of them were tight and sore from the hard flat walking, which used the same few muscles over and over, step after step, with never a stone or a root or a rise to change it. And his feet hurt. The felt had rubbed. Even with Hild's goose fat on it, the raw place on his right heel came back at every step.
Lyra counted for him again. He did not ask her to. She just began, a little after dark, low, beside him. One. Two. Three. And he let her. And somewhere in the third hundred, or the fifth, he stopped hearing the numbers and only heard the voice, and the hum under it.
They did not find a cairn. Kestrel did not seem to expect one. Marrit walked at the front with her pole, now and then stopping and turning her old face this way and that, as if smelling the wind, and then going on.
And at some time deep in the night, when the cold had got into Corin's fingers and his face and the tip of his nose, and the stars had turned a long way round, Kestrel stopped and said, "Here."
There was nothing to say why here. It was the same as everywhere. Black glass and stars.
"Second hour after midnight," said Kestrel. "About. We rest now till the fourth. Then we walk to dawn." She was already pulling her pack off. "Not long. But you'll need it. The cold out here at the bottom of the night will kill you if you walk through it tired. You stumble. You sit down. You don't get up."
The Ashborn knew what to do. They did it fast.
They laid out the felt again. All of it. Every piece they had, overlapping, in one wide gray patch on the Glass. Then the hides from the shades, on top of the felt, fur side up where there was fur. Then they took the heat stones out of their packs.
Corin had carried his all day and all night, wrapped in its felt, at the bottom of his pack, against his back. A round dark river stone the size of a small loaf. It had been hot as a coal when Arran gave it to him at the Edge, fresh from the last fire of Last Thorn. It had been warm all through the day. He took it out now and unwrapped it, and it was still warm. Not hot. Warm, like a sleeping cat.
"Middle," said Arran. "All of them. In the middle."
They put the stones in the middle of the felt. Ten of them, in a close ring, still in half their wrappings. Then they lay down round them.
Not in a row. In a heap.
Corin had never slept like that. He did not think he had. You lay down on your side with your back against someone's back and your front against someone's front, and your feet tucked in among the stones, and someone's head on your arm, and someone's knees in the backs of your legs. Every one of you touching two others, or three. And then the blankets went over all of you, and the last of the hides, and the furs, everything, pulled up over your heads, so that you were all of you under one great heavy pile in the dark, breathing each other's breath.
It smelled of goat and sweat and smoke and hide. It was very close. Somebody's hair was in his mouth.
And it was warm.
Lyra was against his back. He knew her by her breathing. He did not know who was against his front, in the dark, until they spoke, and then he knew it was Oda, because Oda said something sharp in the round speech and moved her elbow out of his ribs. Somewhere past Oda, Oren was already snoring. Somewhere past Lyra, Vespera said, "If anybody puts their cold feet on me, I'll stitch their eyelids shut," and Arran laughed, the short bark, under the furs.
Then they were all still.
Corin lay awake.
He could not help it. He was too tired to sleep. And his face was near the edge of the heap, where the furs did not quite meet the felt, and through the gap he could see out.
Out over the Glass. Black, and still, and full of stars. Going away to the edge of the world. Lying a hand's width from his eyes.
And then he saw the lights.
The Lights Below
They were under the Glass.
That was the first thing he understood. Not on it. Not above it, like the stars, or lying in it, like the reflections of the stars. Under it. Inside it. Down in the black, a long way down, the way a fish is down in a deep pond when you lean over the side of a boat and look.
Small pale lights.
He had seen them before. From the last tree on the ridge, on the night Kestrel told the story of the child and the old man. Far off, out on the Glass, faint drifting lights, like the ghosts of stars. The lost ones, Kestrel had called them. He had looked at them then from a long way away, and thought they were something in his eyes. A trick of the dark.
They were not a trick.
There was one quite close. A short stone's throw from the edge of the heap, out on the Glass. Perhaps a man's height down. He could not tell. Distance did strange things, out here. But it was close enough that he could see it clearly, through the gap in the furs, lying with his cheek on the felt and the cold coming in round his face.
It was the size of a hand. Or a little smaller. A soft pale blur of light, not white, not quite. A little gray. A little blue. Like the light of a candle seen through a sheet of paper. It had no edges. It was brightest in the middle, and then it faded out, and you could not say where it stopped.
And it was moving.
Very slowly. Drifting. The way a leaf drifts on the slow part of a river, where the water hardly moves at all. Turning a little, as it went. Going nowhere, as far as he could see. Just drifting, down there in the black, all on its own.
Then he saw another. Further off. And another, beyond that. And once he had seen three, he saw that there were many. A great many. More than he could feel. Spread out under the whole of the Glass as far as he could see from the gap in the furs, at every depth, near and far, small and faint and drifting. Some alone. Some in twos, or threes, drifting close together, as if they were keeping each other company. One, very far off and very deep, so faint he could only see it when he did not look at it, that was larger than the rest, and moved more slowly, and seemed to have a kind of shape to it. Long. Like a person lying down.
He did not breathe.
It was like looking down into deep water at night and seeing a great slow shoal of fish. Pale fish, in black water. Not darting. Not feeding. Only drifting, and turning, in the deep cold under the Glass, where the warmth was.
Ash-ghosts.
He knew the word. He did not know where from. Somewhere in the Harvesting Section, perhaps. Some old clerk's joke. Some line in a book. Ash-ghost: what is left of a burned mage when there is nobody there to harvest them. The Ash that does not settle. That goes on drifting, with nothing to hold it, until it fades.
But these had not faded.
He thought of the war. Of the war he could not remember, that nobody could remember, that had been folded and wiped and written over until there was nothing left of it but a sheet of black glass the size of a country. And Sola Vance in the middle of it, with the light coming out of her chest. And all the people who had been here, on this land, when the light came. Soldiers. Mages. People who had lived in the houses that had melted, and walked on the roads that had melted, and farmed the fields that were now this smooth black floor under his cheek.
All of them, burned at once. In one breath. With nobody to breathe them in.
And the Glass had closed over them. And kept them. The Glass keeps things, Kestrel had said, about Marrit's mother's brother. He was very dry.
It kept them. It did not let them be taken. It did not let them burn again. It just held them, down there in the warm dark, drifting.
For sixty years.
Corin felt something go through him that he did not have a name for. It was not fear. It was not quite grief. It was what you feel at a graveside when the grave is so old that nobody knows whose it is anymore. When the stone has worn smooth, and the name has gone, and there is only a hump in the grass and the knowledge that someone is under it. Someone who was loved. Someone who had a name.
He thought of the handprints on the tents at Last Thorn. One for each one carried.
Nobody had carried these.
Nobody had breathed them in. Nobody had pressed their hands in ash on a tent wall. Nobody had said their names. They had simply been here, when the light came, and then they had been under the Glass, and they had been drifting there ever since, alone, in the dark, while the summers laid their thin glaze on the top and the walkers went over and never looked down.
Or looked down, and called them the lost ones, and walked on faster.
He did not walk on.
He could not have walked anywhere. He was lying in the heap with Lyra's back against his and Oda's breath on his face and his feet among the warm stones, and every muscle in him heavy as wet sand. But he did not look away, either. He lay there, with his eyes open, and watched them.
The close one, a stone's throw off, was coming closer.
Not fast. It did not seem to be coming on purpose. It was only drifting, the way it had been drifting, turning slowly. But it was drifting this way. He watched it come. A step closer. Two. It rose, a little, as it came. Up out of the deep black, toward the top. He could see it better. The pale soft heart of it. The way the light seemed to breathe, a little, brighter and fainter, brighter and fainter, very slowly. Like something asleep.
It came to the edge of the felt and stopped there, for a moment, just beyond it. Just under the gap in the furs.
And Corin, without knowing he was going to do it, without thinking at all, pushed his left hand out through the gap. Out from under the furs. Into the cold.
And laid it flat on the Glass.
One Stays
The Glass was cold under his palm.
Not the bite of the noon heat. The other thing. The deep stone cold of the top of the Glass at night, that froze iron where it stood. It went into his gray palm, and his gray palm hardly felt it. Only a kind of dull ache, far off, like the ache in a tooth that has been dead a long time. His black fingertips felt nothing at all.
He lay there with his hand on the Glass and his cheek on the felt, and watched the light.
It was right under his hand now. Or nearly. A little to the side, under the heel of his thumb. And a long way down still. He could see it through the black between his spread fingers, a soft pale glow, like a lamp at the bottom of a well.
It was rising.
Very slowly. So slowly that at first he thought he was dreaming it. That his tired eyes were playing tricks. But no. It was coming up. Up through the black. The pale heart of it getting clearer, and brighter, and larger, the way a lantern does when someone carries it up a cellar stair toward you. Turning a little as it came. Breathing. Brighter, fainter. Brighter, fainter.
It moved under his hand.
Out from under the heel of the thumb, across, until it was under the middle of his palm. Right under it. Exactly. As if it had been looking for that place, and found it.
And it stopped.
It was close now. Very close. Just under the top of the Glass. He could have measured the black between it and his palm with the width of a finger. It lay there, under his hand, a soft round glow the size of his own palm, gray-blue and pale, and it lit his gray fingers from below so that he could see the lines on them. The creases at the knuckles. The black tips. The old pale scar across the back of the first finger, that he did not know the story of.
And it did not sink again.
He waited for it to. He waited for it to turn, the way the others turned, and drift away, and go down into the deep dark, and be lost. That was what they did. He had watched them do it for a long time now. They drifted, and they turned, and they went.
This one did not go.
It stayed.
It lay there under his palm, breathing its slow pale light, brighter and fainter, and it did not move. Not away. Not down. As if it were content. As if his hand were a roof over it, and it had come in out of the cold.
As if it knew him.
He did not know how long he lay there. The stars turned. The cold came in round his face and into his ear and down his neck. Somebody in the heap muttered in their sleep and shifted, and the furs moved, and settled. Lyra's back rose and fell against his. Oren snored, a long way off, on the other side of the world.
And the light stayed.
He thought he ought to feel something from it. He was a harvester. He had spent his whole working life, all the years he could remember and some he could not, feeling for what was left in burnt-out things. Putting his hand on a jar, and feeling the shape inside. A face. A room. A voice. He knew what Ash felt like, under his hand. A cold that was not cold. A kind of pull, like a current in water, drawing you in.
He felt none of that.
The Glass did not allow it. Nothing could be taken here. And he was not trying to take. He was only lying with his hand on the cold black, the way you lie with your hand on a sleeping dog.
But he did feel something.
Not from the light. In himself. Low down. A warmth. Very small. The same small warmth that had been left in his chest that afternoon, when he laughed at the story of the spoon. The same warmth, he thought, that he had heard under Lyra's voice when she counted. Deep. Patient. Like the warmth the Glass kept, down where the lights drifted.
And something else. So faint he almost did not know it was there. In the place in him where the old man had sat. The warm dim place with the furs and the low fire, and the dent in the furs.
Not a voice. Not a word.
Only a kind of—
Attention.
The way a sleeping dog lifts its head, just a little, when someone it knows comes into the room. And then puts it down again.
Then it was gone. If it had ever been there.
Corin lay very still.
And from the dark behind him, from the other side of the heap, low, so low he would not have heard it if the night had not been so quiet, a voice said:
"They don't do that."
Kestrel.
He had not known she was awake. He did not turn his head. He could not have, without moving his hand, and he did not want to move his hand.
She did not say anything else. He heard her breathing, behind Lyra, behind Vespera, at the far side of the heap. Not the slow breathing of sleep. The breathing of someone lying very still, with their eyes open, looking at something they did not understand.
They don't do that.
The light under his palm breathed. Brighter. Fainter. Brighter.
And stayed.
