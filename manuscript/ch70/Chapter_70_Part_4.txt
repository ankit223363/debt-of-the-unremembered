Chapter 70: Onto the Glass, Part 4 of 5: Noon
Noon
The place Kestrel made was a hollow between two swells, where the Glass dipped down into a long shallow trough like the bottom of a wave. There was nothing in it. No shade. No rock. Only the same black-brown shine, and the heat standing up off it.
But it was low. And Kestrel said that was enough.
"Wind goes over," she said. "Eyes go over. Down here, from far off, we are not even a dot."
The Ashborn made the shades.
Corin watched them do it, because he was too tired to help, and because they did not let him. Arran and Sef had carried the poles, rolled up in a long bundle on Sef's back. Thin bone poles, pale and smooth, as long as a man is tall, each one made of three pieces that slotted together end to end. They fitted them together fast, without looking, the way a woman threads a needle she has threaded ten thousand times. Then they stood them up on the Glass.
That was the clever part. You could not dig a hole in the Glass. You could not drive in a peg. So each pole stood in a little round foot. A flat leather bag the size of a plate, filled with river sand. You set the pole in the middle of the bag and leaned it, and the weight of the sand held it, and the sand did not slip on the Glass the way wood or bone would have.
Over the poles went the hides. Big pale hides, scraped thin, sewn together in long strips. Not to keep out rain. There was no rain. To keep out the sky. They pulled them tight over the poles and tied them off at the corners to more bags of sand, so that in a few moments there were three long low shades in the bottom of the trough, like the roofs of tents with no walls. Open on the north side and the south. Low. You had to crawl under.
And under them, on the Glass, they laid out felt. Thick gray felt, folded double. To lie on.
"Never lie on the bare Glass in the day," said Kestrel. "Never. Not for a breath. You think it's not so hot. You lie down. You go to sleep. And when you wake, the side you lay on is cooked like meat."
Corin crawled in under the middle shade and lay down on the felt.
The heat was still there. It came up through the felt from below, steady, like lying on the floor above a bakery. But the sun was gone. That was everything. The hide over him glowed pale gold with the light coming through it, and the air under it was hot and still and smelled of goat and old smoke and sweat. But it was not the glare. The glare was outside, beyond the edges of the shade, white and shaking. He could see the air shake, out there. Rippling over the Glass the way the air ripples over a road in summer, so that the far side of the trough seemed to be underwater.
He took off the eye hide. His eyes hurt. He shut them.
They drank. Kestrel came round with her own water skin and watched each of them drink, and stopped each of them after four mouthfuls with her hand on the skin. Not five. Four. Corin did not count them. He knew it was four because Oren, under the next shade, said so, and swore about it, quietly, by the Saints.
They ate. A little. Flat dark Ashborn bread, from the racks of Last Thorn, gone hard and dry already. A piece each, the size of a palm. A smear of goat fat on it. Some of the dried brown curls that the Ashborn chewed, that tasted of burnt honey and made your mouth less dry. Nobody talked much.
Then Vespera crawled in under his shade.
She did not say anything. She sat down on the felt beside him, cross-legged, small and gray and bent under the low hide, with her blue hands in her lap. And looked at him.
"Arms," she said.
He sat up. He did not argue. He pushed his left sleeve up, and then his right.
The gray was there. Of course it was there. It went up both his arms from the black tips of his fingers, over the backs of his hands, over his wrists, up the forearms, smooth and cold and the color of old ash. Over the elbows. Up the upper arms. And then stopped. A hand's width below the shoulder, less a finger. A clean line, like the line of the snow at the Edge. Gray below. Skin above. Pale and freckled and ordinary.
Vespera took a thread from her rope belt. A length of plain linen thread, with a knot in it. She laid the end of it against the bone at the top of his left shoulder and drew it down along his arm to the gray line. And looked at where the knot came. And frowned.
She did the same on the right arm. And frowned again.
Then she took a needle from the roll at her belt, the finest one, and touched the point of it, very lightly, to the gray, just below the line. And then to the skin, just above.
"Feel that?"
"The top one," said Corin. "Not the bottom."
"Same as at the Edge." She put the needle back. "Same as at Last Thorn. Same as up on the Lip." She sat back. "It hasn't moved, harvester. Not a hair. The knot's where I tied it."
She looked out from under the shade. At the Glass going away, white and shaking, in the noon glare.
"Nothing burns here," she said. "Not even that."
Corin looked at his arms.
He had not known, until she said it, how frightened he had been. How every morning for days, since the fire at Last Thorn, since the Erasers, he had woken and pushed his sleeve up and looked, the way a man with a bad tooth feels for it first thing. Looking for the line to have climbed in the night. Looking for it at the shoulder. At the neck.
It had not climbed.
And it would not, out here. Not as long as he did not burn. And out here, he could not burn even if he wanted to. There was nothing to burn with. The Glass did not allow it.
It was the strangest thing. It was like being told you are safe because your hands have been tied.
"Sleep," said Vespera. She pushed his sleeves back down. "You look like a week-old corpse. Sleep. I'll wake you if anything ticks."
He lay down.
He did not think he would sleep. The heat. The light through the hide. The small sounds of the others shifting on the felt. Oren, under the next shade, beginning to snore. He lay on his back and looked up at the glowing gold of the hide, and thought about small footprints going south, and about a rider with his hat in his hand, and about nothing in his head where the old man should be.
Nothing.
No voice. No mutter. No sense of something watching, from the inside.
And he slept.
He did not know he had slept until he woke. And when he woke, he did not know where he was.
Not at all. It was not like waking in a strange inn and taking a moment to remember. He opened his eyes and there was gold light above him, and heat under him, and he did not know where he was, or why, or what day it was, or how he had got there. He did not know the smell. He did not know the light. For a long, long moment, lying there, he did not know anything at all except that he was lying down, and that he was alive.
It was the most peaceful moment he could remember.
Then a woman's voice said, close by, "Corin," and he knew it. And everything came back. All of it, all at once, the way water comes back into a footprint on a beach.
He sat up too fast and hit his head on the hide.
"Ow," he said.
Lyra was sitting at the open end of the shade with her knees drawn up. She was looking at him with an odd look on her face.
"You slept an hour," she said. "Maybe more."
"I didn't know where I was."
"I know. I saw your face." She looked away, out at the glare. "You looked happy."
Shut Up, Old Man
He crawled out to sit beside her at the open end of the shade, where the hide stopped and the glare began. Not in the glare. Just at the edge of it. With his knees up, like hers, and his back against one of the bone poles, which gave a little, and held.
Out beyond the shade the Glass shook in the heat. White. Blind. Going away to the north in long low swells, each one shimmering, each one seeming to float a little above the next, like the backs of great slow animals swimming. There was no sound. There was not even wind, now. The wind had died at noon. The air did not move. It lay on the Glass like a hand.
Under the next shade Oren was still snoring. A long slow rasp, in and out, like a saw going through wet wood. Under the far one, someone was talking in the round speech, low. Marrit, he thought. And Oda answering.
"What did you mean," he said. "I looked happy."
Lyra did not answer at once. She had a piece of the dark bread in her hand, and she was turning it over and over, not eating it.
"Just that," she said. "You looked like somebody who'd put something down."
He thought about that.
"He's not talking," he said. "The old man. Since the first step. He said two words and then nothing. I told Oren. I didn't tell you."
"I know. I heard you tell Oren." She broke a corner off the bread. Looked at it. Did not eat it. "I heard you counting, too. Before."
"You counted for me."
"Somebody had to. You kept stopping at nine. It was driving me mad."
He almost smiled.
Then she said, still looking at the bread, "You used to argue with him. Out loud."
He looked at her.
"With Malakor," she said. "Before. In the city. When it was just the two of us, in the room over Mrs. Marta's, and later on the road north. You'd be sitting there quite quietly, reading, or eating, or cleaning your rod. And then all of a sudden you'd say something. Out loud. To nobody." She broke off another corner. "Mostly it was rude."
"What did I say?"
"'Shut up, old man.'" She said it in a flat, cross voice, a man's voice, lower than her own. And he knew at once, with a strange cold drop in his stomach, that it was his voice. That she had got it exactly. "'Shut up, old man.' Just like that. In the middle of supper. And the first time you did it, I put my spoon down and looked at you, because I thought you meant me."
Corin stared at her.
"I thought you'd gone mad," said Lyra. "Or that you were very, very rude. I didn't know about him then. And I sat there, with my spoon, trying to think what I'd done. And you looked up, and saw my face, and went red to your ears. Red as a beet. And you said, 'Not you. Saints. Not you. There's an old man in my head and he won't stop talking about soup.'"
And Corin laughed.
It came out of him before he knew it was coming. Not a big laugh. A short, cracked, surprised sound, like a cough, that hurt his dry throat. But a laugh. The first one he could remember in days. In weeks. He did not know how long.
Soup. The old man, going on about soup. He could almost hear it. Linus never could make soup. Too much salt. Always too much salt. A man who can cut a rod like that and can't make soup—
He could almost hear it.
Almost.
The laugh went out of him as fast as it had come. But it left something behind it. A warmth in his chest. Like the warmth the Glass held, under its hard top. Low down.
"I don't remember that," he said. "I don't remember any of it."
"I know."
"The room. The supper. Your spoon." He shook his head. "Nothing."
"I know," she said again. Quieter.
"But it sounds like me. It sounds exactly like me."
"It was exactly like you."
He turned to look at her properly. And she was looking at him. Had been, he thought, all the time he was laughing. With that same odd look on her face she had had when he woke. Her eyes very bright. Her mouth pressed tight at the corners, the way a mouth goes when it is holding something in.
And when he looked at her, she looked away.
Quickly. Out at the glare. At the white shaking swells of the Glass going away north. As if she had seen something out there. As if there were anything out there to see.
He did not say anything.
He thought he knew what it was. What she had been holding in, while he laughed. He thought it was this: that she had told him a story about the two of them, a small foolish story about a spoon, and he had laughed at it the way he must have laughed at it then, red to the ears, in a room he could not remember. And for a moment she had had him back. The man in the room. All of him.
And then the laugh had stopped, and he had said, I don't remember.
She broke the last of the bread in two. And held one half out to him, without looking.
He took it.
They ate it, sitting side by side at the edge of the shade, looking out at the glare. It was very dry. It took a long time.
"Tell me another one," he said.
"No."
"Why not?"
"Because I'll cry," said Lyra, "and I've only got four mouthfuls of water, and I'm not wasting them on you."
He laughed again. Smaller. She did not look at him. But he saw the corner of her mouth move.
The Ticking Again
The afternoon came on, and the heat went past its worst, and began, very slowly, to ease.
Corin did not feel it ease. He felt only that it stopped getting worse. The air under the shade was still thick and hot and smelled of goat. The felt under him was still warm as a hearthstone. The glare outside was still white. But the light through the hide over his head had changed. It had been pale gold. It was going a deeper gold now, like honey. And the shadows of the bone poles on the felt had swung round, and were getting longer.
He lay on his side and watched the shadows. He could not sleep again. He did not want to. He was afraid, a little, of waking again and not knowing where he was. Of how good it had felt.
The others were waking, one by one. Oren's snoring stopped with a snort. Brin was sitting up under the far shade with his reset arm in his lap, rubbing the shoulder above the sling with his good hand, slowly, round and round. Oda was lying on her back with her bone spear across her chest, and her eyes open, looking at the hide. Marrit was asleep still, curled small, with her pole beside her and her old gray-lined face very peaceful.
Kestrel was not under any of the shades. Corin had not seen her go. She was out in the glare, on the top of the south swell, a tall shape with the hide over her eyes and her spear upright beside her, standing quite still. Looking back the way they had come.
She had been there, Arran said, most of the afternoon.
"She doesn't sleep in the day," said Arran. He was sitting at the mouth of the next shade with his bone flute in his hands, not playing it, turning it over. His burned eyebrows had begun to grow back, a little, as dark stubble. It made him look surprised all the time. "She says she'll sleep when we get to the other side."
"What's she looking for?"
Arran did not answer. He put the flute to his lips, and took it away again without playing, and looked south toward his sister.
And Kestrel turned round.
She did it quickly. Not the slow straight turn she used for walking. A quick sharp turn, the way a deer turns its head. And she came down off the top of the swell toward the shades, half running, with her spear held level, and her boots knocking on the hot Glass.
She did not say anything. She did not need to. She came in under the edge of the middle shade and crouched down there, breathing hard, with the sweat running down her shaved head and into the gray line of her scar. And she put one hand flat on the Glass at the edge of the felt. Just outside the shade. In the sun.
And held it there. Though it must have burned.
Corin understood.
He crawled to the edge of the felt and put his own hand beside hers. His gray hand. Flat on the Glass.
The heat bit at once. Even through the gray, even through the dead cold skin that felt so little, he felt it. Like putting your hand on a pan that has been on the fire. He nearly snatched it back. He did not.
And under the heat, through it, very faint—
Tick.
Not a sound. Not in his ears. In his hand. In the bones of his hand. Coming up through the Glass, the way the warmth had come up through his boots in the night. A tiny hard knock, very far off. And another.
Tick. Tick.
Steady. Even. Patient. Like a clock in another room. Like a clock in another house. Like a clock three streets away, that you would never hear at all except at night, with the whole city asleep, and your ear pressed to the wall.
Tick. Tick. Tick.
He took his hand away. It hurt. He held it against his chest.
Kestrel took hers away too. Her palm was red. She did not look at it.
"They're walking," she said.
Nobody asked who.
"When did they start?" said Oren. He had come out from under his shade on his hands and knees, big and rumpled, with his beard crushed flat on one side.
"Before noon. We think. When the top went warm." Kestrel wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "We heard it from the swell. Up there, you can hear it in the air, if the wind is right. Not loud. Like a fly in a far room." She looked at the four from the south. "They walk faster than us. In the day. They do not need shade. They do not need water. They do not need to lie down. All they need is the sun to keep their joints warm, and they have that."
"How far?"
Kestrel looked at Oren. Then she held up her hand. And folded down all but two fingers.
Two fingers.
Corin could feel two.
"Two hours behind us?" said Oren.
"Less, by dark," said Kestrel. "But by dark they stop." She looked up at the sky. At the light through the hide. "And we go."
Oren looked at the two fingers for a long moment. Then he nodded. Slowly. And something in his face that had been tight all day went a little loose.
"Two fingers ahead," he said. "That's all a march is."
Corin had heard him say that before. Somewhere. On some night, by some fire, that he did not quite remember. But he remembered the words. Two fingers ahead. That's all a march is, lad. Keeping it two fingers.
It had been true then. It was true now.
It was still a very small number.
The ticking went on. He could not hear it now, with his hand off the Glass. But he could feel it, all the same. Somewhere under him. Under all of them. Coming up through the felt and the hot Glass into his bones. Very faint. Very far. Steady and patient and certain, like a heartbeat that is not yours.
He lay down again on the felt.
And waited for the dark.
