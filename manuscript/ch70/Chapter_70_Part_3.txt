Chapter 70: Onto the Glass, Part 3 of 5: Dawn on the Glass
Dawn on the Glass
Dawn came up on the right hand, in the east, and it came up twice.
Corin had seen a great many dawns. Over roofs and chimneys and the river. Over fields white with frost, on the road north, with Oren stamping his feet beside him and Lyra still asleep under her coat. He thought he knew what a dawn was. A gray at the bottom of the sky that gets paler, and then goes yellow, and then the sun.
He had not seen one like this.
It began as a thinning. The stars in the east went faint, low down, first the small ones and then the big ones, as if somebody were breathing on them. The black of the sky there went to a deep blue, like ink in water. And at the same moment, exactly the same moment, the black of the Glass under the sky went to the same deep blue. There was no line between them. The blue came up out of the Glass and down out of the sky at once, and met in the middle, and spread.
Then gray. Then a pale cold green, like the inside of a leaf. Then, along the very edge of the world, a thin line of light. Pink. Then gold.
And the Glass caught it.
All of it. All at once. The whole great flat black floor of the world, from the edge of the sky right up to Corin's boots, went from blue to gray to green to pink to gold, a heartbeat behind the sky each time, like a voice answering a voice. And when the sun came up, the top edge of it, a red-gold coal pushing up over the rim of the east, there were two suns. One coming up out of the sky. One coming up out of the Glass to meet it. Touching at the rim. A single burning point where they met, too bright to look at. And then pulling apart, slowly, the one going up and the other going down, as the sun rose.
The light came across the Glass toward them like water poured across a table.
It was very fast. One moment the Glass ahead of them was gray, and the next it was gold, and the gold came running at them across the flat, and went under their feet, and past them, and on to the west, and the whole world was lit.
Corin stopped walking. He could not help it. Nobody could. The whole line stopped, one by one, and stood with the light coming up on their faces from below, and looked.
And the steam came up.
Corin had not expected that. As the gold light ran across the Glass, a thin white mist began to lift off it. Not everywhere at once. In sheets. Long flat sheets of it, very thin, peeling up off the black the way a skin peels off hot milk, and hanging a hand's height above the surface, and then lifting higher, and tearing, and going to nothing in the light. The night's frost, he thought, the little that had settled on the cold top of the Glass in the dark. Going back up into the sky now that the sun had come for it. All around them, as far as he could see, the sheets of steam lifted and tore and shone pink and gold, so that for a few moments the ten of them were walking through a field of pale burning veils.
Then the steam was gone, and the light came up off the Glass into their faces.
From below. That was the strangest thing. He looked at Lyra, beside him, and her face was lit from underneath, the way a face is lit when someone holds a candle low under their chin to frighten a child. The light was under her jaw and under her nose and in the hollows of her eyes, and the top of her head was in shadow. He looked at Vespera, and she was the same. Oren. All of them. Like people standing over a great fire, looking down into it.
And it was bright. Already, at the very start of the day, it was bright. The Glass threw the sun back up at them like a mirror, so that it came at their eyes from the sky and from the ground at once, and there was nowhere to look that did not hurt.
The Ashborn were ready for it. Corin saw Arran reach into the front of his coat and pull out a strip of pale hide, a hand wide, with two long thin slits cut in it, and tie it round his head over his eyes. Oda did the same. Marrit. Kestrel came back down the line with a bundle of them over her arm and gave one to each of the four from the south, without a word.
Corin tied his on. The world went dim and narrow. Two thin bright lines, side by side, with dark round them. But he could look at the Glass now without his eyes running.
"Like looking out through a shut," said Oren, tying his own. He meant a keyhole. Nobody said so.
He had not known, in the dark, what the Glass looked like.
It was not black. Not in the day. In the day it was every color of smoke. Deep brown-black under his feet, and further out a dark bottle-green, and further out still a kind of purple-gray, like a bruise. It was not quite flat, either, he saw now. It rolled. Very gently, in long slow swells, like the sea on a calm day. A swell might be a mile from top to top. You could not see it in the dark. In the low light of the dawn, every swell had a lit side and a shadowed side, and the whole Glass looked like a sea that had been poured out and frozen in the moment of moving.
And it was not quite smooth. There were lines in it. Long thin cracks, very fine, that ran under the surface like hairs in old glass, going off in every direction. There were bubbles, here and there. Small round ones, like the bubbles in a bottle, frozen inside. And in some places there were streaks of other colors. A thread of rust-red. A ribbon of pale blue. A long swirl of something that might once have been gold, drawn out thin and curled round itself, the way honey curls when you pour it.
This had been land.
He knew it, all at once, looking at it in the light. Not from anything he had been told. From the look of it. This had been earth and stone and grass and trees, fields and roads and houses, and something had come down on it so hot that it had melted all of it together into one thing. And then it had cooled. And that was what they were walking on. That was the floor of the world, here. Everything that had been here, poured out and set hard, like wax in a dish.
He thought of Sola Vance, whose name the old man had given him, and whose bunker lay somewhere out here in the middle of all this. Of what she had done here, sixty years ago, that had made this. Of what kind of fire it must have been.
He looked at Oren.
Oren was standing very still, a few steps away, with the light on his face. He was not looking at the sun. He was looking down. At the Glass under his boots. At the long rust-red thread running away under the surface from his left foot, toward the east.
His face was hard to read. It was often hard to read. The scar made it hard. But Corin saw his jaw move, under the short gray beard, as if he were chewing on something he could not swallow.
"Big," said Oren at last. Very low. "Saints. It's so big."
Nobody answered him.
Kestrel had turned round. She stood at the head of the line with the light behind her, so that she was only a shape, tall and dark with the white cape gone gold at the edges. She was looking back at them. At their faces.
"Now you see it," she said. "This is why we walk at night."
Corin did not understand. Then he did.
Because the sun was already warm on his face. And the warmth that came up through his boots was not the deep slow warmth of the night anymore. It was changing. It was coming up. As the light went across the Glass, the top of the Glass was taking it, and holding it, and giving it back. Not hot yet. But getting hotter. He could feel it through his soles. He could feel it on the backs of his legs, and on his face, and on the undersides of his arms where they hung. The heat of the sun coming down, and the heat of the Glass coming up, and him in the middle.
Like a loaf in an oven. With the fire above and the fire below.
"By noon," said Kestrel, "you will not be able to stand on it with bare feet. By noon the air over it shakes like the air over a fire. By noon, if you walk, you will be dry as a leaf by evening." She held up her water skin and shook it. "Drink now. A little. Not much. Then we find a place."
"A place?" said Vespera. She was looking round, at the great empty shining floor going out to the edge of the sky in every direction. "What place? There's nothing here."
"There is the place we make," said Kestrel.
And behind them, far behind, at the Edge, Corin knew without looking that the sun had reached three dark shapes standing in a row on the black. And that the top of the Glass was going warm under their feet. And that soon, very soon, the cold would go out of their joints, and their little teeth, and their wheels.
He did not look back.
He drank. A little. Not much.
The water was warm already, from the skin lying against his side. It tasted of hide and of the Ashborn camp and, very faintly, of smoke.
The Rider
They walked on into the morning, because there was nowhere yet to stop.
Kestrel was looking for something. Corin could see it in the way she walked now, in the day. Not straight. Weaving a little. Going up each long low swell of the Glass and standing on the top of it, and looking all round, and then going down the other side. He did not know what she was looking for. He did not ask. His mouth was too dry to ask, and the hide over his eyes made the world so narrow that it was hard to think about anything but the two thin lines of light, and the next step, and the next.
The heat was coming up. It came up through the felt they had bound round their boots at the Edge, and through the boot leather, and into his feet. Not burning yet. But his feet were sweating, and the sweat was making the felt damp, and the damp felt was warm and close, like a poultice.
They came up onto the top of a swell. Higher than the others. Corin felt it in his legs, the long slow climb. Then the ground went flat for a few steps, and began to tip down again.
And Oren stopped.
He did not say anything. He just stopped, on the top of the swell, and turned round. And stood looking back the way they had come. South.
Corin turned too.
The Edge was gone. He could not see it. They had come too far, and it was too low, a thin white line lost in the glare. But he could see the land beyond it. The long dark slope going up from where the Edge must be. And the ridge, low and gray. And beyond the ridge, very small and very far, a dark smudge going up into the sky and bending away to the south. Smoke. The smoke of Last Thorn, still going up, a night and a morning after.
And beyond the smoke, beyond the ridge, higher, the long dark line of the Lip. The edge of the high moor they had come down from, days ago, when they first saw the Glass from above. It lay along the whole of the south like a wall, dark brown, with the morning sun from the east lying gold along the top of it.
Oren was looking at the Lip.
Corin looked at it too. He saw nothing. A long dark line. Gold on top.
Then he saw the dot.
One dot. Very small. On the top of the Lip, right above the smoke. It was moving. Very slowly, along the skyline, from the west. A dark dot with the gold light behind it. As he watched, he saw that it was not one dot but two, one on top of the other. A horse. And a man on it.
The horse stopped.
It stopped right above the smoke. Right on the edge of the Lip, where the ground fell away to the thorn slope and the valley where Last Thorn had been. And stood there. And the rider sat on it, not moving. Very small. Black against the gold.
Looking down.
Corin could not see his face. He could not have seen it with the best eyes in the world, from here. He could not see what he wore. He could see only the shape of him, a dark small upright shape on a dark small horse, sitting very still on the top of the Lip with the sunrise behind him. Looking down at the smoke. At the black place in the valley where all the round hide tents had stood, and goats, and bread racks, and a tent of hands.
The rider lifted his arm.
Slowly. The dark shape of the arm going up to the dark shape of the head. And then a small change in the shape of the head. Something taken away. And held. Out to the side, a little. And down.
He had taken off his hat.
And he sat there, on the horse, with his hat in his hand, bareheaded on the edge of the Lip, looking down at the smoke of Last Thorn. For a long time. As long as it would take to say a prayer to the Saints, if you knew one. Longer.
Then he put the hat back on.
And turned the horse. And rode away west along the skyline, slowly, the way he had come, until the dot was gone into the brown of the moor.
Nobody had said anything.
The others had come up onto the top of the swell behind them, and stopped, and seen what Oren was looking at, and looked too. All ten of them stood on the top of the swell in the glare with the hides over their eyes, looking back at an empty skyline.
"Him," said Oren.
That was all. One word. Very low. In a voice Corin had not heard him use before. Not angry. Not frightened. Something else. The voice a man uses for the name of somebody he has fought against for a long time, and hates, and knows better than he knows most of his friends.
Nobody needed to ask who.
Corin found that his gray hand had gone to his left coat pocket. To the small hard round shape of the stone in there. Wren's stone. He did not know why. He took his hand out again.
"He took his hat off," said Lyra. She sounded as if she did not believe it.
"Aye," said Oren.
"For them. For Last Thorn. After he—" She stopped. "After his iron did that."
"Aye."
"Why would he do that?"
Oren did not answer for a while. He was still looking at the place on the skyline where the rider had been.
"Because he's a soldier," he said at last. "Under all the rest of it. Under the gray coat and the iron and the clever talk. Some part of him's still a soldier. And a soldier takes his hat off for the dead." A pause. "Even the ones he killed. Especially them."
He turned round. Away from the south. Toward the north and the glare.
"Doesn't make him less dangerous," he said. "Makes him more. A man who'll take his hat off for the dead won't stop for the living. He's already paid for them in his head. Come on."
He walked down the north side of the swell. After a moment the others followed.
Corin went last. He looked back once more at the empty gold line of the Lip, and the smoke going up from behind the ridge.
Kross had not followed them onto the Glass. Kross had stopped at the edge of it, and taken off his hat, and turned away.
He did not know if that was better or worse.
He thought, probably, worse.
The Footprints
An hour after dawn, or near it, Corin found the footprints.
He did not go looking for them. He was not looking for anything. He was looking at his own feet, through the two thin slits in the hide, the way you do when you are very tired and the ground is the only thing you can trust. Left boot. Right boot. The felt round them gone dark with sweat. The Glass under them, brown-black here, and very smooth, with the long fine hair-cracks running away under the shine.
And then, beside his right boot, a hollow.
Small. Not much longer than his hand. A shallow dip in the Glass, smooth-sided, like the dip a thumb leaves when you press it into soft wax. The bottom of it was glazed over. Shining. The same shine as all the rest of the Glass, as if the hollow had been there so long that the Glass had healed over it, the way skin heals over a cut and leaves the shape.
He stopped.
It was a footprint. He saw that at once. The shape of it was very clear, now that he was looking. The round dent of a heel. The narrow place under the arch. The wider place at the front, where the ball of the foot had pushed down. And at the very front, five small round dips in a curved row, like peas.
Toes.
A bare foot.
A small one.
He looked ahead of it. There was another. A step away. The left foot, this time. And ahead of that another, the right. And another. Going away from him across the Glass in a line. Not a straight line. A little wandering. The way a child walks, who is not quite sure of the way, or is very tired.
He looked behind. The line went that way too. Back across the Glass behind him, toward the south, toward the Edge. Into the glare. He could see four or five of them, little glazed hollows catching the light, and then they were lost.
But it was not going south. That was the thing. He looked at the shape of them again, to be sure. The heel and the toes. The toes pointed south. Toward the Edge. Toward the Lip and the valleys and the way they had come.
The child had been walking out of the Glass. The other way.
"Kestrel," he said.
His voice came out cracked and dry. Too low. But she heard it, ahead. She came back.
She did not ask what. She saw where he was looking, and she looked too, and then she crouched down on her heels by the nearest footprint and put out her hand and touched it. Ran her fingers round the edge of it. Pressed her palm flat into the hollow, and held it there.
The others came up. Lyra. Vespera. Oren. Arran, bending over with his hands on his knees. Nobody said anything.
Corin put his boot down beside the next print. Not in it. Beside it. He did not want to step in it. He did not know why. And he took a step, the way he would have walked, and set his other boot down beside the one after.
The angle was the same.
He saw it, looking down through the slits. The way the small feet turned out a little, the left one more than the right. The way the right foot came down a little short, every time, as if the child were favoring it. His own boots, beside them, turned out at the same angle. The left more than the right. He had never thought about it. He had never looked at how he walked. But there it was, laid out on the Glass beside his own boots, smaller. The same.
Lots of people walk like that, he thought.
He thought it very firmly.
Kestrel took her hand out of the footprint. She stayed crouched there, looking at it.
"The Glass does not take prints," she said. "Not now. Not for a long, long time. When it was new, our grandmothers' grandmothers said, it was soft for a few summers, like toffee in a pan, and things sank into it. But that was before Old Hask was born. Since then it has been hard. Harder than iron. You could drop a rock on it from a cliff and the rock would break." She touched the shining bottom of the print. "Something made it soft. Here. Under these feet. Only under these feet, only for as long as it took to walk. Then it went hard again behind them." A pause. "We have never seen that. Not once. Not in any story."
She ran her finger round the inside of the hollow again.
"And the glaze is what the Glass grows," she said. "Every summer a little. Like the bloom on a plum. Very thin. You can see it best in the hollows, where the wind does not take it off."
She looked up at Corin.
"These are older than the glaze," she said. "The glaze came over them after. It is thick in them. Thicker than a finger." She held up her finger, and put her thumb against it, to show how thick. "That is a lot of summers."
"How many?" said Vespera.
Kestrel stood up.
"Twenty years," she said. "More."
She was not looking at Vespera. She was looking at Corin.
He did not say anything.
He could feel them all looking at him. He could feel Lyra's eyes, beside him, on the side of his face. He could feel Vespera's, sharp, from behind. He did not look at any of them. He looked down at the small glazed hollows going away south, toward the Edge, in a wandering line.
A child. A small child. Barefoot, on the Glass. On Glass that had been hard as iron for longer than anyone alive could remember, and that had gone soft under those small feet, and only under them, and then gone hard again. Walking south. Walking out.
Alone?
He looked for other prints. Bigger ones. Beside the little ones. There were none. Only the one small line.
But the Glass was very big. And wind and summers had had their way with it for twenty years and more. And a big man's boots, with thick soles, might not have sunk in so deep as a child's bare feet. Might not have left anything at all.
Count, boy.
He did not know why he thought of that. The old man's voice, at the first step. The only words he had said. Count.
He did not count the footprints. He could not have. There were too many, and they went too far.
"Corin," said Lyra. Very quietly.
"It's nothing," he said.
He heard how it sounded. It did not sound like nothing.
He walked on. North. Away from the footprints. The others came after him, slowly, one by one, and he heard Kestrel say something to Arran in the round speech, low, and Arran not answer.
He did not look back.
But his feet did. Or something in him did. For a long time after, walking north into the glare, he could feel the line of small hollows going away behind him to the south. The way you can feel someone watching your back in a crowd. A small wandering line, the left foot turned out more than the right, going home.
