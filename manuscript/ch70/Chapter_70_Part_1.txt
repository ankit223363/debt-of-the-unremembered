Chapter 70: Onto the Glass, Part 1 of 5: Splitting
Splitting
The Ashborn of Last Thorn split in two at the Edge, past midnight, with the ticking coming down the hill behind them, and they did it in less time than it takes to bake a loaf.
Corin watched them do it.
He had seen armies move. He thought he had. Somewhere in him, on a shelf, there were men in gray coats moving through streets in a line, and soldiers in old coats with pikes, and the sound of drums. He did not know whose those were. But he had never seen anything like this. A whole people, in the dark, at the end of the world, deciding to be two peoples, and becoming it, without a shout.
Kestrel said a few words. Low. In the round Ashborn speech. And the line along the Edge broke up and came together again in two pieces. A big one, and a small one.
The big one was everybody. The mothers with their babies. The old men on their sticks. The children. The goats, herded in a bleating knot by three boys with switches. The men with their broken spears. The woman who had lost old Ennis, held up between two others. Sixty, seventy, more. Corin could not feel how many. He only saw that it was a great many, and that it was most of them.
The small one was ten.
Kestrel. Arran. Brin, holding his left arm against his chest. Oda, with her spear. Sef, with his singed hair. Marrit, with her pole. And the four from the south. Oren, Lyra, Vespera, Corin.
Ten. He could not feel ten either. But he could see them. He could count them on his fingers, one hand and then the other, and get to the end of the second hand, and stop. That was ten. That was all of them.
All of them that were going.
The big piece of the Ashborn had already begun to move. East. Along the Edge. Walking on the last of the snow, with the Glass on their left hand, a step away, black and shining, and the dark slope going up to the ridge on their right. There was no path. They did not need one. The Edge was the path. The clean straight line where the snow stopped and the Glass began. It went away east into the dark, as straight as a rule, as far as Corin could see, and they followed it.
Salt Wells, Kestrel had said. Two days. The cousins.
He watched them go. A long dark line of people and goats, moving away along the line of the snow, getting smaller.
And behind them, up the long slope toward the ridge, he could hear the ticking.
It was slower now. Much slower. Step, and sink, and drag. The rotten snow was holding them. But it was still coming. Down the slope. Toward the Edge. Toward the place where the people of Last Thorn had stood a moment ago and where now ten people stood instead.
It did not turn east.
Corin listened for it to. He listened very hard. For the ticking to change, and turn, and go along the slope after the long dark line of the families, toward the mothers and the babies and the goats.
It did not. It came straight down the slope. North. Toward him.
It has only ever wanted him, Kestrel had said.
He found that he was glad.
It was a strange thing to be glad of. He looked at it, the gladness, in himself, the way he would have looked at something on a shelf. And he understood it. If the iron wanted him, it did not want them. The children with their painted noses. The girl who had thrown her spear down. The little boy with the runny nose who had pulled Lyra into the dance. If it wanted only him, then they could walk away east along the Edge in the dark, and it would not follow them, and they would get to the Salt Wells, and the cousins would take them in, and they would build new tents and press new hands on them.
And he would walk north.
With nine people who had chosen to walk with him.
He looked at them. In the dark, at the Edge, with their packs on and their water skins and their felt-wrapped stones, still warm. Kestrel tall and straight, her face to the Glass. Arran with his eyebrows gone. Brin with his arm. Oda with her spear. Sef. Marrit, old and straight, leaning on her pole. Vespera, small and gray, with her needles. Oren, big and steady, with his notched sword. Lyra.
Lyra, with her face wet and her sword stained black and her feet that did not know how to dance.
Nine people.
He did not know how to feel about it. He did not know whether he had the right to let them. He thought, probably, he did not.
He did not say so.
He had learned, at least, that much. That when people chose to walk beside you into the dark, the worst thing you could do was tell them they should not have.
Goodbyes
The goodbyes were short. There was no time for long ones. The ticking saw to that.
But there were goodbyes.
The girl with the baby who had held Sef's hand at the naming came back along the Edge from the long dark line of the families, running, with the baby on her hip, and caught Sef by the front of his tunic, and said something into his face, very fast and very low. And he said something back. And she let go of him and turned and ran back to the others without looking round. Sef stood where she had left him for a moment, with his hands at his sides. Then he hitched his pack higher on his shoulders and came and stood with the ten, and did not look east again.
An old man came to Marrit and touched his forehead to hers. Just that. Forehead to forehead, the gray lines touching, for a long breath. Then he went.
A woman pressed a bundle into Brin's good hand. Bread, wrapped in a cloth. He tried to give it back. She would not take it.
And the children came for Arran.
Corin heard them before he saw them. Running back along the Edge from the families in a clump, in their furs, with the mothers calling after them, angry and frightened. Six of them, seven. They came at Arran in a rush and grabbed him round the legs and the waist and the arms, the way they had at the naming, and hung on. And howled.
Arran did not play the goat tune this time. He knelt down on the wet gravel, so that he was the same height as them. And put his long arms round as many of them as he could reach. And said something. Low. Over and over. In the round Ashborn speech. Corin did not understand the words. But he understood the voice. It was the voice you use to a child who is frightened of the dark.
The children stopped howling, one by one. They let go. The mothers came and took them by the hands.
All but one.
The girl. The serious one, from the path, who had thrown her spear down at the naming and dragged the two little ones out from under the falling Eraser. She had let go of Arran. But she had not gone back to her mother. She was standing on the gravel by the Edge, with her bone spear in her hand and her round shaved head very straight, looking at the ten.
Not at Arran. At Lyra.
She walked up to her. Lyra was standing beside Corin with her pack on and her hands hanging at her sides. The girl stopped in front of her and looked up. A long way up. She was very small.
Then she put her free hand inside her fur. And took something out. And held it up.
It was a bone charm. One of the little carved ones that had hung in strings from the tent frames and clicked in the wind. On a loop of thin leather. Yellowed bone, smooth with handling, no longer than a finger. Corin could not see what shape it was, in the dark. Then the light from the far fire caught it.
A bird. A tall thin bird, standing on one leg, with its long neck curved and its long beak tucked down. Carved very simply, very small, by someone who had watched such birds for a long time.
A heron.
Lyra looked at it.
The girl said one word. Very serious. And held it higher.
"She says it's from her tent," said Arran quietly, behind them. "It hung over her bed. She's had it since she was a baby. It's the only one that didn't burn." A pause. "She says you dance like one. She says you should have it. For when you can't anymore."
Lyra did not move.
Corin saw her face. Saw it go still, and then crumple, and then go still again, by force. She bent down. Slowly. Until her face was level with the girl's. And held out her gloved hand.
The girl put the heron in it. And closed Lyra's fingers over it with her own small cold ones. Very carefully. The way you would close someone's hand over a coin they might drop.
Then she stepped back. And touched the gray line on her own face. Two fingers, forehead to nose. Quick and neat. The way Arran did it. The way you greeted someone. Or said goodbye.
And turned. And ran back along the Edge to the long dark line of the families, with her spear held up straight over her head so as not to catch, the way she had run on the first night, down the path through the thorns.
She did not look back.
Lyra stayed bent down for a long moment, with the heron in her closed hand.
Then she straightened up. And put the loop of leather over her head, and tucked the little bone bird inside her coat, against her chest. And pressed her hand on it, once, flat.
She did not say anything. Neither did Corin.
The long line of the families went away east along the Edge into the dark. Getting smaller. Getting fainter. Until it was only a darker smudge on the dark, and then not even that, and there was only the line of the snow going away east, straight as a rule, empty.
And the ticking, coming down the slope.
Kestrel turned round.
She turned her back on the slope and the ridge and the red glow of Last Thorn beyond it, and on the empty line of the Edge where her people had gone. And faced north. Toward the Glass.
And the nine turned with her.
It lay in front of them, one step away. Black. Smooth. Shining. Warm. With all the stars of the sky lying in it. Going out from the clean line of the snow into the dark, flat, forever.
"Now," said Kestrel.
And she stepped onto it.
The First Step
Kestrel's boot came down on the black, and nothing happened.
Corin had not known he was waiting for something to happen until it did not. A crack. A hiss. A flash of white light, coming up out of the black. Some great slow hum, waking under the ground and rising to meet them. But there was only the sound of a boot on something hard and smooth. A small flat knock, like a knuckle on a table. And Kestrel standing on the Glass, a step out from the snow, with her spear in her hand and her back to them. Not looking round.
She took another step. Knock.
And another.
Arran went next. He did not stop at the line. He walked off the wet gravel and onto the black in the same long stride, as if it were the floor of a tent, and went after his sister. Knock. Knock.
Then Marrit, in her heavy furs, with her hand on the old bone knife at her belt. Then Oda, very quick, very light, with her head down. Then Sef. Sef stopped at the line. Corin saw his shoulders go up and down once, hard. Then he stepped over.
Brin went with his good hand held out a little from his side, for balance, and his reset arm strapped tight across his chest in its sling of hide. He walked very carefully, like a man on a frozen pond.
Then it was the four of them, at the edge of the snow. The four from the south.
Vespera looked at Corin. He could not see her face well in the dark. Only the pale shape of it, and the glint of her eyes.
"Together?" she said.
"Together."
Oren's hand was on his shoulder. Corin had not felt him put it there. It was just there, big and heavy and warm through the coat, the way it had been on the road north whenever the ground got bad. Under Corin's left arm was the ash crutch that Silas had given him, the one Oren had carried out of the fire, with its foot bound up in a fat wad of goat-wool felt so that it would not skid. On his right side, Lyra. She did not take his hand. She stood very close, so that her shoulder was against his arm. He could feel her breathing through both their coats.
They stepped out together.
Boot. Crutch. Boot. The felt on the crutch foot made a soft, dull sound on the black, not a knock at all. More like a hand laid flat on a table. His bad leg took the step and held. It ached, the long deep ache that had not left it since Silas opened the wound, but it held.
The Glass was warm.
He had known it would be. He had felt the warmth on his face from the Edge, and seen the snow melt in a clean line where it met the black. But knowing was not the same as feeling it come up through the soles of his boots, into his heels, into the bones of his feet. Not hot. Not like a stove. Like a stone wall at the end of a summer day, when the sun has gone down but the stone still holds it. A slow, deep, patient warmth that seemed to come from very far below.
And smooth. So smooth. His boot did not slip, quite. But it did not grip either. It was like standing on the bottom of a bowl.
He looked down. And saw himself.
Not clearly. It was too dark for that. But there was a man under his feet, upside down, looking up at him. A dark shape against stars. A dark shape with a bigger dark shape at its shoulder, and a smaller one close at its side. The Glass gave back everything. The sky. The stars. Him.
He took a second step.
And Malakor spoke.
Count, boy.
Two words. In the old dry voice. Very close. As close as it had ever been. Close as his own thoughts. Not angry. Not mocking. Not the voice that had laughed at him in the dark so many times, or sneered, or gone on and on about Linus and his clever pages. Just two words, said plainly. The way a man might say them to a child at the start of a long walk. Count your steps. Count your breaths. Keep count, so you know how far you've come.
Corin stopped walking.
Count what? he thought. Count what, old man?
Nothing.
Steps? Days? The dead?
Nothing.
Malakor.
Nothing.
He stood very still on the warm black glass with the stars under his feet, and listened. Listened the way you listen in a house at night, when you think you heard a footstep on the stair. With your whole body. With your skin.
There was the wind, small and dry, coming from the north. There was the knock of the others' boots, going on ahead. There was Oren's breathing beside him, and Lyra's, and Vespera's. And behind him, faint now, faint but steady, the ticking. Coming down the slope.
And in his head, where the old man lived, where the old man had lived for so long now that Corin could not remember the shape of his head without him, where there had always been something—a mutter, a dry chuckle, a sense of being watched from the inside, like a cat watches from under a bed—
There was nothing.
Not quiet. Quiet was a thing. Quiet had a shape, like a room with the fire gone out. This was not quiet. This was as if the room had never been there.
"Corin?" Oren's hand closed on his shoulder. "Walk, lad. Standing still's the worst thing you can do on bad ground."
He walked.
One. Two. Three. He did count, after all, in the end. He could not help it. His feet were counting under him. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.
And then the next one, and the feeling went away from him the way it always did now, the way a word goes when you reach for it. The step was there. His foot came down. But the number for it was not. There was just more than nine. A blur. A gray place where the numbers went to live after he could not hold them.
He started again. One. Two.
Count, boy.
Had the old man known? That he could not count past nine anymore? That every count he started would break on the same place, the way waves break on the same rock?
Of course he had known. He had taken the numbers. Or the spells had. It did not matter which. It was the same thing in the end.
One. Two. Three.
Lyra's shoulder against his arm. Oren's hand on his shoulder. Vespera's boots, knock, knock, half a step behind.
He did not look back at the Edge. He thought that if he looked back, he might go back. And there was nothing back there now but the ticking, and the empty line of the snow, and a dead town burning on the other side of a ridge.
So he looked ahead. At the dark shape of Kestrel, small and straight, far out on the black. At Arran's long shape behind her. At the others, in a loose line, getting smaller.
And under them, under all of them, the stars. Lying in the Glass. As if they were walking across the top of the night sky, and the night went down forever under their feet.
Nothing
They walked for what Corin thought was an hour. He could not have said. There was no bell out here. There was no clock. There was the slow turn of the stars, and his feet, and nothing in his head.
He kept testing it. The way you keep touching a tooth that has come out, with your tongue, to feel the hole.
Old man.
Nothing.
Malakor. Are you there?
Nothing.
He remembered what the old man had said, on the road north, in the snow, before they ever came to Thistle Ford. When I stop talking it won't mean I've gone. He held on to that. He turned it over and over in his mind, like a smooth stone in a pocket. It won't mean I've gone. It won't mean I've gone.
But it was hard. It was much harder than he had thought it would be.
Because he had not known, until now, how much room the old man took up.
It was like living in a small house with someone who talks all the time. You stop hearing them, after a while. You think you want them to be quiet. You think, if they would only be quiet for one evening, you could hear yourself think. And then one evening they go out. And the house is so big. So big and so empty. And you walk from room to room, and every room has a place in it where they would have been standing, and they are not there, and the empty places are louder than they ever were.
That was what his head was like.
There was a place where the old man had sat, in him. Corin had made it himself, under the last tree on the ridge, the way Kestrel had told him. Don't hold him. Let him sit in you, the way an old man sits by a fire. He had made the place like the inside of Old Hask's tent. Warm and dim. A small fire. Furs. And the old man had come into it and settled, the way a cat settles, turning round twice before it lies down.
The place was still there. Corin could feel it. The furs. The fire, low. The dent in the furs where someone had been lying.
But nobody was lying in it now.
Or somebody was, and Corin could not see them. Could not hear them. Could not feel them breathe.
He did not know which was worse.
He thought about the jars. All the jars he had carried, in all the years of the Harvesting Section. All the burnt-out mages he had walked into, the way you walk into a ruined house, and gone through room by room, picking up what was left. Bits of faces. Bits of rooms. A song with half the words gone. A smell of bread. He had always known, when he walked a jar, that there was someone at the bottom of it. Someone who had been. You could feel the shape of them, like the shape a sleeper leaves in a bed.
That was what the old man felt like now. A shape. Not a person. The place where a person had been.
Is this what you meant? he thought. Count. Count what is left. Is that it?
Nothing.
He found that his throat was tight. He swallowed. It did not help. He looked down at the Glass, at the stars under his feet, and the stars swam. He blinked hard and they stopped swimming.
He was so tired. He had not slept since the night before last, in the tent of hands, before the Erasers came. He had broken nothing, burned nothing, carried nothing. And still he was so tired that his bones felt hollow.
The silence made it worse. It was strange, but it was so. When the old man was there, even when he was not saying anything, there had been a kind of hum. A hum under everything, the way there is a hum under a city at night, even when the streets are empty. Carts a long way off. Water in the drains. Somebody's door. You do not hear it. But you hear it when it stops.
It had stopped.
And under it, where it had been, there was only Corin. Just Corin, by himself. A man with gray arms and black fingertips and holes in his head, walking across a black glass sea in the dark, with three people he loved and could not wholly remember, and six people he hardly knew.
He had not been alone inside his own head for a very long time.
He did not like it.
He thought he would get used to it. People got used to everything. That was what Vespera had said, when she broke the empty jar under her heel on the children's stone. You get used to it. And then you put it down, and you nearly fall over.
He thought perhaps the old man had been something he had got used to carrying. And now the weight was gone, all at once, and he was walking strangely. Leaning, a little. As if to make up for it.
"You're walking crooked," said Oren.
Corin looked round at him.
The big man was not looking at his face. He was looking down at their feet. At Corin's boots, on the Glass, beside his own. The scar from his temple to his mouth was a pale line in the starlight, and his short gray beard was white with frost at the edges.
"You're leaning," Oren said. "More than the stick makes you. Left. I had a man once, on the border, carried his pack on one shoulder for a whole march because the strap was broke on the other side. Then we got him a new strap, and he took it off, and for a whole day after he walked like a gate that's lost a hinge."
Corin did not answer.
"Is it the old man?" said Oren. Quieter.
Corin had not told him. He had not told any of them. But Oren was Oren. Oren watched feet. Oren had watched soldiers' feet for thirty years, on roads and in mud and on the walls of towns, and he knew when a man was carrying a weight, and when he had put one down.
"He's stopped talking," said Corin.
Oren walked for a while without saying anything. Knock. Knock. His big boots on the Glass.
"My old sergeant stopped talking, at the end," he said at last. "Before he died. For two days." He held up two fingers, in the dark, so that Corin could see. "He didn't go, though. He was still there. You could tell. He still squeezed your hand."
Corin looked at the two fingers.
Then he reached up and put his gray hand over Oren's big one, where it lay on his shoulder, and pressed it.
Oren's fingers tightened. Hard. Twice.
They walked on.
