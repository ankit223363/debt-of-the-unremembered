Chapter 84: The Siege of the Eye, Part 4 of 5: What Was Burned
What Was Burned
And while he held, he thought about what it had been.
He could not help it. A man thinks, in the middle of a long blow. Between one bang and the next there was time, a breath, two breaths, and his head went where it went. It went to the thing he had burned.
He did not have the word. But he had the things.
That was how it was, every time. The word went, and the things stayed. He could not say the name of the stuff on a pond in winter, but he could see it. White, and gray at the edges, and cracked, with the black water moving under it. He could feel it under his boots, the give and the creak, the cold coming up. He could remember falling through it once as a boy and his father hauling him out by the collar. He had all of that. He only did not have the word.
So now.
He stood with his back against the iron thing and his sword up and the gold shaking, and he saw them. All of them. One after another, the way you see the cards go by when a man shuffles a pack.
The one on the farmhouse in the hills where he was born, the old one, wood, split down the middle with a crack you could see daylight through, that his mother stuffed with rag every winter. It had a latch you lifted with a string. You pulled the string from outside and the latch went up inside with a clack.
The one at the barracks. Iron-bound. Painted green once. With a little square window in it, at head height, with bars, so the sergeant could look out and see who was knocking before he opened it.
The ones in the long war. He did not remember many of those. Tents had flaps. Ditches had nothing. But he remembered one. In a burned village, in the second year. A farmhouse with the roof gone and the walls standing and the one at the front still on its hinges, painted blue, with a horseshoe nailed over it for luck, standing open on a room full of nothing. He had stood in it for a long time. He did not know why.
And the ones on Crown Walk.
The great black one at the front, with the brass knocker shaped like a lion's head and the tree-and-crown carved in the stone over it, that he had opened every morning for twenty-two years for the post and the milk and the boy from the bakery. The green one at the bottom of the garden, in the wall, that led out to the lane, that the lass used to climb over instead of going through because she said it was more interesting. The one at the top of the back stair, to the servants' rooms, that stuck in wet weather.
And the study.
The one to his lordship's study. Dark oak, heavy, with a brass handle worn pale by his lordship's hand. With the light coming out under it at night in a thin gold line along the floor, where it did not quite meet the boards.
He had stood outside it every night for twenty-two years.
Not every night. Most nights. Whenever his lordship worked late, which was always. Oren would take the last rounds of the house, the front and the back and the garden and the stables, and check the bolts, and then he would come up the main stair, quietly, in his stocking feet so as not to wake the house, and stand outside the study. With his back to the wall. Beside it. Not in front of it. Beside. And listen.
He could hear his lordship in there. The scratch of a pen. A page turning. Sometimes the old man talking to himself, low, in the voice he used when he was working something out. Sometimes, in the last years, nothing at all for a long time. And then the tapping. Four. Two. Nine. Three. On the arm of the thing he sat on, by the fire, over and over, in the dark. Oren had not known then what it was. He had stood outside and listened to it and not known.
And then, at last, the light under it would go out. And his lordship would come out, and see him there, and stop.
Oren. You needn't wait up.
No, sir.
Every night you say no, sir, and every night you're here.
Yes, sir.
And his lordship would laugh. A little. A tired laugh. And put his hand on Oren's shoulder going past, for just a moment, the way you would steady yourself on a post. And go off down the passage to bed with his candle. And Oren would stand there a moment longer by the dark wood, with the gold line gone from under it. And then go to bed himself.
Twenty-two years.
And on the last night, he had stood there, and the light under it had not gone out. It had stayed on all night. And there had been no pen. No pages. No talking. Only, at the end, a long time after midnight, a sound that he had not understood at the time and understood now. A sound like a man breathing out for the last time. Over and over. Burning. Every room in him.
And Oren had stood outside it and not gone in.
He had not gone in. He had put his hand on the brass handle, worn pale, and stood there with his hand on it, and not turned it. Because his lordship had told him once, If I ever shut this and tell you not to come in, Oren, don't come in. Whatever you hear. And he had said, Yes, sir. And so he had stood there, and kept his word, and held it, all night, with his hand on the handle. While on the other side of it his lordship burned.
Bang.
His teeth jarred. The gold shook.
All of that.
That was what he had burned. Not the things. He still had the things. He had the green one in the garden wall and the blue one in the burned village and the dark oak with the gold line under it and his hand on the brass handle all night. But the word that held them all together. The word he had used for them, all of them, all his life, every time he said I'll stand by the— or Somebody at the— or Mind the—.
The word he had given was the word for the thing he was.
He was a man who stood beside them. That was all he had ever been. A soldier at the barracks gate. A guard on Crown Walk. A captain at the top of a stair. Every place he had ever been worth anything, he had been standing beside one of those. Keeping it. Holding it. Listening at it.
And now he could not say what it was he had been standing beside.
He thought he ought to feel something. A great hole. A pain. He had thought, the first time, years ago, that he would. And it was never like that. It was only like this. Like reaching for a step in the dark and finding the floor a little lower than you thought. A jolt. And then you were standing on the floor, and it was a floor, and you went on.
He went on.
Bang.
He held.
The Song
It was the child who began to sing.
Oren did not know why. Afterward, when he thought about it, he thought perhaps she did not know either. It came out of her the way the lad's humming came out of him, or the lass's counting. Not chosen. Something to hold on to.
He heard her behind the man's elbow. A small sound. A breath. And then a note.
Low. Thin. Shaking.
Then another. High.
Then a little lower.
He knew it. Everybody knew it. He had heard it on the log outside the tent at Last Thorn, from the bone flute, the night they came. He had heard the Ashborn children play it on reeds. He had heard the lass hum it in the dark when she thought nobody could hear. He had heard the lad hum it in his sleep. Three notes. The tune everybody's grandmother knows, that stops too soon.
Only the child did not stop.
She went on. Up. The fourth note. Going up past the high one and hanging there, high and thin and wavering, like a question nobody had ever answered. And then on, down, into the rest of it. Into words.
She was singing it the way a child sings in the dark, to keep the dark off. Fast and too high and a little flat, with her breath catching in the wrong places. Her voice shook so hard that some of the notes came out in two pieces. Oren could hear her teeth chattering between the words.
And on the first note, the very first, the low one, every iron man stopped.
Oren saw it.
He did not believe it at first. He was standing with his back half against the curve of the dome and his sword up in front of his face and the gold pouring out of it into the wall, and his whole body shaking with the blows, and the iron man in front of him had its arm drawn back for the next one. Its great flat shovel hand up beside its egg head. Ticking.
And the child sang her first note.
And the iron man's arm stopped.
Not slowed. Stopped. In the air. Drawn back. Its hand hanging there, a foot from the gold.
And its ticking stopped.
Oren heard it stop. On the Eye, in that silence, with the low note of the child's voice going out across the black glass, he heard the ticking stop. Not in one iron man. In all three. The one in front of him. The one in front of the man on the right. The one in the middle, in front of Kestrel and the gap. All three at once. Their arms in the air. Their egg heads still. Silent.
For one tick.
One. The space of one tick, where a tick should have been, and was not.
It was like the moment in a clock shop when every clock on every wall stops at once, and you look up from the counter, and the silence is so loud that you cannot breathe.
Then the next tick came.
All three at once. Tick. And the arms came down. And the shovel hands hit the gold. Bang. Bang. Bang. And the shock of it went into Oren's teeth. And they went on. The ticking. The pounding. As if nothing had happened.
The child sang on. She had not seen. She had her eyes shut. She was singing with her eyes screwed tight shut behind the man's elbow and her fists clenched at her sides, and she had not seen anything at all.
Oren had seen.
He stood there with his teeth jarring and the gold pouring out of his sword and he thought, That stopped them. He did not understand it. He did not know how. He did not know what it meant that a child's thin shaking voice singing an old tune could stop three iron men for the space of one tick. He only knew that he had seen it.
And so had somebody else.
Off to the left, out on the glass, the pale one had lifted his head.
He had been writing. All this time. Through the stones and the blue fire and the pounding, standing a little apart from it all on the black glass in his near-white coat, with his notebook open on his hand and his silver pencil moving. Calmly. As if he were sitting in a garden, writing a letter to his aunt.
Now his head had come up. Sharp. All at once. Like a dog's head when it hears its name.
He was looking at the child.
Not at the wall. Not at the iron. At the child. At the small white face with its eyes shut, behind the man's elbow, singing. And his own face had changed. Oren saw it change. The smile had gone out of it. Not into anger. Into something else. Something very still and very sharp, the way a fishing heron's face is still and sharp when something moves under the water.
Then the pale one looked down at his book.
And wrote.
Quickly. Not neatly, the way he had been writing before. Quickly. His silver pencil going fast across the page, and stopping, and going again. And then he looked up at the child once more. For a long moment.
And went on writing.
Oren felt something go cold in his belly that had nothing to do with the blows.
He could not have said what. He did not know what the pale one had written. He did not know why it mattered. He only knew that he had seen a thing happen that should not have happened, and that the pale one had seen it too, and had written it down. And that whatever he had written, it was about the child.
The child sang on. With her eyes shut. Into the second verse.
The iron men did not stop again.
The Man Goes Out
The man moved while the child was singing.
Oren did not see him decide. He never saw him decide. He saw him standing at his right, a pace off, still, with his bound hands loose at his sides and his eyes on the iron man in front of him on the other side of the gold. And then he saw him move.
Not forward. Sideways. To the right. Along the line of the wall.
The gold wall ran from Oren's sword out in a curve on either side until it met the dome. On the right, it met the dome a few paces past the man. Where the curve of the dead woman's house came round. And where it met it, Oren saw now, for the first time, it did not quite meet it.
There was a rib there. One of the iron bands that went over the dome like the ribs of a basket. It stood out from the stone, gone soft and glassy with the old heat, a hand's width proud of the wall. And the gold came round to it and stopped. As if it could not climb over the iron. As if it would not touch it. And between the edge of the gold and the stone behind the rib, there was a gap.
Not a big one. A hand and a half, perhaps. Narrow. The kind of gap a cat goes through.
The man went to it.
"Tansy," said Oren. Through his teeth. "Tansy, stay—"
She was still singing. Her eyes still shut. She had not seen the man go. She was standing where he had told her to stand, behind the man's elbow, only the man's elbow was not there anymore.
The man had stopped by Brin.
Brin was at the end of the line of Ashborn, on the right, with his back to the dome and his ribs strapped, holding his spear in both his big hands. Pale. Sweating. The man stopped in front of him and looked at the spear.
Then he put out his bound hands and took hold of it.
Not by the shaft. He could not have held the shaft. His fingers would not close. He took it the other way, the way Oren had seen men carry a long thing when their hands are hurt: in the crooks of his elbows, both arms bent, the shaft lying across them like a bar across a gate. And Brin, startled, slow, let go.
And the man turned and went sideways through the gap.
One moment he was inside the gold. The next he was outside it. On the open glass. With the iron.
"No," said the lass. "No, no, no—"
Oren could not move. He could not let go of the sword. If he let go of the sword, the gold would go. He knew that the way you know your own heart beats. He stood there with the sword up and the gold pouring out of it and the blows going into him, and he watched.
The man went along the outside of the wall. Quick. Low. With the spear across his bent arms. Toward the iron man on the right. The one in front of the place where he had been standing. Its back was to him. It was pounding on the gold. Drawing back. Hitting. Drawing back.
The man came up behind it.
And as its arm came back, he went in under it, and drove the end of the spear shaft into the back of its knee.
Not the point. The butt. The blunt end of the shaft, held across his arms, rammed in with his whole weight behind it, into the crease at the back of the iron knee where the plates met and the brass showed. Oren heard it. A crack. A grinding. The shaft went in between the plates and jammed there, hard, and stuck.
The iron man's leg seized.
It staggered. It lurched, sideways, toward the gold, on one stiff leg, with its arm still drawn back. And the man let go of the spear and went for its back. For the plate. The brass plate in the small of its back, where the key went in, where the spring was.
He got one hand on it.
One bound hand, flat, on the brass.
And the iron man turned.
It turned on its good leg the way a bull turns. Fast. Faster than anything that big should turn. Its whole body came round, the stuck leg dragging, the spear shaft snapping off in the crease of its knee with a crack like a whip. And its drawn-back arm came round with it. Not at the gold. At the man.
It hit him across the chest.
Oren saw him go.
He went the way a sack goes when a carter throws it off the back of a cart. All at once. Sideways and up. His long body bent in the middle, his arms flung out, the cut strips of the child's shirt fluttering off his hands. He went up off the glass and through the air in a long low curve, and Oren watched him go, and watched him go, and he went farther than a man should be able to go. Ten paces. More. Twenty feet of air under him. And came down on the black glass on his back and slid, spinning slowly, a long way further, and stopped.
And lay still.
The child had stopped singing.
Oren did not know when. The song was gone. In its place, behind him, there was a sound he did not want to hear. A thin high sound, not a word, not a scream. The sound a small animal makes in a trap.
The iron man turned back to the gold. Its stuck leg dragged. It lifted its arm. It hit the wall. Bang.
And out on the glass, twenty feet off, the man moved.
He rolled over. Slowly. Onto his front. He put his bound hands flat on the glass. He pushed. He got his knees under him. And then his feet. And he stood up.
He stood up the way he had stood up beside the way in, an hour ago. All at once. Without any fuss. Like water rising. He stood on the black glass in the low red light with blood running out of his nose into his beard and one sleeve of his coat torn half off, and he looked at the iron man limping at the wall.
As if nothing had happened.
As if he had sat down for a moment, and now he was getting up again.
The child made her sound again. Smaller.
"He's all right," said Oren. Through his teeth. He did not know if it was true. "He's up, lass. Look. He's up. Open your eyes."
He did not hear if she did.
The man stood out there on the glass, outside the gold, alone. Twenty feet from the wall. With nothing in his hands. With the four in gray behind him and the pale one to his left and Kross standing very still beyond them all. And he did not look at any of them.
He looked at the iron man with the stuck leg. And began, slowly, to walk back toward it.
