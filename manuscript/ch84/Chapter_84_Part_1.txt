Chapter 84: The Siege of the Eye, Part 1 of 5: The Child on the Step
The Child on the Step
Oren carried the girl down the iron steps asleep over his shoulder and came back up for the silent man, who would not come.
He had known the man would not come. He had asked him every night since the Eye, and every night the man had looked south and said nothing. But a captain asks. That was the rule. You asked a man, even when you knew the answer, because one night the answer might be different, and if you had stopped asking you would never know.
So he climbed back up the twelve steps in the small hours, slowly, with his hand on the rusty rail and his back grinding at every step like a cart with a dry axle. One. Two. Three. He did not count them the way the lass did. He felt them. Each one ringing under his boot, low and hollow. Twelve. And then the iron landing, and the cold coming in through the gap where they had slid the big iron door nearly shut, and the starlight.
He put his shoulder to the gap and looked out.
The man was sitting where he always sat. To the left of the gap, from out here. His back against the curve of the dome. His long legs crossed. His bound hands palm up on his knees. And beside him on the glass, the blanket Oren had put down a minute ago, folded, where he would not use it.
He was looking south.
Oren looked south too.
Three hundred paces off across the black glass, there was the camp. He could see it clear enough. Four lanterns, turned low, in a rough ring, and the dark heaps of men asleep round them. And a little way off from the ring, on his own, a man sitting up. A slight shape. A hat on the glass beside him. Not moving.
Him.
Oren had seen him in the city. More than once. A slight gray man in a gray coat, at the bottom of the steps of the Ministry, at the door of the Bourse, at the foot of the white stair in the Spire. Kross. The Inquisitor. The lass's father's friend, or his jailer, or both. Oren did not know which. He did not think the lass knew either.
He walks in the dark, he had said, on the Glass, three nights ago, when he first saw the four yellow lanterns coming on past the frozen iron. And it had been true. And now here he was, sitting on the black glass three hundred paces off in the small hours, with his hat beside him, not asleep.
And in front of the camp, in a row, facing this way, the three iron men.
Standing still. Frozen, the way they froze in the black dark. Their blank egg heads catching a little starlight. Like three men waiting outside a house for somebody to come out.
Oren looked at them for a long time.
Then he looked down at the man by the door.
"They'll come at first light," he said quietly.
The man did not answer. Of course he did not.
"You know that," said Oren. "You've been watching them all night. You've been watching that way for days. I thought you were looking at nothing." He shook his head slowly. "You weren't. You were looking for them. You knew they'd come over that edge."
The man went on looking south.
"Come in," said Oren. "There's a fire. You can sit by the stair. Nobody'll make you go further. You can watch the door from the inside as well as the out."
Nothing.
Oren crouched down. It hurt. He did it anyway, slowly, one hand on his knee, until his face was level with the man's. Close. He had not been this close to him before. The man smelled of old smoke and dust and cold, and of something else under those, something Oren could not name. Like the smell in an old church, perhaps. Like stone that has had a great many people pray on it.
The man's face was thin. Very thin. The bones of it stood out under the skin like the ribs of a boat under a sail. There was a cut down one cheek, from the ear to the beard, scabbed over, where the steel spring had whipped across him in the Ridges. His beard was dark and untidy and had gray in it, and his eyes were dark, and deep, and they did not move from the south.
But they were not empty. Oren saw that now. Close up, in the starlight. He had thought, at first, in the Fold Hills, that there was nobody behind those eyes at all. A house with all its lamps out. He had been wrong. There was somebody. A long way back. Like a man sitting in the last room of a big house, with all the doors between shut, listening to somebody knock at the front.
"Suit yourself," said Oren gently.
He straightened up. His back cracked.
"But I'm taking the girl," he said. "She's not sitting out here when that lot comes. She's a child."
The man did not move.
And then he did.
Not much. His head turned. A little way. From the south, toward the girl. Toward the small hump of blankets and coat against his left side, where Tansy lay asleep with her mouth open and her hair sticking up out of the top of the blankets like a tuft of dry grass.
He looked at her.
Then he looked at Oren.
And Oren could have sworn, afterward, though he would not have said it to anybody, that the man nodded. Not with his head. With his eyes. The way you nod at a man across a room when you cannot speak because of the noise.
Then he looked south again.
Oren bent and lifted her.
She did not wake. She was a light thing. Lighter than she should have been. He had carried his lordship's girl up to bed when she was ten and fell asleep on the stair counting, and she had weighed more than this one did at twelve. He got his hands under the blankets and his own coat that was over the top of them and brought the whole bundle up off the glass and settled it over his left shoulder, with one hand flat on the back of it where her head was. The way you carry a sleeping child home from a fair.
She mumbled something into his shoulder. Something about a pie.
"Hush," said Oren. "It's only me."
She went quiet.
He bent once more, awkwardly, with her held against him, and picked up the folded blanket from the glass beside the man and put it down again closer, against the man's knee, where he would have to move it to get up.
The man did not look at it.
Oren went back in through the gap. Sideways, because of the girl and because of his size, with his head bent under the iron. He stood on the landing a moment with her on his shoulder and looked back out through the gap. At the man sitting alone now beside the dome, in the cold, with the blanket against his knee. Looking south. At the lanterns. At the three iron men. At the slight shape sitting up beside his hat.
And then Oren went down the steps.
One. Two. Three. Ringing. With the child asleep on his shoulder and his back grinding. Twelve. And along the passage in the dark, in the old man's track, to the guard room and the red glow of the brazier.
The lass was awake. She was sitting up by the fire with her knees drawn up and her bandaged hand in her lap, and she looked up when he came in and saw what he had on his shoulder, and her face did a thing that he had seen it do before. When she was ten, on the wall at the back of the house, and he caught her before she fell.
She did not say anything. She moved over, on the floor, and made a space by the fire, close to the brazier but not too close, and he knelt and laid the child down in it, blankets and coat and all, and tucked the coat round her. She did not wake. She turned over on her side toward the warmth and put her thumb in her mouth.
Twelve years old, and her thumb in her mouth.
Oren knelt there and looked at her.
"He wouldn't come," said the lass. Very low.
"No."
"Did you think he would?"
"No." Oren got up, slowly, with a hand on the wall. "But you ask."
Heard
The lad was awake too.
Oren saw it when he turned from the child. The lad was lying on the bench under the brown coat on its hook, on his side, with the old man's book under his head. His eyes were open. In the red light from the brazier they looked black.
Oren went and sat down on the end of the bench by his feet. It creaked. He did not care.
For a while neither of them said anything.
"What did you knock?" said the lad. Low. So as not to wake the child. "The other night. When he knocked on the door. You knocked back."
Oren looked at him.
"Lyra told me," said the lad. "In the morning. It's in the book. She said you knocked something back. Not the four, two, nine, three. Something else. She said it was her father's." He was quiet a moment. "I don't remember. I wasn't there. I was asleep."
"Heard," said Oren.
"Heard?"
"That's what it says. When you knock it. Heard." Oren rubbed his beard. "It's an old call. Older than me. The army used to beat it out when a message came in, so the man who sent it knew it had got there. A quick run. A stop. A slower run. And one more, on its own, at the end." He put out his right hand and knocked it on the wooden seat of the bench beside the lad's foot. Softly. Tap-tap-tap-tap. Stop. Tap. Tap. Tap. And one more. Tap. "Like that. Heard. It means, I've got your message. I'm here. Go on."
The lad lay looking at the place on the bench where Oren's knuckles had been.
"Again," he said.
Oren did it again.
The lad put out his own hand. His left. The gray one, with the black fingertips that could not feel anything. He laid it on the bench beside Oren's. And he knocked. Slowly. Too slowly. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Stop. And then he stopped, and frowned, and looked at his hand.
"The slow bit," he said.
"Three. And then one."
The lad did it. Tap. Tap. Tap. And then one. Tap.
"That's it," said Oren.
The lad did it again, by himself, a little faster. Then again. Oren watched his gray knuckles on the old gray wood in the red light. The lad's lips were moving. Counting. He could count that far. Four, and three, and one. All under nine.
"Her father did that," said the lad. "On her hand. In the white room. At the end. She told me. When she said she was there, he did that." He stopped knocking. "And I did it too. On his. When he did the four, two, nine, three on mine. It's in the book. I wrote it. I tapped heard." He was quiet. "I didn't know what it meant. I just knew it was the thing you do back."
"Aye," said Oren. "It is."
They sat.
Across the room the child turned over in her sleep and said something about a goat.
"Oren," said the lad.
"Lad."
"I'm frightened."
He said it quite plainly. Not whispering. Not ashamed. The way he said everything, now, since the city. Since he had stopped being able to keep anything in his head long enough to pretend about it. I'm frightened. As if he were saying, It's cold, or My leg hurts.
Oren did not say anything for a moment.
"So am I," he said.
The lad turned his head on the old man's book and looked at him.
"Are you?"
"Saints, lad. I've been frightened every morning of my life before a fight. Every single one. The ones who say they're not are either lying or mad, and I've served under both, and I'd rather have the liars." He looked at the brazier. "Being frightened's not the trouble. Everybody's frightened. The trouble's what you do with it."
"What do you do with it?"
Oren thought about that. Nobody had ever asked him.
"I stand on it," he said at last. "Like a step. I find the place in me that's frightened, and I put my foot on it, and I stand up on it. And then I'm a bit taller than I was. That's all." He shrugged. "It doesn't go away. It's just under your boot instead of in your throat."
The lad was quiet for a long time.
"She's not frightened," he said. "Sola. In me. I can feel her. She's not frightened of anything." He looked at his gray hand on the bench. "If I let her, she'd go out there and do it. Tomorrow. She'd walk out and tear their backs off. I can feel her wanting to. In my hands." He closed the hand. "But I'd have to burn her. And I promised."
"Aye. You told us."
"And Vespera says it goes to my face."
"She does."
"What would you do?" said the lad. "If it was you."
Oren did not answer at once. He looked at the lad's closed gray fist on the bench. At the gray going up the wrist under the cuff. At the lad's face in the red light, thin and white and young, younger than he was, with the dark eyes looking at Oren as if Oren might know.
He did not know.
"I'd not burn," he said slowly. "If I didn't have to. That's the first thing. You don't burn because you can. You don't burn because it'd be quicker, or because it'd look grand, or because there's a bit of somebody in you who wants you to." He paused. "You burn because somebody's going to die if you don't. And then you burn as little as you can. And you choose, if you've time."
"And if I haven't time?"
"Then you reach," said Oren. "And you find out afterward what you've given."
The lad looked at him.
"Is that what happens to you?"
"Every time."
The lad said nothing for a long while. Then he put his gray hand flat on the bench again, beside Oren's, and knocked. Softly. A quick run of four. A stop. Three, slower. And one more, alone.
Heard.
Oren looked at the lad's hand.
Then he knocked it back.
And neither of them said anything else, and after a while Oren got up off the end of the bench, slowly, with a hand on his back, and went and sat down against the wall by the doorway with his sword and his whetstone.
Before Dawn
Nobody slept after that. Not really.
They did not say they were not sleeping. They lay where they were, round the red brazier, under their blankets, with their eyes shut. But Oren could hear them. You could always hear, in a barracks, when men were not sleeping. The breathing was wrong. Too careful. Too quiet. Like men listening.
He did not lie down. He sat on the floor with his back against the wall by the guard room doorway and his sword across his knees and his whetstone in his hand, and he worked the stone along the edge. Slowly. The long even strokes. Eyes shut.
The stone caught, near the point. It always caught there. At the notch.
He did not need to look at the notch. He knew where it was the way you know where a missing tooth is. It had been there since before the Glass. A little bite out of the edge a hand's width from the point, from some fight he had half forgotten. And the iron man's neck in the Ridges, two nights ago, sparks and a silver scratch and the blade skidding off, had not done it any good. It would not come out. Not with a whetstone. It needed a forge, and a smith, and a day. He knew that. He went on drawing the stone along it anyway. Out of habit. Because his hands wanted something to do, and that was what they knew.
Across the room, Kestrel was not lying down either.
She was sitting cross-legged by the other wall, near the passage, with her spear across her knees. The way she always sat. Facing the way out. Her eyes were open. She was not looking at anything.
Oren watched her for a while.
"Captain," she said. Without turning her head.
"Kestrel."
"At first light."
"Aye. As soon as they can see."
Kestrel nodded slowly. "The iron does not like the dark. But it does not mind the cold. As soon as the sun is up, it will walk." She was quiet a moment. "And the men behind it. They have water?"
"Not much. Not enough. I saw the casks on their backs, coming in. Lying flat." Oren drew the stone along the edge. "They'll want this place. Not just for the lad. For the well."
"Then they will come hard."
"Aye."
She was quiet again.
"How do we hold it?" she said.
It was not the kind of question she usually asked. Kestrel did not ask questions. She told you things, in her flat calm voice, the way you would tell a child where the edge of the cliff was. Oren looked at her.
"We hold the top of the stair," he said. "There's only one way in. The iron one. If they want in, they come through that gap, one at a time. A man at the top of a stair with a sword can hold a gap like that against ten men, if he's fresh, and if they're coming up at him." He stopped. "They're not men, though. The iron. You can't hold iron with a sword. We found that out."
"Yes."
"So we hold the men," said Oren, "and we keep out of the iron's way, and when the iron comes close, we do what we did in the Ridges. Jam the knees. Go up the back. Pull the spring." He looked at the edge of his sword in the red light. At the notch. "The tall one did it twice. Bare-handed. I don't know who else can."
Kestrel did not answer that.
Behind her, under a blanket by the brazier, Oren heard the lad move.
He had not been asleep either. Oren had known that. He had been lying on his side on the stone floor with the old man's book under his head and the green book inside his shirt, and his gray hands tucked under his chin, and his eyes shut. Not humming. That was how Oren knew. The lass had told him, on the Glass. He hums when he's not afraid. If it stops, he's frightened.
He had not hummed all night.
Now he sat up. Slowly. With his stiff leg out in front of him.
"I can do it," he said.
Nobody answered him.
"The springs," said the lad. "I can do it. Here. In the Eye." He held up one gray hand and looked at it. "She's in my hands. Sola. I can feel her. In the Ridges I stood up in front of it and I didn't fall down. That was her." He closed the hand slowly. "Here I can do more than stand."
"You can," said a voice from the bench.
Vespera. Lying flat on her back under two blankets, with her boots side by side on the floor below her and her blue hands folded on her chest like a dead woman's. Her eyes were shut. She did not open them.
"You can, harvester," she said. "And it'll cost you. Here. Not like the Glass. Here, everything costs." She opened her eyes and looked at the stone ceiling. "You know what you'd have to do. To use her properly. Not just stand. Fight."
The lad did not answer.
"You'd have to burn her," said Vespera. "What's in you of her. Her mornings, her hands, her horse. Burn it, like a candle, to make the hands work. That's what she is in you. Ash. Not you. Hers. And Ash you burn." She turned her head on the bench and looked at him. "And when you burn, the gray climbs."
The lad looked down at his hands.
They were gray to the shoulders, both of them. Oren had seen them. At the Edge, in the snow, the lad had rolled up his sleeves to wash in the meltwater, and the gray had gone all the way up, under the cloth, smooth and cold as river stone, to the round of the shoulder. And stopped there. Just. A line, at the top of each arm, like the high-water mark on a wall.
"I promised," said the lad slowly. "The doctor. Silas. In the city. I promised I wouldn't burn myself."
"She's not yourself," said Vespera. "That's the clever bit. That's what you'll tell yourself." She shut her eyes again. "It'll still climb."
Nobody said anything for a long time.
Oren drew the whetstone along the blade. The long even stroke. It caught at the notch.
And he thought about words.
He could not help it. He had been thinking about them since they came down off the gray stones onto the Eye, two days ago, and the hum stopped, and he felt them. All of them. His words. Moving inside him. Like fish in a net. On the Glass they had lain still, all of them, the ones he had left. Safe. Nobody could take them. He could not have burned one if he had wanted to. He had slept, out there, for the first time in years, without counting them.
Now they moved. He could feel them, if he went still, the way you can feel your own heart if you go still enough. Swimming about in him. Waiting. Each one a little flash of silver. Each one a thing he could give to the light, if he chose, and get something back for.
He had six holes already. He knew them. He did not need the lad's book to tell him. The shut, that was one. The thing on a gate with a key. The hard cold, that was two. The stuff on a pond in winter. The round things the boys beat, before a march. The thing you sit on. The little one for soup. The white flowers. Six. Six words gone, six times he had needed something more than a sword could give him, and paid.
And tomorrow, today, in a few hours, he might need a seventh.
Which one?
He did not know. You did not know, until it came. That was the thing nobody told you, when they taught you to burn. They told you to choose. Pick a word you'll not miss, lad. Something small. Something you hardly use. And you tried. You lay awake and thought about which. And then the moment came, and a man was coming at you with a blade, and there was no time to choose, and you reached in and took the first silver thing your hand closed on.
And it was gone. And you found out afterward what it had been.
He drew the stone along the blade.
Across the room, the lass had lain down again. She was lying on her side by the brazier, between the sleeping child and the wall, with her bandaged hand tucked under her cheek. Her eyes were open. She was looking at him.
He did not know how long she had been looking.
She did not say anything. Neither did he. They looked at each other across the dying red of the brazier, the old soldier and the girl he had met on a wall, and Oren thought, she knows. She knows what I'm thinking. She's thinking it too.
He looked away first. Down at the sword.
The stone caught at the notch.
And up the stair, through the gap in the iron, the dark began, very slowly, to go gray.
