Chapter 84: The Siege of the Eye, Part 5 of 5: Kestrel
Kestrel
Kestrel went after him.
Oren saw her go. Out of the corner of his eye, past his own shaking sword. One moment she was in the gap behind him with her spear upright and her white cape hanging. The next she was moving. Along the curve of the dome, to the right, toward the narrow place where the gold did not meet the iron rib. Fast and low and silent, the way she had moved across the glass on the night of the Ridges.
"Kestrel," said Oren.
She did not stop.
"Kestrel! Back! Get back in the line!"
She stopped.
Not because of him. He knew that. She stopped at the narrow gap with one hand on the iron rib and her spear in the other, and stood there. Looking out. At the man, walking slowly back across the open glass toward the iron one with the stuck leg. At the four in gray behind him with their hands in their coats. At the pale one, writing. At Kross.
And Oren saw her see what he had seen. That it was open glass out there. That there was nothing to put your back to. That there were three iron men and four men with stones, and the man had nothing in his hands, and if she went out through that gap she would be one more body on the glass with nothing in its hands. And her people behind her, without her.
She stood there for a long moment with her hand on the rib.
Then she came back.
She did not say anything. She came back along the curve of the dome and took her place in the gap again, in front of the iron thing, with her spear upright beside her. And her face was perfectly still. But Oren saw her hand on the shaft of the spear, and her knuckles were white.
"He chose," said Kestrel. Very low. Not to him. To herself. "He chose to go."
"Aye."
"Then we let him."
"Aye."
She did not look at Oren. She looked out through the thinning gold at the man walking on the glass.
"This one had a brother," she said. "Who chose."
Oren did not say anything. There was nothing to say. He thought of the yard in the Ridges. The hide over the long body with its face bare to the stars. The plait with the red thread laid on its chest. The bone flute in Kestrel's coat, over her heart, that she had played from the top of the dome at sunset with her own last note going down.
Bang.
The gold shook. His teeth jarred.
Behind him the child was crying now. Not the trap sound. Proper crying, the way a child cries, with great gulping sobs and her breath going in and out in hiccups. The lass had her. Oren could not see, but he could hear. The lass had let go of his arm and gone back and got hold of the child, and was holding her. He could hear the lass's voice under the crying, low and quick and steady. Saying the same thing over and over. He could not hear what.
Then he could.
"Count with me," the lass was saying. "Count with me, Tansy. One. Two. Count with me. Three. He's up. He's walking. Look. Four. Five. He's all right. Count with me. Six—"
And after a while, thin and hiccupping, the child's voice came in with hers.
"Seven. Eight."
"Nine," said the lass. "Ten. Good. Eleven. Keep going."
"Twelve," said the child. "Thirteen."
"Fourteen," said the lass. And Oren heard her voice catch on it. Just a little. Just once. And go on. "Fifteen. Keep going, Tansy. Don't stop."
Oren stood with his back to the iron thing and his sword up and the gold going thin, and listened to them count, and something in his chest that had been clenched like a fist since the small hours came very slightly undone.
Brin had come forward. Without his spear. He stood just behind Oren's right shoulder, where the man had stood, with his big empty hands hanging and his ribs strapped and his slow round face set. Waiting. Oren did not know for what. He did not think Brin knew either. He only thought that Brin had seen the man go out with his spear, and had come to stand in the man's place, because somebody should.
Sef was at the gap behind Kestrel. Oda and Marrit on either side of it, against the dome. Their spears low. Their gray-lined faces turned toward the open glass, where the man walked.
And out there, the man had reached the iron one with the stuck leg.
It had its back to him again. It was hitting the gold. Limping, its stuck leg dragging, the broken stub of Brin's spear still jammed in the back of its knee like a thorn in a horse's hoof. Bang. Bang. Every blow a little crooked, now, because of the leg. Oren felt the difference. The blows from that one came into him a little to one side.
The man stood behind it, a pace off, and looked at its back.
At the brass plate in the small of it. Where the key went. Where the spring was.
He did not go for it. Not yet. He stood and watched it, the way you watch a horse before you go up behind it, to see which way it kicks.
And behind him, on the glass, the big man with the broken nose had taken his carbine off his back.
Oren saw it. He saw the big man swing it round and up and set the butt to his shoulder and lay his cheek against the stock. Aiming. Not at the wall. At the man. At the thin long back in its torn coat, a pace behind the iron one with the stuck leg, twenty paces from the barrel.
And Oren could not move. He could not let go of the sword.
"Harrow."
It was not loud. It came across the glass from behind the four in gray. Quiet. Level. Without any weight on it at all.
The big man's cheek came off the stock.
He did not lower the carbine. He held it where it was, at his shoulder, and turned his head. And Oren saw Kross, twenty paces back, standing exactly where he had stood all morning, with his bare right hand at his side. Not moving. Looking at the big man.
That was all. Just looking.
The big man looked back at him for a long moment.
Then he lowered the carbine. Slowly. And swung it onto his back again.
And the pale one, off to the left, had seen that too. Oren saw his head turn. Saw him look at Kross. Saw him look down at his book.
And write.
The Crutch
He heard them from the stair.
Not at first. At first there was only the pounding, and the child counting, and his own breath. But the gap in the iron thing was at his back, and the stair went down behind it into the dark, and sound came up stairs. It always had. In every house he had ever lived in, the stair was where you heard things you were not meant to hear.
He heard Vespera.
Her voice. Thin and sharp. Coming up out of the dark at the bottom, from the step where she had laid out her needles.
"Sit down."
And then the lad. Lower. Oren could not hear the words. Only the sound of him.
"Sit down, harvester." Vespera's voice cracked on it. "Did you hear me? I said your face. I've told you. I'll not tell you again."
The lad's voice again. Louder. And this time Oren heard it.
"They need me."
"They need you alive with your name in your head. That's what they need. That's what that girl up there has been reading into you every morning for a week. That's what I put on your skin." A sound. Something falling over on the step. A pot. "You go up there and do what she wants you to do with your hands and you'll come back down with it gone. You hear? Your name. It's in your mouth, harvester. That's where it goes. The gray. It goes in your mouth."
A silence.
Bang. The gold shook. Oren's teeth jarred.
"I'm not going to burn me," said the lad.
Oren heard it quite clearly. The lad's voice had gone quiet again. Steady. The way it went when he had written a thing down in the green book and was reading it back.
"I'm going to burn her," said the lad. "Not me. Her. What's in me of her. You said so yourself. She's Ash. She's not me."
"I said that's what you'd tell yourself."
"Then I'm telling myself."
"It'll still climb."
"I know."
A long silence.
Oren stood with his back to the iron thing and his sword up and the gold going thinner, thinner, in front of him, and listened to the silence down the stair. He could not see them. He could see the shapes of them in his head. The little old woman with the blue hands, on her feet now, he thought, standing in front of the lad on the bottom step, with her palm flat on his chest. And the lad. Standing too.
"She's a good soldier," said the lad. Very quietly. "Sola. She doesn't want me hurt. I can feel that. She doesn't want anybody hurt. She only wants to stop them." A pause. "She wants to stop them so badly. She's been wanting it for sixty years. She stood on a horse for sixty years wanting it."
Vespera did not answer.
"I'll give her back to them," said the lad. "That's all. I'll give her back. What she was for. It's not taking. It's giving."
"It's burning."
"Is there a difference?"
The silence went on so long that Oren thought it would not end.
Then Vespera said something. Very low. He did not catch it. It might have been a curse. It might have been the lad's name. It might have been something else, a word in the northern tongue she still had a little of under her city one, that Oren did not know.
And then there was a sound he did know.
A long dry clatter. Wood on stone. Rolling. The sound a stick makes when you let go of it and it falls and bounces and rolls away along a floor.
The crutch.
He had left it.
And then nothing. No steps. Only the old woman's breathing, ragged, down there in the dark, and the lad's, and the bang of the iron on the gold. As if the lad were standing at the foot of the stair with his hand on the rail, and could not yet make himself climb.
Not for a Wall
The gold was going thin.
Oren felt it before he saw it. A kind of tiredness, coming up into his arms from the sword. Not his own tiredness. He was tired, Saints knew, his back was one long burning ache from the hip to the neck and his teeth hurt and his hands had gone numb on the hilt. But this was something else. A tiredness in the light. The way a candle gets tired near the end, and the flame goes small and blue at the bottom, and leans.
Then he saw it.
The wall had been gold. Deep gold at the rim, the gold of Vespera's pot, and paler in the middle, like honey held up to a window. Now the middle was going pale. Paler. Like honey with water in it. He could see through it more clearly than he had. The iron man in front of him, drawing back its arm, was not blurred anymore. He could see every rivet on its chest plate. He could see the scratch his own sword had made across its neck in the Ridges, two nights ago. A long bright line.
Bang.
The gold shook. It had not shaken before. It shook where the shovel hand hit it, the way water shakes in a bucket when you kick it. And the shake went out along the whole curve of the wall, both ways, to the dome, and came back.
And the shock that came into Oren's hands was worse. Much worse. It went up his arms like a cart going over cobbles and into his shoulders and he heard himself grunt.
The word was running out.
He understood it then. Not with his head. With his body, the way you understand that the ground is giving way under your feet on a riverbank before you have time to think it. A word was only a word. One word. It had been a big one, a word he had used every day of his life, a word with a great deal of him in it. It had made a big wall. But it was one word, and the iron men were hitting it, and every blow took something out of it, and there was only so much in it to take.
It was going.
And when it went, there would be nothing between the iron and the lass and the child and the way down.
Bang. Bang.
The gold shook again. In the middle, near the gap, where the third iron man was hitting it, a thin line appeared. Pale. Almost white. Running up the wall from the glass, crooked, like a crack in the frost on a window.
Oren's words came.
He did not call them. They came anyway. They had been waiting all this time, swimming in the dark of him, close, closer, watching his hand. And now his hand was shaking on the sword and the wall was going and they knew it. They came up all round his hand like fish round bread. Silver. Bright. Pushing at his fingers.
Me, they said. Me. Take me. You need another. Here.
He could feel them. He could feel every one. Rope. Bread. Morning. Horse. Lad. Lass. He could take any one of them. He could close his hand on any one of them and burn it and the wall would come back up, deep gold, honey-gold, Vespera's gold, and hold. For a while. For another while.
And then go thin again. And then another word.
And another.
He knew how it went. He had seen it in the long war, in the old men who had come back from it. In the veterans' hospital on the river, where he had gone once as a young man to visit a sergeant who had taught him. Rows of beds. Men sitting up in them, with their mouths open, trying to say something, and nothing coming. Men who had held a wall, once, somewhere, on a bad day. And then held it again. And again.
His hand began to close.
He did not decide. That was the thing. That was always the thing. He did not decide. His hand closed, on the hilt, and the fish came up under his fingers, and one of them was there, right there, the brightest, the closest, and his fingers were closing on it—
Something gripped his arm.
Hard. Very hard. Above the elbow. So hard it hurt.
He looked down.
It was the lass.
She had let go of her sword. He had not seen her do it. Her bent sword was lying on the black glass at her feet. And her left hand, her bare left hand, was round his arm, above the elbow, through his coat sleeve, and her fingers were digging in so hard that he could feel her nails through the cloth. Her bandaged right hand was pressed flat against his chest, over his heart.
She was looking up at him. Her face was white. Her eyes were wet. Her mouth was shut very tight.
Then it opened.
"No," she said.
Oren stared at her.
"No," said the lass again. Louder. Her voice cracked on it. "No. No more. Not for a wall."
He opened his mouth.
"Not for a wall," she said. She was shaking his arm now. Shaking it, as if she could shake him awake. "You've got six holes, Oren. Six. I've read them. In the book. I've read them every morning. I know every one. I know what you call them now. The shut. The hard cold. The thing you sit on." Her voice went up and broke. "You're not giving it another one. Not for a wall."
The gold shook. Bang. The crack in the middle ran higher.
"Lass," said Oren. He did not know what he was going to say. "Lass, if it goes—"
"Then it goes." She did not let go. "Then it goes and we fight. With swords. Like people. Like you taught me. From the hip." Her fingers dug in. "You told me in the horse's shadow. If I lose it, you'll have it. Remember? You said that. I've got it. I've got the cut. You don't have to give them any more of you. You don't have to give them anything."
The fish swam round his hand. Silver. Close.
Me, they said. Me.
Oren looked down at the girl he had met on a wall.
She had been ten. Sitting on the top of the back wall of the garden on Crown Walk, in a green dress torn at the knee, with her legs hanging over the drop on the far side and her hands flat on the coping, looking down at the alley as if she were thinking about jumping. And he had been new. A week in the house. A big man in a borrowed coat, straight out of the army, who did not know what to do with his hands. And he had stood at the bottom of the wall in the garden and looked up at her and said, That's a long way down, miss. And she had looked at him and said, I know. I've counted the bricks. And then she had slipped.
And he had caught her.
He had not thought about it. He had just put out his arms.
And she had looked up at him, from his arms, with her face white and her green dress torn, exactly the way she was looking up at him now. And she had said, You didn't have to do that. And he had said, Yes, miss. I did.
He looked at her now. On the black glass of the Eye, with her hand round his arm and the wall going thin.
And he let the fish go.
He opened his hand. Inside. Not on the sword. Inside, where the words were. He opened his fingers, and the bright one that had been closest, the one he had almost closed on, slipped away from them. He did not know which one it was. He would never know. It slipped away into the dark of him and swam off and was gone, back among the others, safe.
"All right," he said. "All right, lass."
She did not let go of his arm.
The gold wall shook. Bang. The crack went all the way up to the rim. And at the rim, where it was deepest, Vespera's gold, it began, very slowly, to fade.
Sola's Hands
He heard the steps behind him.
The iron steps. The twelve of them, going down from the gap into the dark. He heard them ring. Not the way they rang under his own boots, deep and slow. Not the way they rang under the lass's, quick and light. Not the way they rang under the lad's, step and crutch, step and crutch, a heavy note and a light one.
They rang fast.
Very fast. One-two-three-four, ringing up out of the dark, so close together that they ran into each other. Like a man running up a stair.
No crutch.
Oren turned his head.
He could not turn far. He could not let go of the sword. But he turned his head enough to see the gap, over his shoulder, past Kestrel's white cape. And he saw the lad come up out of it.
He did not come up the way the lad came up stairs. The lad came up stairs like a heron climbing a bank. Slow. Careful. Stiff leg first. Crutch, then foot, then crutch. Looking down at each step to be sure it was there.
This was not that.
He came up out of the dark low and fast, with his head down and his shoulders forward, taking the steps two at a time, and his stiff leg did not seem to be stiff at all. He came up into the gap and through it, sideways, without touching the iron frame, without bending his head because he did not need to, he had gauged it exactly. And he stopped on the black glass just behind Kestrel, in the low red light. Balanced. On the balls of his feet. Light. Still.
He had no crutch.
He had left it on the stair. Or at the bottom. Somewhere behind him, in the dark, with Vespera and her needles. He stood there without it, with his weight even on both legs, and the lame one did not shake.
And his hands were closed.
Both of them. His gray hands, gray to the shoulder under his coat, with the black dead fingertips. Closed into fists. Hanging at his sides. Not tight. Easy. The way an old soldier carries his fists when he is walking into a fight he has walked into a hundred times before. Loose at the wrist. Ready.
Oren had seen men move like that. Not many. A few. In the long war, the old ones told of a few, and in his own war he had seen two or three. Sergeants, mostly. Men who had done it so long that their bodies did it without them. Who walked into a fight the way other men walk into a kitchen.
The lad had never moved like that in his life.
But she had.
Oren knew it the way he had known the voice, in the dark, on the Glass. Vance. He knew it in his blood. He looked at the lad standing on the black glass behind Kestrel, light and still, with his gray fists easy at his sides and his head a little down, and it was not the lad he saw.
It was a big woman with a braid pinned up under an iron helmet and a burn scar down her jaw. Standing in a ditch. Waiting for a gray army to come up the road.
The lad's face was white. Whiter than Oren had ever seen it. His mouth was shut hard. And his eyes, when they turned toward Oren, were his own. Frightened. Very frightened. And sorry.
But the rest of him was not his.
"Lad," said Oren. His voice came out rough. "Lad. What are you—"
The lad did not answer him.
He moved.
Past Kestrel. Past the four Ashborn. Between Oren and the lass, so close that his coat brushed Oren's arm. And he did not stop. He went past them, to the front, to the very edge of the gold. Where it was thinnest. Where the crack ran up it, pale and crooked, from the glass to the fading rim.
He stood there. With the gold a hand's width from his face. And the iron man on the other side of it, drawing back its arm.
And behind Oren, on the stair, far down, Oren heard Vespera's voice. Thin and sharp and furious. Calling something. A name. The lad's name.
The lad did not turn round.
The Word That Is Gone
He turned his head. Just his head. And looked back at Oren over his shoulder.
His eyes were his own. Frightened, and sorry, and very clear.
"Hold the door," he said.
Oren opened his mouth.
He meant to say it back. The way you do. The way he had said it a thousand times, on a thousand walls, to a thousand men going out past him into the dark. Aye. I'll hold it. Go on. The words were there. They were in his mouth. He could feel the shape of them.
"Hold the—" he said.
And stopped.
The lad was looking at him. Waiting.
"Hold the—" said Oren again. "The—"
It was not there.
He reached for it. He reached the way you reach for the rail on a stair in the dark, without looking, knowing it will be there because it has always been there. He reached for the word the lad had said. The word for the thing at his back. The iron thing, with the gap down its side, slid back on its runners into the curve of the dome. The thing he was standing in front of. The thing he had been standing in front of all his life.
His hand closed on nothing.
Nothing at all. Not a shape. Not an echo. Not the faint ghost of a sound, the way some of the others had left a ghost behind them for a while, a kind of itch, before they went for good. There was nothing. A clean place. Swept. As if there had never been anything there.
He knew what it was. He could feel the cold iron of it at his back, against his shoulder blades. He knew it was the way in. He knew it was iron. He knew you went through it, and knocked on it, and held it. He knew he had burned the word for it an hour ago, standing on this glass with his back to it, to make the wall that was going thin in front of him now.
He had not known, until this moment, that it was gone. Not really. Not the way you know a thing when you go to use it and it is not there.
He stood with his mouth open.
"Hold the…" he said. "The…"
The lad was still looking at him. And Oren saw the lad's face change. Saw him understand. Saw it go through him, the knowing, like a cold wind through a coat. The lad's eyes went to the iron thing behind Oren, and back to Oren's face, and Oren saw him know what had happened, and what it had cost, and who had paid it.
The lad did not say anything.
He only nodded. Once. The way one soldier nods to another, across a line, when there is no time for anything else.
Then he turned back to the gold.
And Oren stood behind him with his sword up and his mouth still open on a word he would never say again, and the lass's hand still gripping his arm, and the wall going thin in front of them all, and could not find it.
