Chapter 84: The Siege of the Eye, Part 3 of 5: The Man Stands Up
The Man Stands Up
It was the child.
She had woken. Of course she had. Nobody could have slept through the ticking. She was standing in the gap behind Kestrel, with Oren's big coat still round her shoulders like a cape and its hem dragging on the iron, and her hair standing straight up on one side where she had slept on it, and her face white as milk.
"No," said Oren, and Kestrel, and the lass, all at once.
The child looked at them. Then she looked past them. Out at the glass. At the iron men, coming.
And then she looked to the side. Along the curve of the dome. At the man.
He was still sitting there. To the left of the gap, from out here. With his back against the dome and his bound hands palm up on his knees and the folded blanket against his knee where Oren had put it. Looking south. He had not moved since the small hours. He had not moved when the stone cracked and the blue-white fire hit the frame above Oren's head. He had not moved when Oren came out and drew his sword, or the lass, or Kestrel and her four.
The iron men were fifty paces off.
And he got up.
Oren saw it at the edge of his eye and turned his head. He could not help it. The man got up the way he did everything, all at once and without any fuss, like water rising in a well, like a cat getting off a wall. One moment he was sitting. The next he was standing, at his full height, taller than the lad, nearly as tall as Oren himself, thin as a rake, with his long arms hanging loose at his sides and his bound hands open.
The folded blanket fell off his knee onto the glass. He did not look at it.
He walked along the curve of the dome. Five steps. Six. To Oren's right side. The side the lass was not on.
And stopped there. A pace off. Facing the glass. Facing the iron men.
He did not look at Oren. He did not look at anybody. He stood with his bound hands loose at his sides and the long scabbed cut down his cheek and his dark untidy beard, and he looked at the iron men coming, the way he had looked south all night. Steadily. Without blinking. Without any sign at all.
Nobody had asked him.
Oren looked at him sideways for a long moment.
The man's hands were a mess. Oren could see that from here. Tansy's strips of gray shirt were black with old blood across the palms and fingers, and stiff, and the new blood had come through in places and dried again in brown blotches. He had torn the springs out of two iron men with those hands, in the Ridges, and his fingers had gone in among the wheels to the bone. Vespera had looked at them, on the beach, and gone white, and wrapped them again, and said nothing at all. She did not often say nothing.
He could not hold a sword with those hands. He could not hold a spear. He could not hold anything.
He had come anyway.
"All right," said Oren quietly. To the man. Not looking at him. "All right, then. You take the right."
The man did not answer.
But he did not move away, either.
And behind them, in the gap, the child had stopped asking if she could come out. Oren heard her go quiet. And then he heard her step. One step. Out onto the glass. Behind them. Behind the man. With Oren's big coat dragging off her shoulders, and her bare feet on the cold black glass, and her breath coming quick and short.
"Tansy," said the lass. Sharp. "Back. Get back in."
"No."
"Tansy—"
"No." The child's voice was shaking. It was very small. "He's out. If he's out, I'm out. I keep the door. I said. We said. We keep the door."
Oren did not turn round. The iron men were thirty paces off.
"Then keep it from behind him," he said. "Right behind him. Don't come past his elbow. Not a hair past it. Do you hear me?"
A small sound behind him. A gulp.
"Yes, Captain."
"Good lass."
He did not look back to see if she had done it. He did not need to. He felt her. A small warm thing, close behind the man's right elbow, shaking.
And so they stood.
On the black glass of the Eye, in front of the dead woman's door, in the low red light of the morning, with the dome at their backs. An old soldier with a notched sword. A girl with a bent one in the wrong hand. A man who could not speak with hands that could not hold anything. A child in a coat too big for her, behind his elbow. Kestrel in the gap with her spear, and four Ashborn with theirs, two of them hurt. And down the stair, out of sight, an old woman with her needles laid out on a step, and a lame man with gray hands sitting beside her, being told to wait.
Oren had stood in worse lines.
He could not, at that moment, remember when.
The iron men came on. Twenty paces. Fifteen. Tick. Tick. Tick. He could see them clearly now. Every plate. Every rivet. The fish-scale overlap of the iron on their shoulders. The brass of their joints, scratched and dented. The single finger-wide slit across each blank egg face, and in each slit, far back, a glint, like an eye at the back of a cave.
And on the glass beside them, off to the left, the pale one had stopped walking.
He had stopped and lifted his head from his book. He was looking at them. At the line in front of the door. And he was smiling. And then his lips moved.
Oren did not hear the word. He did not think he was meant to. But he saw the iron men hear it. All three. At once. He saw their heads come round on their brass necks with that little whirring click, and their stride change, lengthen, and the ticking go faster. Louder.
They were not walking anymore.
They were coming.
The Word
The first iron man came at the lass.
Oren had known it would. He did not know how. Something in the way its head turned. It came off the line of the other two at a long slant, ticking hard, straight at the left end of their little line, where she stood with her bent sword in the wrong hand.
She did not step back. He would remember that afterward. She set her feet and lifted her blade and waited for it, with her face white and her mouth shut tight.
And behind it, on the glass, the big man with the broken nose brought his hand down on his carbine.
The flash came. Blue-white. Oren's eyes shut and opened. The fire came across the glass in its crackling line and hit the curve of the dome a yard to the lass's left and the stone there went black and spat little bright chips, and the lass flinched, and in that flinch the iron man was on her.
Oren moved.
He went across in front of her the way he had gone across in front of his lordship once, on the Crescent, when a carriage horse bolted. Not thinking. His shoulder in front of hers. His sword up. And the iron man's arm came round, the great shovel hand at the end of it, flat, at the height of a man's head, and hit his blade.
It was like being hit by a door swung in a gale.
The sword did not break. It was a good sword. It had been his father's and his father's father's and it did not break. But it went back into him, the whole weight of the blow, down the blade and the hilt and into his hands and his arms and his shoulders and his bad back, and he went back with it. Two paces. His boots skidded on the glass. His back hit the dome. And the breath went out of him in one hard grunt and did not come back.
The iron man came on.
It did not stop to look at what it had done. It never did. It lifted its other arm.
And beside it, the second one had come in at the man. On the right. And the third, in the middle, straight at Kestrel and the gap.
And off to the left, the young one with the big ears was holding up his hand with something in it, closed, and his face was white and his split lips were moving, and Oren knew that in a moment there would be another flash, and another line of crackling blue fire, and this time it would not miss.
Oren had his back to the dome.
He had no breath. He had a notched sword and a bad back and the dome behind him and three iron men in front of him and a child behind a silent man's elbow and a girl at his side with her sword in the wrong hand, and he could not hold it. Not with a sword. He had known that last night, by the brazier, talking to Kestrel. You could not hold iron with a sword. He had known it in the Ridges. He knew it now, with his back against the dome and the iron man's other arm coming up.
He needed a wall.
Not a sword. A wall. Something between them and it. Something it could not walk through.
And his words came.
They came up toward his hand all at once, the way the fish in the weir pool come up when you throw in bread. Silver. Hundreds of them. Flickering in the dark of him, crowding, pushing. Take me. Take me. Here I am. He did not choose. There was no time to choose. Nobody ever had time to choose, at the end. That was the lie they told you, the old ones, when they taught you. Pick something small. You never picked. You reached.
He reached.
And the first thing his hand closed on was the thing at his back.
The thing he was standing in front of. The iron thing, slid back on its runners into the curve of the dome, with the gap down one side and the stair behind it and the dark and the fire and the old man's book and the lad. The thing a man stands in, when he is keeping something. The thing he had stood outside, every night, for twenty-two years, on Crown Walk, with his lordship's lamp lit on the other side of it. The thing on the front of a house and the back of a house and the end of a passage. The thing you knock on.
He had stood in one his whole life.
His hand closed on the word for it.
And he burned it.
It went up out of him like a breath. Not like pain. He had thought, the other six times, that it would be like pain, and it never was. It was like a breath going out. A long one. Like the breath you let out at the end of a day, when you sit down at last. It went up out of his chest and through his arms into his hands and into the sword, and out of the sword into the air, and the air caught fire.
Gold.
It came up off the glass in front of him in a line. Not fast. Not slow. The way frost comes up on a window on a cold night, if you watch it. A line of light. It came up out of the black glass in front of his boots and went out to the left, past the lass, curving, all the way to the curve of the dome on that side, and out to the right, past the man and the child behind his elbow, curving the other way, all the way to the dome on that side. A half-ring. A wall. As high as his head. Higher. Taller than the iron men.
It was thin. He could see through it. It was like looking through the gold glass in a church window. And it was not quite gold. It was paler at the middle and deeper at the edges, at the top, where it ran along like the rim of a cup. And that rim was a color he knew.
The gold round the top of Vespera's little pot. The one Hask gave her. The one she inked the lad with.
Exactly that gold.
The iron man's arm came down.
It hit the wall.
And the wall held.
Oren felt it hold. He felt it in his own body, the way you feel a blow on a shield in your arm. The shovel hand hit the gold a foot in front of his face, flat, with the whole weight of the iron behind it, and stopped. Dead. As if it had hit a cliff. And the shock of it came back through the gold and into his hands and his arms and his chest, and his teeth clacked together, and the old ache in his back went up like a flame.
But it held.
The iron man drew its hand back.
It stood there for a moment on the other side of the gold, a pace away, ticking. Its blank egg head tilted. The slit in it turned this way and that. As if it were looking at the wall. As if it did not understand what the wall was.
Then it hit it again.
Pounding
They did not stop.
That was the thing about the iron. It did not tire. It did not get angry. It did not give up. It had been told to go through, and there was a wall, and so it hit the wall. And when the wall did not break, it hit it again. And again. The same way each time. The same force. The same arm, drawn back the same distance, brought down in the same place. Like a man splitting logs who has been told to split one log and will go on until it splits.
All three of them.
The one in front of Oren. The one in front of the man, on the right. The one in the middle, in front of Kestrel and the gap. Drawing back. Hitting. Drawing back. Hitting. Not together. Each one in its own time. So that there was never a moment when one of them was not hitting.
And every blow went into Oren.
He had not known it would. Nobody had told him. When you burned a word for a wall, the wall was you. Of course it was. It was made of you. Of the thing you had given. And so every blow on it, anywhere along its whole curved length, came back to you. Into your hands. Up your arms. Into your teeth. Down your spine into your heels.
Bang.
His teeth jarred.
Bang.
His knees shook.
Bang. Bang.
He stood with his back half against the dome and his sword held up in front of him in both hands, point to the sky, and the gold coming out of the blade in a soft steady stream into the wall, and he took it. He did not know what else to do. He set his feet on the glass and took it. Each blow. The way he had taken his father's stick across his knuckles a thousand times as a boy, learning the cut. The way you take rain.
Bang.
Off to the left, the young one with the big ears cracked his stone.
The flash came, blue-white. The fire came across the glass, crackling. And hit the gold wall a yard from the lass's head.
And went to pieces.
Oren saw it. The blue fire hit the gold and burst, the way a wave bursts on a rock, into a spray of tiny blue sparks that flew out in every direction and fell hissing onto the glass and went out. The gold did not even shiver. Oren felt the fire hit it, a sharp hot sting in his left hand like a wasp, and that was all.
Another flash. The big one with the broken nose, this time. And then the other two. One after another. Blue-white, blue-white, blue-white, the crackling lines coming across the glass from four places, all at the wall, all at once. And all of them bursting on the gold in sprays of blue sparks, and falling, and going out.
It was beautiful. That was the strange thing. Oren stood there with his teeth jarring and his back on fire and his hands shaking on the hilt, and the blue sparks burst against the gold all along its curve and fell like snow, like falling stars, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.
Behind him somebody laughed. A short wild sound. Sef, he thought. Or the child.
The Enforcers stopped throwing.
He saw them stop. He saw the big one with the broken nose lower his hand and stand looking at the wall with his mouth open. The young one with the big ears had his hand in his coat, and it stayed there. The other two looked at each other.
And behind them, on the glass, Kross had not moved.
He was standing where he had stopped, twenty paces back, with his bare right hand at his side and the girl one step behind his shoulder. He had not thrown anything. He had not said anything. He was looking at the wall. At the gold of it. And at Oren behind it, holding it up.
Oren could not see his face clearly. The gold was in the way. But he saw that Kross's head was a little on one side. The way a man looks at a thing he has not seen in a long time, and is trying to remember where he saw it last.
Bang. Bang.
The iron men did not stop.
They never stopped. They hit, and hit, and hit. The gold held, and held. And every blow went into Oren and shook him to the roots of his teeth, and he held.
He did not know for how long. Time went strange. He knew that he was breathing, because he could hear it, loud and rough, in and out through his teeth. He knew that his arms were shaking, because he could see the sword shaking in front of his face. He knew that the lass was beside him, close, at his left, with her bent sword down and her eyes on him. He knew that the man was at his right, standing, not moving, with his bound hands loose at his sides, looking at the iron man on the other side of the gold as if he were reading it. And the child behind the man's elbow. Shaking.
And behind them all, in the gap, Kestrel.
And he knew one other thing.
He knew it the way you know, in the middle of the night, that the fire in the grate has gone out. Without looking. A cold place, where a warm one had been.
The word was gone.
The one he had burned. The thing at his back. He reached for it, once, in the middle of the pounding, without meaning to. The way you reach for the rail on a stair in the dark. He reached for the word for the iron thing behind him, the thing he was standing in front of, the thing with the gap down its side that led to the stair.
And his hand closed on nothing.
He knew what it was. He could feel it at his back, the cold iron of it against his shoulder blades, through his coat. He knew it was iron, and that it slid on runners, and that it had been shut for sixty years until he cut his hand and pressed his blood on the sun carved in it. He knew what it was for. He knew that you went through it, and that you knocked on it, and that men like him stood beside it all their lives.
He did not have a word for it.
The way in, he thought. The iron one.
That would have to do.
Bang.
His teeth jarred. The gold held.
Oren held.
