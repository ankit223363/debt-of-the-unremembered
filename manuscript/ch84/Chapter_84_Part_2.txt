Chapter 84: The Siege of the Eye, Part 2 of 5: Ticking
Ticking
He heard them before he saw them.
He was at the top of the stair by then. On the iron landing, with his shoulder against the edge of the gap, looking out. He had gone up as soon as the gray came, and Kestrel with him, and they had stood there together in the cold without speaking and watched the night go out of the Eye.
It went slowly. The stars went first. Not all at once. The small ones, then the bigger ones, fading, the way lamps go out along a street at dawn when the lamplighter comes round with his pole. And as they went out of the sky they went out of the glass too, the ones under his feet, so that the Eye went from a well full of stars to a floor of plain black, and then not black. Gray. Dark gray, and then lighter. Like smoke.
And then the east went pale.
Not gold. Not yet. Only pale. A long thin line of paler gray along the far rim of the bowl, behind the shore, where the sun would come. And the cold got worse. It always did, at that hour. Oren had stood a thousand dawns on a thousand walls, and it was always coldest just before the light, as if the night were holding its breath.
The man was still sitting by the door. He had not moved all night. The blanket was still against his knee where Oren had put it.
And then, across the glass, very faint, something began to tick.
Oren felt Kestrel go still beside him.
It was one at first. Tick. Tick. Tick. Thin and dry and far off, like a clock in another room. Then another one began, a little out of step. And another. Three. Ticking. Out of step, and then not. Falling together, the way men fall into step on a march without being told, until they were all three ticking in one beat. Tick. Tick. Tick.
A roomful of clocks.
Oren had heard that sound in the Ridges, bouncing off the glass walls, coming from everywhere at once. He had heard it in the dark on the Glass, far off behind them, every night for a week. He did not think he would ever not hear it again. He thought he would hear it at night in his bed, if he ever had a bed again, for the rest of his life.
"They have woken," said Kestrel.
"Aye."
"Then it is time."
"Aye."
Neither of them moved.
The pale line in the east went paler. And then, at one point on it, low down, it began to go gold. A small hot spot of gold, like a coal blown on. It spread along the rim. It grew. And then the top edge of the sun came up over the far shore of the bowl, a thin red-gold rind of it, and the light came across the Eye.
It came flat. Long and low and level, straight across the black glass from the east, the way light comes through a door when somebody opens it in a dark house. And everything it touched threw a shadow. Long ones. Longer than anything had any right to throw. The dome threw a shadow across the glass to the west so long that Oren could not see the end of it. The man sitting by the door threw one. Oren himself, standing in the gap, threw one, back down the stair behind him.
And out on the glass, three hundred paces off, the iron men threw theirs.
They were walking.
Oren watched them come.
Three of them. In a row. Not close together. A few paces apart, the way skirmishers walk, so that one blow could not take two. They came across the black glass at the pace of a man walking to market, not fast, not slow, with that heavy smooth stride he had come to know. Each foot came up and went forward and came down. Never a stumble. Never a check. Tick. Tick. Tick. Man-high, iron-plated, with their brass joints catching the low red light and flashing, and their great shovel hands hanging at their sides, and their blank egg heads turned toward the dome.
And under them, walking upside down, there were three more.
Oren had not seen that before. On the Glass you could not see it. The Glass was dark and thick and gave back only a smoky blur. But the Eye was clear, and the light was low, and it lay on the top of the glass like water lies on the top of a pond on a still morning. And so the glass was a mirror. And under each iron man, there was another iron man, upside down, walking on the underside of the world, foot to foot with the first. Step for step. Tick for tick. Its head pointing down into the deep, toward the drowned roofs.
Six of them. Three walking toward him on the glass. Three walking toward him under it.
Oren did not like it. He did not know why. It was only the light.
Behind the iron men came the men.
Four of them. In gray coats. Walking in a line, a little way back, with their carbines on their backs and their short swords at their hips, and something else in their hands that Oren could not see from here. Small. Held close. The big one in front, the one with the broken nose. Then a young one, thin, with big ears, walking stiffly. Then two more. Their shadows went out long across the glass to the west, and under them their upside-down shadows walked too.
And behind the men, alone, one man.
Kross.
He walked at the same pace as the iron men. Not hurrying. A slight gray shape in a gray coat and a plain gray hat. His hands at his sides. The right one bare. Oren could see it from here, the pale shape of it against the gray coat. A naked hand. He walked as if he were walking across the floor of his own office.
And one step behind his left shoulder, the girl.
The cadet. Oren knew her. He had seen her at the foot of the stair in the Spire, the day his lordship died. Small and fair and very straight, in a white coat with silver buttons, with her hair coming down out of its pins. The lad's book said her name was Wren and she had lied for him once. She walked one step behind Kross the way Oren had walked one step behind his lordship, for twenty-two years, down the Crescent and into the Council and out again.
And off to one side, to the left, on his own, a pale one.
Very pale. Near white. A thin man in a long near-white coat and a gray hat, walking a little apart from all the rest of them, as neat and clean as if he had stepped out of a tailor's shop. With something black in his hands. A book. And something that flashed, silver, when he moved. A pencil.
He was writing as he walked.
Oren looked at him for a long time.
"That one," he said.
Kestrel did not ask which one. "Yes," she said.
"That's the one who burned your camp."
"Yes."
Oren did not say anything else. He did not need to. He looked at the pale man writing as he walked, and he felt his own hand close on the hilt of his sword, and he made it open again. Slowly. Finger by finger.
Not yet.
They came on across the glass. The iron men in front, ticking, with their upside-down twins walking under them. The four in gray. Kross and the girl. The pale one off to the side. All of them throwing their long shadows west across the Eye in the low red-gold light of the morning. Coming.
Oren turned his head and spoke down the stair.
"They're coming," he said.
He did not shout. He did not need to. The iron carried it.
And below him, in the passage, he heard them get up.
Stones
The first one cracked a stone at two hundred paces.
Oren saw it happen. He saw the big one in front, the one with the broken nose, take one hand off the strap of his carbine and hold the other up in front of him. Closed. Something in it. Small. And then the hand opened, and the man brought it down hard, flat, onto the barrel of the carbine, the way you would crack a nut on a table.
And there was light.
It came out of his hand all at once. Blue-white. Bright. So bright that Oren's eyes shut by themselves, and when he opened them there was a mark on the inside of his eyelids, a blue blot, floating. It had lasted only a moment. A flash, like lightning in a summer storm, with no sound. But in the moment it lasted, Oren had seen something in it.
A face. Not the man's. A woman's. Young, laughing, with her head thrown back. And a room behind her, with a window, and the sea outside the window. And then gone.
Somebody else's. Somebody he would never know. A woman laughing in a room by the sea, years ago, who had sold that laugh, or had it taken, or burned it on a bad night for money. And somebody had put it into a stone. And the stone had gone into the Bank, and out of the Bank into a Ministry store, and into the big man's pocket. And now he had cracked it on his carbine on the black glass of the Eye, and it had gone up in a flash of blue-white light like a moth in a lamp, and it was gone.
And where it had gone, the light had gone somewhere. Oren saw it go. A thin line of blue-white fire, crackling, straight across the glass from the big man's hand to the dome. Fast. Faster than an arrow.
It hit the iron frame of the gap a foot above Oren's head.
There was a bang. A hiss. A smell of hot metal, and something else, sweet and burnt, like sugar on a stove. And when Oren looked up, there was a black mark on the iron of the frame the size of his hand, smoking, with the rust round it gone bright and red.
Kestrel had not moved. Her face had not changed. She looked at the black mark above Oren's head and then out at the big man on the glass.
"They have bought stones," she said.
"Aye."
"We saw them, at Last Thorn. On the night of the burning." Her voice was flat. "The men in gray did not come into the camp that night. They stood back on the slope and threw those into the tents. With the iron."
Oren did not say anything. He looked at the black mark on the frame.
On the Glass, they could not have done that. On the Glass, nothing could be burned. Not a stone. Not a word. Not anything. The big man could have cracked his stone on his carbine all day long on the Glass and nothing would have happened except that he would have hurt his hand. That was why they had not used them, all those days. That was why they had walked behind the iron, and waited.
Here they could.
Here everybody could.
Oren felt it then. Properly. For the first time since the gray stones. He had felt it a little, these two days, at the back of everything, the way you feel a draft from a door you have forgotten to close. But now, with the smell of burnt sugar in his nose and the black mark smoking above his head, he felt it all at once, the way you feel the cold when you step out of a warm house.
His words.
They were all there. All of them. Every word he had left. Swimming. Silver. In the dark of him, the way the fish swim in the deep pool under the weir at home, turning, flashing, going nowhere. He could feel each one. Small ones and big ones. Ones he used every day and ones he had not used in years. Bread. Horse. Morning. Sister. Rope. Lad. Sword. Each one a little cold flicker in the dark.
And each one waiting.
That was the thing. They were waiting. On the Glass they had been still, lying at the bottom of him like stones in a stream bed, and he had slept. Here they were not still. Here they moved toward his hand. When he thought of them, they came. Close. Closer. The way a fish comes to the top of a pond when it sees your shadow, because it thinks you have bread.
Take me, they said. Not in words. In the way they moved. Take me. Burn me. You need something. Here I am.
Oren stood in the gap at the top of Sola's stair with his hand on his sword and the iron men coming across the glass, and felt his own words swim up toward his hand, and was more afraid than he had been since he was twelve years old with a pike he could not lift, standing in a line of men in the rain.
He did not let any of them come. Not yet.
He made his hand stay where it was. On the hilt. Closed.
Out on the glass, the big man with the broken nose was reaching into his coat for another stone.
The Big One
Oren knew him.
He had not seen it at first, at three hundred paces in the low light. A big man in a gray coat with a carbine on his back. There were a great many big men in gray coats. But now the man was a hundred and fifty paces off and coming, and he had his hand in his coat for another stone, and he was walking a little apart from the other three, at the front, and Oren saw the way he walked.
The weight on the back foot. The left shoulder a little forward. The head up, but the eyes down, on the ground two paces in front, the way you watch for a ditch or a trip-rope when you have walked into enough of them. And the way his free hand hung, not loose, not tight, a little away from his side, so it could go to the hilt of the short sword at his hip without catching on the coat.
Oren had walked like that for twenty years. Every man he had served with had walked like that. You could put a man who had been a soldier in a lord's coat or a beggar's rags or a bishop's robe, and if he had marched long enough you would know him by the way he walked across a yard.
The big one had marched long enough.
The river companies, Oren thought. From the way he held his shoulder. The river companies, that went up the estuary in flat-bottomed boats in the last war and came off them into the mud with their carbines over their heads. Oren had been with the hill companies. He had not seen much of the river men, except once, on a bad morning, at a ford, when they had come up out of the reeds on his left flank just as his own line was breaking and held it. He did not remember their faces. He remembered that they had come.
He did not know this one. He would never know if they had stood in the same mud. But he knew the walk.
And he knew, looking at him, that the man knew his.
The big one had stopped. Just for a moment. Halfway into his coat for the stone. His head had come up, the eyes off the ground, and they had gone across the hundred and fifty paces of black glass to the gap in the dome, and to Oren standing in it with his sword out and his weight on his back foot and his left shoulder a little forward.
They looked at each other.
The big man's face did not change. He had a broken nose, broken more than once, Oren could see that even from here. A heavy face. Tired. The face of a man who has been doing a job for a long time that he does not much like, and has stopped asking himself why.
Then he looked down again. At the ground, two paces in front. And took the stone out of his coat. And walked on.
Oren let his breath out.
"You know him," said Kestrel beside him. Not asking.
"No." Oren watched the big man come. "I know what he is."
"What is he?"
"Somebody like me," said Oren. "Who took the other road."
Kestrel did not say anything to that.
Oren looked at the others. The young one with the big ears, walking stiffly behind the big one, with something held in his fist against his chest. He did not walk like a soldier. He walked like a boy on his first day at a new school, trying not to look frightened, and looking frightened. His lips were split. His face was the color of whey.
The other two Oren did not know and could not read. Ordinary men. Gray coats. Carbines. They walked the way Enforcers walked in the city, a little swagger, a little too close together. Men used to people getting out of their way. Not men used to people standing in it.
And behind them, Kross.
Oren looked at him longest.
He did not walk like a soldier either. He did not walk like an Enforcer. He walked like nothing Oren had a word for. Quietly. Evenly. With his hands at his sides and his bare right hand very white against the gray of his coat. He did not look at the ground two paces ahead. He did not look at the gap, or the dome, or Oren. He looked at the iron men walking in front of him, the backs of their egg heads, the brass plates in the small of their backs. Steadily. As if he were counting something.
And once, only once, while Oren watched, his eyes left the iron men and went across the glass to the right. To the man sitting by the way in, with his back to the dome and his bound hands on his knees.
And stayed there. For a breath. Two.
And went back.
Oren did not know what it meant. He did not think anybody did. He filed it away, the way he filed everything away on a morning like this, in the place at the back of his head where a soldier keeps the things he will think about afterward, if there is an afterward.
Then the iron men came within a hundred paces, and there was no more time to look at anybody.
Who Stands Where
They had talked it through in the night, round the red brazier, in low voices, while the child slept. Kestrel and Oren mostly, and the lass putting in a word, and the lad listening. It was not much of a plan. It was the only one there was.
Oren would hold the top.
Not inside. Outside. That had been the lass's thought, and he had argued and then seen she was right. If they held the top of the stair from the inside, on the iron landing, they could hold the gap against men, one at a time, for as long as their arms lasted. But the iron men would not come one at a time. The iron men would come to the gap and put their shovel hands on the edge of the big iron door and push. And Oren had seen what an iron man could do to a farm gate. He did not think a dead woman's door, rusted on its runners for sixty years, would hold against three.
So he would stand outside it. On the glass. In front of the gap. With the curve of the dome at his back, and the door behind him, and the stair behind that. And make them come to him.
It was a bad place to stand. He knew that. It was flat and open and there was nothing to put your back against but the dome. But it was the only place where a man with a sword could stand between the iron and the way down.
He went out through the gap now. Sideways. With his head bent under the iron frame and the smell of burnt sugar still hanging round the black mark the stone had left. And he stood on the black glass in front of the door, in the low red light, with his back to the dome, and drew his sword.
It came out of the sheath with a long dry whisper. His father's sword. His father's father's. Plain and long and heavy at the point, with the leather on the hilt worn smooth in the shape of three men's hands, and the notch near the tip that would not come out.
He held it low. Point down. In front of him. The way you hold a sword when you are waiting.
The lass came out after him.
She did not ask. She came out sideways through the gap with her own sword in her left hand, because her right was still bandaged from the Ridges, and stood at his left. A pace off. Where he could see her out of the corner of his eye and she could see him. She had done it without being told, the way a good soldier finds her place in a line. He did not tell her to go back in. He had stopped telling her that, somewhere on the Glass.
Her sword was bent. He could see it. The last two fingers of the blade, near the tip, bent a little to one side, and the notch she had put in it in the Ridges, a finger from the point, and the long scratch down the flat. It would not go all the way into its sheath. It needed a smith. Like his.
"Left hand," he said, low.
"I know."
"It's not your good one."
"I know." She did not look at him. She was looking at the iron men coming. "It'll have to be good today."
He did not say anything to that. There was nothing to say. He only moved his feet, a little, so that the line of his body was turned toward her, and if anything came at her from the left he would be half a step nearer.
Then Kestrel came out. And the four with her.
She had picked them in the night. Sef, quick and young and silent, with the gray stone from the shore still in his fist and a bone spear in the other hand. Marrit, thin and steady. Brin, slow and big, with his ribs strapped tight under his hide coat and his face gray with the pain of it, holding his spear in both hands. And Oda, with her right arm still bound to her chest, holding her spear in her left, the way the lass held her sword. Kestrel had told the two who were hurt to stay below. They had both looked at her, and not answered, and come up anyway, and she had not told them again.
They stood behind Oren and the lass. In a line. Against the curve of the dome, two on each side of the gap. With their spears held low and their gray-lined faces set. And Kestrel stood in the middle of them, right in front of the gap, with her own spear upright beside her and her white fur cape hanging from her shoulders, and her eyes on the iron men.
Oren looked back once, over his shoulder, through the gap. Down the stair.
Vespera was on the bottom step. He could see her in the light from the guard room, small and gray and sharp, with her rope belt of needles and bottles and her blue hands busy. She had laid things out on the step beside her. Needles, longest to finest, in a row. Her little pots of ink. Her gold-rimmed pot, the one Hask had given her on the beach, with its lid off. Strips of clean cloth. A tin of salve. As if she were setting out a sewing table.
And beside her, on the bottom step, with his crutch across his knees and his back against the wall, the lad.
He had wanted to come up. Oren had seen it in his face. He had stood at the foot of the stair with his crutch, looking up at the gray light in the gap, and his gray hands had been opening and closing at his sides, and Oren had seen them make the signs. Four fingers. Two, flat. The fold. Three, to the heart. Over and over. Without his knowing.
And Vespera had got up off the step and gone to him and put her hand flat on his chest.
Oren had watched her do it. A small old woman with blue hands, standing in front of a man a head taller than her, with her palm flat on his shirt, over the place where she had written his name on him in ink. Not pushing. Just resting there. And the lad had stopped moving.
"If you burn here," Vespera had said, quite quietly. Oren had heard it from the top of the stair. Everybody had. "If you burn her here, harvester. The gray goes to your face. Not to your shoulder. There's no more shoulder left. Your face."
The lad had not answered.
"Your face," said Vespera again. "Do you hear me? I've watched it climb on a hundred men. I know where it goes. It goes up your neck and over your jaw and into your mouth. And when it's in your mouth, it's in your words." She took her hand away. "Sit down. Sit down by me on the step and hold my needles. If they need you, they'll call."
And he had sat. On the bottom step. By her.
He was sitting there now. Looking up the stair at the gap. At Oren's back in it, against the light. His face very white. His hands still.
Oren turned back to the glass.
The iron men were close now. A hundred paces. Less. Coming on at their market pace across the black glass with their upside-down twins under them and their long shadows going away west. Tick. Tick. Tick. Behind them, the four in gray, spread out a little now, with their hands at their coats. Behind them, Kross. One step behind Kross, the girl in white. And off to the left, alone, the pale one, writing.
Oren set his feet.
He had stood in a great many lines in his life. On a great many mornings. He knew how it went. You set your feet, and you looked at the thing coming at you, and you found the place in yourself that was not afraid, or that was afraid and did not care, and you stood on it. And you waited.
He found it. He stood on it.
And behind him, through the gap, from the stair, he heard a small voice say, "Can I come out?"
