Chapter 62: Sola's Grandchildren, Part 4 of 5: A Candle
A Candle
They did not get far. At the edge of the square, by the squat church, Lyra stopped.
She had not meant to. Her feet stopped by themselves, the way Corin's had turned left at the Two Sisters. She stood on the swept stones in front of the church door and looked at it.
It was a plain door, of gray weathered oak, with iron studs and a ring for a handle. Over it, cut in the stone of the arch, there was a saint. A small one, worn almost smooth, a woman in a long robe with her hands held out in front of her, palms up, as if she were showing you that they were empty. Lyra did not know which saint it was. On the Hill every saint had a name and a feast and a particular color of candle. Up here, she thought, they might not bother with names.
"Lyra?" said Oren.
"I want to go in." She did not look at him. "Just for a moment. Tomorrow's—" She stopped. "I want to light a candle. For tomorrow. Since I won't be there."
Oren did not say anything. He nodded, and stepped back, and stood by the door with the packs, the way he used to stand outside her father's study when there were visitors.
She pushed the iron ring, and the door swung inward, and she went in.
It was dark inside, and cold, colder than the square, and very quiet. The hum was quieter in here. That was the first thing she noticed. Not gone. Never gone, now. But muffled, as if the thick stone walls held some of it out. The church was small. Smaller than Saint Bride's. A low whitewashed room with a stone floor and wooden benches and two narrow windows of plain greenish glass, and at the far end a stone table with a white cloth on it, and on the wall behind the table, painted straight onto the whitewash, a sun.
Not a saint. A sun. Yellow, with rays.
And under the sun, on a shelf of rough stone along the wall, there were candles. Little ones. Plain tallow stubs, no bigger than her thumb, standing in a row in a bed of sand. Some of them were lit. Six, seven, eight. Their small yellow flames stood up straight and still in the cold air, and their light lay on the whitewashed wall and on the painted sun and made the sun look, for a moment, as if it were really shining.
There was a box of unlit ones beside them. And a flint and steel.
Lyra went down the aisle between the benches. Her boots were loud on the stone. She stopped in front of the shelf, and looked at the small flames, and did not know what to do.
On the Hill, you paid for a candle. You put a coin in a slot in a brass box, and a verger in a black gown gave you a white wax candle as long as your hand, and you lit it from a lamp, and you said the name of the person you were lighting it for, aloud, so that the saint would hear. There was a way of doing it. Aunt Cressida had taught her when she was small. There was a way of doing everything.
Here there was no box. No verger. Only the tallow stubs in their sand, and the flint, and the eight small flames.
She took a stub out of the box. Her gloves were too thick to hold it, so she pulled the right one off, and the cold of the church bit into her bare fingers. She held the stub up to one of the lit candles, and touched its wick to the flame, and the wick caught, and a new small flame stood up, shaking a little, then steadying.
She set it in the sand at the end of the row.
Then she stood there with her bare hand cold and her other hand in its glove, and did not know what to say.
She had thought she would say his name. Aurelian Thorne. The way you were supposed to. But when she opened her mouth, the name would not come. It was too big, and too formal, a name for a coffin plate, a name for a Council paper. It was not what she had called him.
She stood for a long time.
Then, very quietly, she began to count.
"One," she said. "Two. Three. Four."
The stairs of the house on Crown Walk. From the hall to the first landing. Fourteen of them. She had counted them every day since she was nine years old, coming down to breakfast, going up to bed, running up and down in her stockings when nobody was looking. She had never known why she counted them. Her mother had told her to, once, before she went away, and Lyra had done it ever since, and never asked why. And on Thursday afternoon, in a white room at the top of a gray house, her father had tapped them out on her palm, one by one, with his last breath, and stopped on fourteen, the landing, and died.
"Five. Six. Seven. Eight."
The little flame shook. She did not know if it was her breath, or the draft from the door.
"Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen."
She stopped. She held her breath.
"Fourteen," she whispered. "That's the landing, Father. That's all of them."
The flame stood up straight.
She stood and watched it, with her eyes stinging, until she was sure she was not going to cry. She had cried enough today. Then she put her glove back on, slowly, finger by finger, and turned round.
Corin was sitting on the end of the back bench, by the door.
She had not heard him come in. He must have come in very quietly, on his crutch, while she was counting. He was sitting with the crutch across his knees and his cap in his hands, and he was not looking at her. He was looking at the painted sun on the wall, over the candles. He did not say anything as she came back up the aisle.
She stopped beside him.
"How long have you been there?" she said.
"Since four." He looked up at her. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to listen. I only came to see you were all right." He was quiet for a moment. "I couldn't count it. Past nine. But I heard where you stopped."
She did not say anything.
"He stopped there too," said Corin. "On Thursday. I was there. I had my hand on his."
"I know."
"I just—" He stopped. He looked down at his cap. "I wanted you to know I remember that. I didn't burn that. That's mine to keep."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment.
Then she sat down on the bench beside him, close, and they sat together in the cold little church with the nine small flames burning at the far end of it, under the painted sun, and did not say anything at all, until Oren put his head in at the door and said, very gently, that the boy was waiting.
The Sun and Pike
The inn was halfway down the hill, on the north side, where the street began to turn into the north road.
It had a sign. Lyra saw it from a long way up the street, swinging a little in the wind on an iron arm over the door: a board painted with a yellow sun, and crossed in front of the sun, a pike, a long spear with an iron head. The paint was old and the yellow had gone the color of mustard, but somebody had touched up the edge of the pike's blade with new silver paint, quite recently, so that it shone.
THE SUN AND PIKE, said the letters along the bottom of the board.
She read them out for Oren without being asked. He nodded, and looked at the sign for a long time, the way he had looked at the names on the cross.
Inside, the inn was one low room with a sanded floor and a fire, and benches against the walls, and a long counter of dark wood at the back with barrels behind it. It smelled of ale and wood smoke and wet dogs and onions. There were only a few people in it at that hour: two old men playing a game with black and white stones on a board by the fire, a woman mending a net in the corner, and the innkeeper.
The innkeeper was a round, bald, cheerful man with a red face and a leather apron and enormous forearms covered in white hair. He looked at them when they came in, and his eyebrows went up, and then they went down again, and he did not say anything about Oren at all. Lyra was grateful for that. He only said, "Sit, sit, you'll want feeding," and went away through a door behind the counter, and came back with three bowls of stew and a loaf and a jug of ale, and set them down, and went back to his barrels.
The stew was mutton again. Mutton and barley and onion, the same as Thistle Ford, but thicker, and with something green in it that tasted of the hills. Oren ate two bowls without speaking. Corin ate slowly, holding the spoon in his fist. Lyra was not hungry. She ate anyway, because Oren was watching her to make sure she did.
The two old men by the fire had stopped their game. They were not looking at Oren. They were very carefully not looking at Oren. Lyra could see their stones sitting on the board, not moving, while the two old men looked at the fire and listened as hard as they could.
The woman mending the net got up after a while, and went out, and came back, and came across the room to their bench.
She was young, not much older than Lyra, with a broad pleasant face and her hair in a thick fair plait over her shoulder. She had something in her hands. Something red.
"Here," she said, and held it out to Lyra.
Lyra looked at it. It was a scarf. A long, thick, knitted scarf, bright red, with a fringe at each end.
"I saw you at the cross," said the young woman. "Your neck. You keep pulling your cap down at the back." She smiled a little. "I had my hair off when I had the fever, two winters gone. I know what it's like. Nobody tells you how cold it gets."
Lyra did not move.
She heard Corin, beside her, go very still. She did not look at him. She knew what he was thinking. She had told him that, yesterday, by the wall, with the honey. Nobody tells you that. The back of your neck gets cold all the time. And he had said, I'll remember.
"Thank you," said Lyra. Her voice came out strange. "I can't—I've nothing to—"
"It's not for paying." The young woman put the scarf in her lap, and folded Lyra's gloved hands over it, briskly, the way Lyra had folded Corin's hands over his bread. "It's for your neck." She looked at Oren, and then quickly away. "For the road," she said. And went back to her net.
Lyra sat with the red scarf in her lap.
Then she unwound it, and wound it round her neck, twice, over the collar of Silas's coat, and tucked the ends in. It was thick and soft and scratchy and it smelled of sheep and smoke. And the back of her neck, for the first time in two weeks, was warm.
She did not look at Corin. She did not trust herself.
It was Oren who spoke, at last, to break it. He put down his spoon and wiped his beard and looked across the room at the innkeeper, who was polishing a pot behind the counter and pretending not to listen.
"Master," said Oren. "The sign. Outside. The sun and the—the long one. The spear."
"Aye?" said the innkeeper.
"Is that hers? The General's?"
The innkeeper put down his pot.
"Everything up here's hers," he said. He came round the end of the counter, wiping his hands on his apron, and stood looking down at Oren with his round red face very serious. "The sun was her sign. She sewed it on her own coat, they say, the morning they marched. Yellow wool, on gray. And the pike was what they carried. Farmers' pikes, made in that smithy up the hill. Sixty of them, out of one smith's shop, in a week." He shook his head. "My great-grandfather painted that sign the year after. He was too old to march. He stood in that door and watched them go down the road, and when they didn't come back, he painted it."
Oren was quiet.
"The children on the road," he said slowly. "They said she made the Glass."
"She did."
"They said she gave all her words."
"Every one." The innkeeper's face did not change. "That's what it took. That's what they say. A thing that big, it takes everything a body's got. She gave every word she had, every name, every song she knew, her own name too, and it came out of her as fire." He was quiet for a moment. "Old folk still swear by her. When the bread burns, or the cow kicks the pail over. Sola's teeth. You'll hear it. My granda said it every day of his life."
Oren had gone white.
Lyra saw it. She saw his big hand, lying on the table by his empty bowl, close slowly into a fist, and his face go white under his weathered brown skin, white to the lips, and his eyes go wide and fixed, looking at nothing.
"Oren?" she said.
"That's it," said Oren. His voice was a whisper. "That's the word."
"What word?"
"My grandfather's." He did not look at her. He was looking at something far away, a long way off, years off. "When the bread burned. When a horse threw a shoe. Under his breath. I never knew what it meant. I thought it was a city word I didn't know." He swallowed. "Sola's teeth."
The innkeeper looked at him for a long moment.
Then he went back behind his counter, and came back with three small cups of something clear that smelled of fire, and set one down in front of each of them.
"On the house," he said. "To the General."
Oren picked his up. His hand was shaking.
"To the General," he said.
By the fire, the two old men lifted their ale mugs, without a word, and drank too.
The Old Men by the Fire
The two old men did not go back to their game.
They sat by the fire with their mugs in their hands and their black and white stones forgotten on the board between them, and after a while one of them turned round on his stool, slowly, and looked at the three of them across the room.
He was the older of the two. He was so old that his face had gone past wrinkles into something else, soft and folded, like a glove that has been worn for a lifetime. He had one eye. The other was a closed, sunken lid with a white scar running down through it. His one eye was bright blue.
"You came up the left road," he said. "From the Sisters."
It was not a question.
Lyra looked at him. "How did you know?"
"Davin's sister said. Last night. When she came over the top field." The old man drank from his mug. "She said Abbe said three came up the left road from the Sisters, in the snow. Not the right. The left." He set the mug down on his knee. "Nobody comes up the left road. Not for years."
"Why not?" said Corin.
The old man turned his one eye on him.
He looked at Corin the way Granny Abbe had looked at him, and Widow Brennock, and Davin. Lyra had begun to know that look. It went first to the gray hands, and stayed there a moment, and then went to Corin's face, and stayed there longer. Measuring. Wondering.
"It's the old road," said the old man. "The Ashborn road. They come down it twice a year, in spring and back-end, to trade. Hides and bone and the gray stones, for cheese and wool and salt. That's all that goes on it. That, and—" He stopped.
"And the ones going over," said the other old man, by the fire. He did not turn round. "Say it, Tom."
"And the ones going over," said the one-eyed man. "Aye." He looked at Corin's hands again. "Gray ones. City ones. They come up the drove road from the fens, and at the Sisters they always go left. Always. Every one of them. Never asked the way, any of them. Just go left, like water going downhill." He drank. "I've seen four in my time. You're the fifth."
Nobody said anything.
Lyra looked at Corin. He was sitting very still on the bench, with his spoon in his fist and his bowl empty in front of him. He was not looking at the old man. He was looking at his own hands on the table.
"I'm not going over," he said quietly. "Not the way you mean."
"No?" said the old man.
"No. I'm going through."
The old man looked at him for a long time with his one blue eye.
"Through to what?" he said at last. "There's nothing through. There's the Glass, and then there's more Glass, and then there's the middle, and nobody's ever come back from the middle to say what's there." He shook his head slowly. "The Ashborn go out onto it a little way. A day. Two. To sing to the dead in the trench. Then they come back. Even they don't go to the middle."
"Something's there," said Corin.
"Something's always there." The old man turned back to the fire. "That's what the gray ones always said."
The innkeeper came and took their bowls. He did not say anything, but he put his big hand on Oren's shoulder as he passed, very briefly, and took it away again.
Lyra sat with the red scarf warm round her neck and the taste of the clear burning drink in her mouth, and looked at the fire, and thought.
Four in my time. You're the fifth.
Hild's brother, at Thistle Ford. Rab. Who came home with gray hands and went up over the Lip in the morning and never came back. Lyra had heard Hild tell it, last night, by the fire, with her red hands on her knees.
And this morning, on the road out of Thistle Ford, when the two of them had fallen a little behind Oren and Davin, Corin had told her one more thing. He had told it to her because of rule one, he said, and because he did not know what it meant and thought she ought to. That yesterday, on the left road above the Two Sisters, for a moment, his hand had felt very small. A child's hand. Held in a big, dry, old hand, and pulled gently uphill.
She had not known what to say to that. She still did not.
Just go left, like water going downhill.
She thought about Corin at the Two Sisters, yesterday, standing ten steps up the left-hand road in the deep snow, not knowing how he got there. My feet know this road. I don't.
She thought about it for a long time, and the more she thought about it, the colder she got, even with the scarf, even by the fire.
"Tom," she said.
The one-eyed man turned round again.
"The four. The ones you saw." She did not know why she was asking. She only knew she had to. "Did any of them have a child with them?"
The old man looked at her strangely.
"A child?"
"A boy. A small boy. With someone old."
The old man was quiet for a long while. His one eye went to the fire, and stayed there, and something moved far back in it, like a fish moving deep under ice.
"No," he said slowly. "Not one of the four. They were all grown. All on their own." He drank. "But there was talk, once. Years back. Not here. Over Cray way, past the Lip, at the Ashborn edge. Talk of an old man and a lad, going out onto the Glass in the back-end, in the first frost. And coming back again in the spring, the two of them. Coming back." He shook his head. "Nobody believed it. Nobody comes back."
"When?" said Corin. His voice was very quiet.
The old man shrugged.
"I don't know. A long time. Before the young ones in this room were born." He looked at Lyra. "Before you, I'd say, miss. Or about then." He turned back to the fire, and picked up one of his black stones from the board, and looked at it. "It was only talk. Ashborn talk. They say all sorts."
He put the stone down on the board with a small click.
"Your move, Huw," he said to the other old man.
The other old man moved a white stone, without looking at it, and they went back to their game, and did not say anything else.
Lyra looked at Corin.
He had the green book out. He was writing in it, fast, in his crooked capitals, his head bent close over the page. She could not see what he wrote. When he had finished, he closed the book and put it inside his coat and sat with his hand flat over it, against his chest, the way a man holds his hand over a hurt.
"Corin," she said. Very low.
He looked at her.
She did not know what she had meant to say. She looked at his face, and it was white and still and his eyes were very far away, and she did not know what to say at all.
"It was only talk," she said.
"Yes," said Corin. He did not sound as if he believed it. "Only talk."
Then Davin put his head in at the door and said the light was going, and they got up and went out into the cold.
