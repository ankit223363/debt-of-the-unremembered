Chapter 62: Sola's Grandchildren, Part 3 of 5: Twenty-Three
Twenty-Three
She read them.
She read them slowly, and clearly, in her drawing-room voice, the one her mother had given her and Aunt Cressida had polished, the voice that carried to the back of a room without ever seeming to be raised. She had not used that voice in a long time. Not since the autumn. She had not thought she would ever need it again. She used it now, on the top step of a stone cross in a village she had never heard of until yesterday, with the whole of the village listening in its doorways, and she was glad of it, because it did not shake.
"Hob Vance," she read.
Oren's hand was flat on the stone. She took his finger, the big scarred first finger of his good hand, and laid it in the first letter of the name, in the cut in the stone, so that he could feel it.
"Hob Vance," said Oren.
He said it low. Like a man saying a word in church.
She moved his finger along to the next.
"Talan Vance."
"Talan Vance."
"Annis Vance."
"Annis Vance."
They went along the line together. She read each name, and moved his finger into the cuts of it, and he said it after her. Some of the names were easy. Ralf. Bryn. Corra. Some of them were strange, valley names, old names she had never heard and had to say carefully, letter by letter, the way you step on stones across a river. Piran. Cador. Ifan. Morwen. There were two Tomases, one marked Old and one marked the Younger, side by side. Father and son, Lyra thought. They went into the valley together. There was a Maud and a Nesta and an Elin. There was a Hal and an Ivo and a Rhys. There was a Wynne, and after it, in smaller letters, squeezed in as if the stonecutter had nearly forgotten, and her babe.
Lyra stopped at that one.
She did not mean to. Her voice just stopped, and for a moment she could not make it go again. Oren's finger stayed in the cut of the stone, and he waited, and did not ask why.
"And her babe," she read at last.
"And her babe," said Oren. His voice cracked on it.
She went on.
She did not count them out loud. She counted them inside, the way she counted everything, because counting kept her voice steady. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. Behind her, in the square, nobody moved. The wind came across the top of the hill from the north and pulled at the black shawl of Widow Brennock, standing on the bottom step, and the widow did not lift a hand to hold it.
Seventeen. Eighteen. Nineteen.
"Gethin Vance."
"Gethin Vance."
"Aldric Vance."
Oren's finger was in the cuts of it. He did not say it at once. He stood with his finger in the name and his head bent, and Lyra heard him breathe in, slowly, all the way down, the way a man breathes before he lifts something very heavy.
"Aldric Vance," he said.
His grandfather's father. The man who went into the valley with his sister, while his son carried sacks on a dock in the city and did not come home.
Lyra moved his finger on, to the next name.
And stopped.
There was no next name. There was a space. Between Aldric Vance and the name after it, the stone was smooth. Not empty, the way the stone was empty at the end of a line. Smooth. As if it had been rubbed. As if somebody had taken a chisel and a rasp to it and worked at it for a long time, very carefully, until whatever had been cut there was gone, and the stone was flat again, and only a little paler than the rest.
Oren felt it. She saw him feel it. His finger, moving into the space, found nothing to fit into, and stopped, and moved back and forth across the smooth stone, searching.
"What's that?" he said.
"Nothing," said Lyra. "It's smooth. There's nothing there."
"There was something."
"Yes." She looked at it. "There was. It's gone."
She did not know why her heart was beating so fast. She moved his finger on, past the smooth place, to the next name, and read it.
"Mira Vance."
"Mira Vance," said Oren. But he was still feeling for the smooth place with his thumb.
And then there was one more.
It was the last name in the line, and it was not like the others. The letters were bigger. Twice as big, and cut twice as deep, so deep that even now, after sixty winters, the snow had settled into them and made them stand out white against the gray stone. And under the name there was a line of smaller letters, more words, the only name on the whole of the cross that had more than a name.
Lyra read it. Her voice did not shake. She had made sure of that.
"Sola Vance," she read. "General. She gave her words."
Oren laid his whole hand over it.
He did not say it after her. He did not say anything. He stood on the top step with his palm flat over the big white-filled letters, and his head bowed until his forehead almost touched the stone, and his shoulders moved once, the way shoulders move when a man is trying very hard not to make a sound.
In the square behind them, someone took off a cap.
Then someone else. Lyra heard it more than saw it. The small soft sounds of wool caps coming off, one after another, all round the ring of doorways, like the first drops of rain. Men and women and children. She turned her head a little and saw the old man in the moth-eaten coat, who had come out of his house again and was standing in his doorway with his white head bare. She saw Davin at the edge of the paving, with his cap held in both hands against his chest, and his dog sitting very straight beside him. She saw Widow Brennock on the bottom step take her black shawl down off her head, and stand with her gray hair blowing in the north wind, and close her eyes.
She saw Corin.
He was standing where he had been, a little back from the cross, leaning on his crutch. He had taken off his own wool cap, and he was holding it in his gray-gloved hand. He was not looking at Oren. He was looking at the name. At the big white letters under Oren's hand, Sola Vance, and the words under them. And his face was very strange. Not sad, exactly. Not frightened. Like the face of a man who has heard a name spoken that he knows he ought to know, and cannot place, and it is going round and round in his head looking for somewhere to land.
Twenty-three, Lyra thought. She had counted. Twenty-three names, and one smooth place, and a General.
She stood beside Oren on the top step and kept her shoulder against his arm and waited for him to be ready, and did not hurry him at all.
The Smooth Place
When Oren took his hand off the General's name at last, he did not come down the steps. He moved his hand back, slowly, along the line of names, the way a man walks back along a road to find something he dropped. Past Mira. To the smooth place.
He put his thumb in it.
He pressed it there, in the pale smooth patch of stone between Aldric and Mira, as if he could feel through the stone to whatever had been cut there once. His thumb was big and the space was small, and his thumb covered it. He stood like that for a long time.
Then he turned round and looked down at Widow Brennock on the bottom step.
"What was here?" he said.
The widow had put her shawl back up over her head. She held it closed under her chin with one thin hand. She looked up at Oren, and her long pale face did not change at all, but her eyes did. They went hard and bright and cold, like the river ice.
"A name," she said.
"Whose?"
"We don't say it."
Oren waited.
The widow looked past him, at the smooth place under his thumb. When she spoke again her voice was quite low, and quite even, and Lyra thought she had said these words many times before, perhaps to her own children, perhaps to theirs. The words had the worn, smooth feel of something said over and over until it fits the mouth like a stone fits a hand.
"He was Aldric's brother," said Widow Brennock. "The General's brother too. The youngest of them. On the night before they marched, he went down the valley road in the dark, on his own, to them in the gray. To their camp. And he told them which way the road ran, and where the fords were, and how many pikes we had, and which way the General meant to come." Her hand tightened on the shawl. "He went over to them in the gray. So we took him off."
Nobody said anything.
"Mira was his wife," said the widow. "She woke in the morning and he was gone, and she knew where. And she took his pike off the wall and went into the valley with the General anyway, in his place." She looked at the name after the smooth patch. Mira Vance. "So we kept her on. And we took him off. With a chisel, when the stone was new. My grandmother held the lamp."
Oren took his thumb away from the smooth place.
He looked at it. At the pale flat patch of stone. Then he looked at his own thumb, as if he expected something to be on it.
"What happened to him?" said Lyra. She did not mean to speak. It came out by itself.
Widow Brennock looked at her for the first time.
It was a long look. It went over the boy's breeches and the dead man's coat and the short hair under the cap and came to rest on Lyra's face, and stayed there, and Lyra had the feeling, very strongly, that the widow was seeing something in her face that Lyra could not see herself.
"He lived," said the widow. "That's what happened to him. He was in their camp, down the valley, when the sun came. Behind their lines. They'd kept him back, to show them the way after. So he lived." Her mouth went thin. "He went south with them in the gray, after. To the city. He had children there, I'm told. And they had children. And they've got his name, whatever they call themselves now." She let go of her shawl. "Not on this stone, they haven't. Not on this stone."
She bent and picked up her little hearth broom from the step, and swept the snow off the place where Oren had been standing, carefully, as if he had brought something in on his boots that ought to be swept away.
Then she straightened up.
"You're Aldric's line, Captain," she said. "Not his. I can see that in your face. You've the General's jaw." She looked at him a moment longer. "She'd be glad you came. She'd be glad somebody came."
She walked away across the square to her own door, black and straight, with her broom, and went in, and shut it.
Oren came down the steps.
He came down slowly. At the bottom he stopped and stood in front of Lyra, and looked at her, and she saw that his eyes were wet and red and his beard was wet too, and he did not seem to know it.
"The General's jaw," he said. He touched his own chin with his good hand, wonderingly, under the gray beard. "Saints. I always thought it was my mother's."
Lyra laughed. She could not help it. It came out of her like a hiccup, wet and sudden, and she put her hand over her mouth, and then she found she was crying, too, a little, the way you cry when something is too sad and too funny at the same time to hold. She did not know if she was crying for Oren, or for the woman who took her husband's pike off the wall, or for the baby squeezed in small on the stone, or for her own father, who was going into the dark tomorrow without her.
Oren put his good arm round her and held her against his chest, against the rough wool of his coat and the flour-sack sling, the way he had when she was six and fell off the garden wall on Crown Walk.
"There now," he said into her cap. "There now. There now."
Over his shoulder, through her wet eyes, Lyra saw Corin.
He had not moved from where he stood. He was still holding his cap in his hand. But he was not looking at them. He was looking at the cross, at the north side of it, at the line of names going round. At the smooth patch between Aldric and Mira. His face had gone very thoughtful, and very still, and he was rubbing his black fingertips together inside his gloves, slowly, round and round, the way he did when something troubled him that he could not name.
Then he took the green book out of his coat, and wrote something in it, standing up, with the book held against his chest.
She did not see what he wrote. Later, when he read the day's page aloud to her, she heard it. It was only one line.
ONE NAME CUT OFF. HE WENT OVER TO THEM IN THE GRAY. HE LIVED. HE HAD CHILDREN.
The Smithy
The smith came out of his door while Oren was still holding her.
He was a big man, bigger than Oren, with a chest like a barrel and arms like the trunks of young trees, bare to the shoulder in the cold, and black with soot to the elbow. He had a leather apron on, scarred and burned. His face was red from the forge and his beard was short and singed at the ends. He stood in the wide door of the smithy with the red glow behind him and looked at the three of them in the middle of the square, and then he jerked his head, once, back toward the inside.
"Come in out of the wind," he said. "There's a fire."
There was a fire.
The smithy was the warmest place Lyra had been in a week. The heat came at her through the door like a wall, and she walked into it and felt her face go stiff and then soft and then burn. Inside, it was dark and red. The forge stood in the middle, a great hearth of brick with a hood over it like a giant's hat, and coals in it glowing orange and yellow and, at the very heart, almost white. There was a bellows beside it, a huge leather bag like a sleeping animal. There was an anvil, black and shining on its top where the hammer had struck it ten thousand times. The walls were hung with tools, tongs and hammers and punches and files, all black, all hanging in rows on nails. The floor was beaten earth, and it was covered with tiny bright flakes of iron that crunched under her boots.
And it smelled of iron. Hot iron, and coal smoke, and horse, and sweat. A clean, hard, honest smell.
On the back wall, alone, above the forge, there hung a horseshoe.
Lyra saw it because she could not help seeing it. It was enormous. It was as wide as a cartwheel's hub, as wide as her two hands spread out side by side, and thick as her wrist, and black with age and with something else, a dull reddish crust, like old rust gone hard. It was too big for any horse. It was too big for any horse that had ever lived. It hung on a great iron spike all on its own, with nothing else round it, the way you hang the one picture in a room that matters.
"What's that?" said Lyra, before she could stop herself.
The smith did not look round. He was pumping the bellows, slowly, and the coals were breathing brighter with each pump.
"The General's horse's," he said. "A spare. My great-grandfather kept it." He let go of the bellows. "This was her house, once. The top house. Before it was a smithy." He looked at Oren. "Sit. Give us that."
Oren looked at him. "Give you what?"
"The sword. It wants an edge. I can hear it wants an edge from here."
Oren did not argue. Lyra had never seen him give his sword to anyone. Not once. Not to her father, not to the armorer on Crown Walk, not to anyone. He unbuckled it now, one-handed, and held it out, and the smith took it and drew it from the scabbard and turned it in the red light and looked along the edge with one eye shut.
"Aye," he said. "It's had a hard winter."
"It has," said Oren.
The smith took it to the grindstone in the corner, a big round wheel of gray stone on a frame, with a trough of water under it. He sat at it and began to work a treadle with his foot, and the wheel began to turn, and he laid the edge of Oren's sword against it.
The sound filled the smithy. A long, thin, singing scream of steel on stone, and sparks flying off it in a stream, orange and bright, falling and dying on the earth floor.
Oren sat down on an upturned bucket by the forge and watched.
He had his sling off. Lyra did not see when he had done it. One moment it was there and the next it was hanging over his knee, a limp flour sack, and he was flexing his left arm slowly, bending it and straightening it, opening and closing the hand. He winced, once. Then he did it again.
"Is it all right?" she said.
"It'll do." He bent the arm again. "It'll do. It's been a week. The doctor said a week." He looked at his hand, opening and closing. "It'll hold a shield. Or a—" He stopped. His face went still, the way it went. Then it came back. "A strap. A pack strap. It'll hold a pack."
"Oren."
"Aye?"
She sat down on the earth floor beside the bucket, with her back against the warm bricks of the forge, and pulled her knees up. Corin had come in behind them and was standing just inside the door, leaning on his crutch, watching the sparks. The smith was grinding. Nobody could hear them over the scream of the wheel.
"I'll read for you," said Lyra.
Oren looked down at her.
"From now on," she said. "Anything you need. Signs. Letters. Names on stones. Labels on jars, so you don't eat the wrong thing." She tried to smile. It did not quite work. "I'll read it all for you. And you'll write for me."
Oren was quiet for a moment.
"I can't write," he said.
"I know."
"I can't write any more than you can, Lyra. I can't make a letter. I'd not know an A from a hoof print."
"I know." She did smile then, a little. "So we'll both have to get Corin to do it. And we'll both complain about his letters."
Oren looked at her a long moment.
Then he laughed. It came out of him all at once, his short loud barking laugh, so loud that the smith looked round from the wheel, and the sparks went sideways. Oren laughed until he had to put his good hand on his knee, and his eyes ran again, and Lyra did not know if it was the laughing or the other thing.
"His letters," Oren said, when he could. "Saints. They walk about the page like drunk men."
"They lean on each other."
"They fall over."
"I heard that," said Corin, from the door.
They both looked at him.
He was leaning on his crutch with his face perfectly straight. "I can hear you," he said. "Even over the stone. I'm lame, not deaf." He paused. "And they don't fall over. They lean. It's different."
Lyra put her face down on her knees so that nobody would see it, and her shoulders shook.
The smith finished. The wheel slowed and stopped, and the scream died, and the smithy was suddenly very quiet except for the breathing of the coals. He held Oren's sword up in the red light and turned it, and the new edge caught the light and ran with it, one bright silver line from hilt to point.
"There," said the smith. He sheathed it and held it out, hilt first. "No charge."
"I'll pay," said Oren. "I've coin."
"No charge," said the smith again, in exactly the same voice. He did not let go of the hilt until Oren had stopped reaching for his purse. Then he let go. "Not in her house," he said. "Not to a Vance."
He went back to his forge and picked up his hammer, and did not look at any of them again.
A Name Nobody Says
When they came out of the smithy, Widow Brennock was waiting for them.
She was standing on the swept stones outside the wide door, with her black shawl over her head and a basket over her arm. The basket had a cloth over it, and from under the cloth came the smell of new bread, warm and nutty and a little sour. She held it out to Lyra without a word.
Lyra took it. It was heavy. Under the cloth there were four round flat loaves, brown and rough, still warm enough to feel through her gloves.
"Oat bread," said the widow. "It keeps. It'll keep a week if you don't eat it." She looked at Oren. "You'll eat it."
"I will," said Oren.
"Aye. You've the look of a man who eats." Something moved at the corner of her thin mouth. It might have been nearly a smile. Then it was gone, and she turned to go.
"Mistress Brennock," said Corin.
Lyra had not heard him come up. He had been behind them in the smithy door, on his crutch, and now he was beside her, close, and he was looking at the widow with that plain, careful look he had, the look that asked questions without being rude about it.
The widow stopped. She turned back.
"Aye?"
"Can I ask you something?"
She looked at him. At the crutch, the leg. At the gray gloves, and the gray wrists between the glove and the cuff. Her face did not change. But Lyra saw her eyes go to the gray and stay there a moment too long, the way everyone's did.
"You can ask," said the widow.
Lyra felt a small jolt at that, the same as she had felt at Thistle Ford. You can ask. It seemed to be what the valley people said. It was what she had said to him, yesterday, on the road. She did not know why it should make her chest tight.
"Do you know a place called Oakhaven?" said Corin.
The square went still.
Not the way it had gone still for Oren's name. That had been a waiting kind of stillness, a held-breath, hopeful stillness, like a crowd waiting for a ship. This was different. This was the stillness of a door shutting.
Lyra saw it in the widow's face first. She saw the long pale face, which had been nearly kind a moment ago, go flat and closed and hard, all at once, as if someone had drawn a blind down over a window from the inside. The widow's hand, the one that held her shawl closed under her chin, went white at the knuckles.
Then Lyra saw it in the square. In the doorways round the edge of it, where people were still standing, watching. She saw a man who had been about to go in stop with his hand on his door. She saw a woman pull her child closer. She saw the old man in the moth-eaten coat, in his doorway, turn his head away.
Nobody had heard, she thought. They were too far away to hear. And yet every one of them had heard.
"Who told you that name?" said Widow Brennock. Her voice was very low.
"It's in my book," said Corin. He put his hand on his coat, over the place where the green book was. "On the first page. It says I was born there. I don't remember it." He paused. "I don't remember anything from before I was ten."
The widow looked at him for a long, long time.
Lyra watched her face, and saw something happen in it. Something come up from very deep down and fight with the closed, hard look, and lose, and go back down again.
"Then you'd best not say so," said the widow. "North of the fens. To anyone."
"Why?"
"Nobody says that name here." She drew her shawl tighter. "Not since them in the gray went up the valley road with their lamps."
"When?"
But the widow was already shaking her head.
"It was a fever," she said. She said it the way you say a thing you have been told to say, and have said so many times that you have nearly come to believe it. "That's what they wrote. That's what the Council wrote, and sent up on a paper with a seal on. A fever, in the north valleys. Everybody gone. Burned, to stop it spreading." She looked at Corin's hands again. "There's no Oakhaven. There's no road to it. It's not on any map. Nobody lives there. Nobody goes there." Her voice dropped even lower, until Lyra could barely hear it over the wind. "And nobody comes from there, harvester. Nobody."
She turned, and walked away across the square, very straight and very fast, and did not look back. She went in at her own door and shut it hard. Lyra heard the bolt go across.
One by one, round the ring of the square, the other doors shut too.
Not slammed. Just shut. Quietly, one after another, the way the shutters go up on a street of shops at the end of the day. The man with his hand on his door. The woman with her child. The old man in the moth-eaten coat. In a few breaths the whole square was empty, and every door round it was closed, and only the smithy stood open, with its red glow and its steady ringing hammer.
Corin stood in the middle of the empty square on his crutch.
He did not say anything. He looked at the shut doors, all the way round, one after another. Then he looked down at his own gloved hands.
Lyra went and stood beside him. She did not touch him. She held the basket of warm bread against her chest, and stood beside him, close.
"Fever," said Corin at last, very quietly.
"That's what she said."
"She didn't believe it."
"No." Lyra looked at the widow's shut door. "No. She didn't."
She thought of her father's study, the way Corin had described it to her in the crypt, after he walked the stone. The green lamp. The moon clock. A faded blue cloth book on her father's desk, a parish register, with a name on the cover. Oakhaven. And the Council, voting money for a fever that never was. And men in gray going door to door with lamps, and a man with a gentle voice behind them.
Corin had told her all of that. In front of everybody, on Tuesday, because of rule one. She had not forgotten a word.
He had walked it in her father's memory. He knew it the way he knew everything now. Like a story in a book.
She wondered if that was easier for him, or harder.
"Come on," said Oren gently, behind them. He had buckled his sharpened sword back on, and he was holding the bundle from Granny Abbe under his good arm. "Davin says we eat at the Pike before the north road. He says it's seven more miles to the Lip, and nobody walks it hungry."
They went. Behind them, across the empty square, the hammer in the smithy rang on and on, steady and slow, like a heart.
