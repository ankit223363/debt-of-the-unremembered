Chapter 62: Sola's Grandchildren, Part 5 of 5: Davin
Davin
The north road out of Haverstock was not a drove road. It had no walls. It was only a track, two ruts in the snow with a hump of white between them, going away across a high bare moor toward a line of dark hills.
The moor was the emptiest place Lyra had ever seen.
There were no trees on it. There were no walls, no sheep, no houses, no birds. There was only snow, going away on every side in long low swells like the sea, white and gray and faintly blue in the hollows, under a sky that was white and gray and faintly blue too, so that sometimes she could not tell where the one stopped and the other began. Here and there a rock stuck up out of the snow, black and bare. Here and there a stiff brown stalk of dead heather. That was all.
The wind came across it from the north and never stopped.
It was colder than the drove road. It was colder than anything. Lyra pulled her cap down over her ears and her collar up over her mouth, and tucked her hands inside her sleeves, and still the cold got in. It got in at her wrists and her ankles and the back of her neck, where her hair used to be. She had told Corin yesterday that nobody warned you how cold the back of your neck got with short hair. She had not been exaggerating.
And the hum was louder.
She could hear it properly now, out here. Not just feel it in her teeth. Hear it. A low, endless, wordless note under the wind, coming from straight ahead, from the dark hills, from beyond them. It was not an unpleasant sound. That was the strange part. It was almost soothing. Like a cat purring in another room. Like the sound the sea makes in a shell.
Davin walked in front, with Flint at his heels.
He had not said anything since the cross. He had stood at the edge of the paving with his cap in his hands while Lyra read the names, and when it was over he had put his cap back on and walked off toward the north road without a word, and they had followed him. He walked fast, for a small boy. He did not look back.
Oren went after him, with the packs. Lyra went after Oren. Corin came last, on his crutch, slow but steady, his breath smoking in front of him.
Lyra watched the two of them as she walked.
Oren was different since the cross. She could not have said how. He walked the same, with the same long soldier's stride, the packs on his back and his sharpened sword on his hip and his left arm loose now at his side, out of its sling, swinging a little stiffly. But he held his head differently. Higher. And every so often, without seeming to know it, he put his good hand up and touched his own jaw, under the gray beard, the way a man touches a new coat to be sure it is still there.
The General's jaw.
Corin was different too, but the other way. He had been quiet at Thistle Ford. Now he was more than quiet. He walked with his head down, watching the ruts, and his crutch went into the snow and came out of it and went in again with a steady, patient rhythm, and he did not look up at the moor at all. Only, now and then, at the dark hills ahead. His limp was worse. She could see that. The cold was getting into the leg, and the long climb up to Haverstock had cost him, and he was paying for it now, step by step.
She thought about the red scarf round her neck, and the woman at the inn who had given it to her, and nobody tells you how cold it gets.
She thought about Hild's pot of green grease, in Corin's pack.
She thought about the widow's bread, and Granny Abbe's apples, and the smith who would not take Oren's coin.
Not to a Vance.
They had been given so much, in two days. More than anyone had given them in the city in two months. And every single thing they had been given, she thought, had been given to them the way you give things to someone who is going away. Someone you are not sure you will see again.
She did not like that thought. She put it away, and counted instead. Two hundred and six. Two hundred and seven.
After an hour they stopped by a big black rock that stood up out of the moor like a broken tooth. There was a hollow in the snow on its south side, out of the wind. They sat in it, all four of them, and the dog, and ate a little of the widow's oat bread and one each of Granny Abbe's dried apples. The bread was hard and nutty and very good.
Corin had the green book out. He was reading it, quietly, the way he did at every rest, as if to make sure the day had not changed while he was walking.
Then he looked up at Lyra.
"Will you check it?" he said. "Today. What I've written. I'll read it. You tell me if I've got it wrong."
She looked at him.
She did not trust herself to answer. She nodded.
He read it aloud, slowly, in his plain flat voice, holding the book close to his face in the white light. The jug. The bread. The Two Sisters, and his feet going left. The boy on the ridge. Granny Abbe and her cold pipe. The doll. He read it all, everything he had written since Reed's End. And then, at the bottom, the new lines, the ones from this morning, from the cross.
SATURDAY. HAVERSTOCK.
SHE READ OREN HIS FAMILY. TWENTY-THREE NAMES. HE SAID EVERY ONE AFTER HER.
ONE NAME CUT OFF. HE WENT OVER TO THEM IN THE GRAY. HE LIVED. HE HAD CHILDREN.
A WOMAN WOULD NOT SAY OAKHAVEN. SHE SAID FEVER. SHE DID NOT BELIEVE IT.
He stopped. He looked at her.
"Is that right?"
Lyra opened her mouth, and found that she could not speak, and shut it again. She nodded.
"You can't count to twenty-three," said Oren suddenly. "You told me. On the barge. You said anything past nine is just a noise to you."
Corin looked at him. "I can't."
"Then how did you write it?"
"She said it." Corin looked at Lyra. "At the cross. Under her breath, when she'd finished. Twenty-three. I wrote down what she said." He paused. "I don't know what twenty-three is. But I know she counted it. So it's right."
Nobody said anything for a while.
Lyra looked down at the snow. She found that her hands had come out of her sleeves, and were holding each other, very tightly, in her lap.
It was Davin who broke the silence.
He had been sitting a little apart from them, on the edge of the hollow, with Flint's head on his knee, feeding the dog bits of his oat bread. He had not seemed to be listening. Now, without looking up from the dog, he said, "They come up here, sometimes."
They all looked at him.
"Them in the gray," said Davin. He said it to the dog. "Clerks. With books. They come up the north road from Haverstock with their books under their arms, to count the sheep, and the people, and see what we owe." He gave Flint another piece of bread. "My granda saw some. Three. When he was a boy."
"What happened?" said Lyra.
"They went up to the Lip." Davin pointed north with his chin, at the dark hills ahead. "To look over. They always want to look over. Granda said they stood up there on the top for a long time, looking at the Glass. And then they came back down." He scratched Flint behind the ears. "And they didn't know why they'd come. They had their books, and they didn't know what the books were for. One of them asked Granda the way to the city, and he'd come from the city that same week." Davin shrugged. "They went home. Nobody's come since."
Corin had gone very still.
"They forgot," he said.
"Granda said it's the hum." Davin looked up for the first time, at Corin, at his gray-gloved hands on the green book. "Granda said if you've got a lot in you that isn't yours, it comes loose up there. Like a coat in the wind. Clerks have got a lot in them that isn't theirs. Numbers and that." He looked back down at the dog. "Granny Abbe says the Glass is a bad neighbor but a good wall."
"She said that to us," said Lyra.
"She says it to everybody," said Davin. "She says it so much the sheep know it."
Oren laughed his short bark of a laugh. Davin did not smile. But Lyra thought, watching the side of his small freckled face, that he was pleased.
She looked at Corin.
He was looking north, past the black rock, at the dark hills, and his hand had gone to his coat, to the place where the book was, and stayed there.
If you've got a lot in you that isn't yours.
He had a dead woman's whole life in him. Vivienne's. And an old man, living in the place where Lyra used to be. And the gray, to the shoulder.
She wondered what would come loose in him, up there, in the wind.
She did not ask. She did not think he knew.
The Last Cairn
The dark hills, when they reached them, were not hills at all.
Lyra understood that slowly, as they climbed. From the moor they had looked like a line of low black hills against the sky. But as the track went up into them, the black turned out to be rock, bare rock, with no snow on it, and the rock went up in one long steep slope like the side of a roof, and at the top of the slope there was nothing. Only sky. As if the world had a rim, here, and they were climbing up to the edge of it.
The snow stopped first. That was the strange thing. Halfway up the slope, the snow on the track simply ended, in a ragged line, and above it the ground was bare: black rock and frozen grass and loose gray stones that rolled under their boots. The air was not warmer. If anything it was colder. But no snow lay on the ground above that line. It was as if something had swept it all away.
"The wind," said Oren. But he did not sound sure.
The hum was very loud now. Lyra could feel it in the soles of her feet, through her boots, coming up out of the rock. She could feel it in her teeth and in the bones of her face and in her chest, where her heart was. It was not unpleasant. It was not anything. It was simply there, everywhere, all the time, like the cold.
They climbed.
Corin was slow on the bare rock. The crutch kept slipping on the loose stones. Twice Oren went back for him, and once Lyra did, and once he fell and got up again by himself before either of them could reach him. He did not say anything about it. His face was very white, and there was sweat on it, in spite of the cold. But he did not stop, and he did not ask them to stop.
And he kept looking up. At the top of the slope. At the rim of the world.
There were cairns along the track.
Lyra had not noticed them at first, because they were made of the same black rock as everything else. Small heaps of stones, knee-high, one every hundred paces or so, beside the track on the left. Each one had a stone on top that was not black. A gray stone. A smooth, round, river-washed gray stone, a little bigger than a hen's egg, set on the very top of the black heap like a cherry on a cake. She did not know why. She did not ask.
The last cairn stood a little way below the top of the slope.
It was bigger than the others. Waist-high. Its gray stone on top was bigger too, the size of a loaf. And beside it, Davin stopped.
He did not say anything. He just stopped walking, and stood beside the cairn with his stick in his hand and Flint pressed against his legs, and looked up at the last stretch of slope, at the rim, at the sky. The dog had its tail between its legs. Lyra had not seen it do that before.
"Davin?" she said.
"We don't go past here."
He said it quietly. Not frightened. Just a fact. The way he might have said this is the end of the field or the river's too deep here.
"Granny Abbe said," said Lyra. "She said you'd stop at the last cairn. She said we weren't to take you past it."
"Aye." Davin looked at her. "I wouldn't have come anyway." He looked up the slope again. "Nobody goes past here. Only the Ashborn. And the gray ones, the ones that come up the drove road in the snow." He looked at Corin. "And they don't come back."
Corin did not say anything.
Davin looked at him for a long moment, the way he had looked at him from the ridge yesterday, in the snow. Careful. Measuring. A small, freckled, serious face, under a knitted cap.
Then he turned to Lyra.
He put his hand into the pocket of his cut-down coat, and took something out, and held it out to her on his palm. He held it out stiffly, at the end of his arm, the way a child holds out something to a horse that might bite.
It was a stone.
A small stone. Smooth and round and flat, the size of a large coin, and gray. Not the black of the rocks round them. A soft, pale, dove gray, like the stones on top of the cairns. And all the way round it, perfectly even, there ran a thin white ring, a line of some other, paler stone through the gray, as clean as if somebody had drawn it with a brush.
"For luck," said Davin.
Lyra took it. It was cold. It was very smooth, smoother than any river stone she had ever held. It fitted the hollow of her palm exactly.
"Thank you," she said. "Where did you get it?"
Davin shrugged. "Granda found it. Up there. When he was a boy, when the clerks came." He pointed with his chin at the rim. "He went up after them, on his own, to see. He found it on the top. He said they come from the other side. From the middle. He said the wind brings them, sometimes." He scratched his nose. "Everybody's got one. Everybody in the valleys. You put it on a cairn when somebody goes over. For luck." He looked at the cairn beside him, at the big gray stone on its top. "That one's for my uncle. He went over. With gray hands. When I was little."
Lyra looked at the stone in her hand, with its clean white ring.
She had seen a stone like it before. She was sure of it. She tried to think where, and could not. Then she thought: Corin. Corin had a stone like it. In his coat. A gray stone with a white line round it. Somebody had given it to him. She could not remember who.
She looked at Corin.
He was looking at the stone in her palm, and his face had gone very still. His gloved hand had gone to the pocket of his coat, the left-hand pocket, and was resting there, flat, over something small and round.
He did not take it out. He did not say anything.
"Thank you, Davin," said Lyra again.
Davin nodded. He looked at Oren, and then at Corin, and then, very quickly, at Lyra. Then he turned round and called the dog, and went away down the slope the way they had come, fast, sliding on the loose stones, not looking back. Flint went after him with its tail still down. At the line where the snow began again, the boy stopped, and turned, and lifted his stick over his head, once.
Then he went on down toward the moor, and got smaller, and smaller, until he was a dark dot on the white, and then nothing.
Lyra put the stone inside her glove, against her palm, where she could feel it.
Then she turned round, and followed the others up the last of the slope, to the top.
The Glass
The top of the Lip was a ridge of bare black rock, no wider than a road, running away to the east and the west as far as she could see.
Lyra came up onto it last. Oren was already there, standing very still with the packs on his back. Corin was beside him, leaning on his crutch, breathing hard. Neither of them said anything. Neither of them turned round when she came up.
She came and stood beside them, and looked where they were looking.
And the world ended.
That was what it was like. That was the only way she could think of it, afterward. As if she had climbed a stair in a house she knew, in the dark, and at the top of it there was no landing. No floor. Only air.
The ground fell away in front of her. Not a cliff. A long, steep slope, going down and down from the black rock at her feet, gray and brown and dull, covered with a dense low tangle of something that she saw, after a moment, was thorn. Thorn bushes, small and dark and leafless, growing so close together that they made a sort of rough gray fur over the whole slope. And all of them, every single bush, leaned away. Away from what lay at the bottom. Bent hard back toward the ridge where she stood, as if they had spent their whole lives trying to climb up out of the valley and get away.
At the bottom of the slope, a long way down, the thorns stopped. There was a line of taller trees there, thin and black, standing in a row like people at the edge of a river who do not want to get their feet wet. And beyond the trees, beyond everything, as far as she could see, to the edge of the sky and past it, there was the Glass.
It was flat.
She had known it would be. Everyone said the Glass was flat. But she had not known what flat meant. Flat like a table. Flat like a pond on a still day. Flat in every direction, east and west and north, without a hill, without a hollow, without a tree or a rock or a house, all the way to the line where the sky came down to meet it, and the sky came down very far away indeed.
And it shone.
The sun was low, now, in the west, behind thin cloud. A weak, white winter sun. And the Glass took that weak light and threw it back up into the sky doubled, so that the whole world below the Lip was full of a strange, cold, upward-falling brightness, like the light inside a shell. The Glass was dark. That was the strangest thing. It was dark, a deep green-black, like the color of a bottle held up to a candle. And yet it shone. It shone the way wet slate shines in rain. The way the inside of an oyster shines. It shone everywhere at once, without any one bright place, so that she could not look at it for long without her eyes beginning to water.
No snow lay on it. Not one patch. On the slope below her the thorns were dusted white, and the trees at the bottom had snow along their branches, but where the trees ended, the snow ended, in a line, and beyond the line there was only the green-black shining, bare and clean and enormous.
And the hum.
Up here on the Lip there was nothing between her and it. It came straight up off the Glass and hit her, the way heat comes off a fire when you open an oven door. She felt it in the soles of her feet and in her teeth and in her breastbone and behind her eyes. It filled her up. It was so low and so steady and so everywhere that after a moment she could not tell anymore whether she was hearing it or whether she had always been hearing it and had only just noticed.
The wind came off the Glass into her face. It was cold. But it was not as cold as it should have been. There was something in it. A faint, faint warmth, like a breath. And a smell. Very faint. Sweet and dusty and a little burnt, like sugar left too long on a stove.
She found that she was holding her breath.
She let it out, and it smoked in front of her face and blew away behind her over the ridge.
"Saints," said Oren. Very quietly. "Saints and all their candles."
That was all he said.
Lyra looked at Corin.
He was standing a little in front of them both, at the very edge of the ridge, where the black rock gave way to the thorn slope. He had let go of the crutch with one hand. It was leaning against his side, and his gray-gloved hand was hanging loose. His face was turned toward the Glass, and the cold upward light from it lay on his face and made it pale, almost silver, and his eyes were open very wide.
He was not frightened. She could see that. She had seen him frightened. This was not that.
He looked, she thought, like a man who has walked a long way in the dark and come round a corner and seen a house. A house with its windows lit. And stopped, and stood, because he knows the house. Because he is sure he knows it. Because something in him, something older than he is, knows exactly which door it is and which stair goes up inside it and which room at the top is his.
And he does not know the house at all.
His lips moved.
"I've seen this," he said.
Lyra did not move. She did not say anything. Beside her, she felt Oren go still.
Corin's hand came up, slowly, the gray glove, and touched the side of his head. Behind his left ear. The place where the skin was burned, where the lamp at the Bourse had scorched him. He pressed his fingers there, as if something hurt.
"I've seen this," he said again.
Then his face changed. Something went out of it. The wide eyes narrowed, and the silver light on his face seemed to go gray, and he took his hand away from his ear and looked at it, at the glove, as if it belonged to somebody else.
"No," he said. "No. I haven't." He was quiet for a moment. "I don't know."
He put his hand back on the crutch.
Nobody said anything.
The wind blew off the Glass. The hum went on and on. Far below them, at the bottom of the long gray slope of leaning thorns, in the line of black trees at the very edge of the shining, something moved.
Lyra saw it, and pointed.
It was smoke.
A thin gray thread of smoke, going up out of the trees. Going up straight, at first, in the still air down there, and then catching the wind off the Glass and bending, and leaning back toward them, toward the Lip, the way the thorns leaned. A sweet, gray smoke. Even from up here, she thought she could smell it. Not peat. Not wood. Something else. Something like burnt honey.
And then, as she looked, she saw that it was not one thread. It was many. Many thin gray threads of smoke, going up out of the last line of trees at the edge of the Glass, leaning back on the wind.
Somebody lived down there.
Ask at Last Thorn, Granny Abbe had said. Ashborn don't lie about the dead. They carry them.
Lyra closed her hand inside her glove round Davin's stone, with its white ring, and held it very tight. Then she started down the slope toward the smoke, and after a moment, without a word, the other two came after her.
