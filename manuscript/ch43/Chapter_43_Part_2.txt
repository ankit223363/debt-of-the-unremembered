Chapter 43: The Auditor at the Bourse, Part 2 of 5: The Lamp Boy on the Stair
The Lamp Boy on the Stair
The stair was very steep.
She went down it with one gloved hand on the rope that ran along the wall on iron rings, and the other holding up the skirt of her coat, and her eyes on the steps. They were worn into hollows in the middle, every one, by a hundred years of feet carrying trays and buckets and coal. The hollows were slippery with damp. Gant went ahead of her with a lantern, talking. She did not listen to what he said. She listened to the hum.
It grew as they went down. Not louder, exactly. Deeper. As if it were not in the air at all but in the stone, in the steps under her shoes, coming up through the soles into her feet and her knees. She had felt it in the doorway. Now she felt it in her teeth. In the back of her neck. In the fine small bones of her gloved hands on the rope.
She knew what made a hum like that. Every Auditor did. It was the sound a great many memories make when they are kept close together, in one place, under one roof, for a long time. The Bank hummed like that, very faintly, from the cold rooms in the Third Basement, if you stood in the hall at night when the doors were shut. It was why the night porters wore wool in their ears.
This was stronger than the Bank.
She made a note of that. Not in her book. In her head. Stronger than the Bank.
Nine steps down, the stair turned.
It turned to the left, sharply, round a square stone pillar, so that you could not see what was below until you were on the turn. Gant went round it with his lantern. And stopped.
"Oh dear," he said.
Elara came round the pillar behind him, and stopped too.
There was a man sitting on the stair.
On the ninth step, right at the turn, where the stair was narrowest. Sitting, with his back against the pillar and one leg drawn up under him and the other stuck out straight across the step in front of him, so stiff and straight that it might have been a log of wood. It blocked the stair from the pillar to the wall. Nobody could have got past it without stepping over it.
He was an old man. A lamp boy. She could see that from his clothes. The black oily coat, too short in the sleeves. The black gloves, shiny with oil, with the fingers worn through at the tips. A bag of wicks and rags slung across his chest on a cord. A crutch across his lap, of pale ash wood. And the leg. Bound from the hip to the ankle in white linen, under the black trousers, so tight that she could see the shape of the bandage through the cloth.
Gant's lantern lit his face.
It was not a pleasant face. Long and gray and loose, the way old men's faces go when they have been hungry for a long time. A nose that had been broken at least twice and had set crooked. A chin that seemed too small for the rest. White eyebrows, thick and wild. Soot in every line, so deep that it looked like part of the skin. And a squint. The left eye looked away to the left, at the wall, at nothing, as if something just past her shoulder had caught it.
And a mole. Small and dark, under the left eye.
The old man looked up at them.
At Gant. At the lantern. At her.
And his mouth went down at the left corner.
Elara did not know why she stopped breathing.
She stood on the eighth step with her hand on the rope and looked down at the old lamp boy on the ninth, in the lantern light, and the hum came up through the stone into her teeth, and she did not breathe. She did not know the face. She was quite sure of that. She had never seen it in her life. It was an ugly face, and a stranger's, and it did not mean anything to her at all.
But something in her had gone very still. The way it had gone still at her window that morning, when the man on the gray horse looked up.
"Up," said Gant. Pleasantly. "Up, man. Out of the Auditor's way. Can't you see who it is?"
The old lamp boy did not move.
"I can't, sir," he said.
His voice was hoarse and low. An old man's voice, cracked and rough, from the Lower Docks. Not a voice she knew.
"I can't get up, sir. Not quick. It's the leg." He put a gloved hand on the white-bound leg, flat. "It don't bend, sir. I was trimming the north lamps for Mister Castellan and I come down too fast and it went. I'm waiting on the young master. He's gone down for a ladder to get me up." He looked at Elara, with the eye that did not squint. "Begging the lady's pardon."
Gant sighed.
It was a small, soft, patient sigh. The sigh of a man who has a great many things to do and a great many people who make them harder.
"Mister Castellan," he said. "Of course. He does like his lamps." He turned to Elara. "I do apologize, Auditor. One moment. We'll have him out of the way." He looked past her, up the stair. "Mister—" He did not know Gage's name. "Your man. Perhaps he could—"
Elara did not turn round. She did not need to.
She heard Gage come down the stair behind her. Slowly. One step at a time. The steps were too narrow for him, and the ceiling too low, and he had to come down sideways, bent nearly double, with his huge shoulder brushing the wall. She felt him come past her on the eighth step, so close that the edge of his coat touched her sleeve. And she watched him go down onto the ninth, beside the old man, and stop there. And look down.
Mister Gage had a plain face. That was what everyone said about him, in the Bank, when they said anything. A plain, heavy, unfinished face, like a face that had been started in clay and then left. Nobody knew what he was thinking. Nobody ever had.
Elara watched him look down at the old lamp boy.
And she saw his face change.
Not much. Not so that Gant, standing below with the lantern, would have seen anything at all. But Elara had stood beside that face every working day for six years, her book said, and she saw it. Something came into the plain unfinished face. Something that moved under the clay. The way a fish moves under ice.
Then it went away.
Gage bent down. He put one huge arm round the old man's back, under the arms, and the other under the stiff white leg, under the knee. And he lifted him. All at once. The way you lift a child out of a cart. The old man made a sound, a sharp hiss of breath through his teeth, as the leg came up off the step. And then Gage was holding him against his chest, high up, with the stiff leg out straight in front of them both like a pole, and the crutch fallen clattering down the steps below.
And Gage stood there on the ninth step, holding him, and did not move.
He was looking at Elara.
She looked back at him. At the plain face, very close to hers now, a little below her, on the step beneath. And at the old man's face, beside it, against Gage's shoulder. Gray and loose and sooty, with the crooked nose and the squint and the mole. And the mouth. The mouth, gone down at the left corner. Waiting.
She could have said anything.
She knew that. She knew it the way she knew the weight of a debt in a jar by touching it. She could have said, Mister Gant, who is this man? She could have said, Your lamp boy is very young under his paint. She could have said, Mister Gage, put him down. She could have taken off her right glove, there on the stair, and put her bare hand on the old man's face, and read him, all of him, every account, every balance, at the cost of one day of her own.
She did not.
She did not know why. Her book would not say. She would write it down tonight, and look at it, and still not know.
"Thank you, Mister Gage," she said.
Her voice came out quite steady. Cool and clear and even. The voice of the Fourth Desk.
"Put him on the landing below," she said. "By the wall. Where he won't be stepped on." She looked at the old man. Only at his eyes. The one that looked at her, and the one that looked away. "Mind your leg, grandfather."
The old man looked at her for a long moment.
"Yes, lady," he said. Hoarse. Low. "Thank you, lady."
Gage carried him down.
Elara went down after them, one step at a time, with her hand on the rope. Past the place on the ninth step where the old man had sat. There was something on the stone there. Dark. Wet. A few drops. She did not look at it. She stepped over it, and went on down.
At the bottom of the stair there was a small square landing, and a passage going off it, curving away into the dark, lit at intervals by small yellow lamps on brackets. Gage set the old lamp boy down against the wall of the landing, very gently, the way you would set down a basket of eggs. And straightened up. And picked up the fallen crutch from the bottom step, and laid it across the old man's knees.
And then, before he turned away, he did something Elara had never seen him do in six years.
He put his huge hand on the old man's head. On the white wild hair. Just for a moment. The way you put your hand on a child's head when it is going to sleep.
Then he took it away, and turned, and came back to Elara's side, and stood there, bent under the low ceiling, with his plain face closed again like a door.
Gant had not seen. Gant was looking down the passage with his lantern, and talking.
"—along here, Auditor, round to the south. Past the lift. It's a little way. I'm afraid we're a warren down here. Old cellars, you know. The house is built on the bones of three older ones." He turned and smiled at her. "Shall we?"
Elara did not look back at the old man by the wall.
She followed Gant's lantern along the curving passage, with Gage beside her and the clerks behind her, into the hum. And she counted her steps, the way she counted everything.
And behind her, very faint, before the curve of the wall took it away, she heard a young man's voice. Quiet. Polite. Clear. Coming up from somewhere below.
"There you are, Grandfather. I've brought the ladder."
The Counting Room
She counted four hundred and twelve steps from the foot of the kitchen stair to the door of the counting room.
She did not mean to. She always counted. Her book said she always had, since before the book began. Steps, stairs, panes of glass, coins, the beats of her own heart when she could not sleep. It was a thing her body did on its own, the way a clock ticks. She thought, sometimes, at night, that it might be the oldest thing about her. Older than the Bank. Older than the gold tooth. Something left over from before, that nobody had thought to take.
Four hundred and twelve.
The passage went round in a long slow curve to the right, the way a passage goes round the inside of a tower. Stone walls, very old, sweating damp. Yellow lamps on brackets every twenty paces. And between the yellow lamps, every so often, on the outer wall, a different kind of lamp. A small brass house with glass on four sides. Gray. Burning with a light that did not light anything. She counted three of them.
And doors. On the outer wall, between the lamps. Low arched openings into the dark, without doors at all, with a smell of pine and straw coming out of them. And on the inner wall, every so often, a real door. Iron, or oak. Shut.
And the hum, in the floor. Coming from the inside of the curve. From the middle. Getting stronger as they went round, and then not, and then stronger again.
And something else, under the hum. She did not have a name for it. A slow rise and fall in the stone under her feet. As if the floor were breathing.
She made a note of that too. In her head.
The floor breathes.
The counting room was on the inner wall, by one of the gray lamps. A low door, standing open. Light behind it. Gant stood aside to let her go in first.
She went in.
It was a long low room with a vaulted ceiling, and every wall was racked from the floor to the curve of the vault with wooden racks, like the racks in a wine cellar. And in every round hole in every rack there was a jar. Gray glass. Cheap. The kind the Ministry bought by the thousand. Each with its paper label tied round the neck with string.
Hundreds. She did not count them. She would, later. She would have to. That was what she was here for.
In the middle of the room, under a lamp that hung from a chain, there was a long plain table. And on the table, in a row, from one end to the other, there were ledgers. Fourteen of them. Big ones. Bound in canvas, with straps and brass clasps. Lined up side by side with their spines toward her, square and neat. Like good children, Gant had said.
And at the end of the table, a woman was standing.
Small. Thin. Gray-haired, in a dark dress with a white collar, with a pencil behind her ear. Very straight. Her hands folded in front of her at the waist, the way Elara folded hers. Her face was narrow and gray and closed, with deep lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth, and her eyes were small and dark and very quick. They went to Elara the moment she came through the door. And went over her, once, from the hat to the boots. The way a clerk's eyes go down a column of figures.
Elara knew that look. She used it herself.
"Mistress Fane," said Gant. "Who keeps our books. Mistress Fane, the Auditor of the Fourth Desk."
Fane did not curtsy. She inclined her head. One inch. Exactly.
"Auditor."
"Mistress Fane."
They looked at each other across the long table and the fourteen ledgers.
Elara had read Mistress Fane's books for six years. Every Midwinter. She knew the hand. Small and hard and upright, like nails in a board. She knew the way the woman ruled her columns, in red, with a ruler, and the way she made her sevens, with a bar across. She knew the way she wrote a figure she did not want anybody to look at too closely: smaller than the others, and a little to the right, tucked into the corner of the column like a child hiding behind its mother's skirt. She had never once, her book said, seen the woman's face.
It was the face she had expected.
"Shall we begin?" said Gant pleasantly. "Mrs. Pratt is sending down tea. And a little something. She does a very good seed cake." He pulled out a chair at the head of the table, for Elara, and held it. "I'll be just here, Auditor, if you need anything. Anything at all."
He meant behind her chair. Elara knew that. He would stand behind her chair for four hours and smile, and answer every question, and bring out every book she asked for, and watch every page she turned. He did it every year. Her book said so. Gant behind the chair. Four hours. Smiling.
She sat down.
Gage did not. Gage stood by the door, against the wall, between two racks of jars, with his head bent under the vault and his huge hands hanging. Where he could see the door, and the table, and Gant's back.
Mister Harrow sat at her left, with the green ledger open. Young Mister Penn sat at her right, with the ink case and the seals and the scales, very pink, very upright. Mister Harrow cleared his throat.
"Madam," he said. "The Bank's levy first. As always. The tenth part of every sale above the third tier, and the twentieth part of every sale below, since last Midwinter. Against the Bourse's own sale books, and the Bank's copies." He turned a page of the green ledger. "The Bourse paid, in the year, a levy of four thousand one hundred and twelve crowns."
"Four thousand one hundred and twelve," said Elara. She pulled off her right glove, a finger at a time, and laid it on the table. "The first book, please, Mistress Fane."
Fane slid it across the table to her.
Elara opened it.
She did not read it the way other people read. She knew that. She had watched other clerks read ledgers, down a page with a finger, line by line, adding in their heads, their lips moving. She did not need to do that. She turned the pages, one after another, at the speed of a woman turning the pages of a picture book, and she did not read the figures at all.
She looked at them.
The whole page at once. The way you look at a face. And the figures that were right lay flat and quiet on the page, like still water. And the figures that were wrong did not. They stood up. They leaned. They looked at her, the way a liar looks at you when he thinks you are not looking at him.
It was not Ledger-Sight. Ledger-Sight was for people. For the accounts in a living mind, read by the bare hand, at the cost of a day. This was only arithmetic. Her book called it the knack. She had always had it. Since before.
She turned a page. And another. The clerks waited. Gant stood behind her chair, very close, so that she could smell him. Lavender water, and starch, and under it something faint and sweet and sickly, like fruit going soft in a bowl.
She turned forty pages. Fifty. Sixty.
On the sixty-first, a figure stood up.
Small. Tucked into the corner of the column. A little to the right. Like a child behind its mother's skirt.
Elara put her bare forefinger on it.
"This one, Mistress Fane," she said. "The fourteenth of the month after harvest. A sale of—" She looked. "Courage, common. A lot of six. To a buyer on the Hill. Sixty crowns. The levy paid is three. That's the twentieth part."
"It was a second-tier lot," said Fane. Her voice was small and hard and dry, like her writing.
"It was," said Elara. "It was sold at sixty crowns. A second-tier lot of six, common courage, does not fetch sixty crowns. Not on the Hill. Not anywhere. It fetches twelve." She did not take her finger off the figure. "Either the buyer was a fool, Mistress Fane, or the lot was not common courage. And if it was not common courage, it was not a second-tier lot. And if it was not a second-tier lot, the levy owed was six. Not three."
The counting room was very quiet.
Behind her, Gant laughed. A small soft pleased laugh, the laugh of a man watching a very good horse take a fence.
"Three crowns, Auditor," he said. "On a lot of six. You found it in a quarter bell. Every year I tell the Lady, she'll find nothing this time, Fane's been so careful. And every year you find three crowns." He sounded delighted. "Mister Harrow, please, write it down. The Bourse owes the Bank three crowns. With our compliments."
Mister Harrow cleared his throat, and wrote it down.
Elara turned the page.
She did not look round at Gant. She did not need to. She knew what he was doing. He was giving her the three crowns. He was giving her a small mistake to find, early, in the first book, so that she would find it and be pleased, and turn the pages a little faster after that, and be a little less careful in the eighth book and the ninth.
He did it every year. Her book said so.
Gant gives you something small in the first hour. Thank him for it. Then go slower.
