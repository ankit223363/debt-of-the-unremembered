Chapter 43: The Auditor at the Bourse, Part 3 of 5: Friday Lines
Friday Lines
She went slower.
The second book. The third. The fourth. The tea came down from Mrs. Pratt on a tray, with a seed cake, and young Mister Penn ate two slices and dropped crumbs on the seals, and Mister Harrow cleared his throat at him. Elara did not eat. She did not drink. She turned the pages.
The figures lay flat and quiet, page after page. Bought. Sold. Levy paid. The Bourse had had a good year. Childhoods had sold well in the spring. Talents had sold well in the summer. A first love, Tier Three, had gone to a Duchess for nine hundred crowns, and the Bank had had its ninety, to the farthing. A war, Tier Four, was entered in the book for Midwinter, unsold. Lot 39. Eleven years under the colors. One soldier. She looked at the line for a long moment, and did not know why, and turned the page.
Gant stood behind her chair, and smiled, and smelled of lavender and starch and soft fruit.
In the seventh book, she found the bulk lots.
They were in a book of their own. Thinner than the others. Bound in black canvas instead of brown. BULK. LOWER DOCKS, RIVER PARISHES, SALTGATE. And inside, in Mistress Fane's small hard hand, column after column of figures that were not like the figures in the other books.
In the other books, every line was a person. A name. Widow of a lamplighter, Comb Street. Her wedding day. Even when the name was only a girl or a docker, it was one girl. One docker. One day.
In the black book, there were no people.
There were crates.
Blue. Cold. Twelve crates. To the North.
Yellow. Kindnesses, small. Forty jars. Blended. To the Hill, by the case.
Gray. Sad. Sixty jars. Blended. To the Hill.
Black. Fright. Thirty jars. To the Hill, for the theatres.
She turned the pages of the black book, and the figures lay flat and quiet on every one, and the levy was paid on every line, to the farthing. The Bank's twentieth part. Bulk lots were all below the third tier. A twentieth.
Nothing stood up.
She turned another page. And another.
And then she came to a page that was different.
Not the figures. The figures were flat. The page itself. It was a little dirtier than the others, at the bottom corner. A little more handled. As if it had been turned back to, and looked at, and turned back to again. And down the left-hand column, every fourth or fifth line, there was a line that began with the same three words.
Fri. Health. St. Bride's.
She read them.
Fri. Health. St. Bride's. 14 glasses. Slag, drawn. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P.
Fri. Health. St. Bride's. 19 glasses. Slag, drawn. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P.
Fri. Health. St. Bride's. 31 glasses. Slag, drawn, fast. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P.
And near the bottom of the page, the last:
Fri. Health. St. Bride's. 52 glasses, very fast. Good yield. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P.
And beside each line, in the column for the levy, a figure. The Bank's twentieth part. Paid.
She put her bare forefinger on the last line.
She did not say anything for a moment. She was not looking at the figures. The figures were flat. The levy was right. The Bank had had its twentieth part of every glass. Every Friday. Since the summer.
She was looking at the words.
Slag, drawn.
"Mistress Fane," she said. "What is Saint Bride's?"
Fane's small dark eyes moved, very slightly. To Gant. And back.
"A chapel," said Fane. "By the Cut. With no roof. There's a doctor in the crypt. For the fever children."
"And the Health?"
"The Office of Public Health, Auditor," said Gant pleasantly, behind her. "On the Hill. A most excellent office. They have been very good to us." She could hear him smiling. "The poor doctor draws the slag from the fever children. On glasses, the old way. To save their lives, you understand. Slag is very dangerous to keep. The Office collects the glasses every Friday, for disposal. And very kindly, rather than tipping them in the lime pits with the Bank's slag, they let us have them. For a small consideration. And we blend them. The Hill likes a little kindness in its tea." A pause. "Waste not, as my mother used to say."
"And A. P.?"
"The officer of the Office who arranges it," said Gant. "A very charitable man. He gives the consideration to the doctor's work, I believe. Every farthing. For the children." Another pause. "The Bank has had its levy, I think, Auditor? On every glass?"
"It has," said Elara.
She did not take her finger off the line.
She was looking at the page. Not at the figures. At the bottom corner of the page. At the place where it was a little dirtier than the others. A little more handled.
And at a mark.
It was very small. At the very edge of the paper, on the right, beside the last line. Half on the page and half off it, as if someone had held the book open there with one hand, pressing down on the edge with their thumb. A thumbprint. Black. Not ink. Ink was blue-black, and shiny. This was a dull deep black, with a little grit in it, so that when she bent close she could see it glitter faintly in the lamplight, like frost on a coal heap.
Coal.
A coal-black thumbprint, on the bottom corner of the page, beside 52 glasses, very fast. Good yield.
It was fresh. She could see that. The whorls of it were sharp and clear, the lines of the skin, as plain as the lines on a map. Not smudged. Not faded. A few days old at most. And it was not Fane's thumb. Fane's hands were folded in front of her at the end of the table, small and white and clean, with a single faint ink stain on the second finger of the right. And it was not Gant's. Gant's hands were soft and pink and plump, and his thumb would have made a print twice as wide.
This was a long thumb. A thin one. A man's.
She looked at it for perhaps two breaths.
She knew what she would see if she touched it. Not with her finger. With her bare hand. Flat. The way she had laid her hand on the blue print on her own glass desk, on Thursday morning, when it was fading. She would see whose it was. She would read it, the way she read a jar, the way she read a living face, all at once, an account and a balance and a name. And it would cost her a day.
And Gant was behind her chair.
And Fane was watching her hands.
Elara took her bare forefinger off the line. Slowly. And picked up her white glove from the table beside the ledger. And put it on. A finger at a time. The way she always did. And then she laid her gloved hand down flat on the bottom corner of the page, over the black thumbprint, the way you lay your hand on a page to keep it from turning in a draft.
And left it there.
"Mister Harrow," she said. "Note, please. Book seven. Page—" She glanced at the number at the top. "Page ninety-four. The Saint Bride's lines. Levy paid in full. No error." She paused. "And note the source. Slag, drawn, from living persons, by a physician, outside the Bank. Collected by an officer of the Ministry. Sold by the Bourse, blended."
Mister Harrow's pen stopped.
He cleared his throat.
"Madam," he said. "That is—we don't usually note the source. Of bulk lots. Only the tier and the levy."
"Note it," said Elara.
Behind her, Gant had stopped smiling. She knew it without looking. She could feel it in the air at the back of her neck. A small change. A small cold place, where the warm pleased breath had been.
"Is there a difficulty, Auditor?" he said. Pleasantly.
"No," said Elara. "The levy is paid. The Bank has no difficulty." She turned her head, just a little, so that she could see him out of the corner of her eye, standing behind her chair with his round pink face and his gold spectacles. "The Bank keeps a note of where its money comes from, Mister Gant. That's all. So that if anybody ever asks, the Bank will know."
Gant looked at her for a long moment.
Then he smiled again. The pleasant smile, up into the round cheeks.
"Of course," he said. "Of course. Very proper. Mister Harrow, write it all down."
Mister Harrow wrote it all down.
Elara kept her gloved hand flat on the corner of the page until he had finished. Then she turned the page over, with her hand still on it, so that the thumbprint went face down onto the page before it. And closed the black book. And slid it back across the table to Mistress Fane.
Fane took it. She did not look at the corner. She looked at Elara.
And for one moment, across the long table, in the hum and the lamplight, the two women looked at each other. The one who kept the books, and the one who read them.
Then Fane put the black book on top of the others, square, with its spine toward her, and folded her hands at her waist.
"The eighth book, Auditor," she said.
Tier Four
At the half past ten, with eleven books done and three to go, Elara put down her pen and said, "Now the Tier Four lots, please, Mister Gant."
Gant was behind her chair. He had been behind it for two and a half hours. He had not sat down once, though there was a chair by the wall. He had not leaned on anything. He had brought her every book she asked for, and answered every question, and laughed at the three crowns, and at the eleven crowns in the fourth book, and at the forty-one crowns in the ninth, which had not been a mistake at all but something else, and which Mister Harrow had written down in a different column, with his lips pressed together.
Now he said, "Ah."
Just that. Softly. Pleasantly.
"Ah," he said again. "The Tier Fours. Of course. The Bank likes to weigh them itself. Every year. I'd forgotten."
"No," said Elara. "You hadn't."
She did not turn round. She took up her pen again and wrote, in her own small book, on her knee, under the table, where nobody could see. 10.30. Gant says he forgot the Tier Fours. He did not. Then she put the pen down.
"Lots thirty-nine, forty, and forty-one," she said. "Entered in the sale book for Midwinter. A War. A Marriage. A Lord's Last Evening. Tier Four, all three. The Bank's charter, clause nine. No memory of the fourth tier shall be offered for sale within the city unless it has first been weighed and sealed by the Fourth Desk of the Bank, in person." She folded her gloved hands on the ledger. "In person, Mister Gant. Not from a description in a catalogue. I need to see them. And touch them."
"Of course," said Gant. "Of course you do."
He was quiet for a moment. She could feel him thinking, behind her. It was a strange thing, to feel a man think. Like a cat watching a mouse hole, very still, and then the tail moving once.
"They're in the vault," he said. "The Lady's vault. Under the middle of the house. Where the hum is." A small soft laugh. "I'm afraid I can't open it, Auditor. Nobody can, but the Lady herself. And her ward."
"Then send for one of them."
"The Lady is upstairs. In the glass room. She doesn't come down to the cellars any more. She finds the stairs tiring." A pause. "Mister Castellan is in the house. On the north side, I believe. Seeing to the lamps." Another pause. "He's very fond of his lamps."
"Then send for Mister Castellan."
Gant did not move for a moment.
Then he went to the door of the counting room, and leaned out of it into the passage, and said something to someone out there. A boy. Elara heard a thin high voice say yes, Mister Gant, and feet running away along the stone, round the curve.
Gant came back. He did not come back to the place behind her chair. He went to the end of the table, beside Mistress Fane, and stood there, with his soft pink hands folded on his waistcoat over his gold watch-chain. So that he could see Elara's face.
"It may take a little while," he said. "The north side is a long way round."
"I'll do the twelfth book while I wait."
Fane slid the twelfth book across the table.
Elara opened it. She did not look at the figures. She looked at them with the front of her eyes, and the back of her eyes did something else.
It listened.
To the hum. It had been with her for nearly three hours now, coming up through the stone floor and the legs of the chair and the table into her body, steady as a pulse, so steady that she had almost stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heart. And to the other thing, under the hum. The slow rise and fall of the floor. The floor breathes.
She listened, with the twelfth book open under her gloved hands. And turned a page. And another.
And on the fourteenth page of the twelfth book, the hum stopped.
Not for long. For the space of one breath. Perhaps two. But it stopped. All at once. As if something very large, a long way off, in the middle of the house, had heard a sound and turned its head and gone still to listen.
Elara's hands went still on the page.
She felt it go. In her teeth. In the bones of her hands. In the soles of her feet, through her shoes, on the stone floor. The hum that had been coming up through everything for three hours, and was gone. And in its place a silence so deep and so sudden that she heard young Mister Penn, beside her, swallow.
And she heard the floor stop breathing.
In. And then nothing. Held. The way you hold a breath when you are listening for a footstep on the stair.
Nobody in the counting room moved.
Mister Harrow had looked up from the green ledger with his pen in the air. Young Mister Penn had gone white under his pink. By the door, Mister Gage had lifted his heavy head, very slowly, and turned it toward the inner wall. Toward the middle.
At the end of the table, Mistress Fane had not moved at all. But her small dark eyes had gone to Gant. And stayed there.
And Gant was smiling. A little. At nothing. With his head on one side.
Then the hum came back.
All at once, the way it had gone. Up through the floor, steady, deep, a pulse. And the floor breathed out. Long and slow, like a great beast settling back into sleep. And breathed in again.
Mister Penn let out a small high shaky laugh, and put his hand over his mouth.
"Saints," he said. "Saints, what was that?"
"Nothing," said Gant pleasantly. "Old cellars. The house settles. It does it in the cold." He smiled at young Mister Penn. "You'll get used to it."
Elara did not look at Gant.
She looked down at the twelfth book, open under her gloved hands, at the fourteenth page. And she found that she had pressed her right hand down so hard on the page that the glove had creased across the knuckles. And under the glove, her fingertips had gone cold. Colder than the room. Colder than the stone. The way they went cold when she touched an account that was not closed.
She had felt that twice today. At her window, in the morning, when the man on the gray horse looked up. And on the stair. On the ninth step.
And now.
She took her hand off the page.
Something in the middle of the house, she thought, heard something. And listened. And then let it go.
She did not know what she meant. She wrote it in her book anyway, on her knee, under the table. 10.40. The hum stopped. Two breaths. The floor held its breath. Gant smiled. And under it, after a moment: My hands went cold.
Then she turned the page, and went on with the twelfth book.
It was a quarter of a bell before the boy came back. And when he came, he did not come alone.
She heard them in the passage first. The thin quick feet of the boy. And with them, other feet. Not quick. Even. Careful. The feet of a young man who walks the same way every day of his life, and has learned to make no noise doing it.
And something else. A soft knock on the stone at every step, as if something hard were being carried, and touching the wall now and then.
The boy came in, and stood aside.
And Remy Castellan stood in the doorway of the counting room in his gray coat, with his gray gloves on, and a case in his arms.
Remy Brings the Case
She had seen him before. Her book said so. Every Midwinter for six years. The Lady's ward, Castellan. Gray. Young. Polite. Brings the Tier Fours. Says nothing. She did not remember any of the six times. But she knew him at once from the book, the way you know a house from a drawing of it.
Gray coat. Gray gloves. Dark hair cut short and brushed flat. A narrow pale clever face.
Paler than the book said.
That was the first thing she saw. He was very pale. Not the pale of a young man who works indoors by lamplight. A gray pale. The pale of a man who has been very ill and has only just got up. There was a fine wet shine along his hairline, under the dark hair, as if he had been sweating, in the cold of the cellars. And his eyes were too bright. They had a look she had seen before, in the eyes of people who came up the white steps of the Bank to sell, and sat on the glass, and got up again afterwards, and did not quite know for a moment where they were.
He came into the counting room carrying the case in both arms, close against his chest, the way you would carry a sleeping child.
It was a box. Long and narrow, the length of a man's forearm, made of a dark wood so black it looked wet. Very plain. No carving. No paint. A silver clasp on the front, and silver corners, and a silver handle on the lid that he was not using. He carried it underneath, with his arms round it. And as he came closer to the table, into the lamplight, Elara felt the air change.
It went cold.
Not the cold of the cellars. That was a wet cold, a stone cold, that came up from the floor. This came out of the box. A dry cold. A still cold. Like the air in a room where somebody is holding their breath. It came across the table toward her, ahead of the box, and touched her face, and her throat, and her gloved hands where they lay on the twelfth book.
And her fingertips, under the gloves, went colder still.
Remy set the box down on the table in front of her. Very gently. Between her hands and the row of ledgers. Square to the edge of the table, the way Fane set down her books.
Then he stood back, and took his hands away, and stood with them at his sides.
"Auditor," he said.
His voice was quiet and clear and polite. Exactly as the book said. A voice you would not remember.
"The Lady sends Lot forty-one first," he said. "She says it's the one the Desk will want." A pause. "She says the others will follow, if the Desk still wants them, afterwards."
Elara looked up at him.
"Afterwards?"
"Those were her words."
Nobody spoke. Gant, at the end of the table, was smiling at the black box with his head on one side.
Elara looked at Remy's hands.
The gray gloves. Fine kid, very good, cut close. On the right hand, on the side of the little finger, a faint smudge of ink had soaked through from underneath. And on the left hand—
On the left glove, at the ball of the thumb, there was a stain. Small. Round. Darker than the gray. Not ink. It had soaked through from inside, the way blood soaks through a bandage, and spread a little into the fine kid round it, and dried at the edges, and was still wet in the middle.
Fresh. Within the hour.
She looked at it for perhaps a breath.
Then she looked up at his face again. At the gray pallor, and the wet shine at the hairline, and the bright eyes. And he looked back at her. Steadily. Without any expression at all. The way, her book said, he always looked at everyone.
But she saw something. Or she thought she did. Very far back in the dark eyes. Not a door opening. Something smaller. A man standing very still in a doorway, holding something behind his back.
"You've hurt your hand, Mister Castellan," said Elara.
Remy did not look down at it.
"The vault, Auditor," he said. "It opens to the blood of the house. Mine, or the Lady's. One has to cut, every time." A small movement of his shoulders. "It doesn't heal well, in the cold. I'm used to it."
"It's very fresh."
"I've just come from the vault."
"From the north side," said Elara. "Mister Gant said you were seeing to the lamps on the north side."
The counting room went very quiet.
Remy did not change. Not his face. Not his voice. Not his hands at his sides.
"The vault is on the north side, Auditor," he said. "Under the middle. Past the lamps." A pause. "I was trimming the lamps when the boy came. I went straight down. The Lady sent word that I was to bring forty-one first, so I did."
"And the hum," said Elara. "At the twenty to eleven. When it stopped. Were you at the vault then, Mister Castellan?"
She did not know why she asked it. It was not the Bank's business. It was not in the charter. It was not in her book. It came out of her mouth before she had decided to say it, the way her hand had gone flat on the glass at her window that morning.
And Remy Castellan looked at her for a long moment.
"Yes," he said. "I was."
"What happened?"
"The vault knew someone was there," said Remy, "who it hadn't met before." He did not look at Gant. He did not look at anybody but her. "It does that, sometimes. When the house is full of strangers. Tonight there are a great many strangers in the house. The Bank's clerks. The Bank's man. The Bank's Auditor." A pause. "It noticed. And it stopped to look. And then it decided not to mind."
Elara said nothing.
At the end of the table, Gant laughed. A small soft delighted laugh.
"There, you see, Auditor," he said. "I told your young man. Old cellars. The house settles." He beamed at Remy. "Thank you, Remy. You may go back to your lamps."
"The Lady said I was to stay," said Remy. "Until the Desk had weighed it. And take it back down after."
Gant's smile did not change.
"Of course she did," he said.
Remy went and stood by the wall, near Gage, between two racks of jars. With his hands at his sides and his gray face turned toward the table. Gage did not look at him. Gage looked at the box.
Elara looked at it too.
It sat on the long table in front of her in the lamplight, black and plain and silver-cornered, square to the edge of the wood. And the cold came out of it, dry and still, and lay on her hands.
There was a label tied to the silver handle. A paper label, on a string, in Mistress Fane's small hard hand. LOT 41. TIER FOUR. A LORD'S LAST EVENING. C. T., BY PROXY. KEEP COLD.
And under that, in a different hand, much smaller, that she did not know:
Spiral: left.
She looked at the clasp.
It was a plain silver clasp. And pressed into the silver, very small, so worn that she had to tip her head to the lamp to see it, there was a mark. A shape, stamped into the metal by whoever had made the box. Not a seal. A maker's mark. The kind a silversmith puts on the bottom of a cup.
A small folded square. Corner to corner.
She looked at it for a long time.
Then she pulled off her right glove, a finger at a time, and laid it on the table beside the twelfth book. And put out her bare hand toward the box.
"Madam," said Mister Harrow, very low, beside her. He cleared his throat. "Madam, the Tier Fours, you usually—we usually use the scales first. The brass scales. And the reading glass. Before—" He stopped. "Before the hand."
"I know," said Elara.
She did not use the scales.
She laid her bare hand flat on the lid of the black box.
