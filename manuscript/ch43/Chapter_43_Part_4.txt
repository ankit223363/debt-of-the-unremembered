Chapter 43: The Auditor at the Bourse, Part 4 of 5: Weighing
Weighing
The wood was cold.
She had expected that. She had felt the cold coming out of the box since Remy carried it in. But the wood under her bare palm was colder than she had expected. Colder than ice. A cold that did not stay on the skin but went in, through the palm, into the small bones of the hand, and up the wrist, the way water goes up a wick.
She let it.
That was the first rule. Her book said so. The first line in the book, on the first page, before the lost days began, in a hand younger than hers. Do not fight the cold. Let it come. It is only the account opening.
She let it come.
And the account opened.
It was not like reading a person. She had read a great many people, in six years. Laid her bare hand on a forehead, or a wrist, or the back of a neck, and felt them open under it like a book. A person was loud. A person was full. A thousand things at once, all talking, all moving, the way a market is full. Debts and loves and fears and the taste of last night's supper. You had to stand in the middle of it and look for the one thing you needed, the way you look for one face in a crowd.
A jar was quiet.
A jar was one thing. Sealed. Held. Like a room with one person in it, sitting very still, waiting for you to come in.
She went in.
Warm.
That was the first thing. After the cold of the wood, warm. A fire. A big fire, in a big stone fireplace, with brass dogs, burning low and red. She did not see it. Ledger-Sight was not seeing. She felt it. The way you feel a fire on your face with your eyes shut.
A room. A study. She could feel the shape of it round her, the way you feel the shape of a room in the dark. Books. A great many books, on shelves to the ceiling. A desk. A chair by the fire. Leather. Old. Warm from somebody sitting in it.
And somebody sitting in it.
She felt him the way you feel a person in a room before you turn round. A man. Not young. Tired. So tired. Sitting very still in the chair by the fire, with his hands in his lap. Long hands. Thin. White. She could feel them, the way you feel your own hands when they are cold. The long thin fingers, lying quite still on his knees.
And then not still.
Moving. Slowly. Folding something. She could not tell what. It was not paper. It was not anything she could name. Something light, that he was folding in his long thin hands in his lap, by the fire, in half, and in half again, and corner to corner. Small. Smaller. The way you would fold a letter you did not want anyone to find.
Fold it small, said a voice. A woman's. Very far off. Not in the room. In the man's head, remembered. Soft, with an accent from somewhere over the water. Fold it small, my love, and keep it close.
And the long thin hands went on folding.
And under the folding, as they folded, so faint that Elara could not tell if she was feeling it or only remembering something else, the fingers tapped. On the thing they were folding. Each fold, a tap.
Four.
Two.
Nine.
Three.
Her own bare hand, flat on the lid of the black box, under the lamp in the counting room, moved.
She felt it move. She did not tell it to. The forefinger lifted, very slightly, off the wood. And came down. Once.
And as it came down, the room in the jar changed.
Not the study. Something under the study. Something folded inside the folding. Like a page folded inside a letter. She did not open it. She could not. It was too small. Too tight. Folded too many times. But she felt it, inside, the way you feel a stone inside a closed fist.
Cold. A different cold. Night cold. Outside. Wind on a hill. A smell of wet grass, and horses, and woodsmoke, and something burning that was not wood.
And a sound. Very small. Very far down. At the bottom of the folding.
A drum. Not beaten. Tapped. With sticks, on the edge of a board.
And a child's voice. A girl's. High and small and bright, the way children's voices are when they have found something.
Cor, look—
Elara took her hand off the box.
She did it fast. Too fast. She heard her own chair scrape back on the stone. She heard young Mister Penn say madam in a frightened voice. She felt her bare hand come up off the black wood as if it had been burned, and go to her chest, and press there, flat, over the place where her small black book was, in the inside pocket of her coat, against her heart.
She was breathing very fast.
The counting room came back round her. The racks of gray jars. The long table. The fourteen ledgers. The lamp on its chain. Mister Harrow, half out of his chair, with his long neck stretched toward her and his mouth open. Young Mister Penn, white as paper. Gant, at the end of the table, very still, watching her, with his head a little on one side. Not smiling. Fane, beside him, with her hands folded, watching her too.
And by the wall, between the racks of jars, Mister Gage. Who had taken one step toward the table. Only one. And stopped.
And beside him, Remy Castellan. Gray-faced. Still. Looking at her with his too-bright eyes.
Nobody spoke.
Elara made herself breathe slower. In. Out. She counted the breaths. Four. Five. Six. Up to nine. And then again.
Then she felt for it.
The way she did every night. Feeling inside herself for the hole. For the place where a day had been, this morning, and was not now.
She found it at once.
It was not a day from long ago. It never was, with Ledger-Sight. It always took a day from somewhere she could not see, from the dark back of herself, a day she would never know she had had. This one was no different. A hole. Smooth-edged. Gone. She could not tell what had been in it.
But it was warm.
The edges of the hole were warm. The way a bed is warm after somebody has got out of it. She had never felt that before. Not in two thousand four hundred and nine lost days.
She sat in her chair in the counting room under the Bourse with her bare hand pressed flat on her chest, and felt the warm edges of the place where a day had been, and did not know whose day it was.
"Auditor," said Gant softly. "Are you quite well?"
Elara looked at him.
"Quite," she said.
Her voice came out steady. She did not know how.
She picked up her white glove from the table and put it on. A finger at a time. She did not hurry. Her hand was shaking. She did not let it shake while it went into the glove. Then she folded both her gloved hands on the twelfth book in front of her, and looked at the black box, and spoke in the voice of the Fourth Desk.
"Mister Harrow," she said. "Write, please."
Mister Harrow sat down. He picked up his pen. It took him two tries.
"Lot forty-one," said Elara. "A Lord's Last Evening. Weighed by the Fourth Desk, in person, by the hand. Tier Four, confirmed. One evening, entire, unspent. Sealed at the source, and folded at the source. The fold is not to be opened by the Bourse, or by the buyer, except in the presence of the Fourth Desk."
The pen stopped.
"Madam," said Mister Harrow. He cleared his throat. "Madam, that last—the presence of the Desk—that isn't—"
"Clause eleven," said Elara. "Where a memory of the fourth tier is found to be folded, or bound, or otherwise held by art beyond common sealing, the Fourth Desk may require that it be opened only in the presence of the Desk, for the protection of the buyer and the Bank." She did not look at him. She looked at Gant. "The clause has not been used in forty years. It is still in the charter. Write it."
Mister Harrow wrote it.
At the end of the table, Gant had not moved. He stood with his soft pink hands folded on his waistcoat over his gold watch-chain, and looked at her, and his round mild face did nothing at all.
"The Lady," he said at last, very pleasantly, "will be so interested."
"I'm sure she will," said Elara. "Mister Penn. The seal."
Young Mister Penn fumbled in the ink case, and brought out the green wax, and the brass seal of the Fourth Desk, and a candle, and lit it, with his pink hands shaking. He held the wax in the flame. It dripped, green, onto the paper label on the silver handle of the black box. Onto the words KEEP COLD.
Elara pressed the seal into it.
The Fourth Desk. A closed book, with a key across it. And round the edge, very small, WEIGHED AND HELD.
She took the seal away. The green wax set, hard and bright, on the label.
"Thank you, Mister Castellan," she said, without looking round. "You may take it back down."
Remy came to the table. He picked up the black box in both arms, and held it against his chest. He looked at her for a moment over the top of it, with his too-bright eyes in his gray face.
Then he said, very quietly, so quietly that only she could hear it, and perhaps Gage:
"Thank you, Auditor."
And carried it out.
Midnight
She weighed the other two with the scales.
Remy brought them, one after the other. Lot thirty-nine first. A War. In a long plain case of gray wood, with iron corners, heavy, so that he carried it against his chest with his arms straining and set it down with care. Then Lot forty. A Marriage. In a small round box of white leather, with a pearl clasp, light as a hat box. He set it down beside the other, and went back to his place by the wall, and did not say anything.
Elara did not take off her glove.
Young Mister Penn opened the brass scales on the table. They were old scales, older than the Bank, the Bank's book said, made by a maker in a city across the sea whose name had been ground off the base. Two shallow pans of silver on a fine brass beam, and between them a small round reading glass on a stand, like a jeweler's loupe. The case went in one pan. The weights went in the other. And you looked through the reading glass at the place where the beam balanced, and read the tier from the color of the light.
She weighed them both. A War: Tier Four. Confirmed. Eleven years. One man. Unspent. A Marriage: Tier Four. Confirmed. Thirty-one years. Two people. Unspent. The light in the glass went deep blue for both of them, the color of the sky an hour after sunset. Mister Harrow wrote them down. Young Mister Penn dripped the green wax, and she pressed the seal.
Neither of them was folded.
She would have known if they were. She did not need to touch them to know. They sat in the silver pans of the scales, plain and heavy and quiet, the way a jar is quiet. Not like the black box. Not holding their breath.
Remy took them away.
She finished the twelfth book. And the thirteenth. And the fourteenth, which was only a list of the jars in the counting room racks, by number, and which she checked against the racks with Mister Harrow, walking along them with a lamp, reading the labels, at the speed of a woman walking past a shop window. Six hundred and eleven jars. All present. All as listed. The Bourse's books were very good books. Her own book said they always were.
And at the stroke of midnight, with the bells of the city coming down very faint through the stone, so faint that she felt them rather than heard them, she closed the fourteenth book and laid her gloved hands flat on top of it.
"The audit is closed," she said.
Mister Harrow let out a long breath, the way he did every year, her book said. As if he had been holding it since the eighth bell. He began to put the green ledger away into its satchel.
"Owed by the Bourse to the Bank," said Elara, "in levy, from errors found: one hundred and seventy-three crowns. To be paid within the week." She looked at the end of the table. "Mister Gant."
"With our compliments," said Gant.
He had come back to stand behind her chair. She had felt him come. He did not touch the chair. He only stood very close to it, so that she could smell the lavender and the starch, and under them the soft sweet sickly smell of fruit gone over.
"A hundred and seventy-three crowns," he said. "That's eleven more than last year. The Lady will be so cross with Fane." He did not sound as if he thought the Lady would be cross with Fane. "And clause eleven. I shall have to tell her about clause eleven. She does so like to learn something new."
"I'm sure."
"So the Desk will be with us on Midwinter night, then." It was not a question. "At the sale. When forty-one is opened."
"The Desk will be present," said Elara, "when the fold is opened. As the clause requires."
"Of course." Gant moved round, at last, from behind her chair, and came to the side of the table, where she could see him. He smiled at her. The pleasant smile, up into the round pink cheeks. "Then the Lady will want to send you an invitation. Properly. On her own paper. For the Masquerade." He tilted his head. "Everybody wears a mask, Auditor. Even the Bank. It's the custom. The Lady will want to know what color."
Elara looked at him.
"White," she said. "Plain. No ornament."
"White," said Gant. "How very like the Bank."
He bowed. Not low. Just low enough.
Then, as he straightened up, he said, in exactly the same voice, as if it were the same sentence: "Did you happen to see anybody on the kitchen stair, Auditor? As you came down?"
The counting room was quiet.
Mister Harrow had stopped putting the ledger away. Young Mister Penn had stopped packing the scales. By the wall, Mister Gage did not move. By the other wall, Remy Castellan, who had come back from the vault the last time and stood waiting, with his gray face and his bright eyes, did not move either.
"A lamp boy," said Elara.
"Ah," said Gant. "Yes. A lamp boy." He smiled. "An old one, I think? With a bad leg? Sitting on the stair?"
"You saw him yourself, Mister Gant."
"So I did. So I did." He looked at her with his head on one side. "It's only that the Lady has three lamp boys, Auditor. And two of them are young, and quick on their feet. And the third, old Tobias, has a wooden foot, and is at home in Saltpetre Row tonight with the croup. His wife sent word at the sixth bell." A pause. "So I find myself wondering whose lamp boy that was."
Nobody spoke.
Elara looked at him. At the round pink pleasant face, and the gold spectacles, and the small mild eyes behind them, which were not mild at all.
She thought of the old man on the ninth step, with his leg out stiff across the stone, and his mouth gone down at the left corner. I'm waiting on the young master. He's gone down for a ladder to get me up.
She thought of Mister Gage lifting him. And Mister Gage's hand, on the white wild hair, for a moment, at the bottom of the stair.
She thought of the dark wet drops on the ninth step, where he had sat.
"I really couldn't say, Mister Gant," she said. "I don't know your lamp boys. I only know your books."
She stood up.
"And your books are very good," she said. "Except for a hundred and seventy-three crowns. Good night."
Gant looked at her for a moment longer. Then he laughed. The small soft delighted laugh.
"Good night, Auditor," he said. "I'll walk you up."
"No need." Elara took her coat from the back of the chair. Gage came from the wall and held it for her, and she put her arms into it. "I know the way. Four hundred and twelve steps, and the stair. Mister Castellan can light us."
She did not look at Remy. She did not need to. She heard him come away from the wall.
Gant was still smiling.
"Of course," he said. "Remy, light the Auditor up. Carefully. Mind the ninth step." A pause. "It's slippery."
Nine Steps
Remy carried the lantern ahead of them, round the long curve of the passage, and did not speak.
Elara walked behind him and counted. Four hundred and twelve, back the other way. The yellow lamps, every twenty paces. The gray lamps, three of them, on the outer wall. She passed each gray one and looked at it as she passed, and saw its thin dead light lying on the stone, and thought of the young harvester from the Third Basement, years ago, standing on the marble of the Bank's hall with his hands smoking and a look of great surprise on his face.
Her book said she had laughed.
She did not think she would laugh now.
Mister Gage walked beside her, bent under the low ceiling. The clerks came behind. Mister Harrow with the green ledger in its satchel, held against his chest in both arms. Young Mister Penn with the scales. Nobody spoke. The hum came up through the floor, steady and deep. The floor breathed.
Past the bench by the lift.
There were two men sitting on it. She had not seen them on the way down. They had not been there. Now they were. Two huge men, side by side, on a long plank of black oak, in leather aprons, with their bare gray feet out on the stone and their great gray hands flat on their knees. Their heads were bowed. Their eyes were open, looking at the floor.
One of them was humming.
Very softly. A thin small tune, going up a little and down and round again. Under the hum of the house, like a thread under a rope.
Elara stopped.
She did not mean to. Her feet stopped. She stood in the passage in her dark green coat and looked at the two men on the bench, and at the one who hummed, and something in her went still. The way it had gone still at the window, and on the stair, and at the table with her hand on the black box.
Remy had stopped too. A little way ahead, with the lantern. He did not turn round.
"The porters," he said quietly. "The Hollow brothers. They carry for the house." A pause. "They don't hear us. They don't hear anything. You can walk past."
"What is he humming?"
Remy was silent for a moment.
"I don't know," he said. "A carrying song. From the docks. His brother used to sing it, I'm told, before. Now he hums it." Another pause. "He's never once stopped. Not in five years."
Elara looked at the humming man a moment longer. At the square jaw, and the short gray-brown hair, and the empty eyes on the floor.
Then she walked on. Past the bare gray feet. Close enough to have touched them.
She did not.
The kitchen stair came up on the inner wall, past the bench, past the lift. The small square landing at the foot of it, with the passage going off. Empty. Nobody sitting against the wall. Nothing on the floor but a little dust, and a smear on the stone by the wall where something had been dragged, or had dragged itself, and a small dark spot.
Remy went up the stair with the lantern.
Elara went after him, with her hand on the rope.
One. Two. Three. She counted. She always counted. Four. Five. Six. The hum fading a little, behind and below. Seven. Eight.
Nine.
The turn of the stair. The square stone pillar. Remy stopped on the tenth step above, with the lantern held up, and its light fell down onto the ninth.
And there they were.
Five of them. Small. Round. Dark. On the worn gray stone, in the hollow of the step, where an old man had sat with his leg out stiff across it. Not wet now. Nearly dry. Dark red at the edges, going brown. Black in the middle.
She had stepped over them on the way down. She had not looked.
She looked now.
Behind her, on the eighth step, Mister Gage had stopped. She felt him stop. She felt him not move, the way he did not move by the door of the counting room, with his head bent and his hands hanging.
Above her, on the tenth step, Remy stood with the lantern and did not move either.
Mind the ninth step, Gant had said. It's slippery.
Elara pulled off her right glove.
She did it slowly. A finger at a time. The way she always did. And she bent down, on the ninth step of the kitchen stair, with her coat gathered in her left hand, and put out her bare forefinger, and touched the largest of the five dark spots.
It was very small. It was only blood.
It was enough.
The account opened under her fingertip like a door kicked in.
Loud. Not like the jar. A person. A whole person, all at once, the way a market is loud. Too much. Too fast. Thousands of things, crowding, shouting. And most of them gone. That was the first thing. Most of them gone. Holes. Holes everywhere, like a moth-eaten coat held up to the light. More holes than cloth. She had never felt an account with so many holes in it. A great dark house with every room emptied, and the doors standing open, and the wind going through.
And in the rooms that were left, a few things. Very few. Held very tight. Held the way you hold a coal in a cloth.
A book. Against the heart. A small black book, soft at the edges, written in a hand that shook.
A girl's face. In a doorway. With hacked short hair and a hand flat on his chest.
An old woman's bread.
A boy on a coal chute, climbing into lantern light.
And under all of it, at the very back of the house, at the end of the last corridor, past all the empty rooms, a door. Shut. Locked. With a seal on it.
She knew the seal. She had seen it once before. In the autumn. On an account she had opened in the Long Room under the dome, at the Bank, with the rain on the round window. An account so old and so locked that she had had to sit for a quarter of a bell with her hand on the white leather before it would open even a little. And on the inside of the cover, where the Bank's seal should have been, a different one. Small. Gold. A dome, with lines going out from it, like a sunrise.
The Archon's private seal.
On the door at the back of the house.
And on the door, cut into the wood under the seal, a name. Not the name of the account. Another name. A name that someone had carved there long ago, with something small and sharp, a nail perhaps, the way children carve their names on things they do not want to forget. Low down. At the height of a child of six.
ELARA.
She took her finger off the stone.
She did it so slowly that she did not know she had done it until it was done. She was kneeling on the ninth step of the kitchen stair with her bare hand held against her chest, and her coat crumpled in her other fist, and the lantern light on her, and she was not breathing.
Nobody moved.
Above her, Remy held the lantern very still.
Behind her, Mister Gage did not make a sound.
She felt for the hole.
The way she always did, after. Feeling inside herself for the place where a day had been.
It was there. Another one. The second tonight. Smooth-edged. Gone. And like the first, it was warm at the edges. Warmer than the first. Warm like a hand that has just let go of yours.
She stayed there on her knees on the stone a moment longer.
Then she took her white glove, the one she had pulled off, and folded it in half, and pressed it down flat over the five dark spots on the ninth step. And wiped. Once. Twice. Hard. Until there was nothing on the worn gray stone at all but a damp smear that might have been anything. Melted snow, from a boot. Spilled water from a scullion's bucket.
She looked at the glove. At the stain on the white kid.
She put it in the pocket of her coat, inside, against the small black book. And stood up.
"Mister Castellan," she said. Her voice came out cool and clear and steady. She did not know how. "The light, please. It's very dark on this stair."
Remy did not answer at once.
Then he lifted the lantern a little higher, and turned, and went on up the stair ahead of her. And she followed. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. To the top, to the door, to the noise and the heat and the copper pans.
At the top of the stair, in the doorway, with the kitchen roaring behind him, Remy stopped and stood aside to let her pass.
She passed.
And as she did, without turning his head, very low, under the roar of the ranges, he said:
"He walked it. The glass. Twice. It knows him now."
Elara did not stop walking.
She did not ask who. She did not ask what glass. She went on down the length of the great white kitchen, under the copper pans, past the long table and the onions and the birds, with Mister Gage behind her and the clerks behind him. And at the far end, by the last range, the huge woman in the white apron was standing with her hands on her hips. Watching her come. With her small blue eyes.
She did not look away this time.
She looked at Elara's face. And at the right hand, bare, held against the green coat. And at the pocket.
And as Elara came past her, the cook said, low and rough, not to anyone:
"He took the back way. Out through the coal store, with the young master. A quarter bell since." She turned and opened the black iron door of the warming oven, and looked into it. "In case anybody was asking."
Elara went out into the snow.
