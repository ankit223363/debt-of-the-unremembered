Chapter 43: The Auditor at the Bourse, Part 1 of 5: The Orange-Peel Tin
The Orange-Peel Tin
The tin had held candied orange peel once.
Elara did not know who had given it to her. Her book did not say. It was a small round tin, the size of the palm of her hand, painted dark blue, with a picture on the lid of an orange tree in a white pot, and the words SWEETS OF THE SOUTH in curly gold letters round the edge. The paint was worn through to the metal at the rim, where a thumb had opened it a great many times. Not her thumb. The wear was older than that. Older than the Bank. The kind of wear a tin gets in a child's pocket.
She had found it in the drawer of her glass desk on her first day at the Fourth Desk, six years ago, empty except for a faint smell. Her book said that. First day. Found a blue tin in the desk. Smells of oranges. Kept it. She did not remember finding it. She did not remember the first day. She had lost it, a long time ago, on a Thursday, weighing a widow's debt.
But she had kept the tin.
She kept her small things in it. A spare button for her coat. A stub of green sealing wax. A silver pin with a pearl on it that her book said she had never worn. And since Thursday, two small squares of paper. Folded very small, very hard, very tight. The size of a fingernail.
Barley-sugar wrappers.
She had them out on the glass of her desk now, at the eighth bell of the morning, in the hard white light from the three tall windows, side by side. And she was looking at them.
She had looked at them every morning since Thursday. Since she had picked them up off the windowsill of a small room at the top of No. 14 Tallow Street, where her brother had lived for six years, and put them in her pocket because they were evidence. Because somebody neat had left them there for her to find.
She knew now who.
Not from her book. From the Bank. From the Long Room under the dome, and the ledgers there. And from the Inquisition's own reports, which came across her desk every week, sealed, to be weighed for the Bank's interest. She had read them all again, since Thursday, every one for a year, looking. And she had found it in the margin of a report about a raid on a tavern called the Bell, in the autumn. A note, in a neat small hand, very fine, in a corner. Not part of the report. As if someone had been thinking while he read, and his pen had gone on moving.
A small drawing. A square, folded corner to corner. And beside it, a word. Small.
The report was signed S. Kross, Inquisitor.
She had sat at her glass desk for a long time after she found that, with the report in her gloved hands.
Now she picked up one of the wrappers.
She had unfolded it last night. That was new. She had not dared before. She had sat at the desk after the Bank closed, with the green-shaded lamp lit and the lilies in their vase, and taken off her gloves, and picked at the corner of the hard little square with her fingernail. And it had opened. Slowly. Fold by fold. She had counted the folds as it opened. Eleven. And then it lay flat on the glass, a small square of waxed paper, crumpled and creased, with a faint sweet smell still in it, of burnt sugar and lemon. Nothing written on it. Nothing at all.
And she had spent the rest of the evening learning to fold it again.
She had not been able to. Not the way he did it. She had folded it in half, and in half again, and corner to corner, and in and in, eleven times. And it had come out larger than his. Softer. Looser. It sat on the glass beside the other one, the one she had not touched, like a child's drawing beside a master's.
She had tried again. And again. A dozen times, until the paper was soft as cloth, and the lamp had burned down, and the night porter had stopped outside her door twice and gone away. And each time it came out a little smaller. A little harder. A little more like his.
Not the same.
She looked at the two of them on the glass now. His, and hers. Side by side.
Why? she thought. Why learn it? What use is it?
She did not know. She only knew that a man who folded his barley-sugar wrappers eleven times, into squares the size of a fingernail, and left them on a harvester's windowsill for an Auditor to find, was a man who wanted to be understood. In a particular way. On his own terms. And that if she was ever going to understand him, before he understood her, she had better learn to think the way his fingers thought.
She put both wrappers back in the blue tin. Hers on top. She closed the lid on the orange tree in its white pot. She put the tin in the drawer.
And the drums began.
She heard them through the glass. Far off, at first, across the river, from the north bank. A single roll, long and hard, like hail on a roof. And then silence. And then many. Rolling together. Coming nearer.
She got up and went to the window.
She did not usually stand at the windows. An Auditor of the Fourth Desk does not look out. The world comes to her. But she stood at the middle one of the three, in her dark green coat, with her gloved hands together at her waist, and looked down at Lantern Quay.
It was full of people. More than she had ever seen on it. A dark crowd along the edge of the river and along both walls of the bridge, between the blue lamps, very still, in the snow. And down the middle of the bridge, a lane of white. Empty.
And into the far end of the lane, out of Quill Row, came the battalion.
She watched it come. Black, all black, on the white. Drums at the front. Then a single rider on a gray horse. Then the lines. She had read about the Enforcer battalion. Its accounts came across her desk too. Its stones. She had weighed the stones they bought. Other men's words. Other men's days.
She watched the man on the gray horse come over the bridge.
And as he came off the last arch, onto the quay, he lifted his eyes.
To her window.
Elara did not move. She was sure of that. She had stood quite still, with her hands together at her waist. But something in her had moved. Somewhere deep down. The way the floor of a house moves when a heavy cart goes by in the street outside. She did not know the man. She had never seen him before in her life. Her book would have said.
But she knew the way he looked up.
Not at her. She did not think he could see her, behind the glass, against the white sky. At the window. At the place where she was. The way you look up at a window where somebody used to be, a long time ago, when you are passing a house you once lived in.
Then his eyes came down. And he rode on. And she stood at the window with her heart going quick and light, and did not know why.
And then the gray horse stopped.
A big old man in a shabby coat had stepped out into the lane, in front of it. She watched the rider look down at him. She watched the drums stop, and the battalion stop, and the whole of Lantern Quay go still. She watched the rider get down off his horse, quick as a cat, and put his black-gloved hand flat on top of the old man's hand on the parapet of the bridge.
She watched for a long time. She could not hear anything. Only see.
And afterwards, when the battalion had gone and the crowd had gone and the carriage at the foot of her own white steps had gone too, the old man was still there. Standing by the first lamppost. Looking up.
At her window.
She did not know why she put her hand on the glass. She only knew that she did. Flat. The palm against the cold. And that under her glove, her fingertips had gone colder than the glass, the way they did when she touched an account that was not closed.
Then she went back to her desk, and sat down, and opened her ledger, and turned to the page for tonight.
Tuesday. The Midwinter Audit. The Memory Bourse, Cinder Lane. Eighth bell. Mister Gage. Mister Harrow. Young Mister Penn.
And under it, in the same neat hand, because the Fourth Desk did not forget its duties even when the Auditor forgot her days:
Tier Four lots to be weighed by the Desk in person. Lots 39, 40, 41.
The Back Way
The cab came for her at the half past seven.
It was the Bank's own cab, black, with the Bank's seal small and plain on the door in white: a closed book with a key across it. Two gray horses. A driver in a white coat who did not speak and did not look round. It stood at the foot of the white steps in the snow, with its lamps lit, and Elara came down the steps to it under the words FORGET YOUR TROUBLES, with Mister Gage behind her holding the black umbrella over her head, though it was not snowing now.
The clerks were already inside.
Mister Harrow had the seat facing backwards, with the green ledger on his knees and his arms round it. He was a thin, anxious, middle-aged man with a long neck and a high collar and a habit of clearing his throat before he spoke, as if he were asking his throat's permission. He had been a clerk of the Fourth Desk for nineteen years. He had come to every Midwinter audit of the Bourse for nineteen years. He had never once, his book said, enjoyed it.
Beside him was young Mister Penn. Very young. Seventeen. In his first winter at the Bank. With fair hair that would not lie flat and a face so pink with cold and nerves that it shone. He had the ink case on his knees, and the seals, and the scales in their box. He held them as if they might go off.
Elara got in. Gage did not. He never rode inside. He climbed up onto the box beside the driver, and the whole cab tipped toward him as he did it, and the gray horses shifted their feet. Then the cab rolled.
She sat with her back to the horses and her gloved hands in her lap, and looked out of the window at the snow.
They went along Lantern Quay, where the battalion had gone in the morning. The lane down the middle was gray now, with slush and footprints. Past the bridge, with its blue lamps. Up the hill. Through streets she knew by name from the ledgers and had never walked. Mint Street. Saltpetre Row. The Shambles, with its butchers' hooks empty and white with frost. And down again, toward the river. Toward a great dark round shape against the orange glow of the sky, like the back of a beetle.
The green dome of the Bourse.
"Madam," said Mister Harrow, and cleared his throat. "Madam, the front entrance is in Bourse Square. On the south side. With the steps and the portico." He cleared his throat again. "The driver appears to be going round to the back."
"Yes," said Elara. "I asked him to."
Mister Harrow looked at her.
"Mister Gant sent a note," said Elara. "This afternoon. He asked if the Desk would do him the kindness of coming in by the kitchen gate. He said the front is being made ready for Midwinter, and there are workmen on the steps, and the portico is full of ladders." She did not look away from the window. "He said the kitchen is the only honest room in the house."
"Is it, madam?"
"I doubt it," said Elara. "But I wanted to see why he thought I should think so."
Mister Harrow cleared his throat, and did not say anything else.
Young Mister Penn looked from one of them to the other, with his pink face, and held the scales tighter.
The cab went round the side of the great building, along a narrow street with high blank walls on both sides, and stopped under an arched gate with a lamp burning over it. Elara looked out at the gate. At the lamp. At the yard beyond, small and square and walled, full of barrels and crates and black humps under canvas, with snow on everything.
On the wall of the yard, by the kitchen door, there was a small brass house on a bracket. With glass on four sides.
It was dark.
She looked at it a moment longer than she needed to. She knew what it was. The Bank had three of them, in the hall, by the intake doors. Ash lamps. They did not light the room. They lit people. A harvester walking past one would smoke like a damp fire. She had seen it once, years ago. A young harvester from the Third Basement, who had come up to the hall on an errand and walked past the lamp without thinking, and stood there on the marble with his hands smoking and a look of great surprise on his face.
Her book said she had laughed.
She did not remember laughing.
The kitchen door opened, and a man came out into the yard.
He was a big man. Soft and heavy, in a black coat very well brushed, with a white stock at his throat and a gold watch-chain across his waistcoat. A round mild face, pink and smooth as a baby's. Small gold spectacles. Thin fair hair combed carefully over the top of his head. He came across the snow toward the cab with his hands held out a little from his sides, palms up, the way a man comes toward a guest he is very glad to see.
"Auditor," he said. "How very good of you."
His voice was soft and mild and carried a long way.
Gage got down off the box. The cab tipped back the other way. He came round and opened the door and held it, and Elara got out into the snow, and stood, and looked at the man in the gold spectacles.
"Mister Gant," she said.
"Madam." Gant bowed. Not low. Just low enough. "Every year I hope the Bank will send someone else, so that I can complain to you about them. And every year it sends you. I'm beginning to think you like us."
"The Bank sends the Fourth Desk," said Elara. "I am the Fourth Desk."
"So you are." He smiled. It was a pleasant smile. It went up into the round cheeks and made the eyes behind the spectacles crinkle. It did not go anywhere else. "Come in, come in. Out of the cold. Mrs. Pratt has a kettle on. And the books are waiting. All of them. Every one, since last Midwinter, in a row on the counting room table, like good children." He turned, and held out his arm toward the kitchen door. "This way. Mind the step. It's slippery."
Elara went.
The heat of the kitchen hit her like a wall. She stopped just inside the door and let it, and looked. She always looked. Her book said she always had.
It was a great white kitchen, high and long, full of noise and steam and the red glow of three black ranges. Copper pans on every wall, a hundred of them, shining. A long scrubbed table, covered in flour and onions and the bodies of birds. And people. Everywhere. In white, in brown, carrying things, shouting. None of them looked at her. They looked at their hands, or their pans, or the floor. The way people at the Bank looked at the floor when the Auditor of the Fourth Desk walked through the hall.
At the far end, by the last range, a huge woman in a white apron stood with her hands on her hips, watching the kitchen. Her small blue eyes went to Elara, and stopped. And went over her. The green coat. The white gloves. The face.
And stayed on the face for a long time.
Elara looked back at her.
The huge woman did not look away. Not the way the others did. She looked at Elara's face as if she were reading something written on it. And then something in her own big red face changed, very slightly. As if she had read it, and did not like what it said.
Then she turned her back, and opened a black iron oven door in the wall, and looked into it.
"Mrs. Pratt," said Gant pleasantly, beside her. "Our cook. Thirty years. She has a warming oven that has never once been cold. Not in thirty years. She's very proud of it." He held out his arm again. "The stair is at the end. Down, I'm afraid. And steep. I do apologize for the stair."
They went down the kitchen, along the wall, under the copper pans. Gant in front. Then Elara. Then Mister Harrow with the green ledger, and young Mister Penn with the scales, and Mister Gage last, bent nearly double under the hanging pans, so that the people in the kitchen moved out of his way without looking up, the way water moves round a rock.
At the end, a door. Gant opened it.
Cold came up out of it. Cold, and a smell of stone and old fat and onions. And under the smell, so faint that she felt it in her teeth before she heard it, a hum.
"This way, Auditor," said Gant, and stepped through onto the stair. "Mind the step. It's quicker than the front stair, and warmer. The counting room is along the passage at the bottom, round to the south." A small soft laugh. "I always think the Bank should see the kitchen. It's the only honest room in the house."
Elara did not answer him.
She turned her head, instead, to Mister Harrow, behind her in the doorway with his long neck and his arms round the ledger.
"Bring the green ledger first, please, Mister Harrow," she said. "And mind the ink."
And she stepped through the door after Gant, onto the stair, and began to go down.
