Chapter 43: The Auditor at the Bourse, Part 5 of 5: Not Yet
Not Yet
The cab was waiting under the arched gate, with its lamps lit and the snow coming down again, slow and soft, on the backs of the two gray horses.
Mister Harrow got in first, with the satchel. Then young Mister Penn, with the scales. Then Elara. Mister Gage did not climb up onto the box. He stood by the open door of the cab in the snow, with the black umbrella in his hand, not open, and looked at her.
She looked back at him.
"Mister Harrow," she said, without turning her head. "Mister Penn. Would you ride on the box tonight, please. Beside the driver. I'd like Mister Gage inside."
There was a silence in the cab.
Mister Harrow cleared his throat.
"On the box, madam? In the snow?"
"There's a rug."
Mister Harrow looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at young Mister Penn. Then he gathered up the satchel and got out of the cab, slowly, into the snow, and went round to the front, and climbed up. Young Mister Penn went after him, with the scales, very pink, without a word.
Gage got in.
The cab tipped toward him as he did it, the way it always did, and the springs groaned, and the horses shifted their feet. He sat down on the seat facing her, the seat Mister Harrow had sat on, and it was too small for him. His knees came up nearly to his chest. His head was bent under the roof. He took up the whole of his side of the cab, the way a bear would take up the whole of a cupboard.
He shut the door.
The cab rolled.
They went along the narrow street with the high blank walls, and round the side of the great dark building, and out into the white streets. Elara did not look out of the window. She looked at Mister Gage. He looked at his hands, folded on his knees. Huge hands. Plain. With the nails cut very short and square, like a child's.
She did not say anything until they had passed the Shambles, with its empty butchers' hooks white with frost.
Then she said, "You knew him."
Gage did not move.
"On the stair," said Elara. "The lamp boy. You lifted him. And at the bottom, you put your hand on his head." She kept her voice very low, under the noise of the wheels. "I have stood next to you every day for six years, Mister Gage. My book says I have. I have never seen you touch anyone."
Nothing.
The cab swayed. The lamps outside swung past the window, one after another, yellow on the snow.
"And you wrote on your slate," said Elara. "On Thursday. In the little room at the top of the house in Tallow Street. When I was on my knees by his bed. I know. And then I was there." She paused. "And then you wiped it and would not write any more."
Gage looked up from his hands.
His plain unfinished face, in the swinging light of the street lamps through the window. Gray eyes, small and deep-set under heavy brows. A nose like a lump of clay that had been pressed on with a thumb and left. A wide mouth that never moved, because it had nothing to say.
"Where were you, Mister Gage?" said Elara. "Where was there?"
Gage looked at her for a long time.
Then he took the slate out of the pocket of his coat. It was small in his hand, like a playing card. And the chalk. He held the slate on his knee, against the swaying of the cab, and wrote. Slowly. In his big round careful letters. It took him a long time.
He turned it round.
A wagon.
Elara looked at it.
She did not say anything. She could not. She looked at the two words on the slate in the swinging light, and felt her bare right hand, in her lap, go cold to the bone. Colder than the black box. Colder than the blood on the stair.
Gage wiped the slate with the side of his hand. Wrote again.
A blue one.
He wiped it. Wrote again. This one took longer.
I drove it.
The cab went on through the snow. Down the hill. Toward the river. The lamps went past the window, yellow, yellow, yellow.
"Who was in it?" said Elara.
Her own voice. She heard it come out of her mouth. Very small. Not the voice of the Fourth Desk at all.
Gage looked at her. And for the second time in one night, she saw his face change. Something moved under the clay. And this time it did not go away again at once. It stayed. It looked out at her through the small gray eyes for a long moment.
Then he wiped the slate. And wrote. And turned it round.
Children.
And wiped it. And wrote again.
A boy. 10. Tall. Who would not let go of his sister's hand.
And wiped it. And wrote again.
And his sister. 6. Two braids.
Elara did not move.
She sat in the swaying cab in her dark green coat with her bare hand cold in her lap and her white glove, with the blood on it, in her pocket against her heart, and looked at the slate.
Two braids.
She did not have braids. She had never had braids. Her book did not say so, but she knew it the way you know your own face. She wore her hair pinned up close at the back of her head, smooth and dark and tight, the way the Bank liked, the way all the women of the Bank wore it.
She lifted her bare hand from her lap.
And touched the back of her own head. The smooth dark hair, pinned up close. And felt, under her fingertips, under the pins, at the nape of her neck, two places where the hair grew a little differently. A little thicker. Two small ridges, side by side. The way hair grows when it has been plaited in the same place, every day, for years, when it is young.
She took her hand away.
"Mister Gage," she said. "Where did the wagon go?"
Gage wiped the slate.
He held the chalk over it for a long time. Long enough for the cab to go past three street lamps. Four. Five.
Then he wrote. Two words. And turned it round.
Not yet.
"Why not?"
He wiped the slate. He did not write anything else. He only lifted one huge plain finger and pointed. Not at her. Up. At the roof of the cab. At the sky above it, and the snow coming down, and somewhere beyond the snow, on the hill, above everything, a white dome.
She knew what that meant. He had written it once before, in the Long Room, at the top of the iron stair, with the rain on the round window.
He is listening.
Elara looked at the pointing finger for a long moment.
Then she nodded.
Gage put the slate away in his pocket, and folded his huge hands on his knees, and looked at them. And did not look up again until the cab stopped at the foot of the white steps of the Bank, under the words FORGET YOUR TROUBLES, and Mister Harrow climbed down off the box, white with snow, and opened the door.
The Book of Days
Her rooms were at the top of the Bank, under the roof, at the end of a long cold corridor with a strip of brown carpet down the middle and doors on both sides that were always shut. Three rooms. A sitting room with a desk and a hard chair and a small iron stove. A bedroom with a narrow bed and a washstand and a chest of drawers. And a cupboard with a sink in it, which the Bank called a kitchen, though nobody had ever cooked anything there.
The Bank gave these rooms to the clerks of the Fourth Desk, so that they would always be near their work. Elara had lived in them for six years. Her book said so. There was nothing on the walls. There was nothing on the chest of drawers but a hairbrush and a box of pins. There was nothing on the desk but the lamp, and the ink, and the pens, and the book.
She lit the lamp. She lit the stove, with the matches and the paper and the small dry sticks that the boy brought up every morning in a basket. She did not take off her coat until the stove began to tick and the room began, very slowly, to be less cold.
Then she took off her coat and hung it on the back of the door.
She took the white glove out of the pocket first.
She stood with it in her bare hand, by the door, in the lamplight, and looked at it. The fine white kid, soft and creased from her fingers. And on the first two fingers, the dark brown stain. Dry now. Gone stiff. The blood of a lame old lamp boy with a squint and a mole, on the ninth step of a back stair, at midnight.
Her brother's blood.
She did not know that. Not the way the Bank knew things. Not in ink, with a seal. But she had read it. She had touched it with her bare finger and read it, and it had been mostly holes, the way hers was mostly holes. And there had been a door with a dome on it. And a name carved on the wall at the height of a child's hand.
ELARA.
She went to the desk and sat down on the hard chair and put the glove down on the desk, beside the lamp, with the stained fingers turned up.
Then she took the tin out of the other pocket.
She had taken it out of the drawer of her glass desk that morning, before the cab came, and put it in her coat. She did not know why. She had carried it all day, through the four hundred and twelve steps and the counting room and the black box, and she had felt it there against her side, small and round and hard, the whole time. A small round tin, dark blue, with the paint worn through to the metal at the rim, and on the lid an orange tree in a white pot, and the words SWEETS OF THE SOUTH in curly gold letters. It smelled of oranges when she opened it. It always smelled of oranges. She did not know who had given it to her. Her book did not say.
Inside the tin were the two wrappers.
One was still folded, the way she had found it on the windowsill of the little room at the top of No. 14 Tallow Street. Small and tight and neat, no bigger than a fingernail, sharp at every corner. His. The other was hers. She had unfolded that one last night, very slowly, counting. Eleven folds. And then she had spent the rest of the night trying to fold it back, a dozen times, and failing, until the paper was soft as cloth. Tonight she smoothed it out flat again, a square of thin waxed paper with the creases still in it, like the lines on a palm, and a faint smell of burnt sugar and lemon.
She took the flat one out and laid it on the desk, in the lamplight, beside the glove.
Then she opened the book.
The book of days was a thick ledger with a black cloth cover and a red cloth spine, and the Bank gave one to every clerk of the Fourth Desk, and every clerk of the Fourth Desk wrote in it every night before they slept. What they had done that day. Whom they had seen. What they had weighed, and what it had cost them. So that in the morning, if they woke and did not know, they could open the book and find out.
Elara turned to the page with the ribbon in it.
She dipped her pen.
She wrote the date, in the top corner, in her small neat slanting hand.
Then she wrote, as she always wrote, in the plain cold words of the Fourth Desk:
Audit of the Lady Vivienne's house, the counting room. With Harrow, Penn, Gage. Four hundred and twelve steps from the kitchen door to the counting room and back. Fane's books: nineteen errors, totalling one hundred and seventy-three crowns, all small, all to the house's advantage. St Bride's lines, black book seven, page ninety-four: source to be noted.
She stopped. She looked at that line for a long time. Source to be noted. She had put her glove over a thumbprint in coal dust, to hide it. Her book should say so. Her book should say everything. That was what the book was for.
She did not write it.
She went on.
Lot 41, brought by the boy Remy. A black box, iron, with a clasp. Maker's mark: a square, folded. Weighed by Ledger-Sight. Cost: one day. Clause eleven invoked. Sealed: WEIGHED AND HELD.
Lots 39, 40, weighed by scale. No cost.
The Lady will send a mask for the Masquerade, on Midwinter. I chose plain white.
She stopped again.
The stove ticked. The snow went on falling past the small square window, white on black, slow.
She dipped the pen, and held it over the page, and did not write. A drop of ink grew on the nib, and grew, and fell, and made a round black spot on the clean paper, just below the line about the mask.
She looked at the spot.
Then she wrote, under it, in the same small neat hand, but slower:
Blood on the ninth step of the back stair. Read it by Ledger-Sight. Cost: one day.
That is two days lost tonight. I do not know which ones. The edges of both holes are warm.
She put down the pen.
She sat back in the hard chair and put her bare hand flat on the open book, on the wet ink, so that it would smudge, and did not care. She closed her eyes.
Two days.
She tried to find them. The way you feel with your tongue for a tooth that has been pulled. She went back, carefully, day by day, through what she could remember. The Long Room. The rain on the round window. Mister Gage's slate. The room at the top of the house in Tallow Street, and the young man in the bed with his face gray and his eyes shut. Two barley-sugar wrappers on a windowsill. A report with a small drawing in the margin, and the word Small.
There were holes. There were always holes. Her life was a coat that had been eaten by moths for six years, and she had learned to wear it so the holes did not show. But two of the holes were new. She could feel them, the way you feel a draft from a window you did not know was open. Two of them. Warm at the edges. Like a bed somebody has only just got out of.
She could not find what had been in them.
She opened her eyes.
She took her hand off the book and looked at the smudge. Then she picked up the pen again, and dipped it, and held it over the page for a long time.
The Fourth Desk did not write feelings in the book of days. It was not forbidden. It was simply not done. A clerk who wrote feelings in the book was a clerk who might not, in the morning, want to read them. And the Bank needed its clerks to read their books in the morning, every morning, and get up, and go to work.
Elara wrote:
I passed my brother on a stair tonight and did not stop him.
She looked at it.
The ink shone wet in the lamplight. Then it went dull, as it dried.
She wrote, under it:
He was in a disguise. An old lamp boy, lame, with a squint. Gage lifted him up the steps. I told him to mind his leg. I called him grandfather.
I do not know that he is my brother. I know it the way I know I did not have braids. Which is to say I know it wrongly, and I know it anyway.
Gage says: a blue wagon. Children. A boy, ten, tall, who would not let go of his sister's hand. A sister, six, two braids. Gage says he drove it. Gage says: not yet.
The back of my head has two ridges in the hair.
She stopped there. She could not think what else to write. She read it over, all of it, from the date at the top to the last line about her hair. It did not look like a page from the book of days. It looked like a page from somebody else's book. A frightened person's book. A person who did not work for the Bank.
She thought about tearing it out.
She did not. You could not tear pages out of the book of days. The pages were numbered, and the Bank checked the numbers every quarter, and a missing page was a mark against you, and three marks was a hearing. She knew that. She had sat at hearings. She had weighed the clerks who stood at them.
She left it.
She turned down the ribbon over the page, and closed the book, and pushed it away from her across the desk.
Then she pulled the flat orange-peel wrapper toward her, into the light.
She had tried a dozen times last night. She did not know why. She did not have a reason she could write in a book. She only knew that a man called Silas Kross, whom she had never met, whose small neat drawing she had found in the margin of an Inquisition report, had folded this little paper and left it on her brother's windowsill for her to find. And that when she had unfolded it, eleven folds, she had felt something in her chest go tight, the way it went tight when the black box spoke to her through the clasp. And that she wanted, more than she had wanted anything she could remember, to fold it back.
Not to fold it any way. To fold it his way.
She smoothed it flat with her bare fingers.
She looked at the creases. They were the map. Every fold he had made had left a line in the waxed paper, and the lines crossed each other, and if you knew which line came first, and which came after, you could follow them back. Like footprints in snow. Like a walk you take in the dark, feeling for each step.
The first fold was easy. Corner to corner. Anyone would start there.
The second was easy too. The third, she had got wrong all of last night, again and again, before she saw now, in the light through the paper, that it went under, not over.
She made the first fold now. Corner to corner. Pressed it flat with her thumbnail.
The second.
The third. Under.
The fourth was where she always stopped. The fourth fold was a strange one, a small tuck, turned back on itself, that did not seem to do anything. It made the paper no smaller. It made it no neater. It was only a little pleat, in the middle of the thing, and she did not understand what it was for.
She held the half-folded wrapper up to the lamp.
The light came through the thin waxed paper, yellow, and showed her the creases, and she turned it slowly, this way and that.
Four.
She thought of the black box. Of the long white hands she had felt through the clasp, folding something, in a study, in the lamplight. Folding to a beat. Four. Two. Nine. Three. And her own finger, on the desk of the counting room, tapping once, without her telling it to.
She looked at the creases on the paper.
She counted them again. Not all of them. Just the ones that went one way. The ones that went over.
Four.
She turned the paper. Counted the ones that went under.
Two.
She sat very still.
Then she made the fourth fold. The strange little tuck. Turned it back on itself. And the fifth. And the sixth — over, over, under. She did not have to think now. Her fingers knew. Her fingers had always known, maybe. The way her finger had known to tap.
Seven. Eight.
Nine.
She stopped at nine. Her hands were shaking.
The wrapper was nearly as small as his. Nearly. There were two folds left. Two more creases on the map. And if she made them, it would be the same as the one in the tin. Small and tight and neat, no bigger than a fingernail, sharp at every corner.
And if you counted the folds, in their groups, the way the paper made you group them — the ones before the tuck, and the ones in the tuck, and the ones after —
Four. Two. Nine.
She did not make the last two.
She put the little half-folded paper down on the desk very carefully, beside the white glove with her brother's blood on it, and looked at the two of them, side by side, in the lamplight.
Three. The last group would be three. She knew it without folding. Home.
He had folded a drum call into a sweet wrapper. A man who ate barley sugar and hummed in towers and wrote the word Small in the margins of his reports. He had folded a drum call into a piece of waxed paper, twice, and left both on a windowsill in Tallow Street. And she had picked them up, because they were evidence, and put them in a blue tin that smelled of oranges, and carried them for five days without knowing what she was carrying.
She did not know what it meant.
She did not know if it was a message, or a joke, or a test. Or a habit, the way some men tap their fingers on a table when they are thinking. She did not know if he had meant her to find it, or if he folded every wrapper that way, every sweet, every day, and dropped them wherever he went, and never thought of them again.
She only knew that the Lady's black box had the same mark on its clasp. A square, folded.
And that she had tapped once.
Elara sat at her desk under the roof of the Bank, with the stove ticking and the snow falling past the window, for a long time.
Then she opened the book of days again, to the page with the ribbon, and dipped her pen, and wrote one more line at the bottom, under the line about her hair. Small. Very small. So small she would have to hold the book close to the lamp in the morning to read it.
He folds them four, two, nine. I think the last is three. I think I knew that once.
She closed the book.
She put the half-folded wrapper back in the blue tin, beside the one that was still whole, and shut the lid on the orange tree in its white pot, and the smell of oranges came out of it as the lid went down, faint and sweet and very old. Older than the Bank. She did not put it in a drawer. She put it on the desk, beside the lamp, next to the glove.
The tin, and the glove. Side by side.
She looked at them for a while. A blue tin that a child had carried in a pocket, a long time ago. A white glove with a lame old man's blood on it. And she thought, without knowing where the thought came from, that if somebody had asked her to weigh the two of them on the Bank's scales, she would not have been able to say which was heavier.
She left them both there, where she would see them when she woke.
Then she turned down the lamp, and went to bed, and lay in the dark under the roof of the Bank with her eyes open, listening to the snow, and to the faint far hum of the great machines in the vaults below, which never stopped, and which she had heard every night for six years, and which she had never once, until tonight, heard as a sound like breathing.
She touched the back of her head. The two small ridges in the hair.
She did not sleep for a long time.
And when she did, she dreamed of a valley at night, and a drum, and a tall boy holding her hand so hard it hurt, who said, Don't let go. Whatever happens. Don't let go.
And in the dream she did not let go.
And in the morning, when she woke, and opened the book, and read the page with the ribbon in it, she could not remember the dream at all.
