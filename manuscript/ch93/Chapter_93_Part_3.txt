Chapter 93: Blue Hands, Part 3 of 5: Through the City
Through the City
The quickest back way to the Dye-Works was not quick at all. It was only the least seen.
Juno took them down Ropewalk Lane, which ran steep and crooked between high blank warehouse walls, so narrow at the top that the roofs nearly touched and the snow lay on the cobbles in a thin gray line down the middle where the sky came through. At the bottom it came out under the arches of the old Corn Bridge. They went along under the bridge in the dark, with the river slapping at the stones on their left and the arches going over their heads one after another like the ribs of some great animal, and their feet echoing. There were people sleeping under the arches. Bundles of rags in the corners, against the walls, with a little fire of driftwood here and there, smoking. One of the bundles sat up as they went past and looked at them with a gray face and lay down again.
Vespera stopped once, under the third arch, and stood looking at one of the bundles for a long time. It was an old man. He was asleep with his mouth open and his hands crossed on his chest, and on the backs of his hands, very faded, so faded you could hardly see them, there were letters. Blue letters. Inked into the skin.
She did not wake him. She stood and looked. Then she went on.
On the far side of the Corn Bridge, the river path went along under the quays. It was a narrow ledge of stone at the foot of the quay wall, just above the water, where the bargemen walked when they were hauling, and it was slippery with ice and green weed, and there was no rail. Brin went along it as if it were a road. Vespera went along it with one blue mitten flat on the wall. Juno went along it the way she went along everything, without thinking about it, because she had been doing it since she was six.
Above them, over the edge of the quay, the city was waking up.
She could hear it. Carts on the cobbles. Somebody shouting about fish. A dog. The bells of the churches beginning, one after another, for the first bell of the day, a long way off and then nearer, out of time with each other, like a lot of people all trying to say the same thing at once. And the Ash lamps along the quay, over their heads, humming.
They always hummed. Juno had stopped hearing it years ago. But she heard it this morning, because Vespera stopped on the ledge and looked up at them and said:
"Listen to that."
Juno listened.
"That's new," said Vespera. "That sound. When I left this city, half of these were still gas. Yellow, and a man came round with a pole at dusk and lit them one by one." She looked up at the blue light coming down over the edge of the quay wall. "Twenty years. And now every one of them is burning somebody."
"Burning somebody?"
Vespera looked at her.
"What do you think's in them?" she said. "What do you think makes them blue?"
Juno did not answer. She had never thought about it. The Ash lamps were the Ash lamps. They had always been there.
"Ash," said Vespera. "Somebody's. A morning. A birthday. The smell of their mother's kitchen. Bought by the Bank for a few pennies and sold to the city for more and burned on a pole to light the quay so the gray coats can see who's out after dark." She turned back to the wall. "That hum's the sound it makes going. Come on."
They went on.
The river path ended at the Fish Stairs. Juno knew the Fish Stairs. Wide old steps, hollowed in the middle by more feet than anybody could count, going up from the water to the old fish market, with a rusty iron rail down one side. The fish market was busy already. She could smell it from the bottom. She could hear the fishwives shouting to each other, high and harsh, like gulls.
They went up the stairs among the fishwives.
That was the best way. Juno had learned it a few weeks ago, following the black cart. If you wanted not to be seen, you did not hide. You went where there were a great many people, all busy, all shouting, and you were one more. Nobody looked at an old woman in a man's cap among the fishwives. Nobody looked at a girl in a boy's coat. They might have looked at Brin. But Brin bent his head and hunched his shoulders and carried his sack, and in the crowd at the top of the Fish Stairs, among the baskets and the barrows and the porters with their great flat trays of fish on their heads, he was only one more big man carrying something.
At the top of the Fish Stairs there was a wall. A warehouse wall, long and blank and soot-black. And on the wall, there were papers.
Juno had seen them before. Everyone had. They had been going up all week, on every wall and post and door in the lower town. Big sheets, gray, printed in black. BY ORDER OF THE COUNCIL OF VERIDIA. And under that, A CLEANSING. And under that, a box, with a black line round it.
SOUGHT.
And a face.
There were a great many of them, along the wall. Twenty, perhaps, in a row, all the same, as if somebody had walked along with a bucket of paste and a brush and slapped them up one after another without stopping. Twenty faces. Twenty times the same young man with dark hair and a long straight nose, looking out at the fish market.
Vespera stopped and looked at them.
Juno looked too. She had looked before, every time she passed. She could not help it.
"They've drawn him too fat," said Vespera.
"That's what everybody says."
"They've drawn him the way he looked before he went north." Vespera's voice was flat. "No gray. Two good eyes. The way somebody remembers him from a Ministry card." She looked at the face for a long moment. "He doesn't look like that anymore. Nobody will know him from that."
"That's good, isn't it?"
"That's very good," said Vespera. "That's the only good thing about it."
Somebody had written on one of the papers. Juno had seen it before, too, last week. Somebody with a stick of charcoal had drawn a ring round the face on the last paper in the row, a ring with lines going out from it, like the sun, like the halo round a saint in a window. And under it, in big rough letters, one word.
ROOF.
Vespera saw it. She looked at it for a long time.
Then she reached out and touched it. With one blue mitten. Very lightly. On the word.
She did not say anything. She turned and went on.
They went along the back of the fish market, behind the stalls, where the fish heads and the guts went into barrels, and the gulls stood on the barrels in rows and screamed at each other. Through a gate, and across a yard. And out into Tanner Street, which went along the river for a long way, past the tanneries, which stank, and the ropewalks, which did not, and the boatyards. And there, at the end of Tanner Street, there was a bridge.
Not a big one. The Cut Bridge, over the mouth of the Mill Cut, where the little canal came out into the river. A low stone hump, just wide enough for a cart.
There were gray coats on it.
Juno saw them before anybody else did. She stopped dead, and Vespera walked into the back of her.
Two of them. In the middle of the bridge, at the top of the hump. With a little white barrier across the road, waist-high, new paint, the same as the ones on Lantern Bridge. And a brazier beside it, glowing. And a table, with a book on it. One of the gray coats was sitting at the table, stamping his feet, with his hands round a tin cup. The other was standing at the barrier, looking at a man with a handcart. Looking in the handcart. Lifting things up out of it, one by one, and looking at them, and putting them down.
Books.
The man with the handcart was old, and thin, and stood with his hands at his sides and his eyes on the ground while the gray coat went through his books.
"Back," said Juno, very low. "Back. There's another way. Over the Cut by the lock gates. It's longer."
Vespera was looking at the bridge.
"That man's a bookbinder," she said. "Or he was. I know his face. Pyle. Oswin Pyle. From Paternoster Lane." She watched the gray coat lift a book out of the handcart and open it and turn the pages and shut it and throw it onto a heap by the brazier. "He's trying to get them out. His books. Out of the shop before they take it. And they've caught him on the bridge."
Juno pulled her sleeve.
"Please," she said. "Please. The lock gates."
Vespera did not move. She stood in the middle of Tanner Street, in her man's cap, with her blue hands in her big mittens, and watched Oswin Pyle's books go one by one onto the heap by the brazier.
The gray coat at the table stood up. He put his tin cup down. He picked something up from beside the brazier. A shovel. He went to the heap of books and pushed the shovel under the edge of it. And lifted. And turned. And tipped the books, a great shovelful of them, into the brazier.
They went up all at once. Orange. Bright. With a great roar and a rush of sparks into the gray morning. And the smell came down Tanner Street on the wind, the smell of burning paper and leather and glue, and something else under it, something sweet and terrible, like hair.
The old man on the bridge made a sound. Juno heard it, all the way down the street. Not a word. A sound.
Brin moved.
Just a little. A shift of his weight. His hand went up to the long thing wrapped in sacking on his shoulder.
And Vespera put out her blue mitten, without looking, and laid it on his arm.
"No," she said.
Brin stood very still.
"Not here," said Vespera. "Not for books. Not today." She was still watching the fire. Her face had no expression on it at all. "Today we save the one book that matters. And then we come back for the rest."
Brin looked down at her from under his hood for a long moment.
Then his hand came down from the sacking.
"The lock gates," said Vespera to Juno. "Show me."
Juno showed her.
They went back up Tanner Street, and along the Cut on the far side, behind the boatyards, through the snow, to where the lock gates stood across the canal, black and dripping, with a narrow plank walk along the top of them. And they went across the plank walk one at a time, holding the rail, with the black water churning in the lock below. Juno did not look back at the bridge. But all the way across, and all the way along the other side of the Cut, and all the way down the last lane to the river, she could smell the books burning.
The Last Arch
The Dye-Works arches ran along the river under the old Weavers' Road, which had been the main road into the city from the east a hundred years ago, before they built the new bridges. Nobody used it now. It went along over the arches on its great brick piers, high above the water, with grass and little trees growing out of its broken parapet and snow lying thick along the top. And under it, in the arches, the dyers had worked.
There were a great many arches. Juno had never counted them. A long row of dark mouths in the brick, going away along the river, each one as tall as two men and as wide as a cart, and each one closed off at the front with something. A wooden wall with a door in it. A grille of iron. A sheet of tin. Planks nailed across. People lived in some of them. People worked in others. A cooper, a rag-sorter, a man who mended nets. And Marjory Kell, in the third from the river end, who did the sailors.
They went past the third arch.
Its front had been a wall of boards, painted green, with a door in the middle and a little window, and somebody had painted an anchor on the door in white. The door was hanging off one hinge now. The window was smashed. The boards round the door had been broken in with something heavy, an axe or a crowbar, in a great ragged hole, and through the hole Juno could see inside. A table on its side. A stool. Things on the floor, scattered, trodden. Bottles broken. Ink spilled across the boards in long dark stains, blue and black and red, frozen now, like old blood.
And on the floor by the door, in the snow that had blown in, a small dark thing, trodden flat.
A lamp. A little tin lamp, the kind you hold in your hand, crushed out of its shape.
Vespera did not stop. She looked at it as she went past. Only that. Then she looked away and walked on, faster, and Juno had to hurry to keep up.
The arches went on. And as they went on, the brick began to change color.
It was red, at the river end, the ordinary dark red of old brick gone black with soot. But a little further along there was a faint blue in it, low down, near the ground. A stain. Like a tidemark on a beach. And further along the blue went higher, and darker, until by the last few arches the brick was blue all over from the ground up to the height of Juno's head. Deep blue, almost black, in great streaks and patches, soaked right into the brick so that the snow lying on the ledges looked blue too, in the shadow.
"Indigo," said Vespera, without looking round. "A hundred years of it. They used to boil it here. In the vats. The whole river ran blue on dyeing days, my mother said. Blue from here to the sea." She sniffed. "They moved the dyers out the year I was born. But the brick never forgot."
And there were the vats.
Juno had heard of them. She had never been this far along. There were three of them, in a row, at the end of the arches, on a wide stone platform at the edge of the river. Great round tubs of stone, each one as wide as a room, as high as Juno's chest, with thick stone walls stained blue-black inside and out. They were full of snow. Snow, and old ice, and under the ice, Juno could see when she leaned over the edge of the nearest one, water. Black water. Very still. With a deep blue color in it, far down, like the color at the bottom of the sea.
And beyond the vats, the last arch.
It was the smallest. Lower than the others, and narrower, tucked in at the very end where the Weavers' Road met the wall of the next warehouse. The brick round it was so blue it was nearly black. And across its mouth, there was an iron grille. An old one, heavy, with thick square bars set close together, and a gate in the middle of it, and on the gate a padlock as big as Juno's two fists.
The padlock was red with rust. Solid with it. It had run down the bars of the gate in long orange streaks and frozen there.
Behind the grille there was a wooden wall, gray, very old, with no window in it. Just boards. And a door, behind the gate, gray too, and shut.
Nobody had been in there for a long time. You could see that. The snow lay in front of the grille smooth and untrodden. Except where the gray coats had come along on Saturday and banged on it, and then gone away. Their boot marks were there, a muddle of them, frozen hard, all round the gate. And a long scrape down one of the bars where somebody had tried to break the padlock with something, and failed.
Vespera stood in front of the grille and looked at it.
She did not touch the padlock. She did not seem to have any key. She stood there for a long moment with her blue mittens at her sides and her face very still. Juno could not tell what she was thinking. She looked, Juno thought, like Juno's mother had looked the first time she went back into the room where Juno's father had died. Not sad. Not anything. Only very careful. As if the room might break.
"Twenty years," said Vespera quietly. To nobody.
Then she turned away from the grille.
She went to the nearest vat. The one closest to the arch, right up against the blue wall. She walked round it, slowly, along the edge of the platform, with one mitten trailing on the stone rim. And at the back of the vat, the side nearest the wall, where nobody walking along the arches would ever see, she stopped. And knelt down in the snow.
Juno went round after her.
At the back of the vat, low down, where its round stone side met the platform, there was a stone. A square one. Darker than the others. Set into the side of the vat like a little door.
Vespera took off her mittens. Both of them. She gave them to Juno to hold. And then she put her two bare blue hands flat on the square stone, one on each side, and pushed. Hard. And then sideways.
The stone moved.
It moved with a long grinding sound, stone on stone, and a little shower of frost and old dirt. It went in, and then it went sideways, and behind it there was a hole. Dark. Square. Just big enough for a person to crawl into, if they were small.
Cold air came out of it. Very cold. And a smell.
Juno breathed it in without meaning to, and stood still.
It smelled of blue.
She could not have said it any other way. She did not know how a smell could be a color. But it was. A deep, old, dusty, sharp smell, like nothing she had ever smelled before. Like the inside of a very old box. Like a winter morning. Like the bottom of the sea. And under it, faint, something else. Something like flowers that have been dried a long time, between the pages of a book.
"The dyers' drain," said Vespera. "Under the vats. It goes back under the wall. Into the arch. They used it to let the old dye out, when it was spent, so it ran back under the floor and into the river." She was looking into the dark hole. "Nobody knows it's there. Nobody living. I made sure."
Brin had come round behind them. He looked at the hole, and then down at himself, at his great wide shoulders, and said something short in his own tongue.
"No," said Vespera. "You won't. You'll stay here. And watch. If anybody comes along the arches, anybody at all, you whistle. The way we did on the Glass." She looked up at him. "And if anybody comes along the top, on the road, you whistle twice."
Brin nodded slowly. He put down the sack. He took the long thing wrapped in sacking off his shoulder and held it in both hands, upright, with the end of it resting on the stone, and stood there, against the blue wall, between the vat and the arch. Like a post.
Vespera looked at Juno.
"You first," she said. "You're smaller. It goes straight for about the length of a cart, and then it turns up. You'll feel the turn. Push up when you get there. There's a board. It'll lift."
Juno looked at the dark hole.
She had walked fourteen miles on frozen feet. She had followed a black cart across Lantern Bridge in front of four gray coats. She had sat in the dark with a tallow stub in a tin lid and read a page of words she did not know, one letter at a time, all night.
She had never crawled into a hole in the side of a stone vat under a river.
"Is it wet?" she said.
"It's dry. It's been dry for twenty years. There's nothing in it but dust and the dark." Vespera's sharp face did something at one corner. "And spiders. But they're asleep. It's winter."
Juno got down on her hands and knees in the snow.
She took a deep breath of the cold blue air.
And went in.
