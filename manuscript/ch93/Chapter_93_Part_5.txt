Chapter 93: Blue Hands, Part 5 of 5: The Girl on the Road
The Girl on the Road
They heard the whistle a little after noon.
They had been going to leave. The box was packed, and the needles rolled, and the drawings tied. Vespera had pushed the box into the mouth of the drain, ready to slide it down to Brin. Juno was on her knees by the hole with the bundle of drawings in her arms, ready to go after it.
And from outside, through the boards, through the iron grille, through the snow and the cold, there came a whistle.
Low. Short. Two notes. And then, after a moment, the same two again.
Vespera went still.
She put up one blue hand, flat, without looking round. Juno froze where she was, on her knees, with the drawings. She held her breath. She did not know she was holding it until her chest began to hurt.
Twice, Vespera had said. If anybody comes along the top, on the road, you whistle twice.
The road. The Weavers' Road. Right over their heads, on top of the arch, on the great brick piers, with grass and little trees growing out of the broken parapet.
Somebody was up there.
Vespera moved. Slowly. Without a sound. She went across the dusty floor to the front of the arch, to the gray wooden wall behind the iron grille, and put her eye to a crack between two of the boards. Juno saw her stand there, very straight, very still, looking out.
Then Vespera turned her head and looked at Juno. And beckoned. One finger.
Juno put the drawings down on the floor, very carefully, so the paper would not crackle. She got up. She went across the boards on her toes, the way she went past Mrs. Doyle's room at night when she was late, and stood beside Vespera, and found a crack of her own, lower down, and put her eye to it.
She could see out.
Not much. A thin slice of the world, between the boards and through the bars of the grille. The snow in front of the arch, with the gray coats' frozen boot marks in it. The edge of the nearest vat, stone, blue-black. The river beyond it, black and moving. And if she turned her head, and pressed her cheek against the cold wood, and looked up, she could see a little way up the outside of the arch. The blue brick going up. And above it, the parapet of the Weavers' Road. Broken. With snow on it. And a little bare tree growing out of a crack, bent over by the wind.
And beside the tree, a person.
Standing on the old road, at the parapet, looking down.
Juno could only see a part of them. A gray coat. A long one, good cloth, buttoned to the throat. A gray hat. And a face, under the hat. Pale. Young. A girl's face.
The girl stood at the parapet with both hands on the broken stones and looked down at the arches. She looked along them, slowly, from one end to the other. She looked at the third arch, Marjory Kell's, with its smashed door. She looked at the vats. And then she looked at the last arch. At the iron grille. At the gray boards behind it.
At the crack.
Juno did not move. She did not breathe. She knew the girl could not see her. Not through a crack between two boards, behind a grille, in the dark, from up there. She knew it. And she did not move anyway, because the girl was looking straight at the place where she was.
The girl looked for a long time.
She had a white face. Very white. With dark marks under the eyes. She looked tired, Juno thought. More tired than anybody Juno had ever seen. Tired the way her mother looked after a night with no sleep when the rent was due. And she had her lip caught in her teeth, and she was not moving at all, and her hands on the stones were in gray gloves, and the gloves were clenched.
Then the girl looked down. Not at the arch. At the snow in front of it.
At the vat.
Juno felt her stomach go cold.
The snow by the vat. Where they had come round it in the morning, the three of them. Where Vespera had knelt down by the square stone. Where Brin was standing now, against the blue wall, between the vat and the arch. Very still. Like a post.
There would be marks. In the snow. New ones. Not frozen like the gray coats'. Soft. Clear. Three people's.
Juno could not see Brin from her crack. She could not see the back of the vat. She did not know if the girl could see it from the road. She did not know if Brin was hidden or not.
The girl stood at the parapet and looked down at the snow by the vat for a long, long time.
Then she did something strange.
She took one hand off the stones. And put it inside her coat. As if she were going to take something out. A book, or a whistle, or a paper. And then she stopped. With her hand inside her coat. And stood there.
And then she took her hand out again. Empty.
She looked once more along the arches. At the smashed door of the third one. At the trodden flat thing in the snow by it, that had been a lamp.
And then she turned away from the parapet, and walked back along the Weavers' Road, the way she had come, toward the city. Not fast. Not slow. With her head down and her hands in her pockets. And the little bare tree bent over in the wind behind her, and the snow began, very lightly, to fall again.
Juno watched her go until she could not see her anymore.
Then she let her breath out. All of it. It went out of her in a long shaking rush, and her knees went, and she had to put her hand on the boards to stay up.
Vespera had not moved.
She was still standing at her crack, with her eye to it, looking up at the empty place by the bare tree where the girl had been.
"Who was it?" whispered Juno. "Did you see? Who was it?"
Vespera did not answer at once.
"A girl in a gray coat," she said at last. Very low. "With a face like the end of the world."
"Was it—" Juno swallowed. "The step women said there was a girl with him. With Kross. In the carriage. Was it her?"
Vespera turned round. Her face in the half-dark was very still.
"I've seen her once," she said. "On the Glass. Behind him. Carrying his bag." She was quiet. "Wren. Her name's Wren."
Juno stared at her.
"She saw," she said. "She was looking right at the snow. She must have seen."
"Perhaps."
"Then she'll tell him. She'll go straight back and tell him, and he'll come, he'll come now, with the gray coats and a smith and—"
"Perhaps," said Vespera again.
"Then we've got to go! Now! Out the drain, out the back, before—"
"No."
Juno stopped.
Vespera had gone back to the middle of the room. She was standing by the three-legged table, with the candle on it, and the tray, and the three needles laid out in a row. She was looking at them.
"Not now," she said. "Not in the daylight. Not out of that drain, with the snow starting, and the marks in it, and a girl up on the road who may have gone to tell and may not." She did not look round. "If we go out now, we leave a trail across the snow from here to Saint Bride's that a blind man could follow. And then they don't get one old woman. They get the crypt. And the children. And Gideon. And you."
Juno did not say anything.
"We wait for dark," said Vespera. "Dark, and the snow, so it covers the marks. Then we go out the drain, and along the river path, under the quays, where there's no snow at all. And nobody follows."
"And if they come before dark?"
Vespera looked at her then.
"Then you go out the drain," she said, "with your coat on. And you don't look back."
Juno opened her mouth.
"That's not a question," said Vespera. "That's what's going to happen." She turned back to the tray. "Go and tell Brin. Down the drain, quiet. Tell him dark. Tell him to stay a post. He's good at it, whatever I said. Tell him I said so."
Juno went.
She crawled down the dark drain on her hands and knees, through the dust, to the square of gray light at the end, and put her head out into the snow, and there was Brin. Pressed flat against the blue wall behind the vat, so close to the stone that his hood touched it. So still that the snow had begun to settle on his shoulders, and on the long thing in his hands, and on the top of his hood, in a little white cap.
He looked down at her without moving his head. Only his eyes.
"Dark," said Juno. "She says wait for dark. She says stay a post." She swallowed. "She says you're good at it."
Brin's eyes moved. Toward the road above. Toward the place where the girl had stood.
"She looked," he said. In his slow, careful southern talk. Each word a stone. "Long time. At this one."
"Did she see you?"
Brin thought about it for a long time.
"Yes," he said.
Juno stared up at him.
"She saw," said Brin. "And she put her hand. Inside. For a whistle." He touched his own coat, over his heart, where the girl had put her hand. "And then. No whistle."
He was quiet for a moment.
"This one does not know," he said slowly, "what that means."
Neither did Juno.
For Later
The afternoon was very long.
Juno had never known an afternoon so long. She sat on the dusty floor of the blue arch with her back against the shelves and her knees drawn up inside her coat, and listened. To the river, outside, slapping against the stones of the platform. To the wind on the Weavers' Road above, which came and went. To the little sounds the arch made, the creaks and ticks of old wood in the cold. To every footstep that went along the river path a long way off, under the quays, and every cart that went over the Cut Bridge, and every gull.
Nobody came.
The snow went on falling, lightly, outside. She could see it through the crack. Small dry flakes, going past slowly. Settling on the vats. Settling on the boot marks in front of the grille, softening them, rounding their edges. Filling them in.
Vespera did not sit. For a long time she walked. Up and down the arch, slowly, from the shelves to the chair and back. She did not make a sound on the boards. Juno did not know how she did it. She walked like Gideon walked. Like somebody who had learned, a long time ago, that sound was dangerous.
Then, when the light through the crack had gone from white to gray, she stopped walking.
She stood by the three-legged table and looked at the tray.
At the three needles, laid out in a row. At the little brass pot. At the folded cloth. Under twenty years of dust.
"Who were they for?" said Juno.
She had not meant to ask. It came out of her before she could stop it, into the quiet.
Vespera did not turn round. She stood with her blue hands at her sides and looked down at the tray for so long that Juno thought she was not going to answer at all.
"A woman," said Vespera. "A young one. With a baby."
Juno waited.
"She came to me one night," said Vespera. "Late. In the rain. With the baby under her shawl. She'd heard there was a list, she said. With her name on it. Her husband's. They were going to be taken. Both of them. She didn't know why. Something her husband's father had done, or been, or known. Something in the family." Her voice was very flat. "She wanted one thing. She wanted her son's name put somewhere nobody could take it. In her own skin. Over her heart. So that whatever they did to her, she'd know she had a son, and what he was called. Even if she didn't know anything else."
The river slapped against the stones outside.
"I said come back in the morning," said Vespera. "I said I'd mix the ink tonight, the old way, and it needs till morning to be ready. Come back at first light and I'll do it. I laid it all out. There." She did not point. She did not need to. "Three needles. The pot. The cloth. All ready. For first light."
She was quiet for a long time.
"She didn't come back," said Juno.
"She didn't come back." Vespera's voice did not change. "They took her in the night. Her and the husband. From their own bed. I heard in the morning. And the baby—" She stopped. "Nobody knew. Nobody I could find. Gone. Like a stone in a pond."
Juno sat very still.
"And I sat in that chair," said Vespera, "all that day. In my own chair. With the needles there in front of me. And I thought, this is what I'm for. I'm the needle. I'm the one who keeps the names. And I couldn't keep one baby's name for one night." She breathed out. "And that night I put my list under the floor and a padlock on my grille and I walked out of this city and I didn't come back for twenty years."
She turned round then.
Her face in the gray light was very old. Older than Juno had ever seen it. Every line in it gone deep.
"That's why," she said. "Since you asked. That's mine. Now it's yours too. Don't spend it."
Juno did not know what to say. She found that her eyes were hot.
"What was his name?" she said. "The baby?"
Vespera looked at her for a long moment.
"She never told me," she said. "She was going to tell me in the morning. When I did it. She said she wanted to say it out loud for the first time while the needle went in. So it would go in with the ink." Her mouth did something that was not a smile. "So nobody ever knew it. Not me. Not anybody. Only her, and she's gone."
She looked down at the tray again.
And then she did something Juno did not expect.
She picked up one of the needles.
The first one. The one on the left. She lifted it off the tray, and blew the dust off it, softly, and held it up to the gray light from the crack. It was long, and very fine, and bright under the dust, and it had a little wooden handle wound round with thread.
"Twenty years," she said. "That's long enough for a needle to wait."
She sat down. Not in the chair. On the floor, beside it, with her back against its leg, the way Juno was sitting against the shelves. She took the black bottle out of the box, the forty-year one, from the very bottom, from the straw. She unstopped it. The smell came out into the room, very strong, sharp and black and old, so that Juno's eyes watered.
Vespera pushed back the sleeve of her left arm, up past the elbow.
The writing went round and round it. Old and new, blue and black. But on the inside of the arm, just above the elbow, in the soft pale skin where the arm bends, there was a space. A small one. Clear. As if it had been kept.
She dipped the needle in the black ink.
"It'll be dark soon," she said, without looking up. "I can't do this in the dark. Get the lamp. In the box, on the end shelf, by the skull. There's oil in it still, I'll wager. Light it, and put it on the floor here by me, and hang the old blanket over the boards behind it, there, on the nails, so the light doesn't go out through the cracks."
Juno got up.
She found the lamp. An old brass one, with a glass chimney gone brown with smoke. There was oil in it. Not much. She lit it from the candle, and the flame came up yellow and steady inside the brown glass, and the blue room changed color again, went warmer, the blue going purple at the edges. She found the blanket. An old gray army blanket, folded on the end shelf, stiff with dust. She shook it out, coughing, and hung it on the long nails in the boards at the front of the arch, behind the grille, over the cracks.
She looked at it. It did not quite reach the top. There was a gap, along the top edge, a finger's width, where the boards went up into the curve of the brick.
"There's a gap," she said.
"Nobody'll see a finger's width," said Vespera. "Not from the river. Not from the road. Put the lamp down here."
Juno put the lamp down on the floor beside her.
And Vespera began.
Juno had seen her sew. She had seen her paint, at Midwinter, on Corin's face. She had not seen this.
Vespera held the needle the way you would hold a pen, but very short, close to the point, between her blue thumb and her first two fingers. She dipped it in the black. She set the point of it against the pale skin inside her arm. And she pushed. Very lightly. Very quickly. In, and out. A tiny black dot, where the needle had been. And then another, beside it. And another. So fast that Juno could hardly see her hand move. Like a bird pecking.
She did not make a sound. Her face did not change. Her lips were moving. Juno could not hear what she was saying. It was very low, under her breath, the same few words over and over, in time with the needle. Like a song you sing to a child to make it sleep.
The dots became lines. The lines became letters.
Juno did not mean to look. She looked anyway. She could not help it. She was sitting very close, and the lamp was very bright, and the letters were black and new and shining on the pale skin.
She read them slowly. The way she read everything. One letter at a time.
S. I. S. T. E. R.
And then, after a gap, the needle still going:
B. A. N.
Vespera's other hand came down over the letters. Flat. Blue. Covering them.
Juno looked up.
Vespera was looking at her. Her sharp black eyes in the yellow lamplight.
"That," she said, "is for later."
"I'm sorry," said Juno. "I didn't mean—"
"I know you didn't. You read the way he writes. You can't help it." Vespera lifted her hand. She looked at the letters she had made. Then she pulled the sleeve of her dress down over them, carefully, so the cloth just touched. "It's not for you. It's for me. For when they've had the rest." She was quiet. "So I don't forget the one thing that matters."
"What thing?"
Vespera looked at her for a long time.
"Who to tell," she said. "And what."
She did not say anything else. She wiped the needle on the folded cloth from the tray, the cloth that had waited twenty years, and laid it back on the tray, in its place, on the left. Beside the other two.
Then she leaned her head back against the leg of the old chair and shut her eyes.
Outside, behind the blanket, the light was going. Juno could see it through the finger's-width gap at the top. Gray, and then darker gray, and then the blue of evening coming down over the river. And far off, across the water, on the other side, the Ash lamps along the quays coming on. One. And then another. And then the whole long line of them, blue, humming, all at once.
And the little yellow lamp burned on the floor of the arch, behind the blanket, and the finger's-width of its light went up through the gap at the top of the boards into the dark.
Put On Your Coat
Vespera slept.
Juno had not thought she would. She had not thought anybody could sleep, sitting on the floor of a blue arch with gray coats looking for them in the snow outside. But Vespera slept. With her head back against the leg of the old chair, and her mouth a little open, and her blue hands lying in her lap, palms up, the way Corin's had lain in his lap the night before he went north. And her breathing went slow and even and a little rough, like a cat purring.
She looked very small, asleep. Smaller than awake. Without her sharp black eyes open, and her sharp voice, there did not seem to be very much of her at all.
And she was cold. Juno could see it. Her lips had gone a bit blue, and not from ink. Her hands, in her lap, were shaking a little, even asleep.
Juno took off her coat.
She did it slowly, so as not to wake her. She held it for a moment, the brown coat, too long in the arms, with the patch on the elbow and the thing sewn in at the hem. Then she leaned over and laid it across Vespera's knees and shoulders, carefully, like a blanket. Tucking it in at the sides.
Vespera did not wake. But her hands stopped shaking, after a while.
Juno sat back against the shelves in her three shawls and hugged her knees and watched the gap at the top of the blanket go dark.
It went dark very slowly. Then all at once. And then there was only the yellow lamp on the floor, and the blue walls, and the drawings, and the big chair with its straps. And outside, the river. And the hum of the Ash lamps across the water.
She must have slept too. A little. Because the next thing she knew, there was a whistle.
One.
Low and short. From outside. From behind the vat.
Juno's eyes came open. She did not move. She did not know, for a moment, where she was. Then the blue walls came round her, and the lamp, and Vespera asleep against the chair with Juno's coat over her. And the whistle.
If anybody comes along the arches, anybody at all, you whistle.
One whistle. The arches.
She got up on her knees. She went to the front of the arch, to the blanket on its nails, and lifted the edge of it. Just a little. And put her eye to the crack in the boards behind.
It was full dark outside. The snow had stopped. The sky had cleared. There was a moon somewhere, behind the Weavers' Road, and its light came down white and cold on the snow in front of the arches, on the vats, on the river. Everything was very sharp and very still. Black and white, like a picture cut out of paper.
And far away, at the river end of the arches, there were lamps.
Three of them. Four. Five. Small, blue, swinging. Coming along the front of the arches, slowly, one behind another. Ash lamps, on poles, the kind the gray coats carried. And under the lamps, dark shapes. Walking. A great many of them. More than she could count, because they were in a line and the lamps only showed the front ones.
And she heard them, now. Faintly, over the river. Boots on the frozen snow. Crunch, and crunch. All in step.
And something else. A heavier sound, at the back of them. A clank. Iron on iron. Like somebody carrying a great many tools in a sack.
They said they'd come back with a smith.
Juno let the blanket fall.
She turned round. Vespera was awake.
She had not moved. She was sitting exactly as she had been, against the leg of the chair, with Juno's coat over her shoulders. But her eyes were open. Wide open, and black, and very sharp, in the yellow lamplight. Looking at Juno.
She did not ask. She did not need to.
Then, from outside, there came the whistle again.
Twice.
Low and short. And then again. Twice.
The road.
Juno went back to the crack. She pressed her cheek against the cold wood and looked up, the way she had looked at noon, up the blue brick, to the parapet of the Weavers' Road above. White with moonlight. With the little bare tree.
There was a carriage on the road.
She could not see all of it. Only the top of it, over the parapet, and the heads of the horses. A closed carriage. Gray, or black, she could not tell in the moonlight. Plain. No gilt. No crest on the door. Two horses, standing very still, with their breath going up in white clouds. And a man on the box, muffled up, with a whip.
And beside the carriage, at the parapet, where the girl had stood at noon, a man.
Tall. Thin. In a long gray coat, buttoned to the throat. A gray hat.
He was not looking along the arches, at the lamps. He was looking straight down. At the last arch. At the grille. At the gray boards behind it.
At the gap.
Juno looked up and saw where she was. She saw the gap at the top of the blanket, a finger's width. And the yellow light coming up through it, onto the curved brick at the top of the arch, inside. And she knew, the way you know a thing in a dream, all at once and completely, that from up there, on the Weavers' Road, looking straight down, in the dark, a finger's width of yellow light was the brightest thing in the world.
The man at the parapet took off his right glove.
Slowly. Finger by finger. The way you would take off a glove to shake somebody's hand. He folded it and put it in his pocket. Then he laid his bare hand flat on the cold stone of the parapet, and stood there, very still, with his bare hand on the stone, looking down.
Juno had only ever seen him from a long way off. She knew who he was.
She turned round.
Vespera had stood up.
She was standing in the middle of the blue arch, by the old chair, in the yellow lamplight. Small and straight. With her ragged gray hair. With her blue hands at her sides. With the brown coat in them.
She had taken it off her shoulders. She was holding it out. Toward Juno. In both hands. The way you would hold out a coat to a child on a cold morning, before school, at the door.
Her face was quite calm.
Outside, the boots were coming nearer along the arches. Crunch, and crunch. And the clank of the smith's sack. And the lamps, swinging blue.
And on the road above, very quietly, a carriage door opened, and shut.
"Juno," said Vespera.
Her voice was very low and very steady. The voice of somebody who has known for twenty years that a thing was coming, and has had a long time to decide what she will say when it does.
"Put on your coat."
