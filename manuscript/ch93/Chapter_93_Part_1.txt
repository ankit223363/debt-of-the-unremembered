Chapter 93: Blue Hands, Part 1 of 5: The Hundredth Step
The Hundredth Step
There were two hundred and twelve steps up Saint Bride's Lane, and Juno knew every one of them.
She knew the one near the bottom that rocked when you trod on its left side. She knew the seventh, which had a crack across it shaped like a lightning bolt, and the thirty-first, which some long-ago mason had cut a little too tall, so that everybody who did not know it tripped. She knew the long flat stretch in the forties where the lane went between two high blank walls and the wind came down it like water down a pipe. She knew where the ice lay in winter, and which steps the step women had salted, and which they had not, because the house at the top of that bit was owned by a man who would not pay.
And she knew the hundredth, best of all. Because the hundredth was the landing.
It was a wide flat place halfway up, where the lane stopped going straight up for a while and went sideways instead. There was a pump there, an old iron one with a long curved handle and a lion's head for a spout, and a stone trough under it, frozen solid now, with a skin of ice as thick as a book. And there was a bench against the wall. A plank on two stones. And on the bench, every evening from the fourth bell until it was too dark to see, there was Old Tull.
Old Tull was sitting there now, in the blue dusk, with a blanket round his shoulders and another over his knees and his clay pipe in his teeth. His pipe was lit. Old Tull's pipe was always lit. He had a face like a crab apple left too long on the tree, all shriveled and red, and a white stubble on it, and very bright small black eyes that saw everything that went up the steps, and everything that went down.
Juno sat beside him on the plank with her hands pushed up the opposite sleeves of her coat.
It was a boy's coat. Brown, and too long, with the cuffs turned back, and a patch on one elbow in a different brown. She had three shawls on under it, and Mrs. Doyle's dead husband's boots on her feet, stuffed with straw, because her own had split in the frost and there was no money for more. She had all her toes. Doctor Silas had said, after the walk, that she might lose two. She had not lost any. She was very proud of that. She wriggled them sometimes, inside the big boots, just to feel them all there.
She wriggled them now.
"Nobody," said Old Tull, round his pipe.
"Nobody yet."
"Nobody all day. Bar the coal boy, and Mrs. Gammon's girl with the pies, and a gray coat at the bottom at noon who looked up and didn't fancy it." He took his pipe out. "They never fancy it. Two hundred and twelve. A gray coat'll stand at the bottom and look up and think, I'll come back when I'm younger." He put the pipe back. "And off he goes."
Juno grinned. She looked down the steps.
You could see a long way from the hundredth. The lane went down below them in a long gray ribbon, step after step, between the walls and the backs of houses, with snow on every step and every wall-top and every window ledge. At the bottom it came out into Fish Lane, and beyond Fish Lane there were the roofs of the lower town, white and jumbled, going down to the river. And the river itself, black, with the blue lamps along the quays beginning to come on, one after another, in long lines. And over on the far side, the Bank, white on its hill, with its lit windows. And the Ministry, gray and square, five floors of it. And far off to the right, up on its own hill, black against the last of the light, the Spire.
She looked at the Spire for a while. She did not like to. But she always did.
"Here," said Old Tull.
She looked down.
Two people were coming up the steps.
They were a long way down still, near the bottom, where the lane came in from Fish Lane. Two dark shapes, one big and one small. Coming up slowly, through the blue dusk, one step at a time. The big one was in front. He was very big. He had a great heavy coat on, a long one, with a hood pulled forward so far over his head that she could not see his face at all. And he had a sack over one shoulder, and something long and thin wrapped in sacking over the other, and he climbed the steps the way a bullock climbs a hill, slowly and steadily and without seeming to notice that it was a hill.
The small one was behind him. Very small. Hardly bigger than Juno. In a shabby black cloak with the hem dragging in the snow, and a woolen shawl over her head, and big mittens on her hands. She climbed fast. Too fast for her size. Every few steps she had to stop and wait for the big one, who did not hurry, and she stood with her hands on her hips while she waited, and Juno could tell from the way she stood, even from the hundredth step, that she was cross about it.
"Know them?" said Old Tull.
Juno stood up.
She did not know them. She did not think she knew them. But there was something about the small one. Something about the way she stood with her hands on her hips. And the big one, who climbed like a bullock, she had never seen him before in her life. She was sure of that. You would not forget him.
They came up past the thirtieth step, and the fortieth, and the long flat stretch where the wind came down. Juno watched them. The wind caught the small one's shawl and blew it back off her head, and she grabbed for it with one mittened hand and missed, and Juno saw her hair.
Gray. Short. Cut all anyhow, in ragged tufts, as if somebody had done it in the dark with a pair of shears.
And Juno knew her.
She had seen her once. Only once. At Midwinter, in the crypt, on the day of the Bourse. An old woman sitting on a stool by the brazier with a little box of paints open on her knees, and Corin sitting in front of her, very still, while she painted his face into somebody else's. A broken nose, and a squint in one eye, and white wild eyebrows. An old lame lamp boy. With her sharp black eyes an inch from his face and her mouth full of pins. And her hands.
Juno looked at the big mittens. She did not need to see under them.
"It's the paint woman," she said. "It's—Tull, I know her. She did Corin's face. Before the Bourse."
Old Tull took his pipe out of his mouth.
"Then go and get her," he said. "Before she falls off the forty-sixth and breaks her neck. That one's got ice on it."
Juno went.
She went down the steps two at a time, the way you could only go down steps you knew in the dark. Past the nineties and the eighties. Past the seventies. Her big boots slapped on the stone. Her breath went up in clouds. She came down into the long flat stretch where the wind was, and the two of them were there, just coming to the end of it, and the big one looked up from under his hood and saw her and stopped.
Juno stopped too. A few steps above them. Breathing hard.
Under the hood, she could see a face now. A broad round face, brown, very still. And on both cheeks, from under the eyes down nearly to the jaw, the skin was gray. Smooth and gray and dull. Like Corin's hand.
She had never seen gray like that on anyone's face before.
The big man looked at her for a long moment, without moving. Then he turned his head, slowly, and looked back down at the old woman behind him.
The old woman had stopped too. She was standing on the step below him, with her shawl round her shoulders and her ragged gray head bare in the wind, and she was looking up at Juno. Her face was small and brown and wrinkled all over, like a walnut. Her eyes were black and very sharp.
She looked at Juno's boy's coat. At her three shawls. At the big boots stuffed with straw.
"You're the girl," she said. Her voice was low and rough, like a file on wood. "The one with the boots. Who walked to the fens."
"Juno," said Juno.
"I know your name. I've been hearing your name for a month. Juno this, Juno that. Juno walked fourteen miles on frozen feet. Juno carried the letter." The old woman sniffed. "He talks about you like you were a saint in a window."
Juno did not ask who. She found she could not ask anything. Her throat had gone tight all at once.
"Is he—" she said.
"He's alive." The old woman came up a step, so that she was beside the big man. "He's alive and he's grayer and he's coming. Five days behind us. Six. He's slow." She looked at Juno with her sharp black eyes. "And he's still a fool. But you knew that."
Juno let out a breath that came out half a laugh and half something else.
"Yes," she said.
"Good. Then we understand each other." The old woman pulled her shawl back up over her ragged hair. "I'm Vespera. This is Brin. Brin doesn't say much in your talk, and what he does say you won't like. He's carrying all my things and he's cold and so am I. Take us up to Gideon's hole before I freeze to this step and they have to chip me off it in the spring."
Juno turned round.
And took them up.
The Crypt Again
Saint Bride's sat at the very top of the lane, squat and black, as if it had climbed all two hundred and twelve steps itself a long time ago and then sat down and never got up again.
It was a small church. The smallest in the city, Mrs. Doyle said, and the oldest, though Mrs. Doyle said that about a great many things. It was built of a gray stone gone black with soot, and it had a short square tower with no bell in it, and a roof that sagged in the middle like an old horse's back. The windows were small and deep and full of colored glass so dirty that the colors only showed when the sun was right behind them, which in winter was never. Over the door, in an iron cage on a bracket, an oil lamp burned. It was always lit. Juno had never seen it out. Mrs. Doyle filled it every morning from a can, standing on a box, muttering.
There had been no priest at Saint Bride's for nine years. The old women came on Sundays and sat in the cold pews and said the prayers to each other, and Mrs. Doyle swept, and that was all.
Juno led them round the side of the church, along the narrow path between the wall and the old graves, where the snow was not trodden. Past the yew tree with its dark branches bent under the white. To the vestry door at the back, which was low and black and studded with nails.
She knocked. Three, and then two.
Nothing happened for a while. Then a bolt went back, and another, and the door opened a crack, and an eye looked out, and under the eye the end of a broom.
"It's me," said Juno.
"I can see it's you," said Mrs. Doyle. "Who's that?"
"Friends."
"Everybody's friends." The eye moved past Juno, to the big hooded shape of Brin, and the small one beside him. "Friends is what they all say. The gray ones said friends, at the gate, at Midwinter. Friends of the parish." The broom did not move. "Friends of whose parish, I said."
"It's the paint woman," said Juno. "From before the Bourse. Who did Corin's face."
The eye went to Vespera and stayed there.
Vespera looked back at it. She did not say anything. She only took off one of her big mittens, slowly, and held up her hand in the light of the caged lamp over the door.
It was blue.
Not pale blue, or a bit blue, like a hand that is cold. Blue. Dark, deep, solid blue, the blue of a jay's wing, or the sea in a picture, from the tips of her fingers to her wrist. And not even. Darker in the cracks of the knuckles and round the nails, and lighter on the backs of the fingers where the skin was smooth. As if she had dipped her hand in a pot of ink every day for fifty years, which, Juno thought, she probably had.
The eye in the door looked at the blue hand for a long time.
Then the door opened all the way.
"Well, why didn't you say," said Mrs. Doyle. "Come in. Come in out of it. Shut that door behind you, it's letting the cold in and the cold's got no business here."
The crypt was down twenty-three steps, under the vestry, with a rope along the wall to hold on to because there was no rail.
Juno went first, because she knew them. Vespera came after her, with one blue hand on the rope and the other holding up her cloak. Brin came last, and had to bend almost double under the low curved roof of the stair, and still banged his head twice, and said something in a language Juno did not know, quite loudly, both times.
At the bottom, there was the crypt.
It was long and low, with a roof of old red brick that curved down on both sides to the floor, like the inside of a loaf of bread. Down the middle of it there were the old stone pillars, thick and short, and between the pillars there were the arches where the dead had been, a long time ago, before somebody moved them. Most of the arches were empty now. Some had straw in them, and blankets. In one there was a table made of a door laid across two barrels, covered with jars and bottles and a pair of scales and a great many books, and that was Doctor Silas's. In the middle of the floor, on its iron legs, there was the brazier. It was burning red. The coal came from Abner Cole, the coal man at number sixteen Tallow Street, who brought a sack every week and would not take any money for it. Nobody knew why. Juno thought she knew why. Abner Cole had a daughter who had gone up the hill in a black cart two years ago, and never come back.
The walls were covered in chalk.
That was the first thing anybody saw, coming in. Words, in white chalk and blue chalk, big and crooked, all along the brick. LYRA. READ. V. DA. SAUL. The SAUL had a line under it, two lines, very hard, since the fire. And low down, near the floor, between two pairs of big boots, in blue: GID.
The boots were Big Gideon's.
He was sitting where he always sat, on the floor against the wall under the word DA, with his big hands on his knees. He was a huge man, a docker, with a broken nose and a long white scar down one cheek from a hook, and his face had been empty for four years. It was not quite empty now. When Juno came in at the bottom of the stair, his eyes moved. They went to her, and to the old woman behind her, and to the huge hooded shape bending under the arch behind that. And then they went to the boy sitting beside him on the floor.
And stayed there.
The boy was Gideon. Little Gideon, though he was not so little. He was about twelve, thin and pale, with a pointed face and straight dark hair and his gray gloves on, cut down at the fingers. He was whittling. He was always whittling. He had a little knife in one hand and a piece of pale wood in the other, and the floor round him was covered in thin curly shavings, and all along the wall beside him, in a row, there were boats. Flat boats, the kind that went up and down the river with coal in them. A whole fleet of them, more than Juno had ever bothered to count, every one with its flat front and its little cabin at the back.
He did not look up. He did not stop whittling. But he said, in his flat quiet voice, "Who's that?"
"Vespera," said Juno. "And Brin."
Gideon looked up then.
He looked at Vespera. He looked at her for a long time, with his pale eyes that never showed anything, because there was nothing in him to show. Vivienne had taken it. All of it. Every Tuesday, for four winters, in her cold room. Every warm thing he had ever felt. He could still see, and hear, and think, and whittle boats, and walk without making any sound at all. But he did not feel anything. Not happy, not sad. Not cold, much. He had told Juno so, the day they gave his da back his name. I haven't got any warm ones.
"You did the lamp boy," he said to Vespera. "His face. I remember."
"I did," said Vespera.
"It was good."
"It was very good," said Vespera. "It was the best face I ever did. And then he went and got it burned off him in a fire, the fool." She looked round the crypt. At the brazier, and the arches, and the chalk on the walls. "So this is the hole."
"This is the hole," said Doctor Silas.
He had come out from behind his table. He was tall and very thin, like a heron standing in a pond, and his spectacles sat crooked on his nose because one of the arms was bent, and one of the lenses had a crack across it like a star, from the day Big Gideon said his son's name. He wiped his hands on a cloth. He looked at Vespera over the top of the crooked spectacles.
"Madame," he said.
"Doctor," said Vespera.
They looked at each other for a while. Juno had the feeling they had met before. Not at Midwinter. A long time before. She did not know why she thought so.
"You're thinner," said Vespera.
"You're older," said Silas.
"We're both older. The whole world's older. That's what happens when you leave it alone for twenty years." Vespera sniffed. "Is there tea? There'd better be tea."
There was tea. Mrs. Doyle had come down the stair behind Brin with her broom, and she put the broom in the corner, and the kettle on the brazier, and was getting the cups down from the shelf before anybody had asked.
Brin had straightened up as far as the roof let him. He stood under the middle of the arch with his head bent and pushed his hood back.
And the children saw him.
There were six of them. They lived under the last arch at the far end of the crypt, on straw pallets, under a heap of blankets. They had been fifty-two, once, the children of Saint Bride's, the ones nobody had come for, before the fire at Midwinter, before the carts. Now there were six. They had been asleep, or nearly, curled up together like puppies. Now six heads came up out of the blankets, one after another, and six pairs of eyes went very wide.
Brin's head was shaved. Round and brown and bare. And on both cheeks, from under the eyes nearly to the jaw, the gray. Smooth and dull, like stone.
Nobody said anything.
Brin looked at the children for a long moment. Then he did something Juno did not expect. He got down on his knees, slowly, on the brick floor, the way a very big man gets down, one knee and then the other. So that his head was lower. So that he was not so tall.
And he smiled.
It was a slow smile, the way he did everything slowly. It started at one corner of his mouth and went across his whole broad brown face until it reached the gray on the other side and kept going, so that the gray smiled too.
"Hello," he said. In the southern talk. Slowly, as if each word were a stone he was carrying. "This one. Is Brin."
The smallest of the children, a girl of about four called Pip, with her thumb in her mouth, took her thumb out.
"You've got a funny face," she said.
Brin thought about this.
"Yes," he said.
Pip seemed satisfied. She put her thumb back in. And after a moment she crawled out from under the blankets, all the way out, in her nightgown, and across the brick floor, and sat down in front of the huge kneeling man, and looked up at him.
Brin looked down at her.
And very slowly, very carefully, he put out one enormous brown finger. And Pip took hold of it. With her whole hand.
"Saints," said Mrs. Doyle softly, by the kettle.
Vespera did not say anything. But Juno, looking at her, saw that her sharp black eyes were very bright in the red light of the brazier, and that she had turned her head away.
