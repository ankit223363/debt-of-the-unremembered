Chapter 93: Blue Hands, Part 4 of 5: Inks
Inks
The drain was dry, and dark, and full of dust, and it went straight for about the length of a cart, just as Vespera had said, and then it turned up.
Juno felt the turn with her head. She put her hands up in the dark and found wood over her. Old boards, rough, gritty with dust. She pushed. Nothing happened. She pushed harder, with her shoulders, crouched in the bottom of the drain with her knees up round her ears, and something gave with a crack and a groan, and a square of boards lifted, and a great soft cloud of dust came down on her head.
She climbed out, coughing, into the dark.
It was very dark. Darker than the crypt at night with the brazier out. She stood still and could not see her own hands. She could smell it, though. The blue smell, much stronger here. And the dried-flower smell under it. And dust, and cold, and old wood.
Behind her and below her, she heard Vespera come up the drain. Slower. Breathing hard. She heard her blue hands on the edge of the hole, and a grunt, and the scrape of her cloak on the boards. And then a little sharp sound. Flint on iron.
A spark.
Another.
And then a small yellow flame, low down, in Vespera's cupped hands, catching on the wick of a stub of candle. And growing. And the dark went back, a little at a time, like water going out of a basin, and Juno saw the room.
She had never seen anything like it.
It was the arch. The inside of it. A long low room with a curved brick roof, like the crypt, like the inside of a loaf, but smaller, and the brick was not red. It was blue. All of it. Every brick in the roof, every brick in the walls, soaked through with a hundred years of indigo, so dark that in the candlelight it looked like the sky just after the sun goes down. The floor was boards. Old gray boards, with gaps between them, and dust on them as thick as felt.
And there were things everywhere.
Shelves. All along both walls, from the floor to where the roof began to curve. Rough wooden shelves, sagging in the middle. And on the shelves, bottles. Hundreds of them, Juno thought. She did not know how many. Little glass bottles, every size, every shape. Round ones, square ones, tall thin ones like fingers, little fat ones like toads. Every one with a cork in it. Every one with a label tied round its neck on a thread, written in a small sharp crooked hand. And every one with something dark inside it. Dried. Cracked. Gone to a crust at the bottom of the glass.
Ink.
And between the bottles, on the shelves, other things. Rows of needles, stuck into flat pieces of cork, as neat as soldiers. Long ones, short ones, fine as a hair, thick as a nail. Little brass pots. Pestles and mortars. Bundles of feathers. A pair of spectacles with tiny round lenses, folded up. A skull. A real one. Small. A cat's, Juno thought, or a fox's. With a bottle of ink sitting inside it, where the brain would be.
On the walls, between the shelves, there were drawings.
Pinned up with long iron pins. Dozens of them, overlapping, curling at the edges, gone brown with age. Drawings of hands. Of arms. Of the backs of people's necks, and the soles of their feet. And on every hand, every arm, every neck, there were patterns. Lines and curls and spirals and little rows of letters, so small you would need a glass to read them. Birds. Fish. Leaves. A ship. A tree. A door with a key in it. Some of the drawings had notes beside them, in the same sharp crooked hand as the labels. Juno could read some of the words. Here, not lower. Two passes. For grief. For later.
And in the middle of the room, under the highest part of the curved roof, there was a chair.
A big one. Old, dark wood, with a high back and wide flat arms. The seat was leather, black, cracked all over like the bed of a dried-up pond, and worn pale in the middle where a great many people had sat. On each wide arm there was a strap. A leather strap with a buckle. Worn pale too, on the inside, where a great many wrists had been.
Beside the chair there was a little table on three legs. And on the table, under the dust, a tray. And on the tray, laid out in a row, three needles and a little brass pot and a cloth. Folded. As if somebody had laid them out ready, twenty years ago, for somebody who was coming in the morning. And then never come.
Juno stood and looked at it.
Vespera had climbed out of the hole behind her. She was standing up now, holding the candle. She was looking at the chair too.
She did not say anything for a long time.
"Is that where you did it?" said Juno at last. Very quietly. Because it seemed like the kind of room where you spoke quietly. "The ink?"
"That's where I did it." Vespera's voice was quiet too. Rough. "Forty years. Four thousand people, perhaps. Five. In that chair." She walked to it, slowly, across the dusty boards, and stood beside it. She did not touch it. "Sailors. Dockers. Old women. Children, sometimes. People who'd lost somebody and wanted to keep the face. People who knew they were going to be taken and wanted to hide one thing where no harvester could get it."
"Can they? Hide it? In ink?"
Vespera looked at her.
"Do you know what a harvester does?" she said.
"He takes things. Out of your head. With his hands."
"With his hands. Yes. He puts his bare hand on you and he reaches in and he takes the thing out, and it comes out as Ash, gray, in a jar, and you don't have it anymore." Vespera held up her own blue hand, in the candlelight. "But a harvester can only take what's in your head. Not what's in your skin." She turned the hand slowly, so that the light moved on the deep blue. "If you put a thing in the skin, with the right ink, and the right needle, and the right words, it's not in your head anymore. It's in you. In the body. Like a scar. Like the way your hands know how to tie a knot. A harvester can't reach it. He can take everything else. Your name. Your mother. Your whole life. But he can't take that."
Juno thought about it.
"Is that what's on your arms?" she said.
Vespera was quiet. Then she pushed back the sleeve of her cloak, and the sleeve of the dress under it, on her left arm, up to the elbow.
Juno had seen it before, at Midwinter, in the crypt, when Vespera pushed up her sleeves to paint Corin's face. She had not looked properly. She looked now.
The arm was covered in writing. From the blue wrist to the elbow, and past it, under the sleeve. Tiny letters, very close together, in rows, going round and round the arm like the thread on a reel. Some in blue. Some in black. Some so old they had gone pale and blurred, like the letters on the old man's hands under the Corn Bridge. Some very new. Sharp and dark.
"Some of it," said Vespera. "Some of it's mine. Things I wanted to keep. Some of it's other people's, that I keep for them." She pushed the sleeve back down. "And some of it's for later."
"For later?"
"For when they come for me." She said it quite simply. The way you would say for when it rains. "So that when they've taken everything else, there's still something left. Something to hold on to. A line or two. In the skin. That says who I am."
Juno did not say anything.
She thought about the book under her straw, in the crypt. HARBOUR TIDE TABLES. With Corin's life in it, a page at a time, in big square capitals, in case he forgot.
"Like Corin's Ledger," she said.
"Like his Ledger." Vespera's mouth moved. "Except that his can burn. And mine can't. Not unless they burn me with it."
She set the candle down on the three-legged table, beside the tray with the three needles. Its light went up the blue walls and over the drawings and into the curve of the roof.
"Now," she said, in a different voice. Her ordinary sharp one. "Work. We haven't got all day. Brin's out there in the snow pretending to be a post, and he's not good at it, he fidgets."
She went to the shelves.
She did not take all the bottles. Most of them were dead, she said. Dried out. Spoiled. Twenty years. She went along the shelves with the candle, very fast, picking up a bottle here and there, holding it to the light, shaking it, reading the label, and either putting it back or handing it to Juno. Juno wrapped the ones she was given in straw from a sack in the corner, the way Vespera showed her, and laid them in a wooden box. Most were dead. A few were not. A few, when Vespera shook them, made a small thick sound inside, like a slow heart.
"Those are the good ones," said Vespera. "The old ones. The ones made the old way. With oak gall, and soot, and a little of the person's own blood, and the right words said over it at the right time of the moon." She held one up to the candle. It was black. Not dark blue. Black, the blackest thing Juno had ever seen, like a hole in the light. "This one I made forty years ago. It'll be good for another forty. Long after I'm worms."
She gave it to Juno.
Juno held it very carefully. It was heavier than it looked. And it was warm. Not from Vespera's hand. Warm by itself. As if something inside it were alive and sleeping.
She wrapped it in straw and laid it in the box, at the bottom, in the middle, where it would be safest.
They worked for a long time.
Vespera took needles off the shelves, in their corks, and rolled them up in a strip of leather and tied it with a thong. She took the little brass pots, and the pestle, and a pair of fine scissors shaped like a bird, with the blades for a beak, that she looked at for a long time before she put them in her own pocket. She took some of the drawings off the walls, carefully, pulling out the long iron pins, and rolled them up, and gave them to Juno. She left most of them where they were.
She did not touch the chair.
She did not touch the tray with the three needles on it, either. She worked all round it, and all round the chair, as if they were not there. Juno noticed. She did not say anything.
At last, when the box was full, and the leather roll was tied, and the drawings were rolled up in a bundle with a string round them, Vespera stood in the middle of the room with the candle and looked round.
At the shelves, half empty now. At the drawings still pinned to the walls. At the chair.
Then she looked at the floor. At the boards. Right under the chair.
"Now," she said. "The thing we came for."
The Binders' List
The chair was too heavy for Vespera to move by herself, and too heavy for Juno, and too heavy, nearly, for the two of them together. They dragged it in the end. One on each arm, pulling, with their heels dug in, while it scraped across the dusty boards with a long shriek that made Juno's teeth hurt. They dragged it as far as the edge of the candlelight and let it go, and it stood there in the half-dark with its straps hanging down, looking at them, Juno thought, like a big old dog that has been made to get off its rug.
Under where it had stood, the boards were cleaner. Paler. A square of them, where no dust had fallen for forty years.
Vespera knelt down on the clean square. She felt along the boards with her bare blue fingers. Slowly. Pressing. Juno knelt beside her with the candle and watched.
At the third board from the end, Vespera stopped. She pressed down hard on one end of it with the heel of her hand.
The other end came up.
Just a little. A finger's width. Enough to get the fingers under. Vespera got her fingers under and lifted, and the board came up whole, and under it there was a space between the joists. Dark and dry. And in the space, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff and brown, there was a box.
A tin box. Flat. About the size of a big book. With a hinged lid, and a little clasp on the front, rusted shut.
Vespera lifted it out. She held it on her knees for a while without opening it. Then she broke the clasp with her thumb. It went with a small dry snap, like a twig.
She opened the lid.
Inside there was a roll of cloth.
Not paper. Cloth. Silk, Juno thought. Very fine, very thin, a pale cream color gone a little yellow at the edges. Rolled up tight, like a bandage, and tied with a thread of blue.
Vespera untied the thread. She unrolled the silk across her knees, a little way, and held it to the candle.
It was covered in writing.
Tiny writing. In ink, black and blue. In the same small sharp crooked hand as the labels on the bottles. Line after line after line, down the silk, so small and close together that from a little way off it looked like a pattern, like the grain in a piece of wood. Juno leaned closer. She could make out the letters, just, if she held her breath and looked very hard. Names. A name, and then something after it. And another name. And another.
"Silk," said Vespera, "because it folds without cracking. Because you can roll it small enough to hide in your hand. Because it doesn't burn easy, and it doesn't rot, and it doesn't tear. And because you can sew it into something." She let the silk lie across her knees. "Paper's for things you want people to find."
"Is that all of them?" said Juno. "The Binders?"
"All of them. Twenty years ago." Vespera ran one blue fingertip down the lines, not touching, just above. "Every Ink-Binder in this city, and where they kept their needles, and what they could do. Who could do grief. Who could do names. Who could do the deep work, the old work, under the skin where nobody looks. Who taught them. Who they taught. All in a line, one after another, back to the old women who taught me when I was younger than you." Her finger stopped. "Sixty-three names."
Juno looked at the silk.
"Why did you write it down?" she said. "If it's so dangerous."
Vespera did not answer for a moment.
"Because I was the needle," she said.
She said it the way people say I was the eldest, or I was the one who stayed. As if it explained everything. Juno waited.
"There's always one," said Vespera at last. "Among the Binders. One who keeps the list. Who knows where everybody is, so that when somebody's taken, the others know, and can hide. So that when somebody's dying, there's someone who knows who to send for to take their needles on. So that it doesn't all go. All the knowing." She looked down at the silk. "That's the needle. The one the thread goes through. And I was it, for twenty-two years. And then I went away."
"Why?"
Vespera's face closed. Like a door. Juno saw it happen.
"That," said Vespera, "is mine."
Juno did not ask again.
Vespera went on looking at the silk for a long time. Her finger moved down it, line by line, slowly. Sometimes it stopped. Sometimes her lips moved, as if she were saying the name under her breath. Once she shut her eyes.
"Half of these are dead," she said. "Must be. It's twenty years. Old Mab. Fennick the sailor. Hollis's mother." She breathed out. "And some of them are in the pens, I'll wager. Marjory. Marjory's on here. I put her on myself, the year she finished, the first anchor she did that didn't make me want to cry." She was quiet. "And the rest are hiding. In arches. Under bridges. Waiting for the gray coats with the lamps."
"So why not burn it?" said Juno. "Now. Here. Then nobody can ever find it."
Vespera looked at her.
It was the sharp look again. The look that decided whether a shirt could be mended.
"Because it's the only place in the world," she said, "where all their names are written down together. Every one. The living and the dead. Who they were, and what they could do, and who taught them." She laid her blue hand flat on the silk. "If I burn this, then when the gray coats have done with the city, there'll be nobody left who knows there ever were Binders at all. Not one name. It'll be as if we were never here." Her voice had gone very low. "And one day, girl, when this is over, if it's ever over, the ones who are left are going to need to find each other. And this is how."
Juno did not say anything.
Vespera began to roll the silk up again. Tight. Tighter than before. Rolling it on her knee with the flat of her hand, until it was a thin cream-colored stick no thicker than Juno's finger. She tied it with the blue thread.
Then she held out her hand.
"Give me your coat," she said.
Juno stared at her.
"My coat?"
"Your coat. The brown one. That's too long in the arms. Give it here." Vespera snapped her blue fingers. "I told you last night. It's a good coat. It's got a lining."
Juno took off her coat.
It was cold without it. The cold of the blue arch came straight through her three shawls. She stood and hugged herself and watched while Vespera turned the coat inside out on her knees, and felt along the lining, along the seam at the back, at the hem. The lining was old brown cotton, thin and patched. Vespera found a place at the bottom of the back, just above the hem, where the lining was sewn to the cloth in a long straight seam. She took the bird-shaped scissors out of her pocket and snipped the thread of the seam, one stitch at a time, for the length of a finger.
She slid the little roll of silk in between the lining and the cloth. Flat. Along the hem. Where it would lie against the backs of Juno's knees when she walked, and nobody would ever feel it.
Then she took a needle from the leather roll, and a thread. Not brown. Blue. A deep blue thread, the color of the brick.
And she sewed the seam up again.
She sewed very fast. Juno had never seen anybody sew so fast. Not even her mother, who did shirts by the piece. Vespera's blue fingers went in and out of the brown cotton like little fish, and the needle flashed in the candlelight, and in no time at all the seam was closed again. Small, tight, even stitches. So small you could hardly see them.
And then, on the lining, just above the seam, she sewed something else. In the same blue thread. Five letters. Very small. Very neat.
SORREL.
She bit off the thread. She turned the coat right side out again and held it up and looked at it. You could not see anything. It was just Juno's coat. Brown, and too long in the arms, with a patch on one elbow.
She held it out.
"Put it on."
Juno put it on. She could not feel anything. She moved her legs, and bent, and walked a step. Nothing. Only a very faint stiffness at the very bottom of the back, against her knees, that she would not have noticed if she had not known.
"Listen to me," said Vespera.
Juno listened.
"If anything happens," said Vespera. "To me. Tonight, tomorrow, any time. If they take me. If you hear that I'm gone." She was looking at Juno very hard. "You don't take that coat to Corin. You don't take it to the crypt. You don't show it to Silas, or Gideon, or anybody. You take it to Sorrel. Under the Tanners' Bridge. The old rope store. Auntie Sorrel. You'll know her. She's got a face like a burned loaf and a tongue like a razor, and she'll tell you to go away. Don't go away. Show her the lining. Show her her name." Vespera's blue hand closed on Juno's sleeve. "Do you understand me?"
"Yes."
"Say it back."
"If anything happens to you," said Juno slowly, "I take the coat to Sorrel. Under the Tanners' Bridge. The rope store. Not to Corin. Not to anybody." She swallowed. "I show her the lining."
"Good." Vespera let go of her sleeve. "Good girl."
"Is something going to happen?"
Vespera did not answer.
She put the empty tin box back in the space under the floor. She put the board back over it, and pressed it down until it clicked. She stood up, slowly, with one hand on her knee, the way old people stand up, and looked round the blue room in the candlelight.
"Something always happens," she said.
