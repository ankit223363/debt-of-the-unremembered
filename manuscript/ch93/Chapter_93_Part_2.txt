Chapter 93: Blue Hands, Part 2 of 5: Those I Owe
Those I Owe
Later, when the tea was drunk and the children were asleep again, with Pip curled up against Brin's side like a kitten against a dog, Juno got out the book.
She kept it under her pillow. Her pillow was a sack of straw, under the second arch, where she slept now, most nights, instead of at home. Her mother did not like it. Her mother had said so, in the purple-doored room at the back of number fourteen, with her arms folded and the forty-one clocks ticking on every wall. You've a bed here. You've a roof. You've a mother. And Juno had said, They've got nobody to read the book, and her mother had looked at her for a long time and then unfolded her arms and gone to put the kettle on. Which meant yes.
So Juno slept in the crypt, with the book under her straw. And went home every Sunday morning to help her mother wind the clocks.
She brought it out now and sat down on the floor by the brazier, across from Vespera, and held it on her knees.
It was a big book, heavy, with a stiff cover of dark green cloth gone soft at the corners. On the front, in gold letters that were mostly rubbed away, it said HARBOUR TIDE TABLES. That was not what it was. Corin had bought it from a stall on the quay, Gideon said, because it was cheap and had a lot of pages, and then he had filled every one of them with his own writing, in black ink, in big square careful capitals. It was one of his Ledgers. The seventh. He had left it with her when he went north.
She had not been able to read a word of it, then.
She could read some of it now. Not fast. Not all. But she knew her letters, every one of them, from A to the end. Lyra had pointed to each one in the crypt with her finger, before they went north, and said its name, and Juno had said it back. And since then she had been teaching Gideon, at night, by the brazier, with the blue chalk on the floor. He had nine letters now. T, H, E, R, A, I, N, B, W. He wrote them over and over, very carefully, in a row, and then wiped them out with his sleeve and wrote them again.
Vespera was watching her.
"That's his," she said.
"It's the seventh one. He left it here."
"I know which it is. I can see his hand on it from here. He writes like a man building a wall." Vespera held out one blue hand. "Let me see."
Juno hesitated.
She did not mean to. She knew it was all right. Corin had told her, anybody who's on our side. But it was hers. It had been hers for weeks, under her straw. She had read it in the dark with a tallow stub in a tin lid, one word at a time, while everybody slept. It felt strange to hold it out.
She held it out.
Vespera took it. She did not open it at the beginning. She opened it near the back, as if she knew exactly where to go, and turned the pages with one blue finger, slowly, until she came to a page that had a heading underlined twice, very hard.
THOSE I OWE.
Juno knew the page. She knew it better than any page in the book. It was the first one she had ever read all the way through on her own, with no help, in the week after she came back from the fens. It had taken her all of one night.
Vespera read it now. Quickly. Juno watched her eyes go down it. Down the names. NED, with a line through it. GIDEON. H.291 TO 340. BIG GIDEON. SAUL, with a line through it. SILAS. MRS DOYLE. WAT. And others. A great many others. And near the bottom, in letters a little bigger than the rest, as if he had pressed harder:
JUNO. SHE WALKED FOURTEEN MILES ON FROZEN FEET. SHE CARRIED THE LETTER.
Vespera's eyes stopped there.
She looked at it for a long time. Then she looked up at Juno, over the top of the book.
"Fourteen miles," she said.
"About that."
"On frozen feet."
"They weren't frozen when I started."
Vespera's mouth did something at one corner. Not quite a smile.
"No," she said. "They never are." She closed the book, gently, on her finger, so as to keep the place. "Do you know what this is? This page?"
Juno thought about it.
"It's the people he owes," she said. "That's what it says."
"It's what he holds on to," said Vespera. "When everything else goes. When he burns, and burns again, and there's less of him every time." She tapped the cover with one blue finger. "Everything in this book is a thing he might lose. His name. His landlady's name. Where he was born. What he ate on a Tuesday. All of it, gone, a page at a time, if he's a fool. Which he is." She looked at Juno. "But this page, he wrote so that he'd know who to come back for. Do you understand? Not who he is. Who he's for."
Juno did not say anything. She found she could not.
Vespera held the book out to her again.
"Put it back under your straw," she said. "Keep it there. Don't give it to anybody but him. Not even me."
Juno took it. She held it against her chest with both arms.
"Is he—" she said. And stopped. And started again. "You said he's grayer."
Vespera looked at the brazier for a while.
"He burned a great deal," she said. "Out there. Over the Glass. More than anybody should. He had to. There were things that wouldn't stop any other way." She held her blue hands out to the red coals. "It's up his neck now. And over his jaw. To the corner of his mouth." She touched her own face, on the left side, low down, by the lip. "There."
Juno looked at the place. She tried to see it on Corin's face. She could not. She had not seen his face since he went north. She could only see it the way it had been, the night he went, by the brazier, white and thin and tired, with his gloves on.
"What happens," she said, "when it gets to his mouth?"
Vespera did not answer at once.
"Words," she said at last. "It takes words. Not memories. The words for things." She was quiet. "He'll look at a cup and not know to call it a cup. He'll say the thing you drink from. He'll want to say your name and not find it."
"Like the captain," said Juno. "With the spoon."
Vespera's eyebrows went up.
"You noticed that."
"Everybody noticed. He'd say the little one for soup. He'd say the thing you sit on." Juno held the book tighter. "He doesn't know he does it."
"No. You never know." Vespera looked at her. "That's the worst of it. You never know what's gone. Only that people look at you oddly when you talk."
The brazier ticked. Across the crypt, under the last arch, one of the children turned over and said something in its sleep, and Brin put out his big hand without waking and laid it on the blankets, and the child was quiet.
"Is he coming back?" said Juno. "Here?"
"He's coming back here. To you. To this hole." Vespera sniffed. "That's what he said. Saint Bride's first. Gideon's people. And then he'll go and do something stupid, I've no doubt, and I won't be there to stop him."
"Why won't you be there?"
Vespera looked at her.
It was a long look. A sharp one. The kind of look Juno's mother gave a shirt when she was deciding whether it could be mended.
"Because I have a thing to do first," she said. "Tomorrow." She turned her blue hands over, palms up, to the coals. "There's something in my old shop. Under the Dye-Works arches. I left it there twenty years ago, when I went away, because I thought I was never coming back. And I was wrong. And now there's a man on the Hill who calls himself the Archon, and he's declared a Cleansing, and he's sending his gray coats round every arch in the Dye-Works with lamps." She closed her hands. "And if they find what I left, a great many people will be very sorry. Me least of all."
Juno sat very still.
"What is it?" she said.
"A list."
"Of what?"
"Of whom," said Vespera. "That's the right word. Whom. It's what they teach you on the Hill." She sniffed. "Of every Ink-Binder in this city. Every one who was alive twenty years ago, and where they worked, and what they could do. And who taught them. And who they taught." She looked at the fire. "I made it myself. In my own hand. When I was the needle."
"The needle?"
"Never mind that." Vespera's voice went sharp. "The point is, it's there. In my shop. In a place I made for it. And the gray coats are looking. And I need someone who knows the city as it is now, not as it was, to take me there by the back ways, in the daylight, without being seen." She looked at Juno. "Someone who walks fourteen miles on frozen feet."
Juno looked at her for a long moment.
Then she looked down at the book in her arms. At the gold letters, mostly rubbed away. HARBOUR TIDE TABLES.
"I know the back ways," she said.
"I know you do. Gideon told me. On the stair." Vespera's mouth did the thing again at one corner. "He said you know them better than the rats."
"That's not true."
"No?"
"The rats know the drains," said Juno. "I don't go in the drains."
Vespera laughed.
It was a short, rough, surprised sound, like a crow taking off. Across the crypt, Doctor Silas looked up from his table, over the top of his cracked spectacles. Big Gideon's eyes moved. Brin did not wake.
"First light," said Vespera. "Before the lists. Wear something that isn't that coat."
"It's the only coat I've got."
Vespera looked at it. At the brown wool, too long in the arms, with the patch on the elbow.
"Then wear it," she said. "It's a good coat. It's got a lining." And something moved behind her sharp black eyes, very fast, and was gone. "Go to sleep."
Juno did not go to sleep. Not at once.
She lay on her straw under the second arch with the book under her and her coat over her, and listened to the crypt. To the brazier ticking. To the children breathing under the last arch, and Brin's slow deep breath among them, like the sea. And to two voices by the fire, very low. Doctor Silas, and Vespera.
She did not mean to listen. But the crypt was small, and the roof was curved, and the voices came along the curve of it the way voices do in a church, so that a whisper by the brazier could be heard at the far end.
"Six," Vespera was saying. "Out of fifty-two."
"Fifty-one went in the carts. At Midwinter. The House of Rest, the notice said. Parents and guardians need not attend." Silas's voice was dry and tired. "I tore it off the door. Much good it did."
"And these six?"
"These six were hidden. Under the floor of the vestry, where the old priest kept the wine. Mrs. Doyle sat on the trapdoor with her broom across her knees all the time the clerk was in the church. He asked her to stand up and she said her knees wouldn't let her." A pause. "He believed her. He was very young."
Vespera made a small sound. It might have been a laugh.
"And the fifty-one?"
Silas did not answer at once.
"Nobody knows," he said. "Not the House of Rest. There isn't one. I went to the address on the notice. It's a warehouse on the Salt Quay with the windows boarded." Another pause. "The step women say some of the carts went up the hill. Toward the Spire. The girl Juno followed one, a few weeks ago. It went over Lantern Bridge and the gray coats waved it through. The Spire. For the Choir. That's what was painted on the side."
"The Choir." Vespera's voice had gone very quiet. "They still have that, then."
"You know of it?"
"Everybody my age knows of it. Nobody knows anything about it." The brazier ticked. "When I was a girl, it was a few children. Orphans. The quiet ones, the ones that didn't speak, or wouldn't. The Spire took them in and taught them to sing in the loft on Saints' days, and people said what a kindness, and gave money." She was quiet. "I went once. To hear them. My mother took me. It was the most beautiful thing I ever heard in my life. And not one of them looked at anybody. Not once. All the way through. They sang like birds in a cage sing. Because they're made to."
Nobody said anything for a while.
"How many now?" said Vespera.
"I don't know. More carts than there used to be. The step women count them. Win Barrow keeps a tally on the wall of the Ministry yard with a bit of chalk, where the gray coats can't see." Silas breathed out. "She said forty since the autumn. Forty carts. Not forty children. Carts."
Juno lay very still under her coat.
"Somebody should find out," said Silas, "where the jars go. And where the children go. And whether it's the same place."
"You said that before. In your letter. Years ago."
"I've been saying it for years. Nobody's ever gone."
"No," said Vespera. "Nobody ever comes back from going, either."
The brazier settled. A coal fell. Juno heard Silas get up, slowly, his knees cracking, and put another lump of Abner Cole's coal on, and sit down again.
"He'll go," said Silas. "Won't he. When he comes back. Corin. He'll go and find out."
Vespera did not answer for a long time.
"He'll go and find out," she said at last. "And it'll cost him. It always costs him. And he'll come back with less of himself and more of everybody else, and he'll write it all down in one of those books of his so he doesn't forget it, and then he'll forget it anyway." Her voice was rough. "And there's nothing I can do about it but sit in this hole and wait and sew."
"That's not nothing."
"It feels like nothing."
"It always does," said Silas. "Thirty-one years a doctor, Madame. It always feels like nothing. That's how you know you're doing it."
Vespera did not answer that.
Juno lay under her coat and listened to them sit by the fire and not say anything, for a long time. And then she did go to sleep, at last, with the book hard under her cheek and the word Choir going round and round in her head like a tune she could not get rid of.
News from the Steps
They went out in the dark before morning, by the vestry door, and down the two hundred and twelve steps in the snow.
Juno went first, with a stub of candle in a jar to see the ice by. Vespera came after her, in her black cloak with the hem pinned up now so it would not drag, and her big mittens, and a man's knitted cap pulled down to her eyebrows over her ragged gray hair, so that she looked like any old woman going to market. Brin came last. He had his hood up and his sack on his back and the long thing wrapped in sacking over his shoulder, and Juno had asked what it was, and Vespera had said a walking stick, and Brin had said nothing, and Juno had decided not to ask again.
At the bottom of the lane they did not go out into Fish Lane. Juno took them left instead, along the back of the houses, through a gap in a wall where a gate had been once, across a yard full of broken barrels, and out by a passage so narrow that Brin had to go through it sideways, breathing in.
At the end of the passage there was the Coal Street pump.
It stood in the middle of a little square where four lanes met, an iron pump on a stone step, with a lamp over it on a bracket. Not an Ash lamp. An old oil lamp, yellow, which the parish was supposed to fill and never did, so the step women filled it themselves, with oil they bought between them, a penny each, every Saturday. Because they liked to see each other's faces.
They were there now.
Five of them, round the pump, in the yellow light, with their buckets and their brushes and their shawls. They came here every morning before first light, before they went off to their steps, to fill their buckets and talk. Juno had known them all her life.
Big Maud Croft, who did the Chancery steps, and was as wide as a door, with arms like a smith's and a laugh you could hear on the other side of the river. Win Barrow, small and sharp, with a voice like a gull's and a gull's way of turning her head to look at you with one eye. The Pole sisters, Bess and Hattie, who did the Exchange steps between them and finished each other's sentences so often that nobody had heard either of them say a whole one in years. And Granny Sedge, who was the oldest, and had done the Bank steps every morning for fifty years, and was bent nearly double, and leaned on her brush like a stick, and saw everything.
They stopped talking when Juno came out of the passage.
Then they saw who it was, and Big Maud laughed, and the talking started again. Then they saw who was behind her, and it stopped again. All at once. Like a door shutting.
Five faces in the yellow lamplight. Looking at the small old woman in the man's cap. And the huge hooded man behind her.
"Morning," said Juno.
Nobody answered.
Granny Sedge straightened up. It took her a long time. She put both hands on top of her brush and pushed, and her bent back came up, slowly, a little at a time, until she was looking at Vespera's face from under the edge of her own black bonnet. She looked at it for a long time. Then she looked down at the mittens.
"Take them off," she said.
Vespera did not move.
"I'm old," said Granny Sedge. "I'm older than you. I've done the Bank steps fifty years, I've seen every face that ever went up them and most that came down. Take your mittens off, Missus, and let me see."
Vespera looked at her. Then, slowly, she pulled off one mitten.
The blue hand came out into the yellow light.
Nobody said anything. Win Barrow drew in her breath, sharp, through her teeth.
"Well," said Granny Sedge at last. Very quietly. "Well, well." She leaned on her brush again, and her back went down. "Sorrel always said you'd come back."
Vespera's face did not change. But Juno, standing next to her, saw her blue hand close into a fist.
"Is she living?" said Vespera.
"She's living. Under the Tanners' Bridge, in the old rope store. Same as ever. Same scowl." Granny Sedge sniffed. "Older. We're all older."
"Is she on a list?"
The step women looked at each other.
"Not yet," said Big Maud. Her laugh had gone. "Not by name. But they've been round the Tanners' Bridge twice this week with lamps. In the arches. Looking." She folded her great arms. "They've been everywhere. Every arch on the river. That's what we were talking about. Before you came."
"Saturday it was the Dye-Works," said Win Barrow, in her gull's voice. "Gray coats. A dozen. With lamps. Before it was light. They went down the whole row from the river end, banging on every grille."
"They had Marjory Kell out," said Bess Pole.
"And her Tom," said Hattie.
"He wouldn't let go of the lamp," said Bess.
"They had to take it off him on the stair," said Hattie.
"He cried," said Bess. "All the way down Ropewalk Lane."
"Like a baby," said Hattie.
They stopped. They looked at each other. Neither of them said anything else.
Vespera stood very still.
"Marjory," she said. "Marjory Kell. Short. Strong. Does the sailors."
"Anchors and mermaids," said Big Maud. "Yes."
"I taught her," said Vespera. "She was fifteen. She had a hand like a bricklayer's and she cried every time she pricked herself." Her voice had gone very flat. "Where did they take her?"
"The Bank," said Granny Sedge. "Down the river doors. To the pens." She looked across the square, as if she could see the Bank from here, through the houses, white on its hill. "They're filling up, the pens. Seventy, I heard. Eighty. Waiting for the weighing. Sunday, they say. The Order of Need doesn't keep Sundays." She sniffed. "Nothing keeps Sundays anymore."
"And the lists," said Win Barrow. "Every day now. Not just Thursdays. Every day."
"How do you know?"
"I do the Ministry back steps. The yard ones." Win turned her head and looked at Vespera with one eye, like a gull. "I see the boy go out with the case. First bell. Every morning. Sundays too." She paused. "It's not the boy anymore. It's a sergeant. Red face. He looks like a man who's had bad news and can't think who to tell."
Granny Sedge leaned on her brush.
"And the Auditor sits up all night," she said. "At the Bank. Fourth Desk. Up at the top, at the river end. I see her lamp. Every night this week, I come up the Bank steps at first light and her lamp's still lit." She was quiet. "She never used to. Six years I've watched that window. She used to go to bed like a decent body."
Juno did not know who the Auditor was. Not really. A name, on papers. A woman at a window, high up, in a green coat. She had seen her once, from the Bank steps, a long way off. Corin had said something about her, once, and then stopped, as if he had said something he did not mean to.
Vespera had gone very still again. Juno saw her look across the square, the way Granny Sedge had looked. Toward the Bank. As if she could see it.
Then she pulled her mitten back on.
"And the gray man?" she said.
The step women looked at each other again.
"Back," said Big Maud.
"Monday," said Win.
"A gray carriage," said Bess.
"No crest on the door," said Hattie.
"Came over Lantern Bridge," said Bess, "at first light, from the north road. Muddy to the axles. Two horses. Lathered."
"Went straight up the Hill," said Hattie. "Not to the Ministry."
"Up the Hill," said Bess, "to the Archon's house."
"There was a girl with him," said Win Barrow. "In the carriage. I saw her face at the window when they went past the bottom of the Ministry steps. Young. Very white. Like she'd been crying a week and stopped."
Juno felt something go cold in her.
She knew that girl. She did not know her, but she knew of her. A girl in a gray coat, with Kross, all the way north and back. Wren. Corin had said her name, once, the night before he went, in a voice Juno had not understood.
Vespera did not say anything for a long time.
Then she said, "Thank you," to the step women. Quite politely. As if they had told her the time.
And she turned to Juno.
"The Dye-Works," she said. "Now. The back way. The quickest one."
"They've already been," said Big Maud. "Saturday. The whole row. They had lamps in every—"
"They had lamps in every arch," said Vespera. "I know. I heard you." She looked at Big Maud. "Did they have lamps in the last one? By the old indigo vats? The one with the iron grille, and the brick gone blue up to here?"
Big Maud opened her mouth. Then shut it.
"No," said Win Barrow slowly. "No. They didn't. I heard Marjory's girl say. They went down the row and they got to the last one and it was shut up, with a padlock on the grille all rusted solid, and they banged on it, and nobody came, and the sergeant said it hadn't been opened in twenty years and they'd come back with a smith." She turned her head and looked at Vespera with one eye. "They said they'd come back."
"Then we'd better be quick," said Vespera.
She turned and walked out of the yellow lamplight, toward the far lane, the one that went down to the river. Brin went after her. He did not say anything. But as he went past the pump he stopped, and turned his hooded head, and looked at the five step women for a moment, the way he had looked at the children in the crypt.
And he bowed. Slowly. From the waist. The way Juno had once seen a gentleman on the Hill bow to a duchess.
Then he went on after Vespera.
The step women stood round the pump in the yellow light and stared after him.
"Well," said Big Maud Croft, at last. "Saints keep us."
"Go on, girl," said Granny Sedge to Juno. "Go on with them. And Juno."
Juno stopped.
"Tell Sorrel," said Granny Sedge, "if you see her. Tell her the blue one's back. Tell her I said so." She leaned on her brush. "She'll know what to do. She always did."
Juno nodded. And ran.
