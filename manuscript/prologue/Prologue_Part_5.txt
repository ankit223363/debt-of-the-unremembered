Prologue: The Night Oakhaven Forgot, Part 5 of 5: The Engines Wake
The Engines Wake
The sun was coming up behind it.
The ridge stood black against a sky of pale gold and rose, high and bare and long, running the whole length of the western side of the valley. Along its top, Kaelen could see the column. Three hundred gray coats, minus eleven, standing in long straight rows, small as toy soldiers. And the black carriages. And the twelve gray wagons with their barred doors, standing empty in a line along the road. Waiting to be filled. And the three tall wooden towers, with the great crystals on top of them.
The crystals were glowing.
They had been faint all night. Three small points of cold blue, like distant stars. Now they were brighter. Much brighter. As Kaelen watched, they went from pale blue to deep blue to a hard, brilliant, electric blue that made his eyes water, and he heard them begin to hum.
It was a low sound. Lower than anything he had ever heard. He did not hear it with his ears. He heard it in his teeth, and in his chest, and in the cobbles under his boots. It went down into the ground and came up again through the soles of his feet. The water in the well beside him began to shiver. Tiny rings spread out across its dark surface, one inside another, over and over.
Above him, in the great oak, the little wooden birds had begun to spin.
"Sergeant," said Tobin. His voice was very quiet. "Sergeant, what happens now?"
Dunmore was watching the crystals. His old face was gray.
"Now they wait," he said, "until the rebels are all down on the valley floor. Until nobody can run back over the hills. And then they let it go."
"Let what go?"
Dunmore did not answer.
On the far side of the valley, the long ragged line of lights had reached the bottom of the hills. They were in the fields now. Coming across the green, wet, shining grass, through the wildflowers, toward the river and the village, singing. Kaelen could hear the song now. He did not know the words. But he knew the tune. Every child in Vaelen knew it. It was the song about the fisherman who caught the moon in his net and had to let it go.
And on the ridge, the three crystals flared.
The Wave
It was not light.
That was the first thing Kaelen understood about it, and the strangest. It came out of the three crystals all at once, in one slow, silent pulse, and it should have been light, because it came from something so bright. But it was not. It was gray.
A wall of gray. Thin and wide and soft, like a sheet of rain seen from very far away. It came off the top of the ridge and rolled down the slope toward the valley floor, and it was taller than the towers, taller than the trees, so tall that Kaelen could not see the top of it. It did not roar. It did not crash. It made no sound at all. It simply came, smooth and steady and quiet, like a tide coming in over flat sand.
And where it passed, the world went still.
Kaelen saw it reach the first field at the bottom of the ridge. A field of tall golden barley, moving in the morning wind. The gray wave passed over it. The barley did not fall down. It did not burn. It went on standing exactly where it was. But the wind stopped moving it. And the gold went out of it. Not the color. Something else. Something Kaelen could not name. It was as if the barley had forgotten that it was barley.
The wave reached a farmhouse. A dog in the yard. A man in the doorway, with a pike in his hand, who had been about to run toward the village. The gray passed over him.
He stopped. He stood in his doorway. He looked down at the pike in his hand, puzzled, and let it fall. Then he sat down slowly on his own doorstep and looked at the morning with a small, calm, gentle smile.
The dog lay down beside him and put its head on its paws.
"No," said Bettany. "No, no, no—"
The wave rolled on. Across the fields. Across the low stone walls. Toward the river. Toward the village. Toward the long ragged line of rebels coming across the valley floor from the other side, who had seen it now, and stopped singing, and were standing in the wet grass with their torches and their banners, staring at the soft gray wall coming toward them.
Some of them turned to run. It was too late. The hills were a mile behind them. The wave was moving faster than a horse.
It would reach the village in less than a minute.
Kaelen looked around him.
At the square. At the gray cobbles and the round stone well and the great old oak with its hundreds of spinning wooden birds. At the doors standing open all around, and the people in them, still looking at the far hills, still smiling, still not understanding what was coming toward them from behind. At the old man in the doorway of the house with the painted kettle. At the miller with his freckled son on his shoulders. At Tobin, beside him, with his round red face gone white and his carrot hair sticking up.
And down the lane, at the very end, by the water, at the last house. With its red door standing open. And a woman sitting on the bank below it, at the edge of the river, in a long gray dress, with her bare feet in the water, looking at the morning with a gentle, empty smile.
Corin Vane. Last house before the water.
My sister is Elara. She's four. She has a hole in her teeth.
Remember it for me.
Kaelen turned to Sergeant Dunmore.
"How big," he said, "is the biggest barrier anyone has ever made?"
Dunmore looked at him. And Kaelen saw in the old man's face that he knew. That he had known since the doorway of the harvester's wagon. Since the river. Since the moment on the hill road when a young soldier stopped to watch two children tickling trout.
"Big enough to cover a city," said Dunmore. "Once. Long ago. They say it cost the mage who made it everything he had. Every memory. Every face. Every word. His own name." He looked at the gray wall rolling toward them across the fields. "They say there was nothing left of him at all, after. Just a body that walked."
Kaelen nodded.
He was not frightened. He noticed that. He would have expected to be frightened. Instead, he felt very calm and very clear, the way the air feels after a storm, as if everything in the world had been washed and set down in its proper place.
"Ash," said Tobin. "Ash, what are you—"
Kaelen walked to the great oak in the middle of the square. He put his back against its trunk, against the rough, deep, living bark. Above him, the little wooden birds were spinning wildly on their strings.
He closed his eyes.
And for the last time in his life, Kaelen Ashford began to whistle.
What He Gave
He started with the small things, because that was how his mother had taught him to start a hard job. Start with what you can carry, love. The heavy things will wait.
The taste of bread went first.
He reached inside himself and found it, the way you find a coin in your pocket without looking. The warm brown taste of the bread in Maren Vane's kitchen. And under it, older, the taste of his mother's bread on a cold morning in two rooms above a chandler's shop, with the steam coming off it and butter melting into it in yellow pools. He held it for a moment. Then he burned it.
It did not hurt. That surprised him. It was like blowing out a candle. A small soft puff, inside him, and then a little warmth, and then the warmth went out of him, out through his back, into the bark of the oak.
And the oak lit up.
A faint gold glow ran up through the great trunk behind him, along the lines of the bark, up into the branches, out to the tips of the twigs. The little wooden birds caught it. For one moment they shone like tiny lamps, hundreds of them, all over the tree.
It was not enough. He knew it was not enough. A taste was nothing. A taste was the smallest spell there was. It would not stop a raindrop.
He kept going.
The color green. He burned it. The green of this valley, the wet shining green he had seen from the hill road with the sun on it, that had stopped his feet. Gone. The color blue. The color of his mother's good shawl. Gone. The smell of the river. The feel of cold water on his hands at the pump. The sound of rain on a slate roof. Gone. Gone. Gone.
The gold glow in the oak grew brighter. It poured out of the tree now, down through its roots, into the cobbles of the square, and spread across them in a ring. A wide gold ring of light on the gray stones, around the well, around the oak, around the eleven soldiers.
He heard Tobin shouting somewhere. He did not listen. He could not listen. He had to keep going.
The gray wave was at the river.
Words
He burned the rivers of Vaelen.
All of them. Every name he had ever learned, in the little school on his street in Veridia, from an old teacher with chalk on her sleeves. The Silverrun. The Weep. The long brown Mother Ash, that ran through the capital and out to the sea. He had never seen most of them. He had only known their names. He let them go.
He burned the kings. Every name of every king and queen of Vaelen that he had ever been made to learn by heart, in order, standing up by his desk with his hands behind his back. He let them all go in one long rush, a whole line of crowned people walking out of his head and into the dark, one after another, and he did not miss them.
He burned his numbers. His letters. The long words and the short ones. Window. Kettle. Pike. Bridge. He felt them go out of him like birds leaving a tree at dusk, all at once, in a great soft rushing cloud.
The gold ring on the cobbles rose up.
It rose off the stones like mist off a pond at dawn. A thin wall of gold light, curving upward, all the way around the square. Then higher. Higher than the houses. Higher than the oak. It curved up over his head, and he felt it close, far above him, like a great gold bowl turned upside down. A dome.
It was not big enough. It covered the square, and the lanes nearest the square, and nothing else. The mill was outside it. The bridge. The schoolhouse on its rise. The fields. The six thousand rebels standing in the wet grass with their torches and their banners, not singing anymore. The last house before the water.
He kept going.
The Hard Things
He burned how to march. How to stand at attention. How to hold a pike. Eighteen months of drills, in the rain, in the mud, with a sergeant shouting in his ear. He was glad to see it go.
He burned how to swim. How to tie his boots. How to write his name. How to button a coat. How to use a short sword, the two times he had used it, and what he had done with it both times.
He burned how to whistle.
That one was hard. That one he held for a long time, standing with his back against the bark, with the gold light pouring out of him and the gray wall coming up the bank of the river toward the first houses. He could feel the tune still in his mouth. The fisherman and the moon. The high part, where the moon begs to go home. You whistle the way other people breathe, my love. He held it the way you hold your breath underwater.
Then he let it go.
The tune stopped. In the middle of a note. He felt his lips go still, and his breath go out of him, and he could not remember why his mouth had been shaped that way.
The dome leaped outward.
It went out from the square like a wave going the other way, a great curved wall of gold racing out across the village in every direction. It went over the houses. Over the lanes. Over the mill and the bridge and the schoolhouse with its ringing bell. It went over the gardens and the apple trees and the low stone walls. Over the last house before the water, and the woman sitting on the bank below it with her feet in the river. It went out across the fields, faster than the gray wave was coming, out and out, over the wet grass and the wildflowers, toward the long ragged line of rebels.
It was still not big enough. He could feel where its edge was. Just short of them. Just short of the six thousand.
The gray wave reached the edge of the gold.
Faces
He heard it hit.
Not a crash. Not a roar. A sound like a great bell, struck once, very far away, deep under the earth. The whole dome shook. He felt it in his bones, in his teeth, in the roots of the oak under his feet. And he felt the gray pressing on it. Leaning. Pushing. Trying to come in. Cold and soft and endless, like the sea pressing on a wall of sand.
The gold began to bend.
He knew then that he would have to give the rest. Not the small things. Not the skills. Him.
He began with the people he loved least, because that was the only way he could begin at all.
The recruiting officer on his street, with his drum and his smile. Gone. The young captain with the clean boots. Gone. The soldier from the bridge whose name he had never been sure of. The Hollow brothers. Bettany with her scar. Gone.
The gold stopped bending. It held.
The old teacher with chalk on her sleeves. The chandler who owned the shop below his mother's rooms and gave him a sugar stick every year on his birthday. The girl in the next street who had kissed him once behind the washhouse when he was fourteen and then laughed and run away. Gone.
The dome began to push back.
Old Dunmore. His walnut face. His long gray mustache. You stupid, stupid boy. Gone.
The dome went out. Further. He felt its edge race forward across the wet grass, past the first of the rebels, over them, over the torches and the pitchforks and the long banners with their two painted words, over the mages with their little floating lights.
Tobin.
He held Tobin for a long time. Tobin Reed, with his carrot hair and his crooked nose, who smelled of flour. Who had carried the other end of the boat in his bare feet without asking why. Where to? He could hear Tobin now, somewhere, very close, shouting his name. Ash. Ash. Kaelen, stop, please, stop—
He let Tobin go.
The gold swept out over the last of the six thousand. Over the very edge of the far hills, where the black pines began. It covered the whole valley floor, from ridge to ridge, from end to end. A vast curved bowl of gold, shining in the morning sun, with the gray pressing on the outside of it everywhere, everywhere, looking for a way in and not finding one.
There was only one thing left that was big enough. He had known all along that it would come to this.
His mother.
The Last Ones
He saw her very clearly. Clearer than he had seen her in eighteen months. Standing at the door of their two rooms above the chandler's shop, the morning he left with the recruiting officer, with her hands in her apron and flour on her cheek, trying not to cry. Write to me. Every week. And for heaven's sake, get your hair cut. He saw the gray coming into her hair at the front, the way it had that last year. He saw her hands, red from the washing. He heard her humming, at the stove, the tune about the fisherman and the moon.
You'll whistle at your own funeral.
He gave her to the light.
And the dome did not just hold. It threw.
All at once, all over, the great gold bowl pushed outward with everything he had given it, and the gray wave, pressing on it from every side, was thrown back. Back across the fields. Back up the slopes. Back the way it had come. Back up the long bare side of the western ridge, faster than it had come down, so fast that the grass bent flat in front of it.
Toward the three towers. Toward the long rows of gray coats standing on the ridge top. Toward the black carriages and the barred wagons and the three great crystals blazing blue.
He did not see what happened when it reached them. He was too far gone by then to see anything.
But he heard it. Three sounds, one after another, high and sharp and terrible, like enormous glasses breaking. The crystals. Shattering.
And after that, from the ridge, a silence. A great, wide, empty silence. The silence of nearly three hundred men who had forgotten, all in the same moment, what they were doing there.
There was very little left in him now.
He could feel the bottom of himself, the way you feel the bottom of a well with a bucket. A few things. Small and hard and bright, like stones.
His own name. He gave it. Kaelen Ashford. It went out of him like a breath.
And one more.
A green slope above a river. A pool with flat gray stones around it, and willows, and dragonflies. A serious small boy with his sleeves rolled up, holding a trout. Looking up the hill at a soldier on the road, measuring, deciding.
Lifting one wet hand. And waving.
I see you. Hello. You are a person, and I am a person, and here we both are.
Remember it for me.
He tried. He held on to it with everything that was left. With hands that no longer knew how to hold. With a mind that no longer had words for hold, or boy, or promise. He held on to it as the last of the light went out of him and into the great gold dome over the valley, and for a moment, one moment, he thought he could keep it.
Then it went too.
Not all of it. Not quite. Something stayed. Too small to have a name. Too small to be a memory. A flicker. Like the last spark in a fire that has gone to ash, that you would never see unless you were looking for it, glowing very faintly, very deep down, where nothing else was left.
And then there was nothing at all.
The Morning After
The dome hung over the valley for a long time.
The people inside it could see it. They stood in their doorways and their gardens and their wet green fields and looked up at it, a vast soft curve of gold arching over them from ridge to ridge, with the morning sun shining through it so that the whole world under it was the color of honey. Some of them were weeping. Some of them were on their knees. The six thousand rebels stood in the grass with their torches burning pale in the gold light and their banners hanging still, and did not move, and did not speak.
Then, slowly, from the top down, like frost melting off a window, the dome began to fade.
It was gone by midday.
Under the great oak in the middle of the square, a young man was standing with his back against the trunk.
He was very still. He did not seem to be breathing, though he was. His gray coat hung open. His boots were unlaced, as if he had forgotten what laces were for. His hands hung at his sides, palms out.
And his hair, which had been dark that morning, was white. Completely white. White as the ash in a cold hearth.
Tobin Reed knelt in front of him on the cobbles. His round face was swollen with crying. He was holding the young man's hands in both of his and saying his name, over and over, the same two sounds, the way you call a dog that has run off into a field.
"Ash. Ash. Kaelen. Kaelen, it's me. It's Tobin. Ash, please."
The young man looked down at him.
His eyes were open. They were pale gray, paler than they had been, so pale they were almost white. And there was nothing in them. Not grief. Not fear. Not even puzzlement. Nothing. As if they were painted on.
Then he took his hands out of Tobin's. Not unkindly. The way you take your hand out of a bush that has caught on your sleeve. And he turned, and walked away across the square, past the well, past the empty bench, past the old man in the doorway of the house with the painted kettle, who stepped back into his house to let him pass and did not take his eyes off him.
He walked over the bridge. Out of the village. Across the wet fields, between the rebels, who parted to let him through and stared after him in silence. He walked to the far hills, and up them, into the black pines. He did not look back.
Nobody followed him. Nobody dared.
Tobin Reed sat on the cobbles under the oak for the rest of the day. When the sun went down, he got up, and walked down the river lane to the last house before the water, where a woman in a long gray dress was still sitting on the bank with her feet in the river, smiling at the evening, and he helped her up, gently, and took her inside, and lit the stove.
He stayed in Oakhaven for the rest of his life.
The Ridge
On the top of the western ridge, among the broken towers and the shattered blue glass and the long rows of gray-coated men sitting quietly in the grass, smiling at nothing, one man was still standing.
Aurelian Thorne had been inside his carriage when the wave came back. He did not know why that had saved him. Perhaps the carriage's dark glass. Perhaps the little blue gemstone his father had made him wear on a chain around his neck since he was a boy, for luck, which had cracked straight across. He did not know. He only knew that he had stepped out of the carriage into a silence like the end of the world, and seen what lay along the ridge, and been sick in the grass.
Now he stood at the edge of the slope with a brass spyglass to his eye, looking down into the valley. At the gold dome fading over the village. At the small white-haired figure walking away across the fields, between the rebels, toward the far hills.
He watched it for a long time.
Behind him, a voice said quietly, "Remarkable."
Valerius stood beside his carriage. He was untouched. His plain dark coat was not even creased. There were four gemstones in his hands, large ones, blue ones, and all four were cracked and dark and smoking faintly, as if they had been very hot. He let them fall in the grass.
"Remarkable," he said again. He was watching the white-haired figure too. His soft gray eyes were very bright. "A whole man. Every scrap. To save one village." He shook his head slowly. "And all it bought them was a morning."
Aurelian Thorne lowered the spyglass.
"The war is over," he said. His voice was hoarse. "The engines are gone. Our men are gone. The rebels have their valley. It's over."
"Yes," said Valerius. "It is. And now, Aurelian, it will never have happened." He turned to his carriage. "Write your report. All of it. Every detail. And then give it to me, and I will put it somewhere safe, and in a year there will not be a man alive in Vaelen who remembers there was ever a war in this valley at all."
He climbed into the carriage. The door closed with a soft click.
Aurelian Thorne stood alone at the edge of the ridge. He did not write a report. Not then. He put the spyglass to his eye one more time, and watched the small white figure until it reached the black pines on the far hills and was gone.
Then he closed his eyes, and did something that his father had taught him to do as a boy, in case he ever saw something the Ministry would not want him to keep. He took the whole morning, every moment of it, and folded it up very small, and put it away very deep, in a place where no harvester would ever think to look.
Witness, he thought. K.
The Hill Road
Far away, on the hill road, on the crest of the last hill before the valley dropped out of sight behind the pines, a small dark wooden wagon with a curved roof had stopped.
Its driver had stopped it to rest the horse. Or perhaps to look back. Linus Hale sat on the driver's bench with the reins slack in his blue-stained hands, and his round spectacles pushed up on his forehead, and he looked back down at the valley, at the great gold light fading over it, and his tired face was wet.
Inside the wagon, a boy was kneeling on a narrow bed, looking out of the small back window.
He did not know what he was looking at. He did not know where he was, or who the man on the driver's bench was, or how he had come to be in this small wooden room full of jars. He did not know his own name. He only knew that far away, down in a green valley he had never seen, there was a light. A soft, enormous, golden light, like the inside of a honey jar with the sun behind it.
And for some reason he did not understand, it made him want to cry.
He did not cry. He looked at it for a long time, with his serious dark eyes.
Then, slowly, without knowing why, the boy lifted one hand, and waved.
The wagon began to move again. The pines closed in. And the light in the valley went out.
In the morning, the Ministry began to forget.
It took them a year.
