Chapter 82: Malakor's Journal, Part 4 of 5: The List
The List
In the afternoon the light changed.
Corin noticed it before anybody said anything. The gray square on the table had moved off the map, nearly. It lay now along the right-hand edge, over the dark green of the eastern hills, and it was not gray anymore. It had gone faintly gold. The dust turning in it had gone gold too. Somewhere far above, he thought, on the black glass of the Eye, the sun had come round and was looking down the shaft.
Lyra had read for a long time. Her voice had begun to go rough at the edges, like a rope that has been pulled too often over the same stone. Vespera had made her stop twice and drink. Kestrel had gone up the stair and come back. Oren had not moved from the doorway at all.
And Corin had sat with his eyes shut, and listened, and let the old man's life go into him through her voice, one page at a time. The room. The rods. The years. Students who came and went. He could not hold all of it. He knew he could not. Some of it was already going, sliding off him like water off a roof. But some of it stayed.
Then Lyra turned a page, and did not start reading.
Corin opened his eyes.
She was looking at the page with her head on one side. Her finger was not under a line. It was at the top of the page, at the left, and it was not moving.
"What is it?" he said.
"A list."
He leaned over. Not close. Just enough to see.
It was a list. Names, going down the page in a column, one under the other, in the hard square upright letters. And beside each name, a little way to the right, a few words. A note. Some of the notes were short. Some ran on over two lines.
At the top of the page, in bigger letters, it said: THOSE I TAUGHT.
Corin looked at that for a long time.
He knew those words. Not those words. Words like them. In the Ledger. In his own crooked capitals, on a page near the back of the green book, and on a page in the one before that, which he did not have anymore. THOSE I OWE. A list of names. Old Jory. Bramble. Ned. Hask. Arran.
He did not say anything. He did not need to. He felt the old man behind his ear go very still.
"Read it," he said.
Lyra read it.
She read the names one by one, and the notes beside them, and her voice was quite flat, the way you read a list in a shop. And the names were strangers' names, and meant nothing to Corin. But the notes were not strangers. The notes were Malakor. Every one of them sounded exactly like him.
"Garrow," Lyra read. "Clever. Quick. Liked it. Rose very high. Wore silver on his shoulders. It was he who met me on the road.
"Sallis. Slow. Good hands. Bad heart. Drank.
"Mother Imber. The only woman they let me teach, and the best of the lot. Could take a thing so softly that the man would thank her and not know why. Went home to her village and did not come back. I hope she is still there. I hope she has forgotten everything I showed her.
"Fenn and Fallow. Brothers. Fought like dogs. Fenn died of it. Of the work. I mean that he took too much and it filled him up and he went out of himself one night and never came back. Fallow sat with him. It is the only kind thing I ever saw Fallow do.
"Hollis the elder. Good with jars. Taught his son. I am sorry for that."
Lyra stopped there for a moment.
"Hollis," she said.
Corin did not know why she said it like that. He looked at her. She shook her head a little, as if to say later, and went on.
There were many more names. Corin lost count of them. He did not try to count. He listened to the notes. Lazy. Kind to dogs. Married a fishwife and left the work, good. Cruel. Very good. Very cruel. Do not let him near children. Afraid of the dark, and so am I. Cried every night for a year and then stopped crying, and I do not know which was worse. Talked too much. Never talked at all. Took his own life on the river stairs in his ninth year of the work. I should have seen.
The gold square of light slid a little farther along the edge of the table.
And then, near the bottom of the page, Lyra read a name, and stopped, and Corin heard her breath catch.
"Corvell," she read.
Corin sat very still.
He knew that name. Not the way he knew Lyra's voice. A different way. A small way, far back. As if the name were a room he had been in once, as a child, and could not remember, but whose smell he would know again if he walked into it. Corvell. Master Corvell. The Harvesting rooms at the Ministry, at the back, on the third floor. Old Corvell's rooms, somebody had said once. Toby. Toby with his red hair and his freckles and his bun.
Corvell had taught Linus. Linus had taught him.
"What does it say?" he said. "Beside it."
Lyra looked at it. She read it slowly.
"Kind," she read. "Careful. Too soft for this work."
That was all.
Three words, and then three more. Corin looked at them over Lyra's shoulder. The hard square letters. They were not pressed as hard as the others. He saw that. The pen had gone more lightly on the paper, as if the hand holding it had been tired, or as if it had not wanted to hurt the page.
He was, said Malakor.
Very quietly. Behind his ear.
He was all of those. He was the last good one. The only good one, if you want the truth, after Imber. He came to me late. I was old. I'd stopped caring. And then this boy came down the stair with his notebook and his careful hands and asked me why. Nobody had asked me why in thirty years. Why you take it slowly. Why the rod turns to the right. Why it hurts them less if you say their name.
He was too soft for it. I told him so. I told him to go and be a clerk. He wouldn't.
A long pause.
It killed him in the end. You know that. You were there.
Corin did not know that. He did not remember it. But he felt the floor of himself tilt, very slightly, like the deck of a boat.
He did not tell anybody what the old man had said. He did not think he could.
"Is that the last?" said Vespera. She had leaned forward over the table from the far side, with both her blue hands on her stick, to see. "Corvell. Is that the end of it?"
Lyra did not answer.
She was looking at the place under Corvell's name. Corin looked too.
There was one more line.
Or there had been. It was not a line now. It was a scar. Somebody had written a name there, under Corvell, in the same hard square letters, and a note beside it. And then somebody had scratched it out.
Not crossed it out. Not drawn a line through it, the way you would cross out a word you had spelled wrong. Scratched it. Gone over it and over it and over it with the pen, back and forth, so hard and so many times that the ink had soaked into the paper in a black clot, and the paper had torn, and there was a hole. A ragged hole, the length of a finger, right through the page. Through the hole you could see the black writing on the page underneath.
The note beside it had been scratched out too. All of it. Nothing left. Only black.
But not the first letter of the name.
The pen had begun its scratching just after the first letter. As if the hand had written the whole name, and looked at it, and then started to tear it out from the second letter on, and had not been able to make itself go back for the first. And the first letter was still there. At the start of the black hole. A tall square capital, clean, in the brown ink. Two straight lines, coming down to meet at the bottom.
V.
Lyra put her finger on the page beside the hole. Not on it. Beside it.
Nobody said anything.
Corin looked at the V.
He thought of his own name. Inked on his chest under his collarbone, where he could not read it. Your name is Corin Vane. Vane. V.
He felt Lyra think it too. He did not have to look at her. He felt her go still beside him, and he knew.
"Is it me?" he said.
He said it out loud. To the room. But he was not asking the room.
Behind his ear, there was a silence so deep that for a moment he thought the old man had gone.
Then: No.
One word. Hard. Like a door shutting.
No, boy. Not you. You were never mine to teach. You were Linus's. And Linus made you kind, and nobody steals kindness, and you'd never in your life have been on this page.
"Then who?"
Never you mind who.
"You scratched it out."
I did.
"Why?"
A long, long silence.
Because I taught him, said Malakor. Last of all. After Corvell. When I was very old and very tired. I taught him everything. Everything I knew. More than I taught Corvell, more than Garrow, more than any of them. Because he was the best I ever had, and he asked, and I was proud. And then I found out what he did with it.
And I couldn't make myself write it down. What he did. I tried. I sat with the pen in my hand for a whole night. In the end I took it out. A pause. The name. Off the page. That was all I could do.
Don't ask me again, boy. I'll not tell you. Not here. Not yet.
Corin sat with his hands on his knees.
Then he leaned forward, slowly, over the table, and put out his left hand, and touched the hole in the page with the tip of one black finger.
He could not feel it. He knew he could not. His fingertips had been dead for weeks. But he put his finger into the hole, and through it, and felt nothing, and looked at his own black fingertip poking up through the paper into the gold light from under the page.
Then he took it out.
"He says it isn't me," he said. "He says it's someone he taught. After Corvell. The last. He won't say who." He looked at Lyra. "He says not yet."
Lyra looked at the V.
She did not ask the question that was in her face. Corin saw her not ask it. She folded her hands together on the edge of the table and looked at the V, and then she looked at him, and said only:
"Then we'll ask him again. Later."
You'll not get anything out of me, said Malakor.
"He says we won't get anything out of him," said Corin.
"Then we'll ask him again anyway," said Lyra.
And behind his ear, for the first time all day, the old man laughed. Short and dry and surprised. The laugh of a man who has been answered back by somebody he did not expect.
Folded Shut
There were no more names after the V.
Lyra turned the page. She did it slowly, with two fingers, holding it by the corner, because the hole had made the page weak and she was afraid it would tear the rest of the way.
The next page was not a page.
Corin did not understand at first what he was looking at. It looked like a page. It was the right color, the old gray-brown of the rest. It was in the right place, sewn into the spine with the others. But it was not flat. It stood up off the book a little, the way the pages had stood up when he first unwrapped it by the fire, swollen. And when Lyra turned it, it did not turn like a page. It turned stiffly, all in one piece, like a board.
And it was small.
Not short. Small. The pages before it had been the size of the book. This one was the size of the palm of a hand. It was a page, sewn in at the spine like all the others, but it had been folded. Folded over and over on itself, down and down, from the size of a whole page to the size of a palm, so many times that it had gone thick and hard, like a little brick of paper. The folds were so sharp that they shone.
Lyra put her fingers on it.
She did not try to open it. Corin saw that. She only touched it, the way you would touch something sleeping. And then she took her fingers away, quickly, as if it had been hot.
"Oh," she said.
She turned the next page. It was the same. Folded small, hard, tight. And the next. And the next. One after another, every page after the list, all the way to the back of the book, folded down into themselves. Each one a little square brick of paper on its sewn edge, the size of a palm, or less. Some were smaller. Some, near the very back, were smaller than a coin.
Corin looked at them. At the little squares, one behind another, like a row of shut doors.
He had seen a square like that before.
He knew he had. Not in his own memory. In the Ledger. A page he had read every morning without knowing he had read it. A small square of folded paper, the size of a thumbnail, under the silver clasp of a box. A square that a man had folded his own death into, under a table, in a study, with the rain on the windows. And had let it slip into the seat of a chair, like a fish going under a boat.
And Lyra's hand. Lyra's right hand, closing a dying man's fingers round a folded square in a white room. Little finger first. Thumb last.
He looked at Lyra.
She was looking at the folded pages. And her face had gone the color of the whitewash on the walls.
"That's my mother's," she said. Very low. "That's how she did it. That's her art. Fold it small."
"Can you open it?" said Vespera.
Lyra put her good hand on the first of the folded pages. She took hold of one corner of it with her fingertips, very gently, and tried to lift it. To unfold the first fold.
It did not move.
It was not stuck. Corin could see that. There was nothing holding it. No glue. No wax. It was only paper, folded. But when Lyra pulled on the corner, it did not come up. Her fingers slid off it. As if the paper were greased. As if it were made of glass. She tried again, harder, with her nails, and her nails slid off too, and left no mark.
She tried a third time.
Then she stopped, and took her hand away, and held it in her lap.
"No," she said. "It won't. It won't open for me." She looked down at her hand. "It wouldn't. It's not mine to open. You pay with the hand you'd use to unfold it. That's the rule. Whoever folded it is the only one who can open it."
Corin turned his head inward.
Malakor.
Nothing.
Malakor. What is it? What did you fold?
A long silence. Longer than any silence all day. Corin waited. The gold light lay along the edge of the table. Somewhere up the stair, faint, he heard Tansy's voice, high and quick, telling the silent man something about a pie.
Then the old voice came.
It was not dry, this time. It was not sharp. It was tired. So tired that it hardly sounded like Malakor at all. Like an old man speaking from the bottom of a well.
That's Oakhaven, said Malakor.
Corin did not move.
That's the crease, boy. The one I told you about. In the city, the night you went into my room. I said there was a crease, and I couldn't see past it. That's it. Those pages.
I wrote it all down. Everything. What happened. What I did. What I found on the road that night. Who I found. Where I took them. Where they are now. All of it, in plain letters, so any fool could read it. And then I folded it. Page by page. Every one. The way her mother's people do it, across the water. I learned it from a book, years ago. It was the only thing I ever learned from a book that worked.
I folded it in the book. And I folded it in me.
A pause.
So nobody could take it. Not off the page, not out of me. Not him with the gentle voice. Not anybody. That was the idea.
Corin found that his mouth was dry.
"He says it's Oakhaven," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange to him. "He says that's the crease. He says he wrote everything down, and then he folded it. In the book. And in himself. So nobody could take it."
Nobody in the room said anything.
Oren had come in from the doorway at last. He was standing at the end of the table, by the oak chair, without touching it, with his big hands hanging at his sides. Kestrel had risen from the floor and stood beside him. Vespera had not moved. All of them were looking at the row of little folded squares in the open book, in the gold light, and nobody was looking at Corin.
Except Lyra.
She was looking at him. And he knew what she was going to ask before she asked it, because it was the thing he was going to ask, and she got there first.
"How do we open it?"
Corin turned inward.
How do we open it?
Silence.
Then, very quietly, so quietly that Corin was not sure afterward that he had heard it at all:
I don't know yet.
Corin looked down at the book. At the folded pages. At the first one, the palm-sized brick of paper, standing up off the spine with its folds shining.
"He says," he said slowly, "he doesn't know yet."
And behind his ear, so faint it was almost nothing, the old man said one more word. Not to Corin. Not to anybody. To himself, perhaps. Corin heard it go by, like a moth going past a candle in the dark.
Yet.
Hollis
For a while nobody did anything.
Vespera had woken up when the folded pages came. She sat on the far side of the table now with her sharp old face bent forward, looking at the book with an expression Corin had not seen on her before. Not clever. Not cross. Almost afraid.
"I've seen that done once," she said. "Once in my life. A woman in a market in the west, across the water, when I was young and went looking for inks. She folded a letter from her dead husband, small, smaller, till it was gone into her fist. And then she opened her fist and it wasn't there. And she cried, and paid me a copper for a pot of blue, and went home." Vespera shook her head slowly. "I asked her where it went. She said, Where I keep him. I thought she was mad."
"She wasn't," said Lyra.
"No." Vespera looked at her. "No. I know that now, girl."
Kestrel had come round the table. She stood at Lyra's shoulder and looked down at the folded squares without touching them. Her face was perfectly still.
"He folded it in himself," she said. "The old one."
"Yes," said Corin.
"Then it is in you. Where he is. Behind your ear."
Corin had not thought of that. He thought of it now. He felt it, the moment she said it. A small hard place, far down behind his left ear, where the old man lived. Like a stone in a shoe. Like the hard little square of paper in Lyra's father's closed hand in the white room, that had never opened, that had gone under with him.
He put his hand up to the place. He did not mean to.
"Yes," he said. "I think it is."
Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and went back to the door.
Lyra was still sitting with her hands in her lap. Corin looked at her.
"You said a name," he said. "Before. On the list. Hollis. You said it like you knew it. You said later."
She looked up.
For a moment he thought she would not tell him. Then he saw her remember the line on his ribs, the one she had chosen herself. He saw her decide.
"It's in your book," she said. "The seventh one. The one Juno has. And some of it's in this one." She touched his coat with her eyes, not her hand, where the green book lay. "Hollis was the name of the man who kept the Lady's books. Vivienne's. At the Bourse. Her records. Every jar she bought and sold, for years. In his hand. Remy said so. And he painted her. Her face. Every night. Two hours, Remy said, sitting in front of her with his little brushes, making her young."
Corin sat very still.
Vivienne. He knew that name. Not from her. Not the way he knew Lyra's voice. From the inside. She had sat in the front room of his mind for six days, shouting. A woman who sold children. A violet bed. A wig on its stand. A bald head and fear and nobody. And oranges, and a broken mirror, and a small brown dog called Biscuit. Old Hask had breathed her out of him at Last Thorn, and died of it, smiling.
"Hollis the elder," said Lyra. "Good with jars. Taught his son." She looked down at the closed book. "I am sorry for that."
Nobody said anything.
I am, said Malakor, very faintly. I am sorry for that.
Corin did not tell her. He thought she knew.
"Are you angry with him?" he said instead.
Lyra did not answer at once. She looked at the book. At her own bandaged hand, lying on the glass beside it. At the painted valley under the glass, going dim now as the light left the table.
"I don't know," she said at last. "I'm angry. I've been angry since the Bourse. Since before. Since my father." She was quiet a moment. "I'm angry at the man who taught Hollis, and at Hollis, and at Hollis's son, and at the Lady, and at the man with the gentle voice, and at the men in gray, and at the Bank. And at my aunt. And at my father, for burning, and at my mother, for going. And at you, sometimes, for not knowing me." She stopped. "I'm so angry that there isn't room in it for one more old man."
She looked at Corin.
"And he's in you," she said. "So I'd have to be angry at you too. And I'm tired of that one."
Corin did not know what to say. He sat with his hands on his knees and looked at her.
Behind his ear, Malakor said nothing at all. But Corin felt him. The way you feel somebody in a dark room who has gone very still because they have heard their name.
"That's not a yes," said Corin.
"No."
"Is it a no?"
She almost smiled. He saw it start and stop.
"It's a not yet," she said. "Like his."
