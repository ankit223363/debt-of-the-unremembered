Chapter 82: Malakor's Journal, Part 5 of 5: The Flute
The Flute
Lyra closed the book.
She did it very gently. She laid the folded pages down on top of each other, the little hard squares fitting together like the scales on a fish, and brought the back cover over them, and then the front cover over the whole, and smoothed the leather flat with her bandaged hand. She did not tie the cord. She left it lying across the top.
Then she put both hands flat on the cover and sat there.
Nobody said anything for a long time.
The gold square of light had gone off the table altogether now. It lay on the floor beside the iron leg, on the stone flags and the gray dust, and it was not gold anymore either. It had gone orange. The color of the fire in the brazier. Evening was coming, up there, on the Eye.
Corin sat with his hands on his knees.
He was very tired. He had not known how tired until the reading stopped. It was the kind of tired that comes after a long walk, when you sit down at last and find that your legs have been shaking for an hour and you did not notice. He had been listening all day. To her voice. To the old man's life going into him one page at a time. To the old man himself, behind his ear, going quieter and quieter as the day went on, until by the end he hardly seemed to be there at all.
Are you there? Corin thought.
Nothing. Then, faintly: Here.
Are you all right?
A dry little sound. Not quite a laugh. I'm a hundred and four, boy, and I've been dead for a good while, and I've just heard my whole life read out loud by a girl who can't write. I'm grand.
Corin did not tell anybody that one either.
Kestrel was the first to move.
She got up from the floor by the door, where she had sat down again after the folded pages. She stood for a moment, looking at the closed book. Then she put her hand inside her hide coat, on the left side, over her heart. And took out the flute.
Arran's flute.
Corin had seen it every day since the Ridges and not seen it at all. It had been in her coat. On its cord, the cord of plaited hide with the red thread through it, that had come off her brother's neck under the stars with his face bare to them. She had buttoned it inside her coat that night, and as far as Corin knew she had not taken it out since.
It was a small thing. Bone, yellowed, smooth from handling, no longer than his hand. There were holes in it. Five, he thought, though he could not count them properly from where he sat. And a notch at the top, for the mouth.
Kestrel held it in both her hands and looked at it.
Then she turned and walked out of the room.
She did not say where she was going. She did not need to. They heard her go along the passage, past the guard room, quick and light on the stone. They heard the iron of the stair begin to ring under her. One step. Two. Three.
Corin looked at Lyra.
She was already getting up.
He got up too. Slowly. With the crutch. Oren was in the doorway still, and he stood aside to let them pass, and then he came after them. Vespera stayed where she was, in her iron chair, with her blue hands folded on her stick and her eyes on the closed book.
"Go on," she said, when Lyra looked back. "I'll mind the old man's book. I'm too old to climb stairs to listen to music. I'll hear it from here."
They went along the passage in the old track. Past the guard room, where the brazier glowed red and Brin was asleep against the wall with his mouth open and one hand on his ribs. To the foot of the stair. And up.
Corin counted the steps going up. He could not help it. He had caught it from her. One. Two. Three. At nine he lost them, the way he always did. But he kept climbing, and there were more, and then there was the iron landing, and the doorway, and the gap where the door had been slid back.
And then there was the Eye.
The light hit him. Not hard, the way it had hit at noon yesterday. It was evening light. Low and red and long. The sun had gone down the western sky almost to the far shore, and it lay there, huge and swollen and orange, sitting on the ring of gray stones a long way off like a coal on a hearth. And the Eye was full of it.
That was the thing he had not expected. The Eye was black. It had been black all day yesterday, and all night, clear and black like ice over deep water. But now the sun was low enough to shine into it sideways, and the light went in. It went down. Down into the clear black, deep, deeper than he could see, so that the whole wide floor of the Eye glowed from inside, red and gold and dark, like the inside of a stove when you open the door. And far down, in the glow, he could see shapes. The roofs of Applegarth. The square tower of the church. The cart with its horse in the middle of a step.
All lit up. As if the village were having a fair. As if somebody down there had lit every lamp.
He stood in the doorway with his hand on the iron frame and could not move.
The Ashborn on guard were sitting where they always sat, on the glass outside the door. Oda and Sef, tonight. They were sitting on their heels with their spears across their knees and they were not looking at the glowing Eye at all. They were looking up.
At the top of the dome.
Kestrel was up there.
Corin did not know how she had climbed it. The dome was round and smooth and fused, gray stone and iron bands run together into a kind of glassy shell, with long red tears of rust running down it. But there were places where the iron bands stood out a little, ridges you could put a foot on, and she must have gone up those. She was standing on the very top of it now, on the round crown of Sola's house, against the red sky. Tall and thin and black against the light, with her white fur cape hanging from her shoulders and her shaved head shining.
Not on the Eye. Corin understood that, after a moment. The Ashborn did not walk on the Eye. It was like walking on their faces. She had climbed up onto the only thing in the whole wide Eye that was not the Eye. The dead woman's roof.
She stood there and looked out over the glowing glass toward the sun.
And then she put the flute to her lips.
Tansy was sitting beside the silent man, to the left of the door. She had been talking. Corin had heard her as they came up the stair, a high quick stream of words. She had stopped. She was looking up at the top of the dome with her mouth open.
The silent man was looking south. As always. He did not turn his head.
Kestrel played.
Three notes.
Low. Then high. Then a little lower.
Corin knew them. He knew them in his chest, before his ears had finished hearing them. Arran had played them on the log outside the tent at Last Thorn, on the first night. Everybody knows that. It's what you play when you're bored. Arran had played them in the chapel nine times while the storm roared, until Kestrel took the flute away. Old Hask had hummed them. Corin hummed them himself, without knowing, when he was not afraid.
Low. High. A little lower.
And then the stop. The place where the tune ends, short, too soon, as if somebody had walked out of the room in the middle of a sentence. The place where there should be a fourth note and is not. Old Hask says there used to be more, Arran had said. It went over the Glass with somebody and didn't come back.
The notes went out over the Eye. They did not echo. Nothing echoed, on the Eye. They went out over the glowing glass, small and clear and thin, and were swallowed by the evening, and were gone.
Kestrel lowered the flute.
She stood for a moment. Then she lifted it again.
Low. High. A little lower.
And then she played a fourth note.
Corin felt Lyra go still beside him.
It was not the fourth note he knew. Not the one Lyra hummed, sometimes, in the dark, when she did not know he could hear, and then stopped, as if she had been caught. Not the one Wren had waited for, in a gray carriage in the city, with her eyes shut. That note went up. It went up past the high one and hung there, high and open, like a question with no answer.
This one went down.
It went down. Below the first note. Below all of them. Low and soft and long, so low the little bone flute could hardly make it, so that it came out half breath, a kind of whispering under the sound. It went down and stayed down, and did not hang, and did not ask anything. It settled. Like a bird coming down onto a branch. Like somebody lying down at the end of a long day.
And then she stopped.
Not short. Not cut off. Finished.
Nobody moved.
Kestrel stood on the top of the dome against the red sky with the flute held against her chest in both hands. Corin could not see her face. She was too far up, and the sun was behind her. He saw only the shape of her, and the white cape, and the bowed head.
Then she turned and began to climb down.
Beside him, Tansy said, very small, "That's not how it goes."
"No," said Lyra.
"That's not how it goes," Tansy said again. She looked at Lyra. Her face was shocked. "The bit at the end. That's not the bit."
"No," said Lyra. Her voice was strange. "It's her bit. She made it up."
Tansy thought about that. Corin watched her think about it, frowning, with her chin on her knees.
"Can you do that?" she said at last. "Just make up a bit? On somebody's tune?"
"I think you can if it's yours," said Lyra. "If you're the one carrying it."
Tansy looked up at the dome, where Kestrel was coming down the rusted iron ridges with one hand and holding the flute against her chest with the other.
"Oh," said Tansy. "Then that's all right."
And beside her, the silent man had turned his head.
Corin saw it. He did not think anybody else did. Lyra was looking at the dome. Tansy was looking at the dome. The man had turned his head, a little way, from the south, and he was not looking at the dome. He was looking at the Eye. At the glowing glass, red and gold, going down and down into the deep. At the drowned village lit up far below, with all its lamps.
The man never looked down. Corin had noticed that, crossing the Eye yesterday. Everybody looked down. Everybody had to. The man never did.
He was looking down now.
Corin watched him. The man's face did not change. But his bound hands, on his knees, had turned over. Palm down. And the fingers were pressed flat against his knees, hard, as if he were holding on to something.
Then Kestrel dropped down off the last iron ridge onto the glass by the door, light as a cat, and the man looked south again, and his hands turned over, palm up, and were still.
Kestrel stood in front of them with the flute in her hands. She did not say anything. Her eyes were dry. She looked at Corin, and then at Lyra, and then at Tansy, and then she put the flute back inside her coat, on the left, and buttoned the coat over it.
"Now he has been played," she said. "On the Eye."
And she went past them into the doorway, and down the stair.
Lights
They stayed at the top.
Corin did not decide to. He found that he did not want to go down into the dark again. Not yet. So he sat down on the iron landing just inside the door, with his stiff leg out in front of him and his back against the curved wall of the dome. And Lyra sat down beside him. A hand's width away. Not touching.
Oren went down and came back with water, and sat on the top step below them with his sword across his knees.
They watched the sun go down.
It went slowly. It sat on the far shore like a coal and sank into it, a little at a time, and the light in the Eye went from orange to red to a deep dark wine color, and the drowned village far below went out, roof by roof, lamp by lamp, from the bottom up, until there was only the tower of the church still lit, and then not even that. And then the sun was a red line on the shore. And then it was gone.
The Eye went black.
And the sky above it went a strange clear green, the color of the glass in old bottles, and the first stars came out in it. And then there were more. And then the Eye began to fill up with them too, falling into it, down and down.
The cold came. Real cold. Corin felt it on his face.
He did not mind it.
After a while he took the Ledger out again.
He did it without thinking, the way his hand went to it before he decided anything. He unwrapped the oilcloth on his knee in the last of the light and found the page where he had written about the old man that afternoon, and under it, below the two lines, he wrote again. Slowly. Pressing hard with the stub of pencil, because he could not feel how hard he was pressing and it was better to press too hard than not enough.
KESTREL PLAYED ARRAN ON THE EYE.
FROM THE TOP OF SOLA'S HOUSE.
A NEW LAST NOTE. HERS. IT GOES DOWN.
THE MAN LOOKED DOWN. ONCE.
He looked at what he had written. It was nearly too dark to see. The letters leaned, the way they always leaned, going down the page like a man going downstairs in the dark.
He turned the book round and held it out to her.
Lyra took it carefully, by the edges, so their fingers would not meet. She held it close to her face to read it in the starlight. He watched her read it. He watched her eyes go along the lines and stop at the last one, and stay there.
"You saw that?" she said. "Him looking down?"
"Yes."
"I didn't."
"You were looking at her."
Lyra was quiet. She read the line again.
"Did he look sad?" she said.
Corin thought about it. He tried to see the man's face again, turned down toward the glowing Eye. The drowned village lit up far below. The bound hands pressed flat on his knees.
"No," he said slowly. "Not sad. He looked like somebody holding on. Like a man on a roof in a wind."
Lyra looked at the line a moment longer. Then she gave him back the book.
"The S in SOLA'S is backward," she said.
He looked at it. It was. He did not know how she could see it in that light. He wrote a new one beside it, carefully, the right way round, a snake standing up and facing the right way, and showed her again, and she nodded.
"It's ugly," he said.
"It's right," she said.
And this time he heard himself say it before she did, inside, the way you hear the next line of a song you did not know you knew. It's right. He did not remember her ever saying it. But he had known she would.
He wrapped the book up again and put it back inside his coat, against his ribs, over the place where her name was written in his skin.
Lyra was quiet beside him for a long time. He did not know what she was thinking. He did not ask. He thought, perhaps, about the miller's daughter who married a king, in a silly song about goats and weddings. Or about the V. Or about the little folded squares in the book downstairs, one behind the other, like a row of shut doors. Or about her mother.
Or about none of those things. He did not know her. He did not know what she thought about.
But he found that he wanted to.
"Lyra," he said.
She turned her head.
He did not know what he had meant to say. He had said her name, and now she was looking at him in the starlight and waiting, and he did not know what came next.
"Thank you," he said. "For reading."
She looked at him a moment longer.
"It was for both of us," she said. "I told you."
"I know. It's written down."
Something happened at the corner of her mouth.
"Then you can't get out of it," she said.
Oren, on the step below them, made a sound in his beard. It might have been a cough.
Then he stood up.
Corin did not see why. He saw only that Oren had stood up all at once, the way he stood up in the night when he heard something, and that the sword was in his hand, and that he was not looking at them. He was looking out through the gap in the door. Out over the Eye. South.
"Oren?" said Lyra.
Oren did not answer. He stepped up past them onto the landing and put his face to the gap. He stood there, very still, with the starlight on one side of his face.
Corin pulled himself up by the edge of the door. Slowly. He looked out past Oren's shoulder.
The Eye lay black and endless under the stars. Far away, all the way round, there was the shore. A faint gray ring in the dark. He could only just see it.
And on the shore, to the south, there were lights.
Small. Yellow. Not the cold blue of Tansy's lamp. Not the pale drifting lights of the lost ones under the Glass. Lantern light. The warm yellow of oil burning behind glass. One. Two. Three. Four. In a line. Very far off, on the gray stones of the beach, where the land came down into the bowl from the south. Swaying a little, the way lanterns sway when the men who carry them are walking.
Four lights. He could count four.
They stopped.
They stood still on the shore, in a row. And then, one by one, they began to move apart. Spreading out, slowly. Along the beach. The way men spread out when they have come to the end of a march and are looking for a place to stop.
Behind his ear, very quietly, so quietly he almost did not hear it, Malakor said, Ah.
That was all.
Oren turned round. He looked at Corin. Then at Lyra. Then he went to the head of the stair, and down it, quickly, three steps, four, five, the iron ringing under him, until he was halfway down and could see along the passage to the guard room and the red glow of the fire. He stopped there. With his sword out.
Corin heard him take a breath.
"Lights," said Oren, down the stair. Not loudly. He did not need to say it loudly. The stair carried it. "On the shore."
A pause. Below, in the guard room, Corin heard somebody sit up. Heard Kestrel's quick light step on the stone.
"Men," said Oren.
