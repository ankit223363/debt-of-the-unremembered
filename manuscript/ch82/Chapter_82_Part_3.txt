Chapter 82: Malakor's Journal, Part 3 of 5: The Bargain
The Bargain
They stopped again at noon. Lyra said it was noon. The gray square had come to the middle of the table, to the place where the river ran past Applegarth, and lay there, and seemed to stop, the way the sun seems to stop at the top of the sky.
Oren went up the stair to look at the Eye and came down again.
"Nothing," he said. "Empty. All the way round to the shore. The girl's asleep in the sun with her head on his knee. He's looking south."
"Still?" said Lyra.
"Still." Oren rubbed his beard. "She told me to tell you she's had three dreams and they were all about pies."
Vespera laughed. Corin found that he was smiling, and did not know when he had started.
They ate the last of the cold stew out of the pot with their fingers, standing round the brazier in the guard room, because nobody wanted to bring food into Sola's room. Nobody said so. Then Kestrel went up the stair and changed the guard, and Sef and Marrit came down and ate too, and sat by the fire with their hands out, and Kestrel told them, in the Ashborn tongue, low and quick, what had been in the book.
Corin did not understand the words. He understood their faces.
Marrit's face went still, and then crumpled, and then went still again. Sef said nothing at all. He only took the gray stone from the shore out of his fist, the one Corin had given him, and looked at it, and closed his hand on it again. And when Kestrel had finished, Sef got up and went to the doorway of the guard room and stood looking along the passage toward Sola's room for a long time. Then he came back and sat down.
"He says," said Kestrel, to Corin, without being asked, "that he would like to hear the rest. After. When it is finished. If the old man does not mind."
Tell him, said Malakor, that I mind very much and he can hear it anyway.
Corin told him. Kestrel told Sef. Sef nodded, gravely, as if he had been given a very great gift.
Then they went back to the table.
The square of light had begun to slide again. It lay now just past Applegarth, on the road going south. Lyra sat down in the iron chair in its edge and opened the book where she had left it, with the cord across the page, and found her place with her finger.
Corin shut his eyes.
"We stayed at the Edge," Lyra read. "There was nowhere else to go. Behind us, to the south, was the Ministry. In front of us, to the north, was the gold. It had cooled by the end of the first week. It went dark. Green, and black, and hard, and you could walk on it, and if you walked on it you heard it hum. We called it the Glass. We did not have a better word.
"We stayed in the field and lived. I do not know how. We ate what the boys brought from the far villages. We built shelters out of the tents and the hides of the dead horses. We sat in rings at night and breathed each other's dead, a little, round and round, so that nobody got full. When somebody died, we breathed them too. The ones who carried the most were the gray-faced ones, and we let them sit nearest the fire.
"It was not a good life. But it was a life. I want that written down too.
"In the third spring the Ministry came."
The room was very quiet.
"They had heard about us. They always hear. A camp of people at the Edge of the new Glass who were carrying the dead of a whole valley inside them. A whole valley. You will understand, if you are reading this, what that would be worth to them. Every memory of every person who had lived in the green valley for a hundred years, every wedding and every harvest and every first word, sitting there in a muddy field in the heads of a few hundred starving people. Ready to be taken. Jarred. Sold.
"They came with carts. And lamps. And men in gray coats with rods.
"My rods.
"I saw them coming up the road from the south in the morning, a long gray line, and I knew every one of those rods, because I had made them. I knew which way they turned. I knew how fast they drew. I knew that by nightfall there would not be a person in that field who could tell you their own name.
"So I walked out to meet them."
Lyra stopped. Corin heard her turn the page. He heard her breathe.
"I did not take a stick. I did not take a white rag. I walked down the road toward them in my muddy shirt with my hands empty, and the man at the front knew me. Of course he knew me. I had taught him. He had been one of my clever boys. He was older now, and he had a gray coat with silver on the shoulders, and he looked at me as if I were a dog that had come back to the house after being lost a long time.
"He said, Master. We thought you were dead.
"I said, Not yet.
"And then I made him a bargain.
"I said, You can have me. All of me. I will come back to the room under the Ministry, and I will work. I will teach again. I will make you rods better than any you have. I will make you jars that never leak. I will give you everything I know and everything I will ever know. I will never run away again. I will die in that room.
"And you will turn your carts round and go home, and you will never come up this road again, and you will never touch one hair on one head in that field. Not now. Not ever. Not their children. Not their children's children.
"He did not believe me. I could see it in his face. He thought I was lying. And so I did the only thing I could think of to make him believe me.
"I took out of my own head, there on the road, the way to the room. The way out of it. The stair, the cupboard, the door with the leaf. How to get from the room to the street. I took it out with my own two hands, the way I had taught him, slowly, so he could watch, and I put it into one of his jars, and I gave it to him.
"And I said, Now I cannot leave. Now I could not find the way out if I wanted to. You can keep that in your pocket.
"And he believed me."
Nobody in the room said anything at all.
Corin had opened his eyes. He was looking at the gray square on the glass. At the little brown road painted on the map, running south from Applegarth toward the black zigzag of the ditch. He could see it, the man walking down it in a muddy shirt with his hands empty. He did not know if he could see it because Lyra's voice had put it there, or because the old man behind his ear was remembering it so hard that it leaked.
"They turned round," Lyra read. "They went home. I went with them, in the back of a cart, with the jar in the clever boy's pocket. I looked back once. I saw the field, and the shelters, and the smoke from the fires, and the people standing at the edge of the field in a row, watching me go. Gray-faced. All of them. The girl with the burned arms was at the front, with a baby on her hip. I do not know whose baby. It was not hers. It was everybody's, by then.
"She lifted her hand.
"I did not lift mine. I was afraid that if I did I would get off the cart.
"I went back to the room. I did not leave it again for forty-two years."
Lyra stopped.
Corin waited for the number to mean something. It did not. Forty-two. It went past him like a bird. Like all of them.
"Is that long?" he said.
Lyra looked at him. He saw her think about how to say it.
"It's about as long as Oren has been alive," she said. "All of it. Every day of it. In one room."
Corin looked at Oren.
Oren was standing in the doorway with his hands behind his back. He had heard. He was looking at the floor, and his face was the face he wore when he was thinking about something a long way off. His whole life, Corin thought. Every road he had walked. Every winter. The army, and the house on Crown Walk, and the long years standing outside the study door, and the girl he met when she was small, and the fens, and the Drove Road, and the Glass.
All of it. In one room. Under the ground.
Corin could not hold the number. But he could hold that.
It wasn't so bad, said Malakor. Don't let her make a fuss. I had my bench. I had my work. They brought me my dinner on a tray. Some days I forgot I was in a room at all.
The old voice was very steady. Too steady.
And I'd do it again, said Malakor. For them. That's the thing, boy. If you're going to think about it, think about that. I'd do it again.
Corin did not tell anybody that. He did not think he could say it out loud.
Kestrel was sitting by the door with her hand flat on her spear. She had not moved. But Corin saw that she had heard something, anyway. In the silence. In his face. She was looking at him.
"The bargain," she said. "It was kept."
"Yes," said Lyra. She looked down at the page. "It says so. On the next page. They kept it. I will give them that. They kept it for sixty years. They never came up that road again."
"No," said Kestrel. "They never came." She looked at the floor. "Not until this winter."
And Corin saw again, the way he had seen it in her face on the night of the burning, the camp at Last Thorn on fire. The tents going up one after another in the wind. The iron men walking through them. The children against the bread racks.
Not until this winter. Not until him.
He did not say anything. There was nothing he could say.
The Thing You Are
The old man did not speak for a long time after that.
Lyra read on. Pages about the room. About the work. About the rods getting better, and the jars, and the Bank growing, and new buildings going up along the river, and the city changing outside a door he could not find the way through. About students. Long lists of things he had found out how to do. Some of it Corin understood, and some of it went past him, and some of it he understood too well, and wished he had not.
How to take a thing so slowly that the man thinks he has only forgotten it.
How to take a thing from a sleeping child.
How to keep a thing cold for a hundred years.
How to make a lamp burn on what you have taken.
Lyra's voice did not change when she read those. It stayed quite steady. But Corin saw that she had stopped putting her finger under the lines. Her good hand had gone into her lap, and her bandaged one lay flat on the edge of the page, holding it down, and she read without pointing.
The gray square moved slowly across the map. Off the road. Onto the green of the hills to the east.
And then, in the middle of a page about the jars, Malakor spoke.
Not loudly. He did not need to. He was right there, behind Corin's ear, where he had always been. But Corin felt it go through him the way you feel a cart go over a bridge you are standing on.
Boy.
Corin opened his eyes.
Listen to me a moment. Never mind her. Let her read. Listen to me.
Corin did not move. He sat in the iron chair with his hands on his knees. Beside him, Lyra's voice went on, steady, quiet, reading about the jars.
Everything the Bank burns from the dead, said Malakor, goes through a door I opened. You know that. You've seen it. You've sat at the desk. Every jar on every shelf in that cold room under the Bourse. Every lamp on every quay that burns blue. Every Ash-mage on the Hill with a pocket full of bought stones. All of it.
The jars. I made the jars. The rods, with the right-hand turn. I made those. I turned the first one on my own bench with my own hands and I was proud of it.
A pause.
You.
Corin did not breathe.
I made you. Not you yourself. Not your face or your name. The thing you are. A harvester. A man who can put his hand on a stranger and take out of them the last thing they saw and carry it home in a jar. There was nobody in the world who could do that, before me. I taught it to Corvell, and Corvell taught it to Linus, and Linus taught it to you. And you were good at it. You were very good. I watched you from Linus's pocket, the way you'd watch a boy you'd taught to fish. You were the best of all of them.
I made the thing you are.
I'd like you to know that, said the old voice. Very low now. Very tired. Before you think too well of me.
Corin sat very still.
Lyra had stopped reading. He did not know when. She was looking at him. They were all looking at him. He did not know what his face was doing.
"Corin?" said Lyra.
He did not answer her at once.
He looked down at his hands on his knees. Gray, both of them, from the black fingertips all the way up under his sleeves to the shoulders. Gray from taking. From burning. From years at a desk, putting his hand on strangers. He could not feel them. He had not been able to feel them for weeks.
The thing he was.
"He says," Corin said slowly. And stopped.
Rule one, he thought. No lies between you. It was written on his ribs. A promise, not a fact, she had said, when she chose it. Which meant he had to keep choosing it.
So he told her. All of it. Slowly. In the old man's words, as near as he could get them. The door. The jars. The rods with the right-hand turn. Corvell, and Linus, and him. I made the thing you are. He said it out loud in Sola's room, with the gray light on the table, and his voice did not shake as much as he had thought it would.
When he had finished, nobody said anything.
Then Vespera, from across the table, said: "Well. That's honest too."
And Kestrel, by the door, said nothing at all. But she was looking at Corin's hands.
Lyra did not say anything either. She sat beside him in the iron chair, a hand's width away, not touching, and looked at his face. And he looked at hers. And he did not know her. He never would. But he saw, in that thin sharp face with the cut lip and the dark eyes, that she was not afraid of him. Not even now. Not even of the thing he was.
He did not know how he knew that. He knew it the way he knew her voice.
He turned his head a little. Inward. Toward the place behind his ear.
You made Kestrel's people too, he thought.
Silence. Then, gruffly: That was an accident.
You said everything that ever mattered was an accident. You wrote it. On the first page.
A longer silence. Then something that might have been a laugh. Dry. Tired. Like a page being turned.
Clever, said Malakor. Who taught you to be clever? Not me.
Nobody, thought Corin. I don't remember.
No, said Malakor. No, you don't.
And he was quiet again.
Corin took the Ledger out of his coat. Slowly. He unwrapped the oilcloth and opened it on his knee and found the page, and took the stub of pencil in his fist. And he wrote, in his crooked leaning capitals, with the letters going down the page like a man going downstairs in the dark:
TUESDAY. MALAKOR MADE THE THING I AM.
HE TOLD ME SO HIMSELF BEFORE I COULD THINK TOO WELL OF HIM.
He looked at it for a long time.
Then he turned the book round and held it out to Lyra so she could check his letters.
She read it. She did not say anything about the letters. She looked at the words, and then at him, and then she said, quite quietly:
"I think he wanted you to write that down."
"Yes."
"I think that's why he said it." She gave him back the book. "So you'd keep it. Even if you forgot everything else. So you'd always know. He didn't want you to love him without knowing."
Corin looked at the line.
Before I could think too well of him.
"It's too late," he said.
He did not know he was going to say it. It came out of him the way the old man's laugh came out of him sometimes, or Arran's. But it was his own. He knew it was his own.
Behind his ear, the old man made no sound at all.
Her Room
Lyra's voice needed a rest. Vespera said so, and nobody argued with Vespera. So for a while in the middle of the afternoon nobody read anything.
Corin got up out of the iron chair. His leg had gone stiff from sitting, and the knee did not want to bend, and for a moment he stood holding on to the back of the chair with both hands while the blood came back into it. Then he took the crutch and began to walk.
He did not go anywhere. He walked round the table. Slowly. Along the left side, past the pushed-back chairs, to the far end, where the light from the shaft did not reach and the table went on into the dark. He had not been to the far end before. Nobody had. He went there now, step and crutch, step and crutch, and his shadow went ahead of him along the floor.
The far end of the table was covered with dust like everywhere else. But there was something on it. Under the dust, on the glass, at the very end. A shape. Round, and flat.
He bent over it.
It was a cup. A tin cup, like the ones they drank from. It stood on the glass over the bottom edge of the map, where the painting stopped, and it had been there so long that the dust had made a little gray hill over it and round it, so that it looked like a cup made of dust.
He did not touch it.
That's mine, said Malakor.
Corin straightened up slowly.
I brought her tea in that, said the old voice. The last night. Before the morning she rode out. She'd sent them all away. Her captains. Her smiths. The boy who carried her maps. She sent them all up the stair and out, and she sat on her own in that oak chair at the top of the table, with the lamp lit, looking at the map. And I came down with a cup of tea because nobody had told me not to.
Corin stood very still at the far end of the table.
She didn't drink it. She let me put it down there, at the bottom of the valley, by the ditch, and she looked at it for a while. And then she said, Well, Malakor.
Just that. Well, Malakor. As if I'd asked her something.
And then she said, If it works, they'll have a road.
The old man was quiet.
I didn't know what she meant. Not then. I thought she meant a road out. For the people. South, to get away. I thought she meant to hold them at the ditch long enough for everybody to run.
She didn't mean that. You know what she meant. You were there. You were her, on the horse, with the road going down in front of you made of light.
Corin did not say anything. He did not think anything. He stood at the end of the table and looked at the little dusty cup.
I left her there, said Malakor. I went up the stair. That was the last time I saw her face. Sitting in that chair. With her boots up on the edge of the table, the way she always sat, and the horse blanket over her knees, because her knees were bad in the cold, and she'd never let anybody see her wear it in the day.
Corin turned his head and looked back along the length of the table at the oak chair. At the striped blanket, folded over its back.
She put it over the chair when she stood up, said Malakor. In the morning. Folded it once and hung it there. So she'd know where it was when she came back.
Corin found that he could not see the chair properly. His eyes had gone hot.
He went back along the table. Slowly. Step and crutch. Lyra was sitting in the iron chair in the edge of the light with her bandaged hand in her lap and her eyes shut. Vespera had gone to sleep, sitting up, with her chin on the head of her stick. Kestrel was in the doorway.
And Oren was standing where he had stood all day. By the head of the table. A pace from the oak chair. With his hands behind his back.
Corin stopped beside him.
For a while neither of them said anything. They stood side by side and looked at the chair.
"You've not sat down," said Corin. "All day."
Oren did not look at him. "No."
"Your back's bad."
"It is."
"There are chairs."
Oren was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "It's her room."
That was all. As if that were the whole of it. And Corin, looking at his face, saw that for Oren it was.
"In the army," said Oren, after a while, "you don't sit down in the general's tent. Not unless she tells you. You stand. You stand by the door with your hat under your arm and you wait to be told. And if she never tells you, you stand till she's done with you." He moved his shoulders. "I stood outside his lordship's study door for twenty-two years. I can stand a day in hers."
Corin looked at the blanket.
"He says she wore it over her knees," he said. "At night. Because her knees were bad in the cold. She never let anybody see her wear it in the day."
Oren went very still.
"He says she put it over the chair when she stood up," said Corin. "The last morning. Folded it once. So she'd know where it was when she came back."
Oren did not say anything for a long time.
Then he did a thing Corin had not seen him do before. He took one hand from behind his back, his left, the one with the cut across the palm bound up in a strip of cloth from where he had opened it on his own sword to wake the door. He put it out. Slowly. And he laid it on the folded blanket. Very lightly. On the top fold, over the back of the oak chair. The way you lay your hand on the shoulder of somebody sitting down, when you have come into a room behind them and do not want to make them jump.
He left it there a moment.
Then he took it away, and put it behind his back again, with the other one.
"Her knees," he said. His voice was rough. "My grandfather's knees were bad. In the cold. He used to sit on the back step in the evenings with a sack over them." He shook his head slowly. "Saints. It's in the blood, then. The knees."
"Are yours bad?"
"They are now."
Corin almost laughed. He did not. But Oren's mouth moved in his beard, and Corin saw it, and knew that Oren had almost laughed too.
"Ask him," said Oren. "The old man. Ask him what she was like. At the table. When she was planning."
Corin turned inward.
He wants to know what she was like. At the table.
A pause. Then the old voice, softer than it had been all day.
Loud, said Malakor. Tell him loud. She'd lean over the map with both fists on the glass and shout at it as if it had done something to her. And then she'd laugh. She'd stamp about this room in her big boots with an apple in one hand and a pointer in the other, and she'd poke her captains in the chest with the pointer when they said something stupid, and they'd love her for it. And at the end of the night she'd sit down in that chair and put her feet up and sing something with no sense in it at all. About a goat. Always a goat.
Tell him she was the best captain I ever saw, and I saw a great many, and most of them were fools.
Tell him she'd have liked him.
Corin told him. All of it. The fists on the glass and the shouting, and the apple and the pointer, and the goat. And the last thing. He said the last thing slowly, so that Oren would hear it properly.
Oren stood very still.
Then he nodded. Once. And looked at the oak chair, and did not say anything at all.
And tell him, said Malakor, suddenly, about the box.
Corin blinked. What about the box?
Why it opens to my word. He'll want to know. He's the sort who wants to know why a thing is shut. A dry sound. She asked me. That last night. When I brought the tea. She said, Malakor, you know about words. Put one on my box. I'll not have the gray men opening it if this goes wrong. Put a word on it that only you can say. For my maps. And for whatever comes after.
So I did. With her watching. I knelt on the floor under the table and put my hand on the lid and put a word on it. And she said, Good. And then she said, If you ever come back here, put something in it worth keeping.
A long pause.
I did, in the end. Didn't I.
Corin told Oren. Oren listened with his head bent. When Corin had finished he looked down the length of the table, into the shadow under it, where the iron box sat in its four chains.
"Whatever comes after," said Oren. "Aye. That's her."
And behind them, in the iron chair, Lyra had opened her eyes, and had been listening, and said nothing. She only looked at the two of them standing side by side at the head of Sola's table. The lame man with the gray hands, and the big one with his hands behind his back. And Corin, turning, saw her face, and did not know what was in it.
"Are you rested?" he said.
"Yes." She pulled the book toward her again across the glass. "Yes. I'm ready."
