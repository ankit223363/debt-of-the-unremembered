Chapter 82: Malakor's Journal, Part 2 of 5: The Oak-Leaf Room
The Oak-Leaf Room
After that, the pages went faster.
Not because Lyra read faster. She did not. She read the way she had read the Ledger to him on the hot glass by the iron horse, all one long afternoon, slowly and carefully, with her finger moving along under each line. But the things in the pages went faster. They went the way time goes when you are young and everything is happening at once.
"I found out I could do it again," Lyra read. "Not only with the dying. With the living too. If I sat close to a man who was hurt, and put my hand on him, and wanted it, I could take the hurt out of him. Not the hurt in his leg. The hurt in his head. The thing he was seeing over and over, the thing that woke him screaming. I could draw it out of him, like drawing a splinter, and it would come into me, and he would sleep.
"The doctors found out. Then the officers. Then I was not on the front end of a stretcher anymore. I was in a tent behind the lines, with a cot and a lamp, and they brought me the men who could not stop screaming, and I took it out of them, and they went back to the lines and fought again.
"That was the second thing I learned. If you can take the fear out of a man, you can send him back to be killed. And they will thank you for it. Both of them. The man and the officer."
Corin did not open his eyes for that one. He sat in the iron chair with his hands on his knees and kept them shut.
He did not have to see it. He had seen it. Not in a tent. In a long gray office on the third floor of the Ministry, at the back, with six desks and a cold stove and a locked rack of silver rods. He had sat at one of those desks. He had taken things out of people and put them in jars and labeled the jars and sent them down to the Bank, and the people had gone home lighter, and some of them had thanked him.
He did not remember most of it. But his hands did.
"When the long war ended, the men in gray came for me," Lyra read. "The Ministry. It was new then. They had not yet put up the big building by the river. They had a few rooms in an old house in the old town, behind the fish market, and a great many ideas. They had heard about the boy in the tent who could take the screaming out of men. They wanted to know if I could take other things.
"I could.
"They wanted to know if I could keep them. Not in me. Somewhere else. In a thing they could carry about and sell.
"I did not know. I said I would find out.
"That was the third thing I learned, and it is the one I am most ashamed of. I said I would find out, and I meant it, and I was glad they had asked me. I was proud. I was twenty-five and nobody had ever asked me anything except to carry."
Lyra turned the page. Her finger found the top of the next one.
"They gave me a room.
"Under the old house. Down a stair, behind a cupboard. A round room, with gray walls, and a door with an oak leaf carved on it, because the man who gave it to me had a sense of humor and said I was to be their tree, and grow them fruit. A bench. A rack. A desk. A cot in the corner, with a gray blanket, for when I worked too late to go home, which was always. I had no home to go to. I had the room. Later they pulled the old house down and put up their big gray building on top of it, and the room stayed where it was, underneath, like a stone under a field.
"I worked in that room for a long time. Years. I found out how to make the rods. The first ones broke. The ones after that held a little and leaked. The ones after that held. I found out that a thing taken out of a man would stay in silver if the silver was turned the right way, in a spiral, to the right, like the thread of a screw. I found out it would stay in glass too, if the glass was cold and sealed quick. Jars.
"I found out how to do it slowly, so the man did not feel it. I wrote that down. I wrote a whole book of it. The book of slow ways. It is still there, in the room, on the shelf above the desk. I hope somebody burns it."
"It's not," said Corin.
Lyra stopped. They all looked at him.
He did not know he had spoken until he heard it. He opened his eyes.
"It's not there," he said. "The book. It's not on the shelf. I've been in that room. It's in the Ledger." He touched his coat, over the place where the green book lay against his ribs. "Seventh one. Or this one. I don't know. The room was there, and the desk, and the rack, and the cot. With the gray blanket." He stopped. Something moved in him. A long way down. Like a fish turning in deep water. "There was an ink stain on the desk. Like a hare. But there was no book on the shelf."
The room was quiet.
Behind his ear, Malakor said nothing for a long time.
Then: Linus took it, said the old voice. Very low. Of course he did. Linus took it to teach from. And then he gave it away, in the end. Didn't he. To somebody who needed it more. A pause. Good boy, Linus. Good boy.
Corin told them.
Lyra was looking at him with a strange face. He did not know what it meant.
"You remember the ink stain," she said.
"I remember it's in the book."
"No. You said it before you looked. You said it like you'd seen it."
He thought about that. She was right. He had not reached for the Ledger. He had not needed to. The hare-shaped ink stain had simply been there, in him, when he went to look. Like the loose step in Davey Linn's mother's kitchen.
He did not know what that meant.
"Go on," he said.
She looked at him a moment longer. Then she looked down and found her place.
"They called me the Grafted because of what I did next," she read. "I will tell you plainly, because I said I would.
"When you take a thing out of a man for long enough, you begin to lose your own things. Not because they are taken. Because they are pushed out. There is only so much room in a man. If you keep putting other men's kitchens into it, sooner or later your own kitchen falls out of the back.
"I began to lose myself. My mother's face. My own name, the real one, the first one. The city I came from. Whole years. I would wake up in the room and not know how long I had been there.
"So I grafted.
"You will know what that is if you have ever lived near an orchard. You take a branch from a good tree, and you cut it, and you bind it onto the stock of another tree, a rough one, a wild one, and if you bind it well and the weather is kind, it takes. It grows. The rough tree bears good fruit. It does not know the branch was not its own.
"I did that to myself. Where I had lost a piece of my own life, I took a piece of somebody else's, a good piece, a piece I had taken from a dying man and kept, and I bound it into the gap. A mother's face where my mother's had been. A boyhood on a farm where mine in the city had been. A first kiss. A wedding. A dog. I bound them in, one by one, and they took. And I went on working.
"By the time I was forty I was more other men than I was myself. I was an orchard of stolen branches on a stump. That is what they meant by the Grafted. They meant it as a compliment."
Lyra stopped.
She did not look up. She sat with her finger on the line and looked at it.
Corin found that his hand had gone up to the place behind his ear.
He took it down again.
Sola's Valley
They stopped for water at what Lyra said was the middle of the morning. Corin did not know how she knew. There was no sun in the room, only the gray square sliding slowly across the glass. But she looked at it and said, "It's moved a hand," and he believed her.
Oren went to the store room and came back with the bucket. They drank from the tin cup, one after another. Corin drank last, and held the cup a long time after it was empty, looking at the map.
The square of light had moved. It lay now on the bend of the river below Applegarth, on a cluster of painted trees with white and pink dots on them. An orchard. In blossom. He looked at it and something in him went very still.
Not him. Her.
He felt it before he understood it. A quiet coming up from somewhere deep, the way water comes up in a footprint on wet sand. A great wide calm, with nothing in it at all. No words. Sola had no words. She had given them all away, sixty years ago, at the lake at the top of this valley, one by one, and gone down to the last of them, and given that too. What was left of her in him had no words. Only hands, and feet, and the way you hold your weight. And silence.
And this. This stillness, looking at the painted orchard.
He did not know what it meant. He thought perhaps it meant mine.
His right hand moved on his knee.
He felt it move. He looked down and saw it. The fingers lifting and closing, quickly. Four, held out, with the thumb tucked in. Then two, flat. Then the whole hand turning over and folding. Then three, drawn toward his chest.
He stopped it. He put his other hand over it and held it still.
In the doorway, Oren had seen. Corin saw him see it. Oren's face did not change. But he took his hands from behind his back and let them hang at his sides, the way a soldier stands easy when his officer has passed.
"Go on," said Corin. His voice was rough. "Please."
Lyra had seen too. She looked at his hands, under the edge of the table. Then she looked down at the book.
"I stayed in the room under the Ministry for nineteen years," she read. "I was very good. I taught. I had students. Clever boys. Some girls. I taught them the slow ways, and they took the slow ways out into the city and used them on people who did not know they were being used, and brought back jars, and the jars went to the Bank, and the Bank grew.
"I was ashamed of it for most of those years. I want that written down. I was not proud the whole time. I was ashamed, and I went on.
"In the nineteenth year something happened that I have since lost. I know it happened. I have the shape of it, like a scar with no memory of the cut. Something one of my students did, to somebody. A child, I think. I think I took the memory of it out of myself afterward and burned it, because I could not live with it. I do not know. That is the trouble with being what I am. You can burn the reason you changed your life, and keep the change.
"I walked out of the room one night and did not go back. I walked north. I had heard there was a woman in the north who had raised the valleys against the Ministry and was beating them. I thought she might hang me. I thought that would be fair.
"She did not hang me."
Lyra turned the page. And Corin saw her face change when she read the first line of the next one, and he did not know why until she read it aloud.
"She was sitting on an iron horse in an orchard when I found her," Lyra read, "eating an apple."
Nobody moved.
"The horse was a thing her smiths had made, at Haverstock, out of old plow iron and cart springs and some clockwork they had taken off the Ministry. It was twice the height of a real horse, and it creaked, and it smelled of hot oil, and the children followed it everywhere. She sat up on top of it in a kind of iron chair with a braid down her back as long as my arm and a scar on her jaw, and she was eating an apple, and she looked down at me with her mouth full, and she said:
"You're the one who taught them to take.
"I said I was.
"She finished the apple. She threw the core away over her shoulder, into the grass. And she said, Can you teach my people not to be taken from?
"I said I did not know. I said nobody had ever asked me that.
"She said, Then carry stretchers until you do.
"And she turned the iron horse round and rode away through the trees, and the blossom came down on her like snow."
Lyra stopped.
In the doorway, Oren made a sound. Not a word. A breath that caught.
Corin did not look at him. He was looking at his own right hand under the table. It had not moved this time. It lay still on his knee, palm down. But he could feel her in it. In the bones of it. Not a memory. He did not have that memory; it was Malakor's, not hers. Only the shape of her hand. The weight of an apple in it. The way the fingers would let go of a core and toss it over a shoulder without looking.
"She talked like a sergeant and sang like a girl," Lyra read, more quietly. "I carried stretchers for her for a year and a half. I never taught anybody anything. I did not know how. I carried. And at night, at the fires, she would sing. Old valley songs. Silly ones. Songs about goats and weddings and a miller's daughter who went to the city and married a king. She had a voice like a blackbird. You would not have thought it, to look at her. You would have thought she would sing like a cow.
"I was happy there. I had not been happy since Davey Linn. I did not know I could be."
She stopped again, and this time she did not start for a while. She sat looking down at the page with her finger under the line.
"A miller's daughter," she said, at last. Very low. "Who married a king."
Nobody answered her. Corin did not know what she meant. He thought she did not know either. He thought she was only saying it to hear it.
Read on, girl, said Malakor. Gently, for him. It's only a song.
Corin did not tell her that one.
The Edge
"I was not at the ditch when the light came," Lyra read. "I want to say that plainly too. I was not brave. I was a stretcher man, and stretcher men are at the back. We had the wounded in tents in a field behind the lines, south of the ditch, a long way south. Near where the snow stops now. I did not know that then. There was no line then. There was only a field, and the tents, and the wounded in them, and a little river running past.
"I saw her go. I saw her ride north up the road alone in the morning with her braid pinned up under her helmet. I did not know where she was going. Nobody did.
"And then a long time after, at noon, I saw the light."
Lyra's voice had dropped. Corin kept his eyes shut.
He knew this. He had been inside it. He had been her, at the top of this valley, at the little lake, going down and down into her own words and giving them away. Hob. Talan. Annis. Ralf. Aldric. And her own name last. And then the roar, and the gold coming out of her chest like burning honey, and the iron horse under her, and the long charge south down the road, and the light pouring out in front of the hooves and laying itself down like a road.
He had been inside it. Not this. Not the other end of it.
"It came down the valley from the north," Lyra read. "Like a wave. Like the sea coming in. Gold. All the way across, from one side of the valley to the other. It came down over the orchards and the villages and the river, and wherever it went, the green went out, and the gold stayed, and shone, and did not move. And I stood in the field in front of the tents with a bandage in my hands and watched it come.
"It came over the ditch. I saw it reach the men standing in the ditch with their pikes. They had turned round to look at it. I saw it go over them and they did not fall down. They stood. They stood and shone.
"And it came on. Toward us. Toward the field. And I thought, this is the end, then, this is fair, and I put down the bandage.
"And it stopped.
"It stopped at the edge of the field. Not slowly. All at once. In a line. A straight line, as straight as if somebody had drawn it with a ruler from one side of the valley to the other. On the far side of the line, gold, and stillness, and shine. On our side of it, mud, and grass, and the tents, and the wounded.
"And between the two, nothing. No space at all. You could have stood with one foot on each side."
"The Edge," said Kestrel.
Corin opened his eyes. She was sitting now. Sometime in the morning she had let herself slide down the wall by the doorway and sat on the floor with her spear across her knees, and her face still to the passage. But she was not watching the passage now. She was watching Lyra.
"Yes," said Lyra. "The Edge."
She found her place.
"Then it began.
"Not at once. The first hour, nothing. We stood and looked. And then, at the line, where the gold stopped, something began to come off it. A gray. Like mist off a river in the morning. It came up out of the gold, all along the line, thin, and the wind took it, and it blew toward us.
"It was them. All of them. Everything they had been. The whole valley. The light had taken their bodies and turned them into glass, and their words, she had given those, but there was more in a man than his words, and the rest of it had nowhere to go. It came out of the glass at the edge, like steam out of a kettle, and blew over us on the wind.
"And the people in the field breathed it in.
"Not a little. Not one kitchen. All of it. A whole valley. Thousands. The wounded in the tents, and the burned ones who had been at the back and lived, and the blinded ones, and the boys who had come up to fetch water, and the women from the far villages who had come to look after us. And the soldiers. The Ministry's men. The ones at the back of the gray army, who had not got close enough to be taken, and had thrown down their coats when they saw the light, and run. Toward us. Toward the only living people they could see. They breathed it too.
"And it was too much.
"You cannot carry a valley. Nobody can. I watched them try. I watched a woman sit down in the mud and begin to say names, one after another, names she had never known, faster and faster, until she was screaming them. I watched a boy walk back across the line onto the glass, as if somebody had called him home, and keep walking. I watched a soldier in a gray shirt sit with his head in his hands and weep because he could not remember which of the faces inside him was his mother.
"They were drowning. In the dead. In each other's dead. I knew what it was because I had been doing it to myself for nineteen years, a little at a time, on purpose, and here it was all at once, in an afternoon, to people who had never asked for it.
"I did not know how to stop it.
"So I did the only thing I knew. I carried."
Lyra had to stop. Her voice had gone. She sat with her good hand pressed flat on the page.
Vespera got up from her chair on the far side of the table. Slowly, leaning on her stick. She came round the table and took the tin cup from beside the bucket and filled it, and put it into Lyra's bandaged hand, and stood there until Lyra drank. Then she took the cup back and went and sat down again, and said nothing.
Lyra wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist.
"I went to the woman who was screaming names," she read. "I sat down in front of her in the mud and put my hands on her face and took some of it. Not all. I could not have held all. Some. As much as I could stand. And she stopped screaming. And then I was full, and I could not take any more, and there was a man beside me who was not screaming yet, and I took his hand, and I said, Breathe. Breathe it in. A little. Out of me. Not all. A little.
"And he did. And it went from me into him, a little, and I was less full. And he was full, but not drowning.
"And there was a girl beside him.
"I do not know how long it took. All night. All the next day. We sat in rings, in the mud, in that field, all of us, the valley people and the soldiers in their gray shirts with no coats, holding hands, and we breathed the dead in, a little each, round and round, from the full ones into the empty ones, until nobody had more than they could carry. Until nobody was drowning. Until everybody was carrying a little of everybody.
"And then we sat there, in the morning, on the second day. And we looked at each other. And nobody could tell anymore who had been valley and who had been Ministry. We were all gray. We all had the same dead in us. We were all one thing.
"And a girl, a young girl, one of the valley girls, with burns on her arms, dipped her finger in the ashes of the cooking fire and drew a line down her own face. From the top of her head to the end of her nose. Gray. I did not ask her why. I think she did not know. And then she drew it on the face of the soldier next to her. And he let her."
Kestrel stood up.
She did it all at once. Corin heard the spear clatter on the floor. She stood up in the doorway, very straight, taller than any of them except Oren, with her shaved head almost touching the stone of the ceiling, and her hands at her sides, and the gray line running down her face from her crown to the tip of her nose, real gray, gray skin, as smooth as a scar.
She did not say anything.
She stood there for a long moment, looking at the book in Lyra's hands, as if she were going to come and take it. As if she were going to walk out of the room. Corin did not know which.
Then she sat down again. Slowly. The way you sit down when your legs have gone. She sat on the floor against the wall with her hands open on her knees and the spear lying where it had fallen, and she looked at nothing.
"He was one of us," she said.
Her voice was quite flat. Quite quiet.
"The first one. He was the first one."
Nobody answered her.
"Hask knew," said Kestrel. "He must have known. He was old. His grandmother's grandmother was in that field." She shut her eyes. "He never told. Not even when he was dying. Not even this one."
Vespera, across the table, said very quietly, "He never told me either, child. I asked him. Thirty years ago, on that beach. He only said, Somebody who knew how. That was all."
"Somebody who knew how," said Kestrel. She opened her eyes. She looked at Corin. Not at Lyra. At him. "And he is in you."
Corin did not know what to say.
Behind his ear, for a long time, there was nothing. And then there was something. Not words. A kind of shrinking. The feeling of an old man in a corner of a room trying very hard to be smaller than he is.
Tell her, said Malakor, at last. His voice was very rough. Tell her I'm sorry. Tell her I didn't think it would last. I thought they'd all be dead in a winter, the lot of them. I didn't think there'd be children. I didn't think there'd be a camp, and tents, and bread, and goats. A long pause. Tell her I didn't know about the line. The one on the face. Tell her I never saw it again. I didn't know they kept it.
Corin told her. All of it. Slowly, the way the old man said it.
Kestrel listened with her face perfectly still.
When he had finished, she reached out, without looking, and picked up her spear from the floor, and laid it across her knees again. And put her hand flat on it.
"We kept it," she said.
