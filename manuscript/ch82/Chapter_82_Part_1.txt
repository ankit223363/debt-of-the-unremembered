Chapter 82: Malakor's Journal, Part 1 of 5: A Square of Light
A Square of Light
The light came into Sola's command room through a shaft in the roof, one gray square of it, and fell exactly on the map table, as if somebody had planned it.
Somebody had, Corin thought. She had.
He stood at the end of the long table with his weight on the crutch and looked at it. The square lay on the glass over the middle of the valley, gray and soft, the color of the sky before rain. It lit up the blue river and the green strips of the fields and the tiny painted houses of Applegarth, and the dust in the air above it turned slowly, gold now, in the light. Everything outside the square was dim. The iron chairs, pushed back. The walls with their black scorch patches. The oak chair at the head of the table, with the striped horse blanket folded over its back.
He did not look at the top left corner of the map. He knew it was there. He did not look.
He had woken in the guard room, on the bench under the brown coat, with the journal under his head and his neck stiff, and for a long moment he had not known where he was.
That happened every morning now. It had happened every morning for as long as he could remember, which was not very long. He woke, and there was a ceiling, and he did not know whose ceiling it was. And then there was a voice.
"Corin."
That was how it began. Every morning. Her voice, saying his name. Before anything else. Before he opened his eyes. And he knew the voice. That was the strange thing, the thing he could never quite get used to. He did not know her. He would look at her face, when he opened his eyes, and it would be a stranger's face. A thin face, sharp at the chin, with dark eyes, and short dark hair cut ragged with a knife, and a cut on the lip. A stranger. But he knew the voice. He had known it in the well, in the dark, inside Sola, when he had not known his own name. He had come up the well on it.
This morning he had opened his eyes, and she was kneeling on the stone floor beside the bench, with the red light of the brazier on one side of her face.
"Corin," she said again. "Good morning. Do you want me to read you?"
He had not known what she meant. Then he had looked down, and seen that his shirt was open, and that there was writing on his chest.
Blue-black writing. Not on paper. In his skin. Neat capital letters, small and thin and very even, running in lines across his chest under the left collarbone and down over his ribs. He could see them. But he could not read them. They were upside down to him. He tried, bending his chin down to his chest, and the letters swam and turned and would not stay still, the way letters do in a mirror.
"Here," she said. "Let me."
And she had leaned over him, close but not touching, and put her finger above the first line. A finger's width above it. Not on his skin.
"Your name is Corin Vane," she read.
He had known that. When she said it, he knew it. It went into him like a nail going into soft wood, and held.
She moved her finger down.
"You are twenty-eight."
That one did not go in. It never did. Twenty-eight. It was a number, and he could not hold numbers past nine. They went past him like birds past a window. He knew it was meant to be his age. He knew it the way he knew that the sea was salt: because somebody had told him. But when he reached for what twenty-eight meant, his hand closed on air.
He let it go. She moved her finger down.
"You were born in Oakhaven."
He was quiet for a moment. She waited. She always waited, at that one.
Then she read the next. "You used to fear the dark." And under it, a little apart, by itself: "Not anymore."
"Is that true?" he said. He said it every morning. She had told him so.
"You said it was. In the chapel. You said, I think it is. Now."
"Then it's true."
She moved her finger down. To his side. To his ribs. And her voice changed a little, the way it always did here. He had noticed that this morning. He did not know if he had noticed it before.
"Lyra Thorne," she read. "Partner."
And under it: "No lies between you."
She stopped there. She took her finger away.
"There's one more," he said. He could feel it. Not with his fingers. They felt nothing. But he could feel where it was, on the left side, low, below his heart. A small one.
She looked at it. Her mouth did something at the corner.
"You walk like a heron," she said.
He had laughed. He did not know why. It was not funny. Or it was, but not that funny. It came up out of him anyway, short and surprised, his own laugh. And she had looked away quickly, at the brazier, the way he had seen her look away before.
And there was another line, below that one, lower, along his lowest rib. In different letters. Old curling ones, like the letters on old stones, that she could not read and he could not read either. Vespera had put that one there and would not say what it meant. For later, she said. When I say.
Lyra did not read that one. She never read that one.
Then she had sat back on her heels and looked at him, and he had looked at her, and for a moment neither of them had said anything at all.
"All right?" she said.
"All right."
He had buttoned his shirt. It took him a long time, with his fingers. She did not help.
Now he stood at the end of Sola's table in the gray light, with the journal held against his chest, and the others were coming in behind him. Lyra first. Then Vespera, slowly, with one blue hand on the wall, grumbling about her knees. Then Kestrel, with her spear, who went at once to the doorway and stood with her back to the wall beside it, facing the passage. Then Oren, who did not come in. He stood in the doorway too, on the other side from Kestrel, with his hands behind his back, and looked at the oak chair.
Well, said Malakor.
The old voice. Dry and close. Behind his left ear, where it lived. Like a bad habit, the old man had said. Like a stone at the bottom of a pond.
Are we going to stand about all morning admiring her furniture, boy, or are we going to read?
Corin did not answer. You did not need to answer, with Malakor. He heard you anyway.
He went along the side of the table to the place where the gray light lay. And there, at the edge of the square, there was an iron chair that had been pushed back, and he pulled it forward. It scraped on the stone. He did not sit in it. He held it for her.
Lyra looked at the chair. Then at him.
"The light's best here," he said.
She sat. He put the journal down in front of her on the glass, in the gray square, over the painted valley. Over the river and the orchards and the little church of Applegarth. And then he lowered himself, slowly, with his stiff leg out to the side, into the iron chair next to hers. Not touching. A hand's width between his elbow and hers.
She untied the cord. She folded back the oilcloth. She opened the cover.
The Book of Malakor, called the Grafted. Written plain, so that any fool can read it.
He could see it from where he sat. The big square brown letters. He could read them, if he went slowly. He had read them last night, over her shoulder. He did not remember last night. But it was in the Ledger, in his own crooked capitals, so it had happened.
She turned the page.
And Corin shut his eyes.
He did it on purpose. He did not know why at first. Then he did. Because it was easier, with his eyes shut, to hear only her voice. To let it come into him the way it had come into him in the well. If he watched the page, he would try to read along, and lose his place, and get tired, and the words would slide off. If he only listened, they might stay.
Go on, girl, said Malakor. He's ready. I'm ready. I've been ready for sixty years.
Corin did not tell her that. She began anyway.
The First Page
She read it slowly. All of it. She did not stop.
"I was born on the far side of the Glass, before there was a Glass, in a city where they burn the dead on pyres and the sky is lit with them all night. I came north at nineteen because I was a fool and wanted a war. I found one. It lasted longer than I did.
"I am an old man now and I am writing this in a room under the ground, in the city of Veridia, with the door shut, and a rod on the bench beside me that I made with my own hands. I have made a great many of them. I am good at it. I want whoever reads this to know that first. I am very good at what I do, and what I do is wicked, and I knew it was wicked for most of the time I was doing it, and I did it anyway, because I was good at it, and because they paid me, and because I was afraid.
"That is the whole of this book, if you want it short. You can stop here. The rest is only how.
"I am writing it plain because I have read a great many books by clever men, and most of them were written so that you would think the man was clever, and not so that you would understand him. I do not care what you think of me. I want you to understand. So I have put it in plain letters and short words, and if you cannot read those, find somebody who can, and make them read it to you, and make them go slowly.
"If you are Corvell, I am sorry. If you are Linus, I am sorry. If you are somebody else, I am sorry for you too, because if you have this book in your hands it means you have come a long way and it has cost you something, and I would rather it had not.
"My name is Malakor. It was not always. The other one was taken off me, a long time ago, by the people I worked for, and I do not miss it. They call me the Grafted, because of what I did to myself. I will tell you about that too. Do not skip it."
Lyra stopped.
Corin opened his eyes. She was sitting very still, with her bandaged hand flat on the glass beside the book and her good hand on the page, and she was looking at the last line.
The room was very quiet. The dust turned in the gray light. Outside the square, the iron chairs stood pushed back from the table as if they were listening too.
Nobody said anything for a while.
Then Vespera, sitting on one of the iron chairs on the far side of the table with her blue hands folded on the head of an old stick she had found somewhere, said:
"Well. He's honest. I'll give him that."
Tell her, said Malakor, that I'm honest on paper. In person I was a liar like everybody else. Paper's easier. Paper doesn't look at you.
Corin told her.
Vespera snorted. But she did not look displeased.
Kestrel had not moved from the doorway. She was standing with her back against the wall and her spear upright beside her, and her face to the passage, as always. But Corin saw that her head had turned, a little. Toward the table. Toward Lyra's voice. As if she were listening with one ear and watching with both eyes.
"A city where they burn the dead on pyres," Kestrel said, slowly. She did not turn her head any farther. "The far side. Kesh-Amaran."
Corin looked at her.
"You know it?"
"This one has never been there. Nobody goes there. It is too far. Beyond the north edge of the Glass there is more Glass, and then mountains, and then more." Kestrel was quiet a moment. "But the old ones tell of it. A city of fires. They burn their dead in the open, on high stacks of wood, and the smoke goes up all night, and they believe the dead go up with it. Into the sky. Free."
"They don't keep them," said Lyra.
"No. They let them go." Kestrel looked at the floor. "We carry ours. They burn theirs. In the south, you sell yours." Something moved at the corner of her mouth, and it was not a smile. "Three ways."
Four, said Malakor. There's a fourth way, boy. You'll find out what it is, by the end of this. Go on.
Corin did not tell anybody that.
"Go on," he said to Lyra.
She looked at him. He did not know what she saw in his face.
"Are you all right?"
"Yes." He thought about it. "He's listening. He's quiet. He wants you to go on."
"Does he mind? Hearing it read?"
Corin listened.
There was a long pause behind his ear. Longer than he expected. And then the old voice came back, but lower. Rougher.
Tell her I mind very much, said Malakor. Tell her it's like hearing yourself on the other side of a wall, saying things you'd forgotten you said. Tell her to go on anyway.
Corin told her.
Lyra looked down at the page for a moment. Then she nodded, and turned it, and smoothed it flat with her good hand, and began to read again.
The Stretcher Boy
The next pages were about the war.
Not Sola's war. An older one. Lyra read the first lines and stopped and looked up and said, "Which war?" and Oren, in the doorway, said, "The long one," before Malakor could say anything at all.
Corin opened his eyes and looked at him.
"My grandfather's father fought in it," said Oren. "Before he came south. Before Sola's time." He shifted his shoulders against the doorframe. "Nobody remembers what it was about. That's why they call it the long one. It went on so long that by the end nobody could remember how it started, and they stopped because everybody was too tired. That's what my grandfather said. They stopped because they were tired, Oren. That's how wars end. Not because anybody wins."
He's right, said Malakor. Tell him he's right. Tell him his great-grandfather was very likely one of the fools I carried. We carried a great many fools. I was one of them.
Corin told him. Oren did not smile. But he nodded, slowly, as if that were a thing worth knowing.
Lyra read on.
"I came north at nineteen because I was a fool and wanted a war, and when I got here they gave me a stretcher. I was angry about that. I had wanted a sword. They said a boy who had never held a sword was no use with one, and a boy with two arms was some use with a stretcher, and they put me on the front end of one with a big slow man called Davey Linn on the back end, and we carried.
"We carried for two years. Up and down the lines. Through mud. Through the rivers. In the long war there was always a river, and always somebody on the other side of it, and always somebody lying in it. We carried them out. Some of them lived. Most did not. You got used to it. That is the first thing I learned, and it is the worst thing I know: you get used to it.
"Davey Linn did not get used to it. Davey Linn talked to every man we carried. He asked them their names and where they came from, and he told them about his mother's kitchen, so they would have something to think about besides their legs. I used to tell him to shut up. I am sorry for that now."
Lyra stopped. She looked at Corin.
"Is he all right?"
Corin listened. Behind his ear there was nothing. No voice. Only a kind of stillness, like the stillness of a man holding his breath.
"He's listening," said Corin. "Go on."
"At a river crossing in our second autumn, a ford with willows on both banks and the water up to our thighs and the arrows coming over like rain, Davey Linn was hit. In the back. Through the stretcher. We had a man on it, and the man was already dead, and Davey Linn was hit through the canvas and the dead man both, and he went down in the water, and I dropped my end and went back for him.
"I got him onto the far bank, under the willows. I do not know how. He was twice my size. I sat in the mud with his head in my lap and the water running off us both and he looked up at me and he was trying to tell me something. About his mother's kitchen. I could see it in his face. He wanted to tell me one more time.
"And then he died.
"And when he died, something came off him."
Lyra's voice had gone very quiet. Corin kept his eyes shut and listened to it.
"I did not know what it was. I know now. I have spent my life knowing now. It was gray. Like breath on a cold morning. It came up off his face, off his open mouth, a thin gray mist, and it hung in the air between us, and I was bent close over him, crying, with my mouth open, and I breathed it in.
"I did not mean to. It was an accident. Everything that has ever mattered in my life has been an accident, and that was the first.
"I breathed it in, and it went down into me, cold, and I thought I was going to die too. I lay down in the mud beside him. And then I was in a kitchen.
"A small one. Low. With a bread oven in the wall, and the door of it open, and the heat coming out. Onions hanging from the beam in long plaits. A blue jug on the table with milk in it. A woman with her back to me, big, in a brown dress, with her sleeves rolled up and flour to the elbows, singing. And I knew that she was his mother. And I knew the song she was singing. And I knew that the third step up from the yard was loose and you had to step over it. I had never been in that kitchen in my life.
"It lasted as long as a breath. Then I was in the mud again, under the willows, with the arrows coming over, and Davey Linn dead with his head in my lap.
"But I still knew about the step.
"That was the first Ash."
Nobody in the room said anything.
Corin opened his eyes. He did not mean to. They opened by themselves.
The gray light lay on the table. On the open book, and Lyra's hand on it, and the painted valley under the glass. The dust turned in the light. Somewhere in the passage, far off, a drip of water fell from the ceiling into a puddle that had not been there yesterday. Plink.
He knew that. Not the kitchen. Not the step. But the thing that had happened. He knew it in his hands. He had done it himself, he did not know how many times. A jar on a desk. A face. A room. The way a stranger's room went into you and stayed, and you knew where the loose step was, and the blue jug, and the song.
He had done it so many times he had stopped noticing.
You get used to it, said Malakor. Very quietly. I told you. That's the worst thing I know.
"Davey Linn," said Kestrel.
Everybody looked at her. She was still standing by the door with her spear. But she had turned her head all the way round now. She was looking at the book.
"He breathed in a dying man's breath," she said slowly. "By accident. Crying. And carried the man's mother's kitchen away inside him." She was quiet for a moment. "That is what we do. At Last Thorn. That is the carrying."
"Yes," said Lyra.
"But he was alone. He did not know to share it." Kestrel's hand went up, slowly, to her face. To the gray line that ran down from her crown over her forehead to the tip of her nose. She touched it. "He carried it alone."
"It was only one kitchen," said Lyra.
"The first one is always only one kitchen," said Kestrel.
And she turned back to the passage.
