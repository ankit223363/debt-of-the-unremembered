Chapter 48: The Man Who Cannot Read, Part 3 of 5: Fever
Fever
The line broke.
It did not break all at once. It broke the way ice breaks on a river in spring, with a crack, and then another, and then a long grinding roar as everything begins to move. Somebody near Bridie Kane snatched up a child and ran. Then somebody else. Then a woman at the top of the Stairs, by the stage, screamed fever too, high and thin, and pulled her little boy out of the line by the arm so hard that he fell over, and dragged him across the fish hall floor between the empty tables on his back, kicking, and away into Fish Street.
And then everybody was running.
Corin had seen crowds run before. At fires. At the Bell, the night of the raid, in the autumn. He had never seen two hundred mothers with children run in the snow down a flight of iced stone steps. He did not want to see it again. They went down the Stairs and up the Stairs and along the river wall in every direction at once, with their shawls flying and their children in their arms or by the hand or by the collar, slipping, falling, getting up, screaming. Not at anything. Just screaming, the way a flock of birds screams when one bird sees a hawk.
And the step women went with them. He saw them. Big women in brown shawls and men's boots, going down the Stairs among the running mothers like barges among rowing boats, steady, not hurrying, with their arms out. Gathering children. Herding. Pushing. This way, lovey. Down here. Mind the step. Keep your hand in your pocket. He saw Bridie Kane go past the bottom of the Stairs with a child under each arm and her bucket hanging from her elbow, and never once look back.
The constables at the river wall had their rattles out. The rattles went round and round, tearing, and made it worse. One of them blew his whistle. Another shouted something about order and in the name of the Archon and was knocked flat on his back in the snow by a woman with twins.
The brown men round the stage did not know what to do. Corin watched them not know. They stood about the stage in their long belted coats, looking at each other, looking at the Stairs, looking at the man at the high desk with the ledger, who had stopped writing and was standing on his stool to see better, with his pen in the air.
And in the middle of it all, between the two braziers, in his high-backed chair with the red cushion, the Under-Secretary of the Office of Public Health sat quite still with the silver box open on his knees, and watched them go.
He did not get up. He did not shout. He did not look surprised, or angry, or frightened. He sat with his gray-gloved hands folded on the lid of the box and his stick across his knees, and watched two hundred mothers and children run away from him through the snow, and his kind pink face was perfectly calm.
The little girl of six was still on the stage.
She had not moved. She was standing in front of his chair, between the braziers, where she had been standing when the scream came, with the sweet in her hand. Her mother was gone. Run, with the others. The girl stood alone on the red-edged stage with the snow falling round her and looked at the empty fish hall, and the empty Stairs, and did not cry.
Pallister looked at her.
Then he leaned forward, and took the sweet out of her hand, very gently. And put it back in the silver box. And closed the lid.
"Run along," he said. Kindly. "Your mother will be looking for you."
The girl ran.
And in less time than it takes to tell, the fish hall was empty. And the Stairs were empty. Forty steps of old gray stone going down to the river, trodden to slush, scattered with dropped things. A shawl. A mitten. A child's wooden horse with its leg broken off. And the snow coming down on all of it, slowly, as if nothing had happened.
Only the stage was left. The red cloth. The braziers burning. The six brown men, standing about it. The clerk on his stool. And the old man in the chair.
And at the corner of Fish Street, under the overhang of the chandler's shop, the barrow.
Corin understood, too late, that they should have run with the others.
Silas understood it at the same moment. Corin felt the barrow jerk as the doctor took hold of its handles. And then stop. Because there was nowhere to go that was faster than a man on foot, and the barrow was heavy, and Fish Street went uphill.
On the stage, the old man was getting up.
Slowly. With the brown man's arm. With his stick. The stiff left leg out in front of him. Down the three wooden steps, one at a time. Across the fish hall floor, between the empty tables, toward the corner of Fish Street. Not hurrying. Not at all. The way an old man walks across his own garden in the evening to look at a rose.
He stopped beside the barrow.
Corin had shut his eyes. He lay on his back on the sacks in the bottom of the barrow with the gray blanket up to his chin and his hands under it, and his face turned up to the snow, and his eyes shut, and his mouth a little open. The way the children lay on their pallets in the crypt. The way a man lies with a fever.
He could smell him.
Peppermint. Sharp and clean and cold, so strong it stung the inside of his nose. And under it, faint, something else. Something sweet and soft and brown. Like pears that have gone past ripe on a windowsill.
"Doctor Silas," said the soft dry voice.
"Under-Secretary."
Silas's voice was quite steady. Corin did not know how.
"I've been meaning to call on you," said Pallister. "For weeks. I'm so sorry I haven't. The Office keeps me terribly busy." A pause. "It was you who wrote all those letters, wasn't it. About the little ones in your crypt. Four, I think. Very clear letters. A very good hand. I have them all on my desk." Another pause. "Forty-one in my care, the last one said."
"Fifty-one, now."
"Fifty-one." Pallister sounded sorry. "Dear me. And one lost, I hear. A little boy. Ned. Such a pity." The snow fell. "And now fever, too. Spotted fever, that good woman said. On my Stairs."
Corin felt him lean over the barrow.
He felt the cold come off him, the peppermint cold, onto his face. He felt the old man looking down at him. At his shut eyes. At his gray cheeks. He did not move. He did not breathe. He made his mouth stay a little open, and his face stay slack, and he thought about nothing. He thought about the glass passage. Think about nothing. He thought about the first page of the Ledger. Your name is Corin Vane.
"How very gray he is," said Pallister softly. "I don't think I've ever seen spotted fever make a man so gray."
Silas did not say anything.
Then Corin felt something touch the blanket. On his chest. Light. Small. A little weight, no more than a moth landing.
"For the patient," said Pallister. "When he wakes. It may help with the fever." He straightened up. Corin heard his knee crack, the stiff left one. "And Doctor. About your little ones."
"Yes?"
"The Office has decided. I signed the order myself, last night." The soft voice was very kind. "We're going to relieve you of them. All fifty-one. It isn't right, a parish doctor with no money and no nurses, keeping so many sick children in a damp crypt under a church. The Office has a House of Rest, out past the tanneries. Clean. Dry. Warm. With a garden." A pause. "Our carts will call at Saint Bride's on Monday morning, to fetch them. Early. Before the Midwinter bells. So they'll be settled in their new beds by the evening, and can hear the bells from there."
The snow fell on the barrow.
"Monday," said Silas.
"Monday." Corin heard the stick tap on the stones. Turning. "Do have them ready, Doctor. Wrapped up warm. And I'd take the patient indoors, if I were you. Fever doesn't like the cold."
The footsteps went away. Slowly. The stick, and the stiff leg, and the brown man's boots. Back across the fish hall floor. To the carriage at the edge of it.
Corin heard the carriage door shut.
He heard the horses start, and the wheels hiss in the snow, and the harness creak, going away along the river toward the bridges. And then nothing.
He opened his eyes.
On the gray blanket on his chest, just under his chin, there was a sweet. A small white thing, in a twist of paper.
He did not touch it.
Silas reached down, very slowly, and picked it up between finger and thumb, and held it up in the gray light, and looked at it. And his long tired face did something Corin had never seen it do. It went perfectly white, and perfectly still, and old.
"Monday," said the doctor again.
And he put the sweet in his coat pocket, beside the others.
Hook Lane
They did not go straight back up the hill.
Silas turned the barrow the other way, at the top of Fish Street, without a word, and pushed it down. Down the hill, toward the river, along the bottom of the Lower Docks, through streets that Corin knew from the Ledger and not from his feet. Narrow ones. Crooked ones. With the snow trodden to brown slush in the gutters and the washing frozen stiff on the lines overhead. Silas pushed the barrow through them, slowly, leaning his long thin weight on the handles, with his breath going up in clouds and his crooked spectacles misting over, so that he had to stop every so often and take them off and wipe them on his sleeve.
Corin did not ask where they were going. He knew.
Hook Lane was a dead end. A short, steep, narrow lane of old houses with their upper floors leaning out over it, so close at the top that the snow could hardly get in between the eaves, and the lane underneath was dark and wet and smelled of drains. At the end of it, a wall. At the foot of the wall, a pump, frozen. And on the left, the third door down, a door that had once been green, with a piece of sacking nailed over a broken pane in it.
Silas stopped the barrow outside.
"Stay here," he said. "Don't move. If anybody comes, you're asleep, and you've got a fever."
"I know."
The doctor took his black bag off the end of the barrow, and went up the one step to the door that had once been green, and knocked. Not loudly. Three times. And waited. And knocked again.
The door opened a crack. A woman's face looked out. Thin. White. With red rims to the eyes.
Corin knew her. Not her name. Her face. He had seen it an hour ago, at the bottom of the stage, with her hands twisted in her shawl, watching her little boy in his red woolen cap go up the three steps on his own.
She looked at Silas.
She looked at his black bag.
And then her face crumpled up, all at once, the way paper crumples in a fire, and she pulled the door wide, and Silas went in, stooping under the low lintel, and the door shut behind him.
Corin lay in the barrow in Hook Lane, in the snow, and listened.
He could not hear much. The house was old, and the walls were thick, and the snow made everything quiet. He heard the woman's voice, high and fast, and the doctor's under it, low and slow. He heard a child begin to cry. Then stop. Then a sound he did not want to hear. A small, wretched, gulping sound, over and over, and the doctor's voice going on and on through it, steady, calm, saying something Corin could not catch.
Steady, it sounded like. Steady now. Good lad. Good lad. Once more.
Then nothing.
Then the woman crying.
Corin looked up at the strip of gray sky between the leaning eaves, with the snow coming down out of it, and did not move.
It was a long time before the door opened again.
Silas came out. He stood on the step for a moment with his black bag in his hand and his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, and looked up at the sky, the way Corin was looking at it. His face was gray. There was something on the front of his coat that he had not wiped off yet.
He came down the one step and put the bag on the end of the barrow.
"Some," he said. "I got some of it. Not all. He'd had it in him an hour. And he's only four." He took his spectacles down off his forehead and put them on, crooked. "He'll sleep now. When he wakes up, he'll know his mother. He'll know his name. He'll know his street." He paused. "He might not know the penny. Or the man who gave it to him. Or the snow on the Stairs. That's the bit that's gone."
"That's not so bad."
"No." Silas looked at the door that had once been green. "That's not so bad. Today." He picked up the handles of the barrow. "And next Friday, if he goes up the Stairs again, and eats another one, it'll take the week before. And the Friday after, the month. And the Friday after that—"
He did not finish.
He pushed the barrow back up Hook Lane, and out of it, and along the bottom of the Lower Docks toward the next one.
There were six more. The step women had known where every one of them lived. Juno had come running up the Stairs to the barrow on the sides of her feet as the crowd scattered, and told Silas, in a quick low voice, with her hand on the edge of the barrow: Poll Danby's boy, Hook Lane. The Raffertys' girl, the court behind the tannery. The two little Webbs, Tar Street. Annie Bright's baby, she gave it hers to suck on, Lock Row. Seven doors, in seven narrow streets, in the snow. And at every one, the doctor stopped the barrow, and took his bag, and went in, and was gone a long time, and came out with his face a little grayer.
Corin lay in the barrow and waited outside every door.
At the fourth one, in a court behind the tannery where the air was so thick with the stink of the pits that it made his eyes water, a drum boy came round the corner.
He was alone. A boy of about fourteen, in the long black coat that was too big for him and the flat black cap with the white eye on it. He had his drum on its strap over his shoulder, and his two sticks pushed through the strap, and a slate in his hand. He came round the corner of the court, walking fast, the way soldiers walk when they are cold, and saw the barrow, and stopped.
He looked at it.
At the gray wood. At the gray blanket. At the white card tied to the end of it, by the boots.
Corin had shut his eyes as soon as he heard the boots on the cobbles. He lay with them shut, with his mouth a little open, and his face slack, and listened.
The boy came closer. Two steps. Three. Corin heard the slate scrape against the brass of the drum as he lifted it. Heard the chalk, squeaking.
Then the boy stopped.
Corin felt him looking. At his face. At the gray of it. At the blanket pulled up to the chin.
And then, very quietly, so quietly that Corin was not sure afterward that he had heard it at all, the drum boy said:
"Fever."
Not to Corin. To himself. The way you say a word that you have just read, to make sure of it.
And Corin heard the chalk again on the slate. Squeaking. Writing.
And then the boots. Going away. Fast. Not walking now. Running. Back round the corner of the court, the way he had come, and away down the next street, with the drum banging against his hip.
Corin opened his eyes.
He lay in the barrow in the stinking court and looked up at the gray sky, and let out his breath. And found that he was shaking. Not from the cold. From something under the cold. He lay there and shook, and could not stop, the way a dog shakes when it has been in the river.
Fever. A boy reading a word off a card. And running.
So that the drum boys would write it on their slates and carry it back to their commander. Court behind the tannery. Doctor's barrow. Fever case. And a man on a gray horse with a scar in his eyebrow would read it at the end of the day, with all the other slates, in some cold room. And go past the doctor's barrow tomorrow, and the next day, without looking at it, because nobody looks twice at fever.
Or he would read it, and remember a barrow at the top of the Steps on Wednesday morning, and a white card. And a doctor from Saint Bride's. And the gray face of a man who did not look like he had any fever at all.
Corin did not know which.
He lay in the barrow and shook until the doctor came out of the door in the court behind the tannery, and put his bag on the end, and looked at him.
"What?" said Silas.
"A drum boy. He read the card. He wrote it down."
Silas stood very still.
Then he picked up the handles of the barrow and turned it round in the narrow court, carefully, and pushed it out the other way. Not the way the drum boy had gone.
"Three more," he said. "Then home."
They did the three more.
The last was Lock Row. A row of tiny houses with their backs to the river wall, so close to the water that the ground floors were always wet and the walls were green to the height of a man's knee. Annie Bright's was the fourth. Silas went in with his bag, and Corin lay in the barrow outside and listened, the way he had listened at every other door, for the gulping sound and the doctor's steady voice going on through it.
It did not come.
What came instead, after a long time, was a sound he had not heard all day. A baby crying. Not weakly. Not the thin wet mewing of the children in the crypt. Loud. Furious. Red-faced, kicking, outraged, the way a baby cries when it is hungry and cold and wants the whole world to know about it. It went on and on, through the green wall, out into Lock Row.
And under it, a woman laughing.
The door opened. Silas came out. His face was gray, like all day. But there was something in it that had not been there at any of the other doors.
"She spat it out," he said.
Corin looked at him.
"The baby. Annie Bright gave her the sweet to suck on, on the Stairs, to stop her crying in the cold. Like the step women said." Silas put his bag on the end of the barrow. "And the baby sucked it for about the count of three, and didn't like the taste, and spat it out down the front of Annie's shawl. And Annie didn't notice, in all the running, till she got home and found it stuck to her." He took off his spectacles. "Too bitter, under the peppermint. The grayroot. Babies know. They've got more sense than the rest of us."
"Is she all right?"
"She's furious," said the doctor. "She's hungry. She wants her mother. She knows exactly who she is and she's telling the whole of Lock Row about it." He put his spectacles back on. "She's perfect."
He stood in the snow outside the green-walled house for a moment, listening to the baby scream.
Then he picked up the handles of the barrow.
"One," he said. "Out of eleven. One that it didn't touch at all." He began to push. "I'll take one."
And all the long way back up Saint Bride's Hill, in the falling snow, past the Shambles with its red hooks, past the corner of Chandler Row with its WANTED posters going soft and gray under the wet, the doctor did not say anything at all. And Corin lay in the barrow and looked at the sky and thought about Monday.
Fifty-one children. In a crypt with one door. And carts, early, before the Midwinter bells.
And under the dome of the Bourse on the same night, at a quarter to midnight, a door marked with a V and a 3, and a jar with an old man's last evening in it, sealed, behind three doors, waiting.
He could not be in both places.
He could not be in either. He could not walk.
At the top of the hill, at the lych-gate of Saint Bride's, under its little snowy roof, Silas stopped the barrow and stood for a moment with his hands on the handles, getting his breath.
"I won't let them go," he said. To the gate. To the snow. Not to Corin. "Not to that house. Not one of them. I don't care whose order it is."
"I know."
"I don't know how."
"I know," said Corin again.
Silas looked down at him in the barrow.
"Neither do you," he said. "Do you."
"No," said Corin. "Not yet."
