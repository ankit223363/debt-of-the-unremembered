Chapter 48: The Man Who Cannot Read, Part 5 of 5: The Lily Paper
The Lily Paper
Oren reached inside Terrence's coat.
The coat was hanging on the iron hook in the wall of the vault, where Lyra had hung it last night, stiff with Dace's blood down the front. Mrs. Fitch had sponged at it with cold water and salt for an hour in the morning, and the blood had gone from brown to a kind of rust, and would not go any further. Oren got up off the floor, with his good hand on the wall, and went to it. And put his good hand into the inside pocket on the left, over where the heart would be. And took out a square of oilcloth, folded small.
He came back and sat down again, against the wall, and unwrapped it on his knee.
Inside the oilcloth there was one paper. He had carried two, on Monday. The other, the Health notice off the board at the Fish Stairs, he had given to Corin on Tuesday, and Corin had read it, and it had gone into the Ledger. This one he had kept.
Corin knew it. He had given it to Oren himself, a long time ago, in Vespera's room in Hook Lane, before the sewers. A single sheet of thick cream paper, folded small, creased and smudged with soot, with one corner scorched brown where a spark had caught it. And the smell. Even from the bench, even through the pear-sweet air of the crypt, Corin could smell it. Faint now. Thick and white and heavy. A room full of flowers with the windows shut.
Oren unfolded it.
He did it slowly, on his knee, with his good hand. The paper was soft from being unfolded and folded again every day for a month. It opened easily. It lay flat on his knee, in the lamplight, and he looked down at it.
Corin watched his face.
He did not know what he had expected. Something. A flinch. A frown. The look of a man who has put his hand on a stove. But there was nothing. Oren looked down at the cream paper on his knee, at the lines of writing on it in a clerk's neat hand, line after line, close, careful, and his face did not change at all. It was the face of a man looking at a pattern. At frost on a window. At the marks on a moth's wing.
He looked at it for a long time.
Then he looked up, at Corin.
"I used to read this," he said. "Every day. On the barge. By the stove, in the afternoons. I don't know why. It made me angry every time." He looked down at it again. "I knew it by heart. I could have told you every word. Now I don't know where the words are." He touched the page with one finger. Lightly. In the middle. "Is this the bit about the children?"
Corin looked at where the finger was.
He did not need to see the paper. He knew it too. Every line. He had read it a hundred times, in Vespera's room, in the sewers, on the barge.
"No," he said. "That's the lands. All my papers, all my lands, all my rights in the Thorne name. The children are lower down."
Oren's finger moved down. Two lines. Three.
"Here?"
"Yes."
Oren looked at the place under his finger. At the marks.
"Read it to me," he said.
Corin read it. From memory. He did not need the page. He lay on the bench with his eyes on the skulls in their niche and said it into the cold air of the vault, slowly, the way you say a thing you wish you did not know.
"I, Aurelian Thorne, being of sound mind," he said. And then the clerk's lines, giving everything away, one after another, the way he had read them on the barge. "All my papers, all my lands, all my rights in the Thorne name, and the keeping of my children." He stopped. Then went on. "I remember that I have two children only. A son and a daughter. I remember there were never any others."
The vault was very quiet.
Lyra, on the floor beside Mrs. Doyle, with the finished gray gown across her knees, did not move.
Oren looked at the paper. At the place under his finger, where the marks said a thing he could not read any more about children that there had never been any others of.
"It smells of her," he said. "Still. The flowers." He lifted it, a little, toward his face, the way he used to on the barge, and breathed in. And stopped. His face did something. "I can smell them. I know what they are. I can see them. White. Big. Like trumpets." His mouth worked. "I can't say them. I couldn't say them before. Now I can't read them either." He lowered the paper. "There was a word on here for them, I think. In her name, or her house. I used to see it every day and it was the only place I could still find it. On the paper. When it was gone from my mouth." He looked at the page. "Now it's gone from the paper too."
Nobody said anything.
Oren folded the cream paper up again. Small. Along its old creases. He held it in his good hand for a moment. Then he held it out, across the vault, to Lyra.
"You take it," he said. "Tonight. To her house. In the pocket."
Lyra looked at him.
"It's no use to me now," said Oren. "It's just marks. And a smell." His voice was very steady. "But it's her hand that made somebody write it. And it's your father's name on it. And there were never any others." He held it out. "Somebody should carry it into that house who can read it."
Lyra took it.
She held it in both hands. She did not unfold it. She did not need to. Then she opened the gray gown across her knees, and found the left side of the waist, inside, and the seam, and the little pocket that Madame Hesper had sewn there on Saturday, for a list. And slid the folded cream paper into it.
It fitted exactly.
As if it had been made for it.
One Word
Lyra went out at the sixth bell to wash at the far brazier, where Mrs. Fitch had a basin of warm water waiting, and Mrs. Doyle went with her with the gown over her arm. And for a while the side vault was quiet. Corin on his bench. Oren against the wall. Gideon in the arch, carving. Bet asleep. The lamp on the floor between them.
Then Oren said, "Corin."
"Yes."
"Can a man learn one word?"
Corin turned his head on the folded sack and looked at him.
"Not read it," said Oren. "I know I can't read. The letters are gone. All of them. I don't know what any of them are any more, or what sound they make, or how they go together. I tried. On the slate at Hesper's. On the board in the mews. Nothing." He looked at his good hand. "But I can still see. I can see the marks. I can see the shape of them." He paused. "I knew the shape of the H. On her sign. I liked it. Like a little gate. I can still see that. I just can't tell you it's an H."
Corin did not say anything. He waited.
"So I thought," said Oren slowly, "a man might learn a word the way you learn a face. Not by its letters. By its shape. All of it at once. The way you know your own front door in the dark, without reading the number." He looked at Corin. "If I saw it written. On a door. On a paper. On a list of names. I'd know it. Not because I'd read it. Because I'd know the shape."
"Which word?"
Oren looked at the arch of the vault, where the girl had gone out.
He did not answer. He did not need to.
Corin looked at him for a long time.
Then he said, "Gideon. Have you still got your chalk?"
Gideon looked up from his barge. Then he put his hand inside his sling, where he kept his knife and other things, and took out a stub of chalk. Blue. Worn down nearly to nothing, the size of the end of a thumb.
"For marking," said Gideon. "For my cold. Corin's going to look for it on Midwinter. On the jars. H, in blue." He held it out. "It's all I've got left."
"We'll only need a little."
Gideon got up and brought it. He did not give it to Corin. He looked at the stone floor of the vault, the flat gray flags, swept clean by Mrs. Fitch that morning. And at the big man sitting against the wall with his arm in a sling. And then he knelt down on the floor in front of Oren, and held the chalk out to Corin again, and said:
"You say it. I'll do it. Your hands are no good any more. I've seen you hold a pencil."
Corin's mouth went down at the left corner.
"Big," he said. "As big as you can. On the floor. In front of him."
Gideon waited.
"First a line," said Corin. "Straight down. As long as your hand. And then, at the bottom, a line going right. Short. Like a boot."
Gideon drew it on the floor in blue chalk. A straight line down. A short line along the bottom. L.
"That's one," said Corin. "Now, next to it. Two lines going up and out, from the middle, like the arms of a man waving. And one line straight down from where they meet."
Gideon drew it. Y.
"Like a fork in a road," said Oren, very low.
"Yes. Next. A straight line down. And at the top, a round bit going out to the right and coming back to the middle. Like a belly. And then a leg, going down from the middle to the bottom, slanting out."
Gideon drew it slowly, with his tongue between his teeth. R. It came out crooked. The belly was too big. He looked at it, and rubbed it out with the side of his hand, and drew it again. Better.
"And the last," said Corin. "Two lines leaning together at the top, like the roof of a house. Or a tent. And a short line across the middle."
Gideon drew it. A.
He sat back on his heels.
It lay on the gray stone floor of the side vault between them, in the lamplight, in blue chalk. Four shapes. Big. Clumsy. A boy's drawing.
LYRA.
Oren looked at it.
He did not move. He sat against the wall with his left arm in its black sling and his good hand on his knee, and looked down at the four blue shapes on the floor in front of him. And Corin, from the bench, watched him look.
It was not the way he had looked at the lily paper. It was not the way he had looked at the gold letters on the Ledger, or the marks on the card in Fothergill's back room. It was the way Corin had seen him look at the girl herself. Every day. From one pace behind, and one to the left. Not reading. Seeing. Every part of it. The way a man who has walked a road every day for twenty years knows every stone in it.
"A boot," said Oren slowly. He put out his good hand and touched the first shape, very lightly, with one finger. Not rubbing it. Just touching. "A fork in the road." The second. "A gate with a belly and a leg." The third. "A tent." The fourth.
He took his hand away.
He looked at it for a long time.
Then he shut his eyes.
Corin watched his lips move. Not making a sound. A boot. A fork. A gate with a belly. A tent. Over and over. The way he said the list of words that were gone, every morning, before he opened his eyes.
He opened them, and looked at the floor again. Checking. The way you check your kit.
Then he shut them again.
"I've got it," he said, after a while. "I think."
"Say it."
"A boot. A fork. A gate with a belly. A tent." He opened his eyes. "That's her."
"That's her."
Oren nodded.
Gideon was still kneeling on the floor with the blue chalk in his fingers. He was looking at the four shapes. Not at Oren. At the shapes. With his head on one side, the way he looked at a crate when he was working out how heavy it would be.
"Is there one for mine?" he said.
Corin looked at him.
"My name," said Gideon. "Is there a shape for it?"
"Yes."
"Is it long?"
"Six."
Gideon thought about it.
"Then not tonight," he said. "There's not enough chalk." He looked at the stub in his fingers. "And I need it for Midwinter. For the H." He put it back in his sling. "But after. If there's an after. Will you show me?"
"Yes."
Gideon nodded. He did not say thank you. He did not smile. He got up off the floor and went back to the arch, and sat down, and picked up his half-carved barge, and went on carving it.
But he did not rub out the blue shapes on the floor. And when Mrs. Fitch came in an hour later with her broom to sweep the vault for the night, he got up and stood in front of them, and would not let her.
"That's Oren's," he said flatly. "Leave it."
And Mrs. Fitch, bent nearly double over her broom, looked at the four blue shapes on her clean floor, and at the boy, and at the big man against the wall with his eyes on them. And swept round them.
After she had gone, Oren did a thing that Corin did not think he knew anybody was watching.
He lifted his good hand off his knee. And with one finger, in the air in front of him, slowly, very small, he drew. A line down, and a short line along the bottom. A fork. A line down with a belly at the top and a leg going out. Two lines leaning together, and one across.
He did it again. And again. The way a child who has just learned to write its name draws it on everything, on frosted windows and in the dust on tables and on the palm of its own hand, over and over, to make sure it is still there.
Then he put his hand back on his knee, and looked at the floor, at the blue chalk, to check. And nodded to himself, very slightly.
Corin looked away, so that he would not see him seeing.
He thought about the first page of the Ledger. About the line he had written at the bottom of it, an hour ago. Read Oren his page every morning. And he thought that tomorrow, before he read it, he would ask Oren to draw the shape for him first. In the air. The four of them. Just to see that it was still there.
And the morning after.
And every morning, until Midwinter, and after, if there was an after. A boot, a fork, a gate with a belly, a tent. The way he read his own name to himself, every morning of his life, so that he would not lose it.
Going to the Lilies
She came back into the side vault at the half bell after seven, and Corin did not know her.
Not for a moment. Not for the space of a breath. He looked up from the Ledger at the sound of her step in the arch, and saw a stranger there, a young lady in gray, and his eyes went down to the page of faces, the way they always did, the way he could not stop them doing. And then up again.
And it was her.
The gown was on her. Mrs. Doyle's stitches and Madame Hesper's pins, gray silk from the throat to the floor, the long sleeves to the wrist, the line of tiny buttons down the back. It fitted. It fitted the way the mask had fitted, as if somebody had measured her without her knowing. The light from the lamp on the floor moved on it like smoke on water when she breathed.
She had gloves on. White kid, up past the wrist, a little yellow with age at the seams. Madame Hesper's own. I wore them to my own wedding.
And her hair. Cropped, still, ragged at the ends, stopping just below her ears. Mrs. Doyle had wet it and combed it back off her face, flat and close to her head, so that it shone dark in the lamplight like the feathers of a wet bird. There was nothing in it. No pins, no ribbon, no comb. It made her neck look very long, and her face very bare.
She looked like her mother. Corin had never seen her mother. He knew it anyway. He could see it in Oren's face, against the wall.
Oren had got up. Corin had not heard him. He was standing by the wall, very straight, the way he had stood in the arch on the barge, with his left arm in the black sling and his right hand at his side. And he was looking at her.
Not at her face. Down. At the stone floor of the vault, between them. At the four blue chalk shapes that Gideon had drawn there. And then up at her. And then down again. Checking. The way you check a name on a door against the name on a letter.
A boot. A fork. A gate with a belly. A tent.
He looked up at her one more time.
"That's you," he said.
Lyra looked down at the floor.
She saw the shapes. Corin watched her see them. Her face did not change. But she stood very still in the arch in her mother's gown, for a long moment, looking down at her own name in blue chalk on the floor of a crypt, drawn by a boy who could not read for a man who could not read any more.
Then she looked up at Oren.
"Yes," she said. "That's me."
Oren nodded.
"I'm coming," he said. "To the bottom of Comb Street. To the carriage."
"Oren, your arm—"
"Is in a sling. And my legs aren't." He did not move. "One pace behind, and one to the left. I can't read the street names. I don't need to. I know the way."
Lyra looked at him for a long time.
Then she nodded.
She came to the bench.
Corin had sat up. He was sitting on the edge of it, with his stiff leg out along it in its splints and his good leg down on the floor, and the Ledger on his knee. He looked up at her.
"The pocket," he said.
"It's in."
"And you know what you're going to say?"
"No." Lyra's face did not change. "I know what I'm going to ask. I don't know what she's going to say."
"Ask her about the nurse," said Corin.
Lyra looked at him.
"At Fallowfield," said Corin. "Your father's nurse. The one who wrote patient taps in his notes and nothing else for two years. Somebody's been paying her. There's a line in Remy's black book. Sundries, Fallowfield, monthly, by the Councillor's hand." He looked at the Ledger. "I didn't tell you. I found it on Tuesday, when you were asleep. I didn't know what it meant. I still don't."
Lyra was quiet.
"Rule one," she said.
"Rule one." His mouth went down at the left corner. "I'm telling you now."
She looked at him for a moment longer. Then she bent down, in the gray silk, carefully, the way you bend in a gown that has been pinned and sewn and does not want to be bent, and put her gloved hand on his face. On the cheek. On the mole under his left eye.
"There you are," she said.
And went.
Oren went after her, one pace behind and one to the left. Gideon went after Oren, with his half-carved barge in his sling and his knife. They went down the long low hall of the crypt between the pallets, past the braziers, past the children, and the children did not look at them. Except one.
Polly, on her pallet near the door, with her face washed and her eyes on the lamp. As the gray gown went past her, rustling, her eyes left the lamp. And followed it. All the way to the door.
Then went back.
The low door opened, and the cold came in, and the snow, and they were gone up the three stone steps into the dark.
Corin sat on the edge of the bench and looked at the arch for a long time.
Then he opened the Ledger, and found the pencil, and wrote. Slowly. In his square careful capitals, pressing hard, because his fingers did not feel the pencil any more. He wrote the day. All of it. The way he always did. So that in the morning, if he woke and did not know, he could find it.
Fri. Fish Stairs, 8th bell. Pallister in person. 200 children. Step women told them: pocket it. P. saw. Made the first boy eat it in front of him. 11 eaten. 137 saved (Silas has them). Bridie Kane cried FEVER and broke the line. P. came to the barrow. Smelled of peppermint and pears. Said: "How very gray he is." Left a sweet on my chest.
P. says the Health's carts come to St Bride's MONDAY morning, before the Midwinter bells, for all 51. To the House of Rest past the tanneries.
Monday is Midwinter.
He stopped. He looked at that line for a long time.
Then he went on.
Moll did not answer Kross. Kross says next Thursday.
Oren can't read. I wrote his SPENT page. Read it to him every morning. He learned one word by its shape: LYRA. A boot, a fork, a gate with a belly, a tent. It's on the floor of the vault in Gideon's blue chalk. Gideon won't let anybody sweep it.
Mrs. Doyle finished the gown by Hesper's pins. The lily paper is in the pocket.
L. has gone to Cressida's.
He stopped again.
On the floor of the vault in front of him, in the lamplight, the four blue chalk shapes lay where Gideon had drawn them. Big. Clumsy. A boy's drawing.
Corin looked at them.
And thought, for no reason he could have written down, that if he ever lost the letters too, the way Oren had, the way he would perhaps have to one day, on some stair, in front of some window, to hold some door, then that would be the shape he would want to keep. That one. Out of all of them.
He did not write that down.
He closed the Ledger. And put his gray hand flat on it. And sat on the edge of the stone bench under the skulls of the old monks, with his stiff leg out, and listened to the bells of the city strike eight, one after another. Saint Clement's first. Then the Ministry. Then all the rest, tumbling. And after them, last, a little late, the great green bell of Saint Bride's over his head, rung by an old deaf man at the bottom of a rope.
And somewhere at the bottom of Comb Street, in the snow, a carriage door opened for a girl in gray.
