Chapter 48: The Man Who Cannot Read, Part 4 of 5: Moll's News
Moll's News
Juno came back to the crypt at the eleventh bell with her coat pockets so full that she walked like a duck.
She came down the three stone steps on the sides of her feet, slowly, with her arms held out from her body so as not to crush anything, and her cap pushed back, and her face red and shining under it. And she came down the hall between the pallets to the side vault, and stood in the arch, and turned her pockets out onto the floor.
Sweets.
Small white things in twists of paper. Dozens of them. They came out of her pockets in a soft rustling heap onto the stone, and kept coming, the way coins come out of a bag that has been shaken. Out of the left pocket and the right. Out of the inside pocket where Gideon's boat had been. Out of her cap, when she took it off. Out of her boots, three of them, warm, when she sat down on the floor and pulled them off.
And then she took a folded square of brown paper out of her coat, and unfolded it, and there were more. And another. And a knotted handkerchief, very dirty, with more inside.
"From the step women," she said. "All of them. They took them off the children on the way home and brought them to Gran's. Gran's kitchen table's covered. She sent me with these and says there's more coming." She sniffed. "Bridie Kane brought eleven in her bucket. Under the brush."
Silas knelt down beside the heap.
He did not touch it at first. He looked at it for a long time, with his long hands on his knees. Then he began to count. He picked them up one at a time between finger and thumb and laid them in rows on the stone floor of the vault, ten to a row, very neat, the way a clerk lays out coins. And Corin, on his bench, watched him, and counted with him in his head, and got to nine, and lost it, and listened to the doctor's voice instead.
It took a long time.
"One hundred and thirty-seven," said Silas at last.
He sat back on his heels.
"And the ones that were eaten?"
"Gran says the step women say eleven," said Juno. "That they know of. Before Bridie screamed. The little boy in the red cap was the first. They know where he lives. Poll Danby's boy, on Hook Lane." She looked at the rows of sweets on the floor. "And maybe some more after, that nobody saw. When everybody was running."
Silas nodded slowly.
"Eleven," he said. "Eleven, and not two hundred." He looked at Corin. "We've been to seven of them already. The step women knew the doors. Salt water, quick, and a finger down the throat. It doesn't get all of it. It gets some." He looked at the floor. "I'll go back out after dark for the other four. If the step women can find them."
He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes.
Then he gathered the hundred and thirty-seven sweets up again, carefully, into the dirty knotted handkerchief, and tied it, and put it inside his coat.
"One hundred and thirty-seven," he said again, to nobody. "Given out in public. On a printed notice. With the Office's seal on it. Under the Under-Secretary's own hand." He put his hand flat on the bundle in his coat. "If there is anybody in this city left who will look at them."
"There's more," said Juno.
They looked at her.
She was sitting on the floor with her boots off and her red frostbitten feet tucked up under her, and her face had changed. The shining had gone out of it.
"Gran says to tell you about Sergeant Moll," she said. "Gran says she should have sent word last night but there wasn't anybody to send, and she didn't want it on paper."
Corin sat up. Slowly. On his elbow, on the bench, with the stiff leg out.
"He came back," said Juno quickly. "Before you ask. He came back. Yesterday. From the Ministry. He went up the Steps at the tenth bell like he was told, and he came home to the Watch house at the second bell after noon. On his own feet. Gran was there. She'd been sitting in his kitchen all morning with the kettle on."
"And?"
"And he sat down in his chair by the range," said Juno, "and didn't say anything for a long time. And then Gran gave him his tea, and he drank it. And then he told her."
She was quiet for a moment, getting it in order.
"The Inquisitor was very kind to him. That's the first thing he said. Very kind, Marta. Kinder than Monday. He gave him a chair by the fire and a cup of tea and a barley sugar. And there was a girl at a desk in the corner in a white coat, writing everything down. And a boy at the door. A cadet. Tall. Standing very straight." Juno looked at Lyra. "Moll said the boy never took his eyes off the floor the whole time."
Lyra did not say anything. She had gone very still, on her pile of sacks.
"And the Inquisitor asked him about the war," said Juno. "About the Commonwealth Foot. About his leg. About a march, a long time ago, up the valleys in the north. About wagons." She paused. "Blue ones."
The vault was very quiet.
"And Moll said what he's always said. That there weren't any wagons. That his leg was a wagon on the north road, and it was carrying flour." Juno swallowed. "And the Inquisitor smiled and said, Of course, Sergeant. And wrote something in his little black book. And then he put the book down, and put his hand on the desk. On the green leather. And tapped."
Nobody had to ask what.
"Four," said Juno. "Two. Nine. Three. And waited."
"And Moll?"
"Moll said he sat there with his hands in his lap and his tea going cold and looked at the Inquisitor's finger on the leather. And he didn't answer." Juno looked at the floor. "He said it was the hardest thing he'd ever done. Harder than the wagon. He said his finger wanted to so much that he had to sit on his hand. Under his leg. Like a little boy in church."
Corin lay back on the bench.
"And the Inquisitor waited," said Juno, "for a long time. And then he took his hand off the desk and smiled and said, Thank you, Sergeant. That will do for today. Next Thursday, I think. Same hour. And do bring your memory again. I'm growing so fond of it." She stopped. "And let him go."
"Next Thursday," said Corin.
"That's what Gran said. She said, next Thursday is after Midwinter." Juno looked at him. "She said you'd know what that meant."
Corin did.
He lay on his bench and looked up at the skulls in their niche and thought about a man who did not hurry. Who let you go home on your own feet, with a barley sugar, and asked you to come back next week. Because he knew you would. Because he knew that by next week, everything he was waiting for would have already happened, on Midwinter night, under a dome, at a quarter to midnight, and he would not need to ask you anything at all.
"And one more thing," said Juno.
She put her hand inside her coat again. The inside pocket. And took out a card.
Not a sweet. A real card. Stiff and white and good, the size of a playing card, with a gilt edge. Folded once. She held it out to Lyra.
"From the old man with the horses," she said. "Tibbs. The one who drives for the Duke. He came to Gran's back door at the seventh bell, while she was counting the sweets, and gave it to her, and said it was for the young lady in gray, and that he couldn't stop, and that he'd be at the bottom of Comb Street with the grays at the eighth bell tonight, and wait one quarter, and no more." Juno paused. "Gran read it. She says sorry. She says she reads everything that comes through her door, it's a habit, and she's too old to stop."
Lyra took the card.
She unfolded it. She read it. Corin watched her lips move. And watched her face, as she read, go from white to whiter.
She read it again.
Then she read it aloud. Not to the vault. To Oren. Who was sitting against the wall with his left arm in a sling of Mrs. Fitch's black shawl, and his eyes on her face, the way you watch the face of somebody who is going to tell you something you cannot find out for yourself.
"His Grace the Duke of Marrow attends Councillor Thorne's Midwinter reception tonight, at Lily House, at the ninth bell, as he does every year. He would take it kindly if his guest of Monday would care to practise on him first. Wear gray. No mask. On her own floor, your aunt likes to see faces. M."
She lowered the card.
"Her house," said Oren. Very quietly. His mouth worked for a moment, at the edge of a word that was not there. "They've named it after the white flowers. The ones with the smell."
"Yes."
Oren looked at the card in her hand. At the marks on it. And Corin saw his face try, for a moment, the way a man tries a door that he knows is locked.
Then he looked away.
Mrs. Doyle's Needle
The gown was ruined.
That was what Lyra said, at noon, standing in the side vault in her shift with the gray silk held up in front of her by the shoulders. She said it flatly, the way you say a thing that is already true. It's ruined. And Corin, on his bench, looked at it, and could not say she was wrong.
The hem was black. Six inches deep, all the way round. Black with the slush of the leads and the gutter, and the yard at Fothergill's, and the snow of the long walk back to Saint Bride's in the dark, where she had held it up in her fist and it had come down anyway. There was a tear in the skirt at the back, a long one, where it had caught on a slate on the roof. There were pins everywhere. Madame Hesper's pins. Dozens of them, all down the side seams, and round the waist, and along the back where the tiny gray buttons were, holding the silk in to the shape of a girl who had lost more than half a stone in a week. Some of them had come out. Most had not. They glittered in the lamplight like frost on a dark window.
"I can't wear it like this," said Lyra. "Not to her house. Not in front of all of them." Her voice did not shake. "And there's nobody to finish it."
There was a silence in the vault.
And from the arch, a loud kind voice said, "Well, my lady, I don't know about nobody."
Mrs. Doyle was standing in it.
She was large and red and cheerful, in her gray apron with her sleeves rolled up over her great red forearms, and she had a jug of water in one hand that she had been taking round the pallets. She put it down on the floor. She came into the vault, and looked at the gown in Lyra's hands, and at the hem, and the tear, and the pins. She looked at it for a long time, the way Corin had seen Madame Hesper look at the mask, through Lyra's telling of it. As if it might break if you thought too hard.
"Forty years," said Mrs. Doyle, "I did piecework. For the slop shops on Rag Lane. Shirts at a farthing the dozen, and drawers at a ha'penny, and once, when I was young and had good eyes, a wedding dress for a draper's daughter that I sat up three nights over and was never paid for." She held out her red hands. "Give it here."
Lyra looked at her.
"It's silk," she said. "It was my mother's. Madame Hesper—"
"I know whose it was, my lamb. I heard." Mrs. Doyle did not lower her hands. "And I know who pinned it. I can see her pins. A good pinner, she was. Every one in the right place, and every one pointing the same way, so you'd know which way to sew." Her loud voice had gone quieter. "She's done the hard part, my lady. She's done all the thinking. All I've to do is follow her pins."
Lyra gave her the gown.
Mrs. Doyle sat down on the stone floor of the side vault, with her back against the wall and the gown across her great lap, the way a woman sits by a fire with a child. She took a little tin box out of her apron pocket. Needles. Thread. A thimble, brass, worn thin at the top. A pair of small scissors on a string round her neck. She threaded a needle with gray silk thread from a reel that Corin did not ask her where she had got. She held it up to the lamp, and squinted at it, and bit off the end.
And began.
Corin watched her.
He could not help it. He lay on his bench with the Ledger on his chest and watched her red fingers go along the side seam of the gray silk, from pin to pin. She took each pin out as she reached it, one at a time, and put it between her lips, and sewed where it had been. Small stitches. So small he could not see them. Her needle went in and out of the silk like a fish going in and out of water, and the silk closed behind it, and where the pin had held the shape, the thread held it now.
One pin. Then the next. Then the next.
And as she went, the pins in the corner of her mouth grew. Two. Three. Four. Sticking out from between her lips, one beside the other, like the whiskers of a cat.
Lyra, standing by the bench in her shift with a blanket round her shoulders, saw it too.
Corin watched her see it. He watched her face. It did not crumple. It went very still, and very old, the way it had gone in the tower when Rowan said the thing about the clock.
She did not say anything. She sat down on the floor beside Mrs. Doyle, close, so that their shoulders nearly touched, and watched the needle go.
It took all afternoon.
Mrs. Doyle did the side seams first, and then the waist, and then the back, the long row of tiny gray buttons and the loops for them. Then she took the hem in her hands and looked at the black on it for a long time and said, "Well, that's coming off," and took her scissors from the string round her neck, and cut it. The whole black six inches. All the way round. In one long slow line, following a row of pins that Lyra had not even known were there, so fine and so close that Madame Hesper must have put them in on Wednesday, before the parcel, before any of it, knowing.
"She'd already marked it," said Mrs. Doyle, holding up the cut strip of black silk. "See? Shorter. For a girl who'd lost weight and wouldn't be wanting to trip on her train going up a stair." She shook her head. "She knew. She knew you'd come back thinner. She'd marked it for the next time before there was a next time."
She turned up the new hem. She sewed it. The light from the lamp moved on the gray silk as she turned it in her lap, slowly, the way light moves on water.
The tear at the back she did last. She looked at it, and turned it this way and that, and then took a piece of the cut black hem and trimmed the black away and kept the gray, a narrow strip, and laid it under the tear like a bandage under a wound, and sewed it there, so close and so small that when she was done you would have had to know it was there to find it.
And then she sat back against the wall, and held the gown up by its shoulders, in front of the lamp, and looked at it.
"There," she said.
It was the same gown. Gray silk, very plain, with nothing on it at all. Long sleeves. A line of tiny gray buttons down the back, so many that you could not count them. The light moving on it like smoke.
Shorter. Narrower. A girl's gown now, instead of a woman's.
Lyra took it.
She stood up, with the blanket falling off her shoulders, and held it against herself. And looked down at it. And then, without a word, she bent down and put her arms round Mrs. Doyle, who was sitting on the floor against the wall with the pins in her mouth, and held her. Hard.
Mrs. Doyle sat very still.
Then she took the pins out of her mouth, one by one, and put them in the tin box, and patted Lyra on the back with one great red hand, twice, the way you pat a horse.
"There's my lamb," she said. "There's my good girl."
And from the floor by the wall, where he had been sitting all afternoon with his sling, watching, Oren said, very low:
"The pocket."
They looked at him.
"In the waist," said Oren. "The left side. In the seam. For a list." He looked at the gown. "Is it still there?"
Mrs. Doyle felt for it. Her red fingers went along the left side of the waist, inside, and stopped.
"It's there," she said. "I sewed round it. I wasn't going to sew up a pocket a woman like that had made."
