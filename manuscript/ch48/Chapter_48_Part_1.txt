Chapter 48: The Man Who Cannot Read, Part 1 of 5: Thursday Night
Thursday Night
They came back at the ninth bell, and Corin knew before they reached the bottom of the steps that something had gone wrong.
He knew it from the sound. He had been lying on his stone bench under the skulls of the old monks since the fourth bell, with the Ledger on his chest and his eyes on the arch of the side vault, listening. He had been listening for five hours. He had heard every bell. He had heard Mrs. Doyle go home at the seventh and Mrs. Fitch at the eighth. He had heard Silas come in from his rounds of the parish and go out again. He had heard Juno come back from the Saltmarsh at dusk on the sides of her feet, and Gideon come in from the top of the Steps with the snow on him and say, flatly, Nobody on the road. Nobody in the lane. Nobody behind the yews. He had heard the children breathe.
And at the ninth bell, he heard the low door at the end of the crypt open. And footsteps on the three stone steps coming down.
Two sets. Not three.
The first was light and quick and uneven, the way somebody walks when they have been walking for a long time in the snow and their feet have stopped being feet. The second was heavy. Slow. One step, and a stop. And another. The way a big man walks when something in him is hurt and he is trying not to let it show.
No third. No long thin dry voice saying something sharp round a mouthful of pins.
Corin had not expected a third. Hesper was not coming to the crypt. But he listened for it anyway, the way you listen for a clock that has stopped.
Then they were in the arch.
Lyra first. In her coat, the too-big one, buttoned to the throat. And under the coat, coming down out of the bottom of it to the floor, gray silk. Her mother's gown. Not dragging now. Somebody had pinned it up, roughly, in a bunch, to keep it out of the snow. There was snow on it anyway, and wet, and dirt, and the gray had gone dark at the hem. She had a parcel in her arms. Brown paper, tied with string. She held it against her chest the way she held the Ledger.
Her face was white. Her eyes were red. She looked at him on his bench and did not say anything.
And behind her, Oren.
He had to stoop under the arch. He always did. But he stooped wrong tonight, crooked, with his left shoulder held stiff and high and his left arm hanging straight down at his side, the hand open, not moving. There was something long wrapped in sacking in his right hand. His sword, by the shape. There was blood on Terrence's coat. A great deal of it, down the front, dried brown and stiff, and more on his trousers, and on his boots. His face under the gray beard was the color of old snow. And he was looking at something. Not at Corin. Not at the vault. At nothing. At the air in front of him, with a strange flat look, the way a man looks at a wall in a room where there used to be a window.
"Silas," said Corin. His voice came out hoarse. "Somebody get Silas."
"He's coming," said Juno, from behind them. She had come up the hall on the sides of her feet. "I sent Gideon. He's at the Pellows' on Church Lane with a baby."
Oren did not seem to hear any of it.
He came into the vault, and put the sacking bundle down on the stone floor against the wall, very carefully, with one hand. Then he stood there. In the middle of the little stone room, under the niche of skulls, with his left arm hanging. And looked round.
At the walls. At the bench. At the Ledger on Corin's chest.
His eyes stopped on the Ledger.
They stayed on it for a long time. On the cover. On the old brown leather, worn soft and pale at the edges, with the faded gold letters pressed into it that Corin had stopped seeing years ago. HARBOUR TIDE TABLES. That was what the cover said. It had always said that. Corin had bought the book secondhand on Quill Row a long time ago, because it was cheap and strong and nobody would look twice at a book of tide tables. He had pasted his own pages in over the tables. But the cover still said it, in faded gold.
Oren looked at the gold letters for a long time.
Then he looked at Corin.
"What does it say?" he said.
Corin did not understand.
"The front," said Oren. "Of your book. The gold." His voice was very low. Very careful. The voice of a man walking out onto a frozen pond, testing each step. "I've seen it every day for a month. I knew what it said. I know I knew." He looked at the gold letters again. "What does it say?"
The vault was very quiet.
Corin looked at Lyra.
She was standing just inside the arch, with the brown-paper parcel against her chest. She did not look at him. She was looking at Oren. And her face was doing a thing that Corin had seen it do once before, in the hay on the barge, the first night, when she knelt beside her father and took his hand and he did not know who she was.
"Oren," said Corin slowly.
"The wall at Hesper's," said Oren. He was still looking at the gold letters on the Ledger. "It needed something big. It was a whole shop window. And a woman with gray gloves." He paused. "I gave it the letters. All of them. Reading. Writing. All of it." He paused again. "It held. Long enough. The girl got over the roofs."
Nobody said anything.
"And Hesper," said Corin. It was not a question. He could see it was not a question.
"At her back door," said Oren. "With her shears. And her pins in her mouth." His face did not move. "The big one. Dace. I put my sword in his leg after. It was too late."
Corin lay on his bench.
He thought of a tall thin woman he had never met, in black, with four pins in the corner of her mouth. Lyra had told him about her. On Saturday night, and again on Wednesday. She sews a pocket into everything now. For a list. He thought of the gray gown, and the list pocket in its left waist seam, and the evidence they had meant to carry in it to the Masquerade. The vial. The letter. The newspaper strip. He thought of a box covered in dove-gray paper, sent to her shop by the front door, in daylight, for anybody to see.
He opened the Ledger. He did not know what for. To have something to do with his hands.
And Oren, standing in the middle of the vault, watched him open it. Watched the pages turn. The pages of Corin's square careful capitals, close-written, crowded, with lines added in the margins in other hands, Lyra's slanting one and Juno's round one and Silas's spidery doctor's scrawl.
Corin saw his face as he looked at them.
It was the look of a man standing in a library in a country whose language he has never learned. Every shelf full. Every book open. And nothing in any of them for him.
"Read me something," said Oren.
Corin looked up.
"Anything," said Oren. "Out of it. Please." He swallowed. "I want to hear that it still says things."
Corin looked down at the open page. He did not choose it. It was the page the book had fallen open at, the way it often did, from being opened there so many times. The first page. His own hand. The oldest writing in the book.
He read it aloud.
"Your name is Corin Vane. You are a harvester. You are not a bad man. You have a sister named Elara. Find her."
Oren listened. His eyes were on the page while Corin read. On the square black capitals. Following them, Corin saw, the way your eyes follow the words when somebody reads to you, out of habit. Following shapes that did not mean anything any more.
When Corin stopped, Oren nodded. Once.
"Thank you," he said.
And then, very slowly, like a tall tree going down when it has been cut through at the bottom, he sat down on the stone floor of the vault with his back against the wall beside his sword, and his left arm in his lap, and shut his eyes.
That was how Silas found him, a quarter of an hour later, coming in with the snow on his hat and his black bag in his hand and Gideon behind him. A big gray-bearded man sitting against a wall with his eyes shut and blood all down his coat. And Lyra kneeling in front of him in her ruined gray silk, untying the knot of his muffler with her red fingers, very gently, the way you untie the bonnet of a child who has fallen asleep in it.
And Corin on his bench under the skulls, with the Ledger open on his chest, not reading it.
Spent
Silas put the shoulder back in at the tenth bell.
It was out. Not broken. Out of its socket, the doctor said, kneeling on the stone floor of the vault beside Oren with his long fingers moving over the big man's shoulder through the torn shirt, feeling, pressing, the way a blind man reads a face. The ball of the arm had been knocked clean out of the cup of the shoulder blade, forward and down, and had stayed there, and the muscles round it had gone hard as rope to hold it where it was. That was why the arm hung. That was why the hand did not move.
"It'll go back," said Silas. "It'll hurt like the devil going back. And then it'll hurt a good deal less." He looked at Oren over his crooked spectacles. "Lie down. Flat. On the floor."
Oren lay down. Flat, on the floor, on his back, with a folded blanket under his head.
Silas took off his own right boot. Then he sat down on the floor beside Oren, facing him, and put his stockinged foot into Oren's armpit, firmly, the way you put your foot against the side of a cart to pull a wheel off. And took hold of Oren's left wrist in both his long hands.
"Gideon," he said. "Hum something."
Gideon, by the wall, hummed. The carrying tune. Low, and steady, going down at the end like a man setting a heavy box on the ground.
Silas pulled.
Not hard. Not fast. Slowly. Steadily. Leaning back, with his foot in the armpit, the whole long thin length of him leaning back like a man hauling on a rope, and his face perfectly calm. And he went on pulling. And on. For a long time. Corin, on the bench, counted. He got to nine, and it went soft, and he started again. Oren made no sound. His face went gray, and then white, and the sweat came out on it, and his good hand found the edge of the blanket and twisted it until the knuckles cracked.
Then there was a sound. Deep inside the shoulder. A dull heavy thunk, like a bolt going home.
Oren let out his breath.
All of it. In one long rush, like a man coming up from under water. And his left hand, lying on the floor at the end of his arm, moved. The fingers curled. And opened. And curled again.
"There," said Silas. He let go of the wrist and put his boot back on. "Now it'll ache for a month, and you'll want a sling for a week, and you won't lift anything heavier than a cup with it until Midwinter. Which I expect you'll ignore, like everybody else in this crypt." He stood up, with a crack from his knees. "Lie still. I'll find a sling."
He went out.
Oren lay on the floor with his eyes shut.
Gideon stopped humming.
The vault was very quiet. Out in the main hall, the children breathed. A brazier ticked. Bet was asleep in her corner, with her head on her bundle. Juno sat on the floor at the foot of Corin's bench with her knees drawn up and her arms round them. Lyra knelt by Oren's head, in her ruined gray silk, very still.
After a long time, Oren opened his eyes.
He did not sit up. He lay on his back on the floor and looked up at the curved stone roof of the vault, at the niche with its iron grille and the stacked brown skulls behind it, and said, to the roof:
"Corin."
"I'm here."
"Your book. The page at the back. The one with the word at the top." Oren's voice was rough and slow. "The one where you write what you've spent."
"Yes."
"Is there room on it?"
Corin looked down at the Ledger in his hands. He did not need to open it to the page. He knew where it was. Near the back. SPENT. In his own square capitals, at the top. And underneath, the long list. His. Wed cellar: a word (mother). Wed drain: three winters. Thu Saltmarsh: rest of first language. Fri sluice: first night at No. 14. Tue Bourse: the bend in my left knee. Nearly a whole page, now. Both sides.
"I'll start a new one," he said. "For you."
Oren was quiet.
"Will you write it?" he said. "For me. Mine. What I've spent. I've been keeping it in my head. Since the river." He swallowed. "I'd like it written down. Somewhere. In case."
"In case what?"
Oren did not answer that.
Corin turned to the back of the Ledger. Past his own SPENT page, and THOSE I OWE, and the page with no heading and the line drawn hard across the top. To the first clean page after them. There were not many clean pages left. He counted them, without meaning to. Six. Seven. Nine. Then it went soft.
He held out his hand.
Lyra put the pencil in it.
He wrote at the top of the clean page, in his square careful capitals, pressing hard, because his gray fingers did not feel the pencil properly any more and he had to press to know it was there:
OREN VANCE. SPENT.
He looked up.
"Tell me," he said.
Oren lay on the floor and looked at the roof.
"The arch," he said. "In the autumn. The first wall. The one with the gold round it." He paused. "The word for the thing you eat soup with."
Corin wrote. Autumn, the arch, Wharf Street: spoon.
"Then the river. On the ice. The first night on the Saltmarsh. The big wall." A pause. "The calls. My brother's calls. The ones he taught me on the kitchen table. All of them. And the word for the things you beat to make them."
The Saltmarsh, the river: the drum calls, all. Drums.
"And after. Small ones. To hold the barge, when the hooks came. One each time." Oren's eyes did not leave the roof. "The thing you turn a key in. The hard water on top of the river in winter. The thing you sit on."
Lock. Ice. Chair.
"And one more," said Oren. "The flowers. The white ones. With the heavy smell." He frowned. "I didn't mean that one. It went when the hooks came into the hay. I reached for something small, and that was what came."
Corin wrote it. Lilies.
He looked at the list. Seven lines. Spoon. Drum calls. Drums. Lock. Ice. Chair. Lilies.
"And today," he said quietly.
"And today."
Oren was quiet for a long time.
"Thursday," he said at last. "Velvet Street. Madame Hesper's shop. The window." He shut his eyes. "Reading. And writing. All of it. Every letter. From the beginning."
Corin wrote it.
Thu, Velvet Street, Hesper's shop: reading and writing. All of it.
The pencil scratched on the paper. It was the loudest thing in the vault.
And Oren, lying on the floor with his eyes shut, listened to it scratch. Corin saw him listening. The way you listen to somebody digging a grave, from the next field.
When it stopped, Oren opened his eyes.
"And under that," he said. "Put her name. Not on that page. On the other one. The one Lyra writes on. Where you put the ones you owe."
"THOSE I OWE."
"Yes."
Corin turned back. To the page that said THOSE I OWE. Full, nearly. Old Jory Hobday. Bramble. Ned. The Hollow brothers. Coker. Wick. Lyra's line, fierce and slanting: I put the spoon in his hand and walked away. Twice.
He wrote at the bottom, in the last space:
Madame Hesper. Agnes.
He stopped.
"Is that all?" he said.
Oren thought about it.
"She held her door," he said. "Put that."
Corin wrote: She held her door.
Then he put the pencil down on the open page, and looked at it. At the two pages. Oren's SPENT, with its eight lines. And THOSE I OWE, with its new last line.
It was his own handwriting. Square. Careful. Black. Exactly the same as every other line in the book.
And the man whose losses they were would never be able to read a single word of it.
Read Aloud
"Read it to me," said Oren.
Corin read it.
He read the whole page, from the top. Oren Vance. Spent. And the eight lines under it, one after another, slowly, in his hoarse tired voice, in the yellow light of the lamp on the floor. Autumn, the arch, Wharf Street: spoon. The Saltmarsh, the river: the drum calls, all. Drums. Lock. Ice. Chair. Lilies. Thursday, Velvet Street, Hesper's shop: reading and writing. All of it.
Oren lay on the floor with his eyes on the roof and listened.
When Corin had finished, Oren did not say anything for a while.
"Again," he said.
Corin read it again.
This time Oren's lips moved with it. Silently. Following. Not the words on the page. The words in the air. Spoon. Drums. Lock. The way a child says a lesson after the teacher, a half-beat behind.
When it was done, he nodded.
"That's right," he said. "That's all of it." He was quiet. "It's strange. Hearing it. I've said it in my head every morning for a month. Six words. Then seven, then eight. It sounds different out loud." He turned his head on the folded blanket and looked at the Ledger in Corin's gray hands. "It sounds like somebody else's."
Nobody answered that.
At the foot of the bench, Juno shifted. She had been sitting very still with her arms round her knees, the way she sat when she was trying not to be noticed, which was not often. Now she unwound her arms, and looked at Oren lying on the floor, and said, in a small voice for her:
"I can only do my letters."
Oren looked at her.
"My letters," said Juno. "A to Z. Gran taught me. And my name. And Tallow Street. And Rope. Because of the Watch house." She looked at her boots. "I can't do the long ones. When Corin gives me a message I get him to say it to me, and I carry it in my head. I've never carried one I could read." She paused. "It's all right. You get very good at remembering."
Oren did not say anything. But he went on looking at her.
"I can't do any," said Gideon.
He was sitting against the wall by Oren's sword, where he had been humming, with his new flat-fronted barge in his lap. Half carved. He did not look up from it.
"Not one," he said. "Not A. Not my name. The Bourse doesn't teach the carrying boys. Mrs. Pratt said a carrying boy who could read would only read things he shouldn't." He turned the little boat over. "I know the V. On the keys. And the chalk marks on the crates. Blue is the north boats and white is the Hill and red is the river. And I know numbers to twelve because of the doors." He looked at the boat. "That's all. It's enough. Nobody ever writes anything to a carrying boy."
Oren lay on the floor and looked from one of them to the other.
A girl of fourteen, sitting on the stones with her arms round her knees, who could do her letters and nothing else. A boy of thirteen against the wall, with no big toes and a bitten arm and no feelings, who could not do even that.
"Bet can't," said Juno. "I asked her. And Mrs. Fitch can't. And Mrs. Doyle can do her name and nothing else, she said, and she's never missed it." Juno looked round at the vault, at the arch, at the long dark hall beyond it with its pallets. "Nobody down here can, much. Not the children. Not their mothers. Lyra can. And Corin. And the doctor." She looked at Oren. "That's all."
Oren was quiet for a long time.
"Then I've come down to the crypt," he said at last. Very low. "Haven't I."
It was not bitter. Corin listened for it, and it was not there. It was the voice of a man who has come down a long flight of stairs in the dark, and reached the bottom, and found other people there already, sitting on the floor.
Lyra had not said anything since she came in.
She was still kneeling by Oren's head, in her ruined gray silk, with the brown-paper parcel on the floor beside her knee. Now she reached down, and untied the string of the parcel, and folded back the paper, and Corin saw what was in it.
Hair.
Long and dark and coiled round on itself like a sleeping animal, with red in it where the lamplight caught it.
He knew it at once. He had watched her cut it, on Saturday night, sitting on a bucket by the brazier in the arches with the doctor's knife in her hand and her face set like a door. He had not said anything then. He had written it in the Ledger, afterward, small. L. cut her hair. Sold it on Comb Street. Did not cry. I did, after, when she was asleep. Don't tell her.
She took a small white card out of the coil of hair. She held it up to the lamp.
"Hesper bought it back," she said. Her voice was very flat, the way his went when he was holding something down. "Yesterday. From the man on Comb Street. Four crowns. She wrote this."
She read it. To all of them. To the vault.
"For Lady Lyra Thorne. Her own. Not to be sold again. Grow it long, child, and wear it up, the way she did. A.H."
She put the card down on top of the hair.
"I read it to Oren," she said. "At Fothergill's. In the back room, with all the bald heads. It was the first thing." She looked at Corin. "The first thing I read to him. After."
Corin looked at her across the vault.
He thought of all the things he had read to people. All the years of it. Sitting on the edge of a bed in a cold room, or on a stool in a tavern, or on a step in the rain, with somebody's empty hands in his and the Ledger on his knee. Reading them their own names. Their own streets. The name of a wife they did not know they had. You live at number nine, Quill Row. Your daughter is called Annie. She has a blue ribbon. He had been doing it for six years. He had thought he was good at it.
He had never once thought about what it was like to be the one who had to be read to. Every day. For the rest of your life.
"Every morning," said Oren, from the floor.
They looked at him.
"The page," said Oren. "Mine. Will you read it to me? Every morning. Before I open my eyes." He looked at the roof. "I used to say it to myself. In my head. The words that were gone. Every morning, like checking your kit before a march. I can still say it in my head. I haven't lost that." He paused. "But I'd like to hear it. From the book. So I know it's written down. So I know it's real, and not just me."
Corin opened the Ledger to the first page.
To his own first page, in the oldest ink, with the words he read every morning of his own life before he did anything else. Your name is Corin Vane. And at the bottom of it, in the margin, where there was still a little space, he wrote, small, in his square capitals:
Read Oren his page every morning. Before anything. He asked.
He showed it to Lyra. She read it. She nodded.
He did not show it to Oren. There was no point.
But Oren, lying on the floor, saw him write it. Saw the pencil move, and stop. And Corin saw him look at the marks on the page, from the floor, upside down, the square black capitals that did not mean anything to him any more.
"What did you write?" said Oren.
Corin told him.
Oren nodded, once, on the folded blanket.
"Good," he said. And shut his eyes. And this time he slept.
