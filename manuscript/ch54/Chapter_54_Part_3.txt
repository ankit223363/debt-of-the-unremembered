Chapter 54: The Night of Masks, Part 3 of 5: The Garden Door
The Garden Door
The porters drew the velvet back.
They did it slowly, one on each side, taking hold of the heavy plum folds and walking them back along the wall, the way stage-hands draw a curtain. And behind the velvet there was a door.
It was small. That was the first thing. After the bronze doors, which were taller than a house, it looked like a door for a child. An old door of dark wood, set deep in the black stone of the wall, with a round top and iron studs and a great iron ring for a handle, black with age. The stone round it was a different color from the rest of the wall. Older. Rougher. As if the whole Bourse had been built round this one small door and had never quite swallowed it.
The ninth bell finished ringing across the river.
And in the silence after it, the door opened.
It opened inward, toward the hall, slowly, on old hinges that did not make a sound. Somebody had oiled them. Lyra noticed that. Somebody had come today, or yesterday, and oiled the hinges of a door that had been shut since before the Duke was born.
The cold came in first.
She felt it across the whole great hot room, all the way from the far wall. A long breath of winter air, sharp and clean, smelling of snow and stone and the river. It went across the black and white floor like water, and round the glass tables, and the vials on the velvet flickered in it, all of them at once, hundreds of little lights wavering. The candles in the chandelier leaned. The flames bent over, all together, the same way, and then straightened.
And a young man came through the door.
Thin, like a pen. In a long black coat without a speck on it, buttoned to the throat, with a silver book and a closed eye and a tiny crown on the breast. A slim black book held flat against his chest with his gloved fingers spread over it. He stopped just inside the door, on the black and white floor, and looked round the room. Fast. All of it. And then he looked down at the floor in front of him, and Lyra saw his lips move.
One.
Lucian.
She had never seen him. She knew him at once. Corin had told her everything.
Behind him came two men in white. Tall men, in long white coats and white trousers and tall white hats, with silver at their shoulders, and short swords at their hips in white scabbards. They came through the small door one after the other, ducking their tall white hats under the round top of it, and stepped to either side, and stood there. Not looking at anybody. Like statues that had walked in out of the snow.
And then the Archon.
Lyra had thought, all her life, that she knew what he looked like. Everybody did. His face was on the coins. Not his face. A face. A profile, very fine, very calm, with a high forehead and a straight nose, looking always to the left, on every copper and silver and gold piece in the city, with the stone eye above it and the words round the edge in the old letters that nobody could read. She had looked at that face on a thousand coins. She had never once thought of it as a real face, belonging to a real man.
The man who came through the garden door was masked.
A plain mask. Black. A half-mask, smooth, covering his face from the brow to the upper lip, with nothing on it at all. No silver. No pearls. No ribbons. Just black. And above it, hair. Dark, going gray, cut short. And below it, a mouth. And a chin. Clean-shaven. Quite ordinary.
He was tall. Taller than the white guards. He wore a long dark coat, very plain, the color of a winter evening, with no chain and no star and no ribbon on it, nothing at all to say who he was. Black gloves. Plain black shoes. He looked like a gentleman who had come to the wrong party, and was too polite to say so.
And the room went quiet.
Not the way it had gone quiet for Lady Vivienne. That had been a quiet like a door opening onto snow. This was different. This was a quiet like the snow itself. Like the whole room had been covered over, softly, in the space of a breath, and every sound in it smothered. The quartet behind the palms did not stop in the middle of a bar this time. It simply was not playing any more, and Lyra could not remember the moment it had stopped. Five hundred people in masks stood very still among the glowing tables. Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. Nobody turned round. But every mask in the room was turned, very slightly, toward the small dark door in the far wall.
And then, all together, as if somebody had given a signal that Lyra did not hear, they bowed.
All of them. The ladies sank down in their silks and velvets, the gentlemen bent at the waist, the servants in plum went down on one knee where they stood, with their trays held level. A great slow rustling wave of it, going round the room from the garden door, round the tables, round the brass box, to the bronze doors and back. Like wind going over a field of wheat.
The Duke bowed. Lyra felt him do it beside her, a great soft mountain bending, with a little grunt of effort.
She found she had bowed too. She had not decided to. Her body had done it. Her mother's gown spreading round her on the black and white floor, her head down, her eyes on the marble.
She looked up through her eyelashes, through the eyeholes of the white mask.
The Archon had not moved. He stood just inside the garden door, between his two white guards, with Lucian in front of him, and looked at the room. At all of it. The tables, the vials, the chandelier, the dome, the bowed heads. He did not seem surprised. He did not seem pleased. He looked the way a man looks at his own garden after a night of frost, to see what has survived.
Then he lifted one hand. A little. Black glove, palm down.
And the room straightened.
The ladies came up out of their silks. The gentlemen straightened. The servants got up off their knees. The quartet behind the palms began to play again, very softly, something slow, something she did not know. And slowly, very slowly, the voices came back. Low at first. Careful. Then a little louder. But not as loud as before. Not nearly as loud. The laughter did not come back at all.
The Archon came into the room.
He walked slowly across the black and white floor, with Lucian a step ahead of him and the two white guards a step behind, toward the middle of the room. Toward the brass box. People moved out of his way without seeming to. A little gap opened in front of him wherever he went, and closed behind him. Nobody looked at him directly. Everybody knew exactly where he was.
There was a chair by the brass box. Lyra had not noticed it before. A plain chair of dark wood with a high back, standing by itself on the black and white floor at the edge of the ring of black squares round the box. Somebody had put it there tonight. And on the other side of the box, a little way off, there was a small table, plain, with a lamp on it, and an inkstand, and a ledger. And a woman sitting at the table.
A woman in a dark green coat, with white gloves, and a plain white mask.
The Fourth Desk. One Auditor.
She stood up as the Archon came near. She did not bow. Lyra saw that. Everybody else in the room had bowed. The woman in the dark green coat stood up from her little table, and stood very straight, with her white-gloved hands at her sides, and inclined her head. Once. The way a clerk inclines her head to a customer at a counter.
The Archon looked at her for a moment.
Then he sat down in the plain chair by the brass box.
And Lucian, standing beside the chair, took something from under his arm. Not the black book. Something else. A case. Flat and square, the size of a large book, made of some dark wood, very old, very polished, with brass corners. He held it out, in both hands, flat, the way you hold out a tray.
The Archon put his hand on it.
Just that. He did not open it. He did not take it. He laid his black-gloved right hand flat on the lid of the dark wooden case, and left it there, resting, the way a man rests his hand on the head of a dog that sits beside his chair.
And Lyra, across the room, between the heads and the masks and the glowing tables, saw the lid of the case for a moment in the light of the chandelier as it swung.
There were four shapes cut into it. Four hollows. Round. In a row. Each one the size of the end of a thumb. Like the slots in a jeweler's tray, where the stones go.
The black glove lay over them. Over all four.
Then the chandelier swung on, and the light went with it, and she could not see the lid any more. Only the hand, resting. Quite still. While the man it belonged to sat in his plain chair by the brass box, in his black half-mask, with his face turned toward the room, and watched the Hill enjoy itself.
"Saints," said the Duke of Marrow, very softly, beside her.
It was the first time she had ever heard him sound afraid.
Faces
After that the night went on, the way nights do, even the worst ones.
Lyra walked round the great round room on the Duke's arm, slowly, the way you walk round a garden, from table to table, under the falling-gold sky of the dome. The Duke stopped at every table and looked at every lot and said something about it, in his soft sleepy voice, and moved on. People came up to him. Lords and ladies and councillors and their wives, in their masks, bowing, saying Your Grace. He spoke to all of them. He introduced her to none of them. My guest, he said, every time, and nothing else, and they looked at her mask and at her mother's gray gown and went a little pale and went away.
She saw faces she knew. Masked, but she knew them. Lady Harriet Langley, in sky blue, with a mask of blue feathers, looking even more like a fish than she had on Friday. The three Pellow sisters, in three shades of pink, clinging together. The Fennimore boys, already very red in the face, being given the red. Lady Ashcombe in black, with her little white dog under her arm and her stick, and no mask at all, because she said she was eighty-three and the Lady could put up with her face or lump it. Lady Ashcombe looked at Lyra across a table of childhoods, and inclined her old head, very slightly. And looked away.
And then her aunt.
Cressida Thorne was standing by a table near the bronze doors, alone, in white silk, with three ropes of pearls round her throat and her old-gold hair up in its crown of silver pins. No mask. No guest. No mask, the list had said. Her face was very white and very beautiful and perfectly calm. She was holding a glass of something she was not drinking. She was looking at the brass box. At the plain chair beside it. At the man in the black half-mask sitting in the chair with his hand on a wooden case.
Lyra counted the pins in her hair. She could not help it. Five.
Her aunt turned her head and saw her.
She saw the gray gown first. Lyra watched her see it. And then the mask. White silk, with a seed-pearl edge, and silver thread round the eyes.
Cressida's face did not change. But her hand on the glass went tight.
She came across. Through the crowd, which parted for her the way it parted for very few people tonight. In her white silk, with her pearls, smelling of lilies. And stopped in front of them.
"Ansel," she said to the Duke.
"Cressida."
"You've brought her."
"I said I would."
Cressida looked at Lyra. At the mask. For a long moment. Her gray eyes, Lyra's father's gray eyes, went over the white silk and the pearls and the silver thread, slowly, the way the Weigher's eyes had gone over her at the carriage door.
"You wore it," she said. Very low. "His present."
Lyra did not answer. The mask held her face still. Under it, her heart was going very hard.
"On Friday you came to my house with your face bare," said Cressida, "and I thought, she's her mother's daughter after all. She'll walk in with her face bare and dare them all." She paused. "And tonight you've put on his mask."
"Yes."
"Why?"
Lyra looked at her aunt through the eyeholes of the white mask.
"Because it's Midwinter," she said, "and everybody wears a mask at Midwinter. Except you."
Cressida went quite still.
Then something happened in her white beautiful face that Lyra had never seen there before. It was not a smile. It was the place where a smile would have been, if her aunt had been a woman who smiled. It went across her face very quickly and was gone.
"Yes," she said. "Yes, darling. Except me." She lifted her glass, a little, toward Lyra, the way you lift a glass to somebody across a table. "I gave mine up two years ago. I find I don't need it."
And she turned, in her white silk, and went back through the crowd to her table by the doors, and stood there alone again, looking at the brass box.
Lyra let out her breath.
They moved on.
At the next table there was a man with silver hair.
He was standing with his back to them, bending over a lot of six small vials, a talent, silver-white. Tall, and lean, in a coat of gray silk with silver thread in it, so fine that it shone like water when he moved. His hair was silver, thick, swept back from a high forehead. Across his gray silk waistcoat, a gold watch chain. He was not wearing a mask. He was holding one, in his left hand, by its ribbon. Silver, very plain. He had taken it off to look at the vials. Or he had never put it on.
He straightened up and turned round.
His eyes were very dark. Nearly black. In a lean handsome face of fifty or so, very clean-shaven, very pleasant, with laughter lines at the corners of the eyes. He looked at the Duke. And then at Lyra.
At the mask.
And she watched him see it. She watched the dark eyes go over it. The seed-pearl edge. The silver thread. And stop, just for a moment, at the bridge of the nose. Where there was a little gray pocket on the inside that he could not possibly see from here. Empty now.
He smiled.
It was a very good smile. Warm and easy and pleased, the smile of a man who has given a present and seen it worn.
"Lady Lyra," said Dorian Sterling. "How very lovely."
His voice was low and warm and pleasant, with a little laugh somewhere at the bottom of it.
"Mr. Sterling," said Lyra.
"It suits you. I was sure it would." He looked at the mask a moment longer. "I had it made for your mother, you know. Years ago. She never wore it. She said where she came from, they only put on masks for people they meant to leave." The dark eyes came back to Lyra's. "I'm so glad her daughter is less particular."
Lyra did not move. The quiet held her face like a hand.
Where I come from, Aurelian, we only put on masks for people we mean to leave. Oren had told her that. Her mother had said it to her father. Not to this man. To her father. In her own house. Oren had heard her say it.
Or she had said it to both of them.
"It's very comfortable," said Lyra. "Thank you."
"Is it?" said Sterling. "I'm glad. You'll want to be comfortable. It's going to be a long night." He looked down at the gold chain across his waistcoat. He did not take out the watch. He just looked at the chain, and touched it, once, with one finger. "They always are, at Midwinter. Especially toward the end."
He bowed to the Duke. He bowed to her. He put on his plain silver mask, by its ribbons, and turned, and went away between the tables toward the gallery stair.
"He touched his watch," said the Duke quietly.
"He didn't take it out."
"No," said the Duke. "He never does. That's how you know he's thinking about the time."
They moved on.
And at the table nearest the brass box, a pink smiling man in a white mask was giving out peppermints.
He had his little round silver box in one gray-gloved hand, open, and he was holding it out to a group of Hill ladies in silk, who were laughing and taking one each, with their little fingers crooked, the way ladies take sweets at a party. He was saying something to them. Lyra could hear his voice. Light and warm and a little high. The voice of a man who has spent his life saying kind things to children.
He saw the Duke and turned, beaming, with the silver box held out.
"Your Grace! A peppermint? To keep out the cold?"
"No, thank you, Ambrose."
"No? No. Quite right. Quite right. You never do." The pink smiling face under the white mask turned to Lyra. And the eyes behind the mask, behind the little gold spectacles that he wore over it, looked at her. At her gray gown. At her white mask. At her gloved hands. The eyes went over her the way they had gone over a coal man's lad on a cart. Like a lamp-post. And then they stopped.
At her chin.
You have your father's chin, Linus Hale had said.
"And for the young lady?" said Under-Secretary Ambrose Pallister, and held out the little round silver box. "One each, now. Only one."
Lyra looked at the white sweets in the box.
She thought of a wide staircase, on Crown Walk, at Midwinter. Of a bottom step shaped like the edge of a shell. Of two children in their nightgowns peeping through the banisters. One each, now. Only one. And don't tell your mother.
She thought of Polly in her bed, humming five notes.
She thought of four children in the crypt of Saint Bride's, by the north brazier, with an old woman holding their hands, at the seventh bell.
"No," she said. "Thank you."
"No? Ah well." Pallister closed the box with a little click. He was still smiling. "Not many takers tonight, I find. It's been rather a trying day for me, your Grace, to tell you the truth." He lowered his voice, as if he were telling a little joke. "I lost forty-seven of my children."
The Duke did not say anything.
"Forty-seven," said Pallister, and shook his white thistledown head. "Gone in the night. Like snow off a roof. I went down myself, at the seventh bell, with my carts and my blankets and my hot bricks, and the crypt was quite empty, except for four poor little things and an old woman." He sighed. "Where they've gone I can't imagine. I do hope they're warm."
He looked at Lyra again. At her chin.
"I do hope somebody is keeping them warm," he said. Quite gently. And smiled, and turned back to his Hill ladies with his silver box.
Lyra found that the Duke's arm under her hand had gone as hard as wood.
He walked her away. Not fast. At the same slow garden pace. Past the next table, and the next. Until they were by the brass box, on the far side of it from the plain chair. Near the little table with the lamp and the ledger, where the woman in the dark green coat and the plain white mask was sitting with a pen in her white-gloved hand.
The woman looked up.
She had very steady eyes, behind the mask. Gray-green. Tired. They went to the Duke, and then to Lyra. And stayed on Lyra. And something moved in them that Lyra could not read. Something careful. As if the woman were looking at a page in a ledger that she thought she had seen before, and could not remember where.
"Your Grace," said the Auditor of the Fourth Desk. "Your card. When you're ready to bid."
As she spoke, her mouth moved, and the light of the chandelier caught her front tooth.
Gold. A single gold tooth, in front, where a tooth had never grown in.
Beware the Bank's auditor with the gold tooth, the Duke had said, a week ago, in his black gallery. She notices everything.
"Thank you, Auditor," said the Duke. "Not yet."
The Auditor inclined her head. And looked at Lyra a moment longer. And then looked down at her ledger, and wrote something, in a small neat hand, and did not look up again.
