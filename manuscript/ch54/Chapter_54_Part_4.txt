Chapter 54: The Night of Masks, Part 4 of 5: The Girl in White
The Girl in White
At the half hour after the ninth bell, the girl in white came down.
Lyra saw her come. She was standing by herself at a table of childhoods near the gallery stair, because the Duke had gone to speak to Lord Ost about a horse, and she had let him go. She was looking at a lot she did not want to look at. Lot 3. A Childhood. City. A small room, a mother who sang, a window over a lane, a cat called Button. Aged three to seven. Whole. Reserve: one hundred and fifty crowns. The vial was a soft gray-gold. Like dust in a sunbeam.
And a long white coat came down the gallery stair behind the iron pillar, step by step, with twelve silver buttons catching the light.
She was alone. The slight gray man was still in the box above, at the rail, with one arm along it. Lyra saw that. She saw him watch the girl come down. He did not stop her. He did not follow her. He sat at the rail and watched, the way a man watches a dog he has let off the lead, to see where it will run.
The girl in white came across the floor to the table of childhoods. And stopped beside Lyra. And bent over the glowing vials, with her white-gloved hands folded in front of her, the way everybody bent over the tables.
She was a little taller than Lyra. Very straight. Fair hair, pinned up tight under the ribbons of a plain white mask, so tight that it pulled at her temples. A smooth pale face under the mask, the mouth set. The white coat was beautiful close to. Shell-white wool, very fine, with a high hook collar, and on the left breast a small silver badge: an open eye.
She did not look at Lyra.
"You're the Thorne girl," she said.
Her voice was very low. Clear and even and careful. Every word set down cleanly, the way a good clerk writes.
Lyra did not answer. She looked at Lot 3. At the cat called Button.
"I won't stay long," said the girl in white. "He's watching. He let me come. I don't know why. He lets me do things, sometimes, to see what I'll do." She looked at the vials. "I came to tell you something. And to ask you something. That's all."
Lyra did not say anything. The quiet held her face.
"I collected Sergeant Moll tonight," said the girl in white. "At the seventh bell. At Mrs. Marta's, on Tallow Street. He'd ridden north and come back. He's in Room Nine now, at the Ministry. In the white room with the two chairs." Her voice did not change at all. "I took him there. Myself. I was ordered to."
Lyra stood very still.
Moll. With his bad leg, and his Commonwealth Foot papers, and his finger that had answered once under a table. Who had sat on the Inquisitor's hand on Thursday and not answered.
"He went quietly," said the girl in white. "He said All right, lass. He put his hand on Mrs. Marta's shoulder before he went." She paused. "And on the way out, in the passage, in the dark, he touched my hand."
She unfolded her white-gloved hands. And turned the right one over, palm up, on the edge of the glass table, by Lot 3. Very small. Where nobody could see it but Lyra.
"Like this," she said.
And with the first finger of her left hand, she touched her own right palm.
Once.
And then twice, quick.
"I don't know what it means," said the girl in white. Her voice was still quite even. But her hand, on the edge of the glass, was not. "I've sat in Room Nine and watched him tap at people. Four numbers. I've written it down. I don't know what any of it means. Nobody's ever told me. And he touched my hand like that. Moll did. Once, and twice. On his way to the white room. As if it was a word." She swallowed. "I want to know what it means. I don't know why. I just want to know."
Lyra looked at the white-gloved hand, palm up, on the edge of the table.
She thought about the box three along from the Lady's. About the slight gray man with his arm along the rail, watching. About Kross on the step of Saint Bride's, lifting his hat to her eye at the hole in the window. About the hand on the stone, waiting. About everything the Inquisitor knew, and wanted to know, and would know if she did this.
She thought about Moll, with his bad leg, in a white room, tonight, because he had ridden north to warn Hessa.
She thought about rule one.
She took off her right glove.
Madame Hesper's thin gray kid, with the pearl button. She undid the button and drew the glove off, finger by finger, and laid it on the table beside Lot 3. And put out her bare hand, and laid it on the girl's white-gloved palm. Gently. Palm to palm.
And with her first finger she touched it.
Once.
And then twice, quick.
"Heard," said Lyra.
The girl in white went quite still.
"It means heard," said Lyra, very low. "I heard you. It's the answer. To somebody who's calling. It's what you say back so they know somebody's there." She did not take her hand away. "He was telling you he heard you, Wren. Whatever you said to him. Whatever you didn't say. He heard it."
The girl in white did not move.
She stood by the table of childhoods in her long white coat with her palm turned up under Lyra's bare hand, and did not move, and did not breathe. And under the plain white mask, very slowly, something happened to her mouth. It came unset. The way a knot comes loose when you have been pulling at it for a long time and suddenly find the right end.
And she began to hum.
She did not know she was doing it. Lyra could see that. It came out of her the way breath comes out of you when you have been holding it. Very soft. Under the music of the quartet behind the palms, under the voices, under everything. Three notes.
Going up.
And stopping. The way they always stopped.
Lyra stood with her bare hand on the girl's white glove.
And she thought of a nursery at the top of a house. Three small chairs. Three cups. A height chart with a third line on it, going up from two to six, and a letter gouged out at the top.
And she thought of her mother's song. All of it. Which she had now. Which Corin had given her on Saturday night, in the crypt, in the dark, with his gray hands in hers. The three notes. And then the fourth.
She did not decide to. Her mouth did it, under the mask. The quiet did not stop it. The quiet only stopped what she did not want.
She hummed the fourth note.
Very soft. So soft that nobody else in the whole great room could have heard it. Just the one note. The one that came next. The one that went up, after the three, toward the place where the song hung.
The girl in white heard it.
Lyra felt her hear it. The white-gloved hand under hers jerked, once, the way a hand jerks when it touches something hot. And then went quite rigid. And the girl's face turned toward her, for the first time. Slowly. And Lyra saw her eyes behind the plain white mask. Gray eyes. Very wide.
"How do you know that?" said Wren Halloway.
Her voice was hardly there.
Lyra looked at her. Through the eyeholes of the Treasurer's mask, with her face held still and quiet under it, the way a pond is quiet. And did not know what to say. And said the only true thing she had.
"It was my mother's," she said.
Wren took her hand away.
Not fast. Not as if she had been burned. Slowly. The way you take your hand away from a door you have just found, in a wall you have walked past every day of your life, and did not know was there. She stood looking at Lyra. Her mouth had set again. Tight. Tighter than before. And her eyes behind the mask were very bright.
Then she turned round, in her long white coat, and walked away.
Across the black and white floor. Between the glass tables with their glowing vials. Not fast. Very straight. To the gallery stair behind the iron pillar. And up it, step by step, with the twelve silver buttons catching the light, to the box three along from the Lady's, where a slight gray man sat at the rail with one arm along it, watching her come.
She sat down beside him.
She folded her white-gloved hands on the rail.
And she did not look at him, and she did not look down at the room, and she did not look at Lyra. She looked straight ahead of her, at nothing, at the air under the dome, with her mouth set and her hands folded, and did not move.
Lyra drew her glove back on. Slowly. Finger by finger. And did up the pearl button at the wrist.
She looked up at the box once. Only once.
The slight gray man had turned his head. Not toward Wren beside him. Toward the floor. Toward the table of childhoods by the gallery stair.
Toward her.
And she saw him lift one hand from the rail, a little. And put it down again, flat, on the gilded wood.
And tap.
She did not wait to see what he tapped. She turned back to the table, and looked at Lot 3. A small room. A mother who sang. A window over a lane. A cat called Button.
She looked at it for a long time.
Kross
He came down from the gallery at the tenth bell, alone, and found her by the table of loves.
Lyra did not see him come. That was the thing she would remember. She had been watching for him all night. Watching the box three along from Lady Vivienne's, with its curtain half drawn, and the two white masks at the rail. And at some point, between one look and the next, between the ninth bell and the tenth, the gray one had gone. Only the white one was left. The girl in the long white coat with the twelve silver buttons, sitting very straight at the rail with her hands folded, looking down.
And then he was beside her.
Not in front of her. Not across the table. Beside her. On her left, the side away from the Duke. Close enough that his gray sleeve nearly touched her gray silk. Bending over the table of loves, the way everybody bent over the tables, with his hands behind his back, looking at the little rose-colored vials on their velvet.
"Lot seventeen," he said. "A boy at a fair. Three summers. Ended kindly." He did not look at her. "I always think that one goes for too little. Don't you? Four hundred crowns. For three summers." He sighed. "People don't know what things are worth."
The Duke, on her other side, had gone very still.
"Your Grace," said the Inquisitor, still looking at the vials. "Good evening. A happy Midwinter to you."
"Kross," said the Duke.
"I won't keep her long. I promise. A word, only. I have some news she'll want." He straightened up from the table, slowly, and turned his white mask toward Lyra. Plain white. Smooth. Like hers, without the pearls. And behind it, pale washed-out eyes. Mild and tired. "Will you walk with me, Lady Lyra? Just to the end of the table. Where it's a little quieter."
She did not answer.
She did not need to. He had already offered his arm. Bent at the elbow, the way he had offered it to Silas on the steps of Saint Bride's. And she found that she had taken it. Her gloved hand in Madame Hesper's gray kid, on his gray sleeve. She did not know why. She could not have said. It was the way you take the hand of somebody who is helping you across ice.
They walked to the end of the table. Six steps. Seven. The Duke did not follow. She felt him not follow. She felt him stand by the table of loves with his glass of Marrowmere in his hand and watch them go.
At the end of the table the Inquisitor stopped.
It was a little quieter there. A little darker. The chandelier had swung away, and they were in the shadow of a pillar, a tall thin black iron pillar, one of the ribs of the dome, going up out of the floor into the dark and the falling-gold glass. Behind it, the gallery stair. In front of it, the room, all lights and masks and voices.
"Your father," said the Inquisitor, "is in my care tonight."
Lyra did not move.
She felt the quiet hold her face. All of it. Still. Like a pond with no wind on it. She felt it hold, and hold, and hold, the way a dam holds, and she did not know how long it would hold, and she did not move.
"He's quite safe," said the Inquisitor. "Quite warm. He came to us at the eighth bell, by the dyke road and the north gate, on a litter, with blankets and a hot stone. Mr. Vell took very great care of him. He's in a room on the Hill now, with a fire, and a bed, and a nurse. Not Mrs. Fell. A better nurse. One who listens." He paused. "I sat with him myself for half an hour, before I came here. He tapped for me. Four, two, nine, three. And I answered him."
The dam held.
"I'm sorry to tell you about the bargewoman," said the Inquisitor.
He said it very quietly. Very kindly. The way you tell somebody that a friend has died, in a hospital corridor.
"Mrs. Doon. Hessa. She was very brave. Mr. Vell says he has never seen anything braver. The barge was frozen in, at a place in the fen called Sedge End, and he had to fire the hay to bring her out. He had no choice. He's very sorry. She went down into the burning hold and brought your father up, out of the hay, onto the top of the stack, with the fire behind her. And she handed him down. Herself. Over the side. To Mr. Vell. Rather than let him burn." A pause. "And then the mast came down."
Lyra stood in the shadow of the iron pillar with her gloved hand on the Inquisitor's gray sleeve.
Hessa.
Big and red and furious, with her arms crossed. Slower. Treading the hay at the Brindle wharf with the fork in her hands. Forking a load as big as herself onto a stack in one swing. Pouring brandy into Wat's shoulder in the dark. Sitting on the deck with Robin on her knee. Because nobody asked me not to.
Robin first.
"The baby," said Lyra.
She heard her own voice. It came out of the still white mask quite steadily. It did not sound like her. It sounded like the voice of somebody reading a list.
The Inquisitor looked at her. For a long moment. With his pale tired eyes behind the white mask.
"Alive," he said. "With his father. In the reeds. Mr. Vell let them go." A pause. "He saw them go, and he let them. He said the old gentleman was what he had come for, and nobody else need be inconvenienced. He's very particular about that, Mr. Vell. He says it's the only thing that makes the work bearable."
Robin first.
She had kept it. Hessa had kept her promise.
The dam held.
She did not know how. She thought, later, that it was not the mask at all. That it was not Vespera's thread of quiet under the silver. That a thread could not have held that. She thought it was something else. Something she had learned in the last two years without knowing she was learning it. In the hall on Crown Walk, at eighteen, when her aunt said Your sister has done this, darling. In Fallowfield, at the bedside, watching her father tap. On the barge. In the arches. In the crypt. A way of holding still while the world came down on you, so that it would not see you break, and would not know where to hit next.
She stood quite still with her hand on his sleeve.
And the Inquisitor took his other hand from behind his back.
And laid it, very lightly, on top of hers. On her gloved hand, on his gray sleeve. A slight, dry, cool hand. And she felt the finger move.
Four.
A pause.
Two.
A pause.
Nine.
A pause.
Three.
And then it stopped. And waited. Resting on the back of her hand. The way it had rested on the stone step beside Silas. The way it had rested on her aunt's banister. Waiting for an answer.
Lyra did not answer.
She did not tap. She did not move her finger. She did not do anything at all. She stood in the shadow of the iron pillar with the Inquisitor's hand on hers, and the dam holding, and her face still and white and quiet under the mask, and let him wait.
He waited for a long time.
Then he sighed. Very softly. And took his hand away.
"No," he said. "No. You won't. Not to me." He looked at her. "You'll answer my cadet, across a table of childhoods, with your glove off, where I can see you do it. You'll give her a word she's been waiting her whole life for, and hum her the next note of her own song. But not me." He put both hands behind his back again. "Your father answers me. And the sergeant answered me, once. And my cadet's song knows the beginning of it. And I know all of it." A pause. "I find that very interesting."
He stepped back. Away from her. So that her hand came off his sleeve.
"Enjoy the rest of your evening, Lady Lyra," said the Inquisitor. "Watch the box. At a quarter before midnight, the floor will open. It's a very pretty thing to see. I wouldn't miss it." He paused. "I won't be. I'll be standing right beside it."
And he went. Up the gallery stair behind the iron pillar, slowly, not hurrying, a slight gray man in a plain white mask, with his hands behind his back. Humming.
She knew the tune.
The Duke was beside her. She had not heard him come. His great soft hand was under her elbow. He did not say anything. He did not ask anything. He stood beside her in the shadow of the iron pillar, and held her elbow, and was there.
After a long time she said, "Hessa's dead."
"I heard," said the Duke. "I'm sorry."
"They've got my father. On the Hill."
"I heard that too."
"I didn't answer him."
"No," said the Duke of Marrow. "No, my dear. You didn't." His hand on her elbow did not move. "I've been watching people not answer that man for forty years. Very few of them manage it with their face. I've never seen anybody manage it with their hand." He was quiet a moment. "Your mother would have been very proud. And very frightened. She was always both at once, about you."
Lyra stood very still.
Then she took her elbow out of the Duke's hand. Gently. And turned. And walked back along the table of loves, past Lot 17, a boy at a fair, three summers, ended kindly. To the middle of the room. To where she could see the brass box, and the plain chair beside it, and the black-gloved hand resting on a wooden case with four hollows in the lid.
And the floor round it. The ring of black squares.
She looked up.
Up past the chandelier, swinging slowly on its long thin chain. Up past the gallery rail, and the plum curtains, and the white mask at the rail of the box three along from the Lady's, sitting very straight with her hands folded. Up to the dome. To the iron ribs, and the thousand panes of glass, and the snow falling on them, catching the light, like ash.
And at the bottom of the dome, on the far side, where the glass met the black stone of the roof, in the great lead gutter as wide as a plank, she thought she saw something. A small dark shape. Lying very flat. Against the glass. Looking down.
She could not be sure. The light was in her eyes.
She looked at it anyway.
Somebody has to be here, she thought. Somebody has to watch. Somebody has to see it.
She looked at the dark shape against the glass for a long time. And then she looked back down at the floor, at the brass box, and the ring of black squares round it, and did not look away again.
