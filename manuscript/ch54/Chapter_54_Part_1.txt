Chapter 54: The Night of Masks, Part 1 of 5: Gray and White
Gray and White
On Midwinter night Lyra Thorne put on her mother's gown in her mother's room, for the second time, and this time there was nobody to pin it.
Madame Hesper was dead. Mrs. Doyle had finished the hem on Friday with Hesper's own pins in her mouth. And there was no looking-glass in her mother's room, because the looking-glass had been sold at the auction with everything else. So Lyra dressed in the dark, by the light of one candle on the bare boards, by touch. The way she had dressed every morning on the barge, and in the arches, and in the crypt, in borrowed clothes, by touch, without ever seeing herself.
The gray silk was cold. It slid over her head and down her arms and settled on her shoulders the way water settles, heavy and soft, and smelled very faintly of the rose petals in the bowl that was not there any more. She did up the hooks at the back herself, reaching round, one by one, the way Hesper had shown her. Twenty-two of them. She counted. She missed one, near the middle, and had to start again.
Then Madame Hesper's gloves. Thin pale gray kid, with a pearl button at each wrist. Hesper's own wedding gloves, which she had given to Lyra a week ago Saturday in the back room of the Needle and Thimble, and which Lyra had worn every day since.
Then the slippers. Gray kid. Her mother's.
Then the list pocket. She put her hand into the seam at the left of the waist and felt what was there. The Lady's list, with its plum ribbon. Silas's fifth letter. The lily paper. The stub of pencil. All the things she needed to carry into that place, folded small, against her side.
And then the mask.
It lay in its gray box on the floor by the candle. She knelt and opened the lid. White silk, seamless, on a frame so light she could not feel it. A seed-pearl edge. Silver thread round the eyes. Long white ribbons. And inside, on the lining, in double gold stitching that caught the candlelight, the face of a clock with its hands at a quarter to midnight.
Vespera had worked on it all afternoon, in the library, by the fire, beside the sofa where Corin lay with his new face setting. She had unpicked the silver round the eyes, very carefully, with her bird-shaped scissors, and put something underneath it, and stitched the silver back over. Lyra had not seen what. A thread. Gray, or perhaps no color at all. A thread of quiet, Vespera had said. So that nobody who reads faces can read yours.
Lyra lifted the mask out of the box and held it up to her face, and tied the long white ribbons at the back of her head, over her short ragged hair.
She felt it at once.
Not the mask. The mask weighed nothing. The quiet. A strange stillness, settling over her face, from the eyes outward, like cool water poured very slowly over warm skin. Her cheeks. Her mouth. Her forehead. She felt them go still. Not stiff. Not numb. Still. The way a pond goes still when the wind drops. She could still move them if she wanted. She could smile. She tried, and her mouth smiled. But when she did not want to, they did not move at all. Nothing showed. Not the fear. Not the grief. Not the tiredness of three nights without sleep. Not anything.
It was the strangest thing she had ever felt. It was like wearing a door.
She went down.
The house was full of sound. Children's sounds. Not crying. Something quieter. A low continual murmur of breathing and small movements and Mrs. Doyle's voice going along the rows on the top floor saying the names, and Rose singing to her baby on the second floor landing, and somewhere a child coughing, and Bet's voice saying There, Kit, there, the way Hannah Fitch said it. The fires were lit in every room on the top two floors. The house smelled of coal smoke and broth and wet wool, and under it, very faint, rose petals.
Corin was in the hall.
He was standing at the foot of the stairs, on the black and white floor, leaning on a stick. Not his crutch. A stick. A tall black one with a silver top, from the Duke, which Tibbs had brought that morning with the livery. And he was wearing the livery. A long coat of gold-colored cloth, the Marrow gold, the color of old honey, with dark blue at the cuffs and the collar and a row of brass buttons stamped with the ship and the key. White stockings. Black shoes with buckles. A white stock at the throat. And white cotton gloves, buttoned at the wrist, over his gray hands.
And Fenton's face.
It had set. Vespera had looked at it at noon and said It's set and gone to sleep in a chair. Long and narrow and sour, with the short pointed gray beard and the long thin nose with the bump on it, and the heavy lids, and the deep lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth, down, like brackets. The red was gone from it. The swelling was gone. It was just a face. An old man's sour face, that had been told bad news and had expected nothing better. It did not look painted. It did not look like anything but itself.
Only the eyes were Corin's.
He was standing with his bad leg out stiff to one side and his weight on the stick, and when he heard her on the stairs he looked up. And she saw him see her. Her mother's gray gown. The white mask with its seed-pearl edge and its silver round the eyes. Her ragged hair over the long white ribbons.
His face did not change. It was Fenton's face. It could not change the way his did.
But the eyes did.
She came down the last stairs. Down the wide bottom step that was shaped like the edge of a shell. And stopped in front of him, on the black and white floor, under the empty hook where the chandelier had been.
They looked at each other. A sour old cellarman in gold livery with another man's face, and a girl in gray silk with no face at all.
"Rule one," said Corin.
His voice was his. Vespera could not change the voice.
"Yes?"
"I can't see your face."
"I know."
"I don't like it." He looked at the mask. "I've got used to your face. I've spent a long time getting used to it. I read it every morning in my Ledger, to be sure it's still there." He paused. "And now I can't see it."
Lyra looked at him through the eyeholes of the white mask, with her face still and quiet under it, the way a pond is quiet.
"It's still there," she said. "Under this. I promise."
He nodded.
Then he put out his hand. His right hand, in its white cotton glove. And took hers, in Madame Hesper's gray kid. And turned it over, palm up. And tapped on it.
Once.
And twice, quick.
Heard.
She closed her hand over his finger.
"Come back," she said. "After. Up the chute. Oren will be there. Juno will whistle. Come back here. To this hall." She heard her voice. It was quite steady. The mask kept her face still, but it could not keep her voice still, and her voice was steady anyway. "I'll be here. On the bottom step. I'll wait."
"I'll come back."
"Rule one."
"Rule one," said Corin.
And then Oren was at the green door at the back of the hall, in Terrence's old coat with the bald fur collar, with his sword on his back in its sacking and his left arm in its black sling. And Gideon beside him, small and thin in his brown jacket, with a lump in the inside pocket where jar H.297 was and a stub of blue chalk in his fist. And behind them, carried in the crook of Oren's good arm because she could not walk, wrapped in three shawls and a boy's jacket, with her bound feet tucked up under her and a tin whistle on a greasy string round her neck beside her grandfather's key, Juno.
They were going first. Out by the stable yard and the Mews Road, on foot, in the dark, down the Hill to Cinder Lane and the coal chute, and the roofs of Exchange Street. They had to be in their places by the tenth bell.
Nobody said much.
Oren looked at Lyra. At the mask. He did not like masks. He had told her so. Where I come from, her mother had said once, Oren said, we only put on masks for people we mean to leave.
"All's well, my lady," he said.
"All's well, Captain."
Then he turned and went out through the green door with Juno in his arm and Gideon at his heels, and the door swung shut behind them, and they were gone.
Corin went at the half hour.
Tibbs took him. Out by the stable gate, on the box of the Duke's carriage, round the Mews Road to the back of the Crescent, to the back door of Marrow House, where a tall sour man in gold livery with a stiff left leg would present himself at the eighth bell to carry the Duke's wine, as he had done every Midwinter for thirty-one years. And the real Fenton was already two hours north on the road to his sister's, with a basket and a bottle and a great deal of money, and would not be back until the spring thaw.
Lyra stood at the foot of the stairs and listened to the carriage go out of the stable yard. The wheels on the cobbles. The horses. The gate. And then nothing.
She sat down on the bottom step, the wide one, shaped like the edge of a shell, in her mother's gown, with her hands in her lap.
And waited for Tibbs to come back for her.
Tibbs Drives
Tibbs came back for her at the three-quarter, and he drove her round to Marrow House the long way, by the Mews Road, in the dark, at a walk, and on the way he told her about the night her mother left.
She did not ask him to. She was sitting inside the carriage, alone, on the blue velvet seat, in her mother's gown and the Treasurer's mask, with her hands in her lap. The carriage was cold. The windows were black. She could hear the horses' hooves on the frozen cobbles of the Mews Road, slow, clop, clop, clop, and the creak of the springs, and the wind.
And then the little hatch in the front wall of the carriage slid open. The one coachmen use to talk to the people inside. A square of darkness, and cold air coming through it, and the smell of horse.
"Miss Lyra," said Tibbs's voice through the hatch.
"Yes?"
"I'm going slow. On purpose. His Grace said take her round slow, Tibbs, and don't bring her to my door before the eighth." A pause. "So I've got a bit of time. And there's a thing I've been carrying. Eleven years. I've been waiting for you to be old enough. And then I was waiting for you to come home. And then I thought you never would." Another pause, longer. "I'd like to put it down, miss. If you'll let me."
Lyra sat very still on the blue velvet.
"Put it down," she said.
The carriage went on at a walk. Clop. Clop. The hatch stayed open. Tibbs did not say anything for a while. She heard him cluck to the horses, very softly.
"I drove her," he said at last. "Your mother. That night. Eleven years ago, the autumn, the last week before the frost. I drove the carriage."
Lyra did not breathe.
"The Captain rode beside," said Tibbs. "Oren. On the gray. He'll have told you that. To the city gate. And at the gate she put her hand out of the window and said, Thank you, Captain. You may go home now. And he went home. He had to. She'd told him to." Tibbs's voice was rough and low. "She didn't tell me to go home, miss. She told me to drive on."
The wheels creaked.
"East," said Tibbs. "Out of the gate, and along the river road, past the old customs house, and out onto the salt flats. In the dark. With the lamps lit. Two hours, at a walk, because she said go slow, Tibbs, the horses mustn't be tired when we get there." He clucked to the horses again. "I didn't know where there was. I didn't ask. You didn't ask your mother things, miss. Not when she had that look. You'll know the look. You've got it yourself. I saw it on your face on Friday, at the bottom of Comb Street."
Lyra put her gloved hand up, without meaning to, and touched the edge of the mask.
"And out on the flats," said Tibbs, "in the middle of nowhere, in the dark, there was a light. On the ground. A lantern. Somebody holding a lantern. A slight somebody, a boy or a woman, I couldn't tell, in a long coat with the hood up, and boots. Standing on the flats alone in the dark, waiting. And your mother said Stop here, Tibbs. And I stopped." A long pause. "And behind the one with the lantern, on the flats, there was something I didn't understand. I still don't. Something big. Long and dark. Lying on the ground, like a great fish on a riverbank. Or a boat with no river. And ropes going up from it into the dark. Up. Into the sky."
A long dark flying shape, Oren had said, on Friday night, on the way back from her aunt's. Over the salt flats. With a light under it. I thought it was a cloud. I thought my eyes were tired.
"She got out of the carriage," said Tibbs. "By herself. She wouldn't let me get down to help her. She had her bag. Just the one bag. And the blue cloak Madame Hesper made her, with the fur in the hood. She stood by the carriage door in the dark for a bit, looking at the light. And then she turned round, and looked up at me on the box."
His voice changed.
"And she said, Tibbs. I've a message for my daughter. Not now. When she's grown. When she comes asking. She'll come asking, Tibbs, one day. She's that sort. And when she does, you tell her this."
The carriage had stopped. Lyra had not felt it stop. They were standing still in the dark of the Mews Road, with the horses blowing, and the hatch open, and the cold coming through it.
"What?" said Lyra.
She heard her own voice. It was very small.
"She said," said Tibbs. And stopped. And started again, very carefully, the way a man says a thing he has said over to himself a thousand times so as not to lose a word of it. "She said, Tell her to keep counting. Tell her I'm counting too. Tell her I'll know."
Nobody said anything.
The horses stamped. The wind went down the Mews Road between the high brick walls and the coach-houses and the dung heaps, and moaned a little in the hatch.
Keep counting.
Lyra sat on the blue velvet seat in the dark with her gloved hands pressed together in her lap.
She had always counted. All her life. Steps, and stairs, and pins, and bells, and children, and spoons. Everything. She did not know why. She had never known why. Rowan teased her about it when they were small. Her governess had tried to stop her. Corin had caught it from her like a cold. I always count, she had said to him on Friday night, on the floor of the crypt. Now more than ever.
And her mother had brushed her hair a hundred strokes every night, and counted them out loud, in a language Lyra did not know.
"Is that all?" she said.
"That's all, miss. That's all of it." Tibbs's voice was very rough. "Then she went across the flats to the one with the lantern, who took her bag, and they went together toward the big dark thing on the ground. And I sat on the box and watched the lantern go, smaller and smaller. And then there was a sort of a rushing sound, a long way off, like wind in a chimney. And the lantern went up."
"Up?"
"Up, miss. Into the sky. Slowly. Like a star going home." He was quiet for a moment. "And then it was gone. And I turned the horses round and drove back to the city, and in at the gate, and up the Hill, and put the carriage away and rubbed the horses down and went to bed over the stable. And in the morning your father said she'd gone to the coast, for her health. And then in the spring he said she'd died there. And there was a coffin, closed, and a funeral at Saint Clement's, and the whole Hill came." He stopped. "I stood at the back. In my good coat. And I never said a word to anybody. Not to your father. Not to the Captain. Not to anybody, eleven years."
"Why not?"
"Because she asked me not to, miss," said Tibbs simply. "In the carriage, before. She said, Nobody, Tibbs. Not Aurelian. Not Oren. Only her. And only when she asks." A pause. "And you've never asked. Not till tonight. And you didn't ask tonight, neither, not properly. But I thought—" He stopped. "I thought you might not come back from that place, miss. Tonight. And I'd be stood at the back of another funeral at Saint Clement's in my good coat, with it still in me. And I couldn't."
Lyra put her hand up to the hatch.
She could not reach it, not sitting. So she stood up, in the carriage, bent over under the low roof, in her mother's gown, and put her gloved hand through the little square hatch into the dark and the cold. And found his shoulder, on the box. The rough wool of the three-caped coat, wet with snow. And held it.
"Thank you, Tibbs," she said.
He did not say anything. She felt his shoulder shake, once, under her hand.
Then he picked up the reins, and clucked to the horses, and the carriage began to move again. Slowly. Clop. Clop. Round the end of the Mews Road. Toward the back of the Crescent, and the stable gate of Marrow House, and the Duke, and the Bourse.
Lyra sat down again on the blue velvet.
She counted the hoofbeats. All the way. She did not try to stop.
I'm counting too, her mother had said. I'll know.
She did not know what it meant. She did not know if it meant anything at all, or if it was just the kind of thing a woman says to a coachman in the dark on the salt flats when she is leaving her children and cannot bear it. She did not know if her mother was alive. She did not know where the lantern had gone, into the sky, eleven years ago.
But she counted. Two hundred and eleven hoofbeats, from the end of the Mews Road to the stable gate of Marrow House.
And she thought that somewhere, very far away, to the east, over the Narrow Sea, perhaps somebody else was counting too.
Exchange Street
The Duke of Marrow was asleep in his carriage when she got into it, or pretending to be.
Tibbs had brought her round by the Mews Road, in the dark, to the back gate of Marrow House, and through the stable yard, and round the side of the great curved white house to the front, where the carriage stood at the foot of the steps with its lamps lit and its four gray horses blowing in the cold and two footmen in gold holding the door. And the Duke was inside. Filling it. He took up the whole of the forward seat and half of the back one, a great soft mountain of a man in dove-gray silk, with his fair-silver curls spread out on the blue velvet cushion behind his head and his eyes shut and his ten rings glittering on his folded hands.
He opened one eye as she climbed in.
"Ah," he said. "The mask."
"Yes, your Grace."
"His mask." The eye looked at it. Pale gray-green. Sleepy. Missing nothing. "Very fine work. Very fine. Whoever made that frame knew what they were doing. I can't see a single join." He shut the eye again. "It suits you. I'm sorry to say. It would."
The door shut. The carriage moved.
Lyra sat on the edge of the back seat, upright, with her hands in her lap and her mother's gray silk spread round her, and looked out of the window. They came down off the Crescent past the gas lamps and the white houses. Every house was lit tonight. Every window. Midwinter on the Hill. She could see through the windows into the rooms as they passed. Fires. Candles. Long tables with white cloths. Children in their nightclothes at an upstairs window, with their faces pressed to the glass, watching the carriages go down.
She had been one of those children. Every Midwinter. With Rowan beside her. Watching her father's carriage go down the Hill to the reception at the Council House, with her mother in it, in gray, and her father in his chain.
She did not look at them after that.
"You'll want to know the form," said the Duke, with his eyes shut.
"Yes."
"It's very simple. It always is, with the worst things." He folded his hands the other way on his stomach. "We go down Exchange Street at a walk, in a line, with all the other carriages. It takes an hour. It always takes an hour. Nobody minds. The point of it is to be seen going down Exchange Street in a line. At the bottom there are torches, and the steps, and the bronze doors. And the Weigher."
"I know about the Weigher."
"You know he stands at the carriage door. You don't know what he does." The Duke opened both eyes and looked at her. "He looks at you. That's all. Very quickly. He looks at you and he looks at me and he looks at the card in his hand, and then he calls out who we are. His Grace the Duke of Marrow, and guest. And everybody on the steps turns round to see. And that's all." He paused. "He's been doing it for forty years. He's never once been wrong about a face. Never once. Masked or not." He shut his eyes again. "So when he looks at you, my dear, don't look back. Look at his shoes. He has very fine shoes. He'll like that."
The carriage turned out of the Crescent.
And down. Down the steep road where the shop windows had one thing in each and no price. Down past the end of Velvet Street. Lyra did look, that time. She could not help it. The shutters of the Needle and Thimble were still up. The black crape was still on the door. Somebody had put fresh flowers on the step, white ones, and they were covered in snow.
Down, and down, to where the windows had prices in them again. And then into Exchange Street.
And Exchange Street was a river of light.
She had never seen it like this. She had seen it from the outside a hundred times, by day, a wide busy dull street going down to the river, full of carts and clerks and men in hats. Tonight it was something else. Every lamp was lit, on both sides, all the way down. And between the lamps, on tall iron poles fixed to the house fronts, torches. Real ones. Flames. Burning in iron baskets, so that the whole street flickered and moved, gold and orange and red, and the shadows of the carriages went sliding along the house fronts beside them like great black animals. And the carriages. A line of them, the whole length of the street, nose to tail, going down at a walk. Black ones and blue ones and green ones and one white one with gold wheels. Each with its lamps lit. Each with its horses, gray and bay and black, blowing clouds into the cold, their harness shining. Each with its coachman high on the box in his house colors and a footman or two standing on the board at the back.
And people. On the pavements, on both sides, behind ropes. Hundreds of them. Ordinary people, from the city, from the river parishes, in their shawls and their caps, packed in tight behind the ropes in the cold, come to watch the Hill go to the Bourse in its masks. Pressing forward. Pointing. A child on a man's shoulders, waving. A woman with a baby, holding it up to see.
Lyra saw their faces in the torchlight as the carriage went past them at a walk. Thin faces. Gray faces, some of them. She did not know if it was the light.
They were cheering.
Not loudly. Not all of them. But some. A ragged cheer, going up and down the street as each carriage passed, the way people cheer a procession. Because it was Midwinter, and there were lights, and horses, and fine ladies in masks, and it was something to see.
She thought of the green paper in Lucian's folder. Sundries, the Hill. To the Council, the balance. For the lamps of the Hill.
She thought of the four children in the crypt of Saint Bride's, who would have been taken at the seventh bell that morning, with Hannah Fitch holding their hands.
The mask held her face still. She was glad of it.
"Horses," said the Duke quietly, with his eyes shut.
She looked.
At the corner where Cinder Lane went off to the left, behind the Bourse, there were riders. Four of them. In a line across the mouth of the lane, very still, on big dark horses. Not Watch. Soldiers. Black coats, long, buttoned to the throat, and flat black caps with a white badge, and silver at the shoulders of the one in front. A tall man on a gray horse, sitting very straight, with no beard, and a face like Oren's would have been twenty years ago, if Oren had never laughed.
He was looking at the carriages. At each one. As it passed. The way a man looks at a column of men on a march, counting.
His eyes went over the Duke's carriage. Over the gold ship and key on the door, not sooted tonight. Over the window. Over her.
They did not stop.
Then the carriage had gone past, and the riders were behind them, and Lyra let out her breath.
Commander H. Vance, Wren's list had said. Exchange Street, four, mounted.
Oren's brother.
And behind him, in Cinder Lane, in the dark, against the wall by the coal chute, Oren. With his sword. Waiting for two short whistles.
She did not let herself think about that.
The carriage stopped.
They were at the bottom of the street. She could see the Bourse through the window now, close, filling it. The great dark front of it, black stone, with the bronze doors standing open in the middle and gold light pouring out of them onto the steps. And the steps, wide and shallow, going up, with a long carpet down them, plum-colored, and torches on both sides. And men on the steps. In plum livery. Dozens of them. Footmen and porters and boys with lamps. And at the bottom of the steps, in a row, six men who were not in plum. In the dark blue of the Watch, with their brass buttons and their tall hats and their long staves. Front steps, six. Constable Hurley.
And above it all, on top of the black stone, the dome.
She had seen it from outside before. She had never seen it lit. It rose up out of the top of the Bourse into the night like a great glass bubble on a black cup, a hundred feet across, made of a thousand panes of glass in an iron frame. And it was full of light. The light came up through it from the hall below, gold and warm, so that the whole dome glowed against the dark sky like a lantern. Like a moon that had come down to sit on a roof. The snow had been swept off it, and the glass shone.
And somewhere up there, on the edge of it, in the great lead gutter that ran round the bottom of the glass, as wide as a plank, a girl of fourteen with bound feet and a tin whistle was lying on her front in the cold, looking down.
The carriage door opened.
A man stood there.
He was tall and very thin and very old, in a long coat of plum velvet so dark it was nearly black, with silver buttons, and silver lace at his wrists, and on a silver chain round his neck a small silver scale, the kind goldsmiths use to weigh gold dust, with two tiny pans that swung. He had a card in one long white hand. His face was long and pale and fine, and perfectly calm, and his eyes were the palest eyes Lyra had ever seen, so pale they were almost white.
The eyes went to the Duke. Then to the card. Then to her.
She looked at his shoes.
They were very fine. Black, and narrow, and polished so bright that she could see the torches in them, and her own white mask, small and pale, like a moon.
The Weigher looked at her for perhaps a breath. Perhaps two.
Then he turned his head to the steps, and the torches, and the hundreds of people behind the ropes, and called out in a clear high carrying voice, like a bell:
"His Grace the Duke of Marrow. And guest."
And everybody on the steps turned round to see.
