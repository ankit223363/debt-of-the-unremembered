Chapter 21: The Hollow Urchin, Part 1 of 5: The Red Door
The Red Door
Exchange Street at night was a river of blue light.
Corin came up it slowly, with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, keeping to the shadows on the left side, where the counting houses stood shoulder to shoulder with their windows dark. He had walked two circles already since the Cut. Three lefts, and three lefts again. Nothing behind him that he could see. But he did not trust that anymore. He did not trust anything tonight.
The street went up the hill in a long straight slope, wide and cobbled and swept clean of snow. And at the top of it, where the hill flattened out, the Memory Bourse sat like a great pale drum that someone had set down in the middle of the city and forgotten to take away.
It was even bigger at night. The pale stone of its walls had gone gray-blue in the dark, and every one of its tall round-topped windows, three rows of them, all the way round, was lit from inside with the same cold blue glow, so that the whole building seemed to float a little above the street, humming, like a lantern made of ice. And over it all, the dome. Huge and smooth and black against the stars, with a thin ring of blue light running all the way round its base like a crown.
There were carriages outside. A long line of them, along the curb at the top of the street, waiting. Black ones and dark green ones and one that was painted cream and gold, with four white horses standing very still in the frost, their breath going up in clouds under the lamps. The coachmen sat hunched on their boxes in great coats and mufflers, with their whips across their knees. Some of them were asleep.
Sunday night, and the Bourse was open. Of course it was. It was always open. People did not stop wanting to buy other people's memories just because it was Sunday.
Corin did not go near the front.
He went down the side, the way he had gone on Thursday. Into the lane. A lane so narrow and so steep that it was nearly a gutter, the Ledger said, in his own hand, from three nights ago. He had read the page twice on the walk up, by the light of the street lamps, to be sure.
The lane was the same. Dark. Narrow. Running steeply down between the great curved wall of the Bourse on one side and the high blank backs of the counting houses on the other. A little blue light spilled over the top of the curved wall from the windows high above, so that the frost on the cobbles had a dim, ghostly glow. Barrels and crates were stacked against the walls. Frozen sacks. A heap of cabbage leaves and onion skins and potato peelings, white with frost, that somebody had thrown out of a door and not swept up. A cat ran across the lane in front of him, low and quick, and was gone into a crack between two barrels.
And halfway down, on the right, in the curved wall, the red door.
It was shut. Of course it was shut. It had been shut on Thursday, too, and he had knocked, and the grating had slid open with a bang, and a big red angry face under a white cap had looked out at him.
There was light round its edges. Yellow light, and steam, curling out through the cracks into the cold. And noise. The clatter of pans and the hiss of something frying and a woman's voice, shouting. And the smell. Roast meat and onions and gravy, so thick and rich and warm that it went into Corin like a blow, and his empty stomach turned over.
Over the door, on its iron bracket, the lantern.
And under the lantern, on the wall, where on Thursday there had been a tiny chalk mark, no bigger than a thumbnail, half hidden by frost—
Nothing.
Corin stopped.
He went close. He put his face almost against the cold stone, in the yellow light from the lantern, and looked.
The frost was still there, in a thin white patch, on the stone under the bracket. But in the middle of the patch, there was a smear. A short, thick, curving smear, where the frost had been rubbed away and the stone underneath showed dark and wet. And at one end of the smear, so faint he almost missed it, a little white dust. Chalk dust. Ground into the stone.
Somebody had rubbed it out.
Not washed it. Not scrubbed it. Rubbed it out with a thumb. Quickly. Hard. The way you rub out a mistake on a slate.
He stood looking at it for a long moment.
An open book. And above it, an eye. Open.
The eye was gone.
He knocked.
Not slow. He would never knock slow again, he thought, as long as he lived. Quick and light, with his knuckles. Three times, and then two.
The grating slid open with a bang.
It was the same face. Big and red and wet and shiny with steam, under a white cap, with flour on its nose and on its eyebrows and in the deep creases on either side of its mouth. Small, hot, angry black eyes. A jaw like a shovel. The face of a woman who had been cooking for three hundred people since five o'clock that morning and had not sat down once and would not sit down until midnight and did not see why anybody else should either.
"What?" said the face.
Then it saw him. And it stopped.
Corin watched it change. The anger went out of it all at once, like the air out of a bellows. What came in instead was something he had not expected. It was not fear, exactly. It was something closer to grief. The small black eyes went wet and bright, and the shovel jaw clenched, and the big red face seemed, for a moment, to sag, as if the bones under it had gone tired.
"Oh," said the cook. "Oh, it's you."
"I've come to see Tam," said Corin. "Tam Hollin."
"I know who you've come to see." The cook's voice had gone low and rough. She looked over her shoulder, quickly, into the steam and noise and yellow light behind her. Then back at him. "You can't."
"Why not?"
The cook did not answer at once. She put one big floury hand up to the grating and held on to one of its bars, as if she needed something to hold. Her knuckles were red and cracked and burned in a dozen places, old burns and new ones, the hands of a woman who had been taking hot pans out of ovens for forty years.
"Because he's gone," she said.
The word dropped into the lane like a stone.
"Gone where?"
"I don't know." Her voice cracked. "I don't know, sir. Nobody knows. Nobody will say." She looked over her shoulder again, and lowered her voice even further, so that he had to lean close to the bars to hear her over the frying. "Last night. After the service. Late. Gone midnight. We were scrubbing down. He came through the kitchen like he always does, on his way down to his bed, with his candle, and he stopped and gave me a kiss on the cheek like he always does, because he's a good boy, he's always been a good boy, he knows I've got nobody." She swallowed. "And he went down the back stair. And there were men on the back stair."
"What men?"
"I don't know their names. I don't want to know their names." Her hand tightened on the bar. "Big ones. From downstairs. From the bottom of the house, where the Lady keeps the things she doesn't put in the vault. They don't come up to the kitchen. Ever. They're not allowed. But they were on the back stair last night, two of them, and they had him by the arms, one on each side, and they were walking him down." Her mouth worked. "He looked back at me. Over his shoulder. And he smiled. Like it was nothing. Like he was going to fetch the coal. And he said, Night, Mrs. Pratt. Don't let the gravy catch." She stopped. "And that's the last I saw of him."
Corin did not say anything.
He was thinking of a young man in a servants' hall on the Hill, in a footman's coat, breaking his bread in half. A pale narrow face. Quick brown eyes. Ears that stuck out a little, because his hair was cut so short. A small white scar through his left eyebrow, like a tick on a page. Name's Tam. Tam Hollin. I'm Lady Vivienne's.
And on Thursday night, in the vault under the Bourse, among the racks of glowing vials. Voice of a sister, laughing. Tam turning round, a few steps ahead, and looking at Corin's face. And something younger coming into his own, just for a moment.
My mother sold it all, Tam had said. Later. In Bram's back room, with the glue pot going blup. To the Bank. A bit at a time. And then she had nothing left, and they took her anyway.
"Did they say why?" said Corin. "The men. Did anyone say anything?"
Mrs. Pratt shook her heavy head. Then stopped.
"One thing," she said. "This morning. When I went up with the Lady's chocolate, like I always do, at seven. She was sitting at her window with her back to me, looking out at the snow. And she didn't turn round. And she said—" The cook's face twisted. "She said, Tell Cook I'm sorry about the boy. But he read the wrong books, and he talked to the wrong people, and he drew on my walls. And she said, Tell her to rub it out."
She looked at the wall beside the door. At the smear in the frost.
"So I did," she said. "With my thumb. Saints forgive me. I did."
The grating stayed open. Mrs. Pratt stood behind it with her hand on the bar, breathing hard. Behind her, in the steam, somebody shouted for her, and she did not answer.
"He's not dead," said Corin.
He did not know he was going to say it until he said it. He did not know if it was true.
Mrs. Pratt looked at him.
"How do you know?"
"I don't." He held her small hot black eyes. "But she said sorry about the boy. Not sorry he's gone. Not sorry he's dead. People like her are careful with words. She hasn't finished with him yet."
The cook stared at him for a long moment.
Then she let go of the bar. She wiped her face with the back of her floury wrist, hard, leaving a long white streak across her red cheek, like war paint.
"If you find him," she said. "If you find him, sir. You tell him Mrs. Pratt's kept his supper. In the warming oven. Like always." Her voice cracked and she pushed it on through the crack, the way you push a cart over a rut. "You tell him the gravy didn't catch."
"I'll tell him."
"And you go careful." She looked up the lane, toward the street, and down it, toward the dark. "They've been asking. All day. The big ones. Asking at the door if anyone's been round asking for Tam. A man in a gray coat. A harvester." Her eyes came back to him. "I said no. I said nobody. I said I'd put a skewer through the first one that did."
"Thank you."
"Don't thank me," said Mrs. Pratt. "Find him."
And the grating slammed shut.
Corin stood alone in the narrow lane in the blue half-dark, with the steam curling out round the edges of the red door, and the smell of roast meat and gravy, and the smear on the wall where the eye had been.
He took out the Ledger and wrote it down. All of it. Tam taken. Saturday night, after midnight. Two big men from "downstairs" in the Bourse. Back stair. "Night, Mrs. Pratt. Don't let the gravy catch." Vivienne: "Sorry about the boy. He read the wrong books, talked to the wrong people, drew on my walls. Rub it out." The eye is rubbed out. They are asking for a harvester in a gray coat.
He looked at the last line.
Then he looked down the lane, toward the bottom, where it ran out into the dark between the barrels.
And saw the boy.
The Boy on the Railings
He was sitting at the very bottom of the lane, where it ran out between two tall walls into a little dark yard.
There was a railing there. An old iron one, waist-high, black and rusted, that ran across the bottom of the lane to stop carts from rolling down into the yard below. It was furred all over with frost. Every bar of it had a white coat, thick and soft as fur, and the top rail glittered in the faint blue light that came down over the wall of the Bourse.
The boy was sitting on the top rail.
Just sitting. With his legs hanging down on the lane side and his hands resting on the rail on either side of him, the way a boy sits on a fence in summer, watching the cows. He was not holding on. He was not swinging his legs. He was just sitting there, perfectly still, in the dark, looking up the lane.
Looking at Corin.
Corin had not seen him when he came down. He was sure of that. He had looked. He always looked now. But the boy had been sitting so still, in the shadow at the very bottom, that he had seemed part of the railing. A lump of frost. A heap of rags someone had thrown over the bars.
He was not a heap of rags. He was a boy. About thirteen, Corin thought. Perhaps a little younger. Small and thin, with narrow shoulders and a long thin neck and a head that seemed slightly too big for it, like a bird's. His hair was fair, almost white, and cut very short and very badly, as if someone had done it with a knife. His face was thin and pointed and pale. Not pale the way Wren's face was pale, from being indoors. Pale the way a candle is pale. Waxy. With a faint blue shine.
And he was wearing a shirt.
That was all. A shirt. Gray cotton, thin and old, with the sleeves torn off at the elbows and the collar missing, so that his thin arms and his thin neck were bare. And a pair of trousers that had been a man's once, cut off at the knee and tied round his waist with string. And nothing on his feet.
Nothing at all.
His feet hung down from the frosty rail. Bare. Small and thin and very dirty, with long toes. And they were blue. Not a little blue, the way your fingers go blue on a cold morning. Deeply, darkly blue, all over, from the ankles down. The color of a bruise. The color of the sky in the minute after sunset. The skin round the toenails had gone white and hard and shiny, like wax.
The frost on the rail under his hands had not melted. Corin noticed that. He could see it in the faint blue light. White frost round each thin hand, untouched, as if the hands were as cold as the iron.
And the boy was not shivering.
Corin stood in the lane and looked at him. He had seen children in the cold before. Every winter, in every street of the Ink Quarter. Children with no coats, with no shoes, with sacks round their shoulders and rags round their feet. He had seen them huddled in doorways, curled up against the warm wall of a bakery, crouched round a brazier with their hands held out, shaking so hard their teeth clattered like dice in a cup.
He had never seen one sit still in it.
The boy's thin chest went up and down, slowly, evenly. His breath came out of his mouth in small white puffs. He looked at Corin with a pair of very large, very pale gray eyes, quite calm, quite clear, the way you might look at a horse going by in the street. Interested, a little. Not much.
"You're the harvester," he said.
His voice was light and flat and quite polite. There was nothing in it. No fear. No cheek. No question. It was the voice you would use to say it's Tuesday.
Corin did not answer at once.
"They've been looking for you," said the boy. "All day. I heard them. Two of Mister Gant's men. At the top of the lane, and at the front steps, and at the kitchen door. A man in a gray coat. A harvester. Thin. Asks for Tam." He tilted his head a little to one side, still looking at Corin. "You're thin. And you've got a gray coat. And you asked for Tam."
"Who's Mister Gant?"
"He keeps the downstairs," said the boy. "For the Lady." He said it the way he had said everything else. Flatly. Like a list. "Do you want to know where they took Tam?"
Corin's heart gave one hard thump.
He did not show it. He made himself stand still, in the middle of the lane, with his hands in his pockets, and look at the thin blue-footed boy on the frosty rail as calmly as the boy was looking at him.
"Do you know?"
"Yes."
"How?"
"I was there." The boy did not blink. "I'm always there. I sit on the back stair at night. Nobody minds me. Nobody sees me. I'm nobody." A pause. "I saw them take him. And I went after them, down the bottom stair and out through the coal door, and along the back way, and I saw where they went."
"Weren't you afraid?"
The boy looked at him. And for the first time, something happened in his face. Not much. A tiny frown, between his pale eyebrows. Puzzled. The way you might frown at a word in a language you did not speak.
"No," he said. "Why would I be?"
Corin walked down the lane toward him.
Slowly. The way you walk toward a bird on a branch. Not because he thought the boy would fly. Because he did not know what the boy would do, and he did not want to find out by being too quick.
The boy watched him come. He did not move.
Corin stopped an arm's length from the rail. Close enough now to see the boy's face clearly, in the faint blue light from over the wall. The waxy pale skin. The large pale eyes. The thin lips, which were not blue, which was strange. And a long scrape on the boy's left forearm, from the wrist almost to the elbow, raw and red and full of grit, as if he had fallen hard on a cobbled street and slid. It was not new. It was not old either. It had not been washed. It had not been tied up. Bits of dirt and gravel were still stuck in it.
The boy did not seem to know it was there.
"What's your name?" said Corin.
"Gideon."
"Gideon what?"
The boy thought about it. It seemed to take him a long time.
"Just Gideon," he said at last. "There was another bit. Once. I sold it."
Corin looked at him.
"Your other name?"
"No. Not the name. The wanting of it." Gideon said it quite patiently, as if he had explained it before, to other people, many times, and they never understood either. "I had a name. A whole one. I still have it, I think. Somewhere. But I sold the bit of me that cared about it. When I was nine. For a hot potato and a pair of socks." He looked down at his own blue feet, hanging from the rail. "The socks wore out."
The cold went into Corin then. Not the cold of the night. Another one.
"You sold—"
"Everything." Gideon looked up again. His pale eyes were perfectly calm. "Not the memories. They don't want those, mostly. Not from boys like me. Boys like me haven't got anything worth buying. Just being hungry, and being cold, and being hit." He said it without any feeling at all. "But the Lady buys other things too. Downstairs. Not memories. Feelings. The bits that come with the memories. The cold you felt. The fright you felt. The being sad. She pays a copper for a fright. Two for a sorrow, if it's a big one. Three for being cold, in winter, because the rich ladies on the Hill like to buy a bit of cold to put in their ices at summer parties. It makes them shiver, and they laugh." He paused. "I sold my cold when I was ten. And my fright. And my sad. One at a time. And then all the rest."
"All of it?"
"All of it," said Gideon. "I haven't got anything left. Not anything. I don't get cold. I don't get frightened. I don't get sad, or happy, or cross." He looked at Corin with mild interest. "I don't get hurt, either. That's the best bit, Mister Gant says. You can't hurt a boy who doesn't feel it. So you don't have to bother."
Corin looked at the raw scrape on the thin arm. At the gravel stuck in it.
At the blue feet.
"Gideon," he said, and his voice did not come out properly. "Your feet. You've got frostbite. Do you know that? If you stay out here all night—"
"I know." The boy looked down at them again, with the same mild interest. "I lost two toes last winter. On the other foot. The small ones." He lifted the right foot a little, so that Corin could see. And there, at the end of the row of long dirty toes, where the two smallest should have been, there were only two round, shiny, puckered stumps. "It didn't hurt. They just went black and then one morning they weren't there." He put the foot down again. "Mister Gant says I'll lose the whole foot one day. He says it doesn't matter. He says I'll still be able to sit on a stair."
Corin could not speak.
He stood in the frozen lane with his hands in his pockets and looked at the boy on the rail, and for a long moment he could not say anything at all.
Do not, said Malakor, very quietly, in the back of his head. Do not take off your coat, boy. I know you want to. Do not.
Corin's hands had already gone to his buttons.
He will not feel the warmth, said Malakor. And you will freeze. And you need to be able to walk tonight. And run, perhaps. And hold a rod. A pause. Help him afterward. If there is an afterward.
Corin's fingers stopped on the top button.
He looked at Gideon. And Gideon looked back at him, with his large pale empty eyes, quite patiently, waiting to see what he would do. As if it did not matter very much either way.
Corin took his hands away from his buttons.
He did something else instead. He put his hand up to his throat, inside his collar, to his scarf. Not a real scarf. A long strip of gray blanket, from his own bed at No. 14, that he had folded and wound round his neck every morning since the snow came, because the Ledger told him to. He unwound it now. It was warm from his neck.
He held it out.
"For your feet," he said. "Wrap them. Please. Even if you can't feel it."
Gideon looked at the strip of gray blanket for a long moment. Then he took it. Politely. The way a child takes a present from a stranger, because that is what you are supposed to do.
He did not wrap his feet. He laid the blanket across his knees, neatly, and folded his blue hands on top of it.
"Thank you," he said.
And then, in exactly the same flat, light, polite voice:
"It'll be a copper. To tell you where Tam is. One copper." He looked at Corin. "I don't want it. I don't want anything. But Mister Gant says always ask for a copper. He says if you don't ask for a copper, people think you're mad."
