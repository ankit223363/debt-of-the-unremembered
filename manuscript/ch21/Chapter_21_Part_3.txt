Chapter 21: The Hollow Urchin, Part 3 of 5: A Card from the Dome
A Card from the Dome
They were halfway up the lane when the carriage door opened.
Corin did not see it at first. He was walking beside Gideon, slowly, because the boy walked slowly in his blanket-wrapped feet, shuffling a little, the way an old man shuffles in slippers. He was thinking about Linden Walk. About first light, and how long it would take to get from Cinder Lane to Pruett's yard, and whether Oren would come, and whether Sergeant Moll would come, and whether either of them would still be alive by the morning if they did.
Then Gideon stopped.
He did not say anything. He just stopped, in the middle of the lane, with his blanketed feet together, and looked up toward the top. Toward Exchange Street, and the blue light, and the line of waiting carriages.
Corin looked too.
One of the carriages had moved. The cream-and-gold one, with the four white horses. It had come forward from the line along the curb, slowly, without a sound, and stopped across the top of the lane. Right across it. So that the lane was closed off at the top by its high cream-colored side, with a gold crest painted on the door, and the four white horses standing very still beyond it, steaming in the frost, their harness glittering.
The crest on the door was a dome. A small gold dome, with rays coming out of it, like a rising sun.
And the door was opening.
Nobody got out. The door just swung open, slowly, on oiled hinges, and stayed open. Inside, the carriage was dark. Corin could see the pale gleam of cream silk on the seat, and a lamp turned very low, and nothing else.
Then, from inside, a hand came out.
A small hand. A child's. In a black glove, fingerless, with the fingers sticking out of it thin and pale and blue at the tips. It held something between two of those blue fingers. A card. Square and stiff and black, with something on it that caught the light from the street.
The hand held the card out into the air of the lane. And waited.
Gideon did not move. He did not say anything. He stood beside Corin with his white head tilted a little to one side, looking at the small blue-fingered hand in the carriage door, with no expression at all.
"Is that one of you?" said Corin, very low.
"Yes," said Gideon. "That's Pin. He does the Lady's carriage." He paused. "He's six."
Corin walked up the lane.
He did it slowly. The way you walk up to a dog you do not know. He kept his hands where they could be seen, out of his pockets, open. He did not look into the carriage. He looked at the hand.
When he was close enough, he reached out and took the card.
The small hand let go of it at once, and went back inside, into the dark, and was gone. Corin heard a tiny sound from in there. Not a word. Just a breath.
He looked down at the card.
It was black. Thick and stiff and smooth, like the inside of a mussel shell. On the front, in the middle, pressed into the black in raised silver, was the same little dome with its rays. And under it, in silver ink, in a large, round, beautiful hand, sloping a little to the right, with long flourishes on the tails of the letters:
Mister Vane.
It is a quarter past eight. You have three hours and three quarters.
I know you have not been to Quill Row. My little ones would have told me. So I can only suppose that you are thinking about it. Please don't think too long. Thinking is so tiring, and it so rarely helps.
The kitchen door. Midnight. All six. And the sister is yours to keep.
If not, I shall have to make other arrangements. I should so much rather not. They are always untidy, and somebody always cries.
V.
And under that, smaller, as if added afterward, with a little smile in the shape of the letters:
P.S. Do give my regards to Gideon. He has been on my back stair for two years and I have never once seen him sit in the lane. I wonder what you said to him.
Corin read it twice.
Then he looked up. Past the open carriage door, at the cream silk seat in the dark, and the low lamp. At the small shape crouched in the far corner of the seat, in the shadow, with its knees drawn up and its blue-fingered hands around them. A child. Very small. In a black coat too big for him and a black cap pulled down over his ears. Six years old. Looking at Corin with large pale eyes that did not blink.
Somebody always cries.
"Pin," said Corin.
The small shape did not move.
"Do you have a pencil?"
A long pause. Then the small hand came out of the dark again. Slowly. With something in it. A stub of pencil, no longer than a finger, chewed at the end.
Corin took it.
He turned the black card over. The back was blank. Smooth and black. A pencil would barely show on it. He pressed hard.
He did not need to think about what to write. He had been thinking about it since Thursday. Since a pale room at the top of a great round building, with colored lights at a woman's throat and a fan pointing at the left side of his chest. Since a vial with a paper label on it. Voice of a sister, laughing. Poor (faded at edges).
He had thought about it in the crypt, and on the river road, and in the coal hole under the grating with a white face an inch from his own. He had thought about it on Lantern Bridge, and in the bird market, and in a room full of small black books, with an oak leaf on the page and a fourth note coming down.
He had thought about Elara.
About a girl of four in braids, sitting in a big soldier's lap on a step by a river, with her hands on her chest. About a cold, brilliant young woman in white gloves with a gold tooth, who had put her bare hand on a glass desk next to his, and made it blaze, and lost a day she never had.
He had thought about whether a laugh in a vial would be her laugh. And whether, if it was, he would know it. And whether, if he knew it, it would be enough.
And he had thought about six books in brown cloth coats, under a loose board in a pen shop on Quill Row. Six years of him. Every face, every name, every street. Every person he had ever knelt in front of and asked what color. Bram's chalkboard. Mrs. Marta's purple door. Juno's brass key. Doctor Silas's crypt. Hessa Doon's black barge. Fenn Briggs's tin of yellow slips.
All of them. Written down. In his own hand.
And Vivienne wanted them so that nobody else ever could.
No. Not quite. Bram had been wrong about that. Corin understood it now, standing in the lane with the black card in one hand and the chewed pencil in the other. She did not want them so that nobody else could read them.
She wanted them so that she could.
She wanted the handles.
He wrote, on the back of the card, in big square letters, pressing so hard that the soft pencil lead broke halfway through and he had to finish with the splintered stub:
NO.
And under it, smaller:
Let them go. All eleven. And Tam. And I will think about it.
He looked at it. It was a lie, the last line. He knew it was a lie. He would not think about it. He was not going to give her the Ledgers, not for eleven people, not for a hundred, not for his sister's laugh. Because if he did, there would be a thousand more people in the counting room by the spring, and every one of them would have a face and a name and a street that he had written down.
But he left it. Because it might buy an hour. And an hour was all he needed.
He held the card out to the dark inside the carriage.
The small blue hand came out, and took it, and took the pencil stub too, and went back in.
The door swung shut.
The four white horses tossed their heads, all at once, and their harness jingled, bright and cold, and the cream-and-gold carriage moved off along Exchange Street, slowly, without hurrying, back into its place in the line. As if nothing had happened at all.
Corin stood at the top of the lane.
His hands were shaking. Not with cold. He looked at them, and made them stop.
That was very brave, said Malakor, in the back of his head. Dry as dust. And very stupid. And now she knows that you are not coming at midnight. So she will not wait for midnight. Will she.
"No," said Corin.
So her men will go out sooner.
"Yes."
And the den will be emptier sooner.
Corin did not answer that. He did not need to.
There was a pause.
Ah, said Malakor. And for the first time in a long time, Corin thought he heard something in the dry old voice that might, in a living man, have been a smile. Ah. Kross was right about one thing, boy. You are good. Even when you don't remember.
Gideon had come up the lane beside him, shuffling in his blanket. He stood looking at the place where the carriage had been, with his head on one side.
"You said no," he said.
"Yes."
"Nobody says no to the Lady."
"I know."
Gideon thought about it for a long time.
"Pin will tell her what you wrote," he said. "He can't read. But he'll give her the card. And she'll read it. And she'll laugh." He paused. "She always laughs. Even when she's angry. Especially when she's angry." Another pause. "And then she'll send Mister Gant's men to Linden Walk. Not at first light. Now. Tonight."
"How long?" said Corin. "Gideon. From the Bell to Linden Walk. How long, for men walking?"
Gideon looked up at the sky. At the stars. He seemed to be counting something.
"An hour," he said. "If they don't hurry. Big Harl doesn't like to hurry." He looked at Corin. "But they won't go till they're told. And she won't tell them till she's had her chocolate. She always has her chocolate at nine. On a Sunday." He paused. "And she'll want to tell them herself. On the back stair. So she can see their faces."
Nine o'clock. And then an hour.
Ten. Perhaps half past.
"Then we haven't got till midnight," said Corin.
"No," said Gideon. "You haven't."
Corin looked up at the great pale drum of the Bourse, floating in its blue light. At the dome, black against the stars, with its thin ring of light. Somewhere up there, at the top, in a pale room with colored lights, a woman in a blue dress was sitting at a window, waiting for her chocolate. And a six-year-old boy with blue fingers was climbing the back stair toward her, with a black card in his hand.
He turned his back on it.
"Come on," he said to Gideon. "We need to find a big man with a sword."
Gathering
They did not go to Saint Bride's.
Corin wanted to. Every step of the way down Exchange Street, his feet wanted to turn left, toward the river and the Cut and the chapel with no roof, where Oren was sharpening his sword by the brazier and Lyra was sitting with Pip under the charcoal hills. He wanted to run down the Cut and climb in through the crack in the vestry wall and tell them. First light. No. Tonight. Ten o'clock. Linden Walk. They're coming for your father.
He did not.
Because a girl called Wisp was sitting on the old bell tower with her feet hanging down, not blinking much. And because a pale man in a beautiful gray coat wanted to see who he would run to.
"The Watch house first," he said to Gideon. "On Needle Street. The back door."
Gideon did not ask why. He did not ask anything. He shuffled along beside Corin in his blanket-wrapped feet, a little behind, the way a dog walks beside a man it has decided to follow but does not much like.
The streets had emptied. It was half past eight on a Sunday night in the deep frost, and nobody who had a fire was out. The gemstone lamps hummed on their tall iron posts, each one in its own small blue pool of light, with the dark between them. Their boots, and Gideon's bundled feet, made the only sound. Crunch, and shuffle. Crunch, and shuffle. Somewhere a dog barked, far off, and stopped. Somewhere a window went up and a woman shouted, Billy! In! Now! and the window went down again.
At every corner, Corin looked up.
At the roofs. At the chimney stacks and the gutters and the high blank gable ends. At the tops of walls. He looked for a small shape, sitting very still, with its feet hanging down. He did not see one. That did not mean anything. He had not seen Gideon either, until Gideon spoke.
"You won't see them," said Gideon, beside him, as if he had heard the thought. "Not if they don't want you to. We're good at sitting still. It's the only thing we're good at."
"Is anyone watching us now?"
Gideon looked up at the roofs. All the way round. Slowly. The way a farmer looks at the sky to see if it will rain.
"No," he said. "I don't think so." A pause. "But I'm not sure. I'm never sure."
The Watch house on Needle Street was a square brick building with a blue lamp over its front door and bars on all its windows, and a yard at the back, behind a high wall with broken glass set into the top of it.
The yard had a gate. Wooden. With a little square window in it, at head height, with a sliding shutter. Corin knocked on the gate, quick and light, and waited.
Nothing happened.
He knocked again. Quicker.
The shutter slid open. A face looked out. Young, pink, round, with a fuzz of fair whiskers that wanted very much to be a mustache and was not, yet. A constable. About eighteen. His helmet was too big for him and had slid down over his eyebrows.
"Station's round the front," he said. "If you want to report a thing, sir, you go round the front, and you ring the bell, and the desk sergeant—"
"I want Sergeant Moll," said Corin.
The young constable looked at him. At his gray coat. At his face. Then down, past him, at Gideon, standing a little behind in his torn shirt and his bare arms and his gray-blanketed feet, in the frost, quite still.
The young constable's mouth fell open.
"Is he—" He swallowed. "Is that boy all right? He's not got a coat. He's not got—saints, he's not got any shoes—"
"He's all right," said Corin. "He doesn't feel it. Please. Sergeant Moll. Tell him it's the harvester. Tell him it's about Wick."
The young constable stared at Gideon a moment longer. Then the shutter slid shut, and Corin heard boots running away across the yard inside, and a door bang, and a voice shout, Sarge! Sarge!
They waited.
It was not long. A minute. Perhaps two. Then the gate was unbarred from the inside, with a scrape and a thump, and pulled open, and Sergeant Moll stood in it.
He was in his shirtsleeves. A big white shirt with the collar off and the sleeves rolled up, over his blue Watch trousers, with braces. He had a mug of something steaming in one big hand and his cold pipe in the other. His boiled-ham face was still gray. Still loose and tired. But it was not porridge anymore. Something had come back into it since the morning. Something hard, under the tiredness, like a stone under mud.
He looked at Corin. Then at Gideon. He looked at Gideon for a long time.
"Inside," he said. "Both of you. Now."
It took ten minutes to tell him.
They stood in the corner of the yard, by the pump, out of the wind, under a lamp in a wire cage. Moll did not interrupt. He listened with his mug in his hand, going cold, and his pipe going cold, and his small sharp eyes going from Corin's face to Gideon's and back.
When Corin got to the counting room, and the eleven, and the girl who cried, Moll put the mug down on the edge of the pump trough. Carefully. Very carefully. As if it might break.
When Corin got to the Lady with the veil and the silver cup, and the flowers, Moll's hand closed into a fist round his cold pipe so hard that the stem snapped.
He looked down at the two pieces. He dropped them in the trough.
"The Bell," he said. "The Bell on Cinder Lane." His voice was very quiet. "I've drunk there. I've stood at that bar. I've stood at that bar with Wick, heaven help me, and bought him his gin." He stopped. "Saints. Saints help me. Under it."
"Will you come?" said Corin.
Moll looked at him.
"The captain," he said. "If I go down a hole on Cinder Lane on a Sunday night without orders, with a harvester the Ministry wants and a—" he looked at Gideon "—a hollow one, I'll lose my coat. And if the captain's in the lily lady's pocket, which he is, I'll lose a sight more than my coat. I'll go in the river under a bridge, same as the doctor did."
"I know."
"Twenty-two years," said Moll. "Twenty-two years in the Watch. Man and boy. I've got a pension coming in three. A little house in Saltings with a garden. My sister's there. She grows beans." He looked down into the trough. At the two halves of his pipe. "Beans."
Corin did not say anything.
Moll was quiet for a long moment.
Then he turned round and shouted, in a voice that made the young constable by the gate jump a foot in the air: "Pyke!"
"Sarge!"
"My coat. My hat. My stick. The big lantern, the dark one, with the shutter. And a pair of boots." He looked down at Gideon's feet. "Small. The smallest we've got. From the lost-property." He turned back to Corin. "And then you go in, Pyke, and you sit at the desk, and if anyone asks, the sergeant's gone home with a bad stomach."
"Sarge, what are you—"
"Go!"
Pyke went.
Moll looked at Corin.
"I can't take any lads," he said. "They'd talk. Or they'd tell. I don't know which of them is the captain's." His jaw moved. "So it's me. And my stick. And whoever else you've got."
"I've got a bargeman," said Corin. "I think. If his wife says yes."
"A bargeman." Moll almost laughed. "Well. That's something. They're big, bargemen."
The Patient Grace was tied up below the coal wharf, where Hessa Doon had said.
She was a long black barge, low in the water, with a red sail furled on her mast and frozen stiff in its ropes. A little cabin at the back, with a tin chimney, and smoke coming out of it, and a light in its one small window. Ice all round her in the river, gray and thick and lumpy, so that she sat in it like a boat stuck in porridge.
Corin did not have to call out. When he came down the stone steps from the wharf onto the frozen planks of the landing, with Moll behind him with the dark lantern and Gideon behind Moll, clumping now in a pair of lost-property boots three sizes too big, stuffed with straw, the cabin door opened, and a man came out.
He was the biggest man Corin had ever seen. Bigger than Oren. Bigger than Briggs. He had to bend nearly double to come out of the cabin door, and when he straightened up on the deck, in the light from the window, he seemed to go on straightening for a long time. Broad, and thick, and slow, with a great bald head like a boulder and a brown beard that came down to the middle of his chest, and hands like spades. He wore a canvas jacket, stiff with salt, like Hessa's, and he had a long wooden pole in one hand with an iron hook on the end of it. A boat hook.
He looked down at Corin from the deck. He did not say anything.
Behind him, in the door, Hessa Doon appeared. With Robin on her hip, wrapped in his gray shawl, asleep.
"That's him," she said to the big man. "That's the harvester." And to Corin: "This is Wat."
Wat looked at Corin for a long time. Then at Moll. Then at Gideon, in his huge stuffed boots.
"Juno came," said Hessa. "This afternoon. She said you said to watch our door." Her gray eyes were steady. "So we watched it. What do you want?"
Corin told her.
Not all of it. Enough. The counting room. The eleven. Tam. The ice door, and the men going out at ten, and the den half empty.
And Linden Walk. An old man in a white room, who did not know his own name. And three carriages reaching for him.
When he had finished, Hessa looked at Wat.
Wat looked at her.
They did not say anything to each other. They did not need to. Corin had seen Hessa's face when she talked about Wick, by the pillar in the arches. He used to give Robin bits of colored paper. To look at. He saw the same thing in it now.
"Wat," said Hessa.
Wat nodded. Once. Slowly.
Then he stepped down off the deck of the Patient Grace onto the frozen landing, with the boat hook in his hand, and the planks groaned under him, and he stood beside Corin, a head and a half taller, and looked down at him, and said, in a voice like a cart going over a wooden bridge:
"Which way?"
And Hessa Doon said, from the cabin door, with her baby on her hip: "And the old man. The one in the white room. If you get him out. You bring him here. To us. Nobody looks at a barge in the ice. Nobody ever looks at a barge."
They went up the stone steps from the landing in a line. Gideon first, then Corin, then Moll, then Wat. The Patient Grace sat in her porridge of ice behind them with the light in her one small window, and Hessa in the cabin door with Robin asleep against her shoulder, watching them go. She did not wave. She did not call after them. She just stood there until they were out of sight.
At the top of the steps, on the empty wharf, Corin stopped.
He took out the Ledger. He opened it at the clean page after Wick and Wren and the two leaves, and he wrote, standing up, in the light of Moll's lantern, with the others waiting.
Sunday, half past eight.
Going under the Bell, Cinder Lane. Counting room. Eleven people. Tam in Gant's room at the back.
With: Sgt. Moll (off duty, bad stomach). Wat Doon (bargeman, Patient Grace). Gideon (hollow, 13, knows the way).
Juno to Oren. Lord Thorne to the barge. Hessa says yes.
He stopped. He looked at what he had written. Then he added one more line, at the bottom, smaller.
If I don't come back: the six Ledgers are under the board by the stove at Bram's. Don't let her have them. Burn them first.
He closed the book.
Moll was looking at him. The big man's small sharp eyes had gone to the Ledger, and then to Corin's face, and stayed there.
"You write everything down," he said. "Don't you. Every last thing."
"Yes."
"I do that," said Moll. "In my book. Little black one." He touched his coat pocket. "Twenty-two years of it. Who got took up, and for what, and who said what, and who lied. The captain don't know I keep it. He thinks I write down my bets." A pause. "I've got a page for the lad on the rubbish heap. And a page for Wick. And a page for you."
"What does it say? The page for me?"
Moll thought about it.
"It says harvester knelt down in the muck," he said. "And asked the old man what color. And under that, first one in twenty-two years as asked." He looked away, along the dark wharf. "That's all it says. That's enough."
Nobody spoke for a moment.
Then Wat shifted the boat hook on his great shoulder, and the wood creaked, and he said, in his slow voice, to nobody in particular, "My Hessa's mam had a book. Down on the marsh. She couldn't write. She tied knots in a string. A knot for every one she'd lost. Big ones for the big ones." He was quiet. "She had a string as long as this wharf when she died."
Gideon was looking up at them. At all three of them. At Corin with his Ledger, and Moll with his hand on his coat pocket, and Wat with his boat hook, looking out at the river. His large pale eyes went from one face to the next, slowly, the way a child looks at grown-ups talking in a language he has not learned yet.
"Why?" he said.
They looked at him.
"Why do you all keep things?" said Gideon. "Books. Strings. If you've lost them, you've lost them. Writing them down doesn't bring them back. Tying knots doesn't." His voice was flat and polite. He really wanted to know. "Why bother?"
Nobody answered at once.
It was Moll, in the end. He looked down at the thin boy in the enormous boots for a long moment, with his tired gray face. Then he put out one big hand and laid it, very lightly, on the top of Gideon's white, badly cut hair. Just for a moment. The way you touch a dog's head as you pass it in a doorway.
"So somebody knows it happened, lad," he said. "That's all. So somebody knows."
Gideon stood very still under the big hand.
He did not lean into it. He did not pull away from it. He just stood there, and let it rest on his head, and looked up at Moll with his pale empty eyes, and the tiny crease came, very faintly, between his eyebrows.
Then Moll took his hand away, and cleared his throat, and said, "Right. Which way, then, lad? Show us," in his big tired Watch-house voice, as if nothing had happened at all.
And Gideon turned, in his huge stuffed boots, and showed them.
