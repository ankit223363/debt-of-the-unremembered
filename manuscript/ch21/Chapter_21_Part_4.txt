Chapter 21: The Hollow Urchin, Part 4 of 5: The Bell
The Bell
Juno found them on the Steps.
Corin had known she would. He did not know how. He had stopped wondering, days ago, how Juno found anybody. She just did. The way water finds the lowest place.
The Steps were a long flight of stone stairs going down the hill from the back of the counting houses toward the river, between the walls of two old warehouses. Steep and narrow and icy, with an iron rail on one side, and a lamp at the top and another at the bottom and nothing but dark between. Gideon went first, because he knew the way, clumping in his straw-stuffed boots, one step at a time, very carefully, holding the rail. Then Corin. Then Moll, with the dark lantern shuttered, a thin line of yellow leaking out of it onto the frost. And Wat last, with the boat hook over his shoulder, ducking under the lamp at the top as if it were a low branch.
They were halfway down when something dropped onto the rail in front of Gideon.
Dropped. From above. From the top of the warehouse wall, which was twice the height of a man. It landed on the iron rail with both feet, crouched, with its hands out for balance, light as a cat. And the rail rang, very faintly, like a struck bell.
Gideon stopped. He did not jump. He did not cry out. He just stopped, and looked at it, with his head on one side.
Moll's stick was up in front of his face before Corin could blink. Behind him, Wat made a low sound in his throat like a dog that has heard a gate open.
"Don't," said Corin. "It's all right. It's ours."
Juno stood up on the rail.
She stood on it the way other people stand on a pavement. Straight and easy, with her hands in the pockets of her huge coat, balancing on a bar of icy iron no wider than a broom handle, over a drop of twenty steps. Her sharp face was red with cold. Her cap was pulled down to her eyebrows. Her brass key glinted on its string.
She looked at Gideon. Then at Corin. Then at Moll, and at Wat, and back at Gideon. At his torn shirt. His bare thin arms. His enormous boots.
"Who's that?" she said.
"Gideon," said Corin.
"He's hollow," said Juno. Not a question.
"Yes."
Juno looked at Gideon for a long moment. Gideon looked back. Pale eyes and black ones. Neither of them blinked.
"I've seen you," said Juno at last. "On the back stair of the dome. Through the coal door. You sit on the third step."
"Fourth," said Gideon.
"Fourth." Juno nodded slowly, as if this were important. Then she looked at Corin. "Gran's bolted the door. Moll knows. The bargewoman knows. The Warden knows, he said thank you, he said tell you he's sitting on his step. Mother Grue knows, she said she'll put a poker through anybody." She took a breath. "And I went back to the church. To tell them I'd done it. And there's someone on the bell tower."
"I know," said Corin. "A girl. Hollow. Called Wisp."
Juno's eyes went to Gideon again. Then back to Corin. Her mouth went tight.
"I didn't go in," she said. "I came round the other way. Over the chapel roof. I didn't let her see. I'm almost sure."
"Juno. Listen." Corin came up the step to her. Close, so that he could talk low. "I need you to go back. Now. Fast. The other way, over the roof, so the girl doesn't see. Go in by the crack and find Oren. Tell him this. Exactly this. The Lady's men are going to Pruett's tonight. After ten. Four of them, maybe five. To take Lord Thorne."
Juno went white.
"Tell him the Watch on the gate at Pruett's is the captain's. Tell him they won't help." Corin held her eyes. "Tell him to take Lord Thorne out. Tonight. Before ten. Him and Mrs. Hobb and anybody else. Take him to the black barge with the red sail, below the coal wharf. The Patient Grace. Hessa Doon is waiting. She knows."
"And Lyra," said Juno. "She'll go. You know she'll go. Nobody'll stop her."
"I know." Corin's mouth went dry. "Tell her to go with him. Tell her to take her sword. Tell her—" He stopped. "Tell her I'm sorry I'm not there."
Juno looked at him for a long moment. Her sharp chin was trembling, very slightly. She clamped it still.
"Where are you going to be?" she said.
Corin looked down the Steps. Into the dark. Toward the river, and Fuller's Yard, and the little bridge, and Cinder Lane.
"Under the Bell," he said.
Juno's face did not change. But her hand came out of her pocket and went to the brass key at her throat, and closed round it.
"Right," she said. Very quietly. "Right."
And she was gone. Up off the rail, onto the wall, onto the warehouse roof, in two quick movements, without a sound. A dark shape against the stars, for one breath. Then nothing.
Gideon was still looking at the place where she had been.
"She's fast," he said.
"Yes."
"She's frightened," said Gideon. "I could see it. She was holding her key." He thought about it. "She's frightened, and she went anyway." He looked at Corin with his pale, puzzled eyes. "Why do people do that?"
Corin did not have an answer. He went on down the steps.
Fuller's Yard was a wide square of frozen mud and cobbles at the bottom of the hill, with the backs of tall dark buildings round three sides and a row of old wooden sheds along the fourth. In the summer, Corin supposed, there were carts here, and horses, and men loading and unloading. Tonight there was nothing. A broken cart with no wheels, leaning against a shed. A heap of frozen manure. A cat on the cart, watching them.
They crossed it in single file, keeping to the shadows of the sheds.
On the far side, a little stone bridge, no wider than a cart, humped over a stream. Corin knew the stream before he saw it. He smelled it. Sharp and sour and chemical, vinegar and rust and something burning. And when he looked down from the bridge, in the thin yellow light from Moll's lantern, he saw the water running fast and black under a skin of steam, with streaks of blue and red and sick yellow-green twisting through it.
The dye-works stream. On its way down to the round hole in the river wall, and the tongue of frozen mud, and the river. The same water he had climbed up beside that morning, with Juno ahead of him like a goat and Oren swearing behind, to the arches. To Wick.
Everything in this city ran into everything else, he thought. Like the dye in that water. Never quite mixing. Never quite apart.
On the other side of the bridge, a lane went up between two high walls. And at the top of the lane, where it met another, wider one, a sign on a post. Old wood, painted black, with white letters, most of them peeled away.
CINDER LANE.
Cinder Lane was not a street anyone would choose.
It was long and crooked and badly lit, with only one gemstone lamp at each end and nothing in between. The houses along it were tall and narrow and old and black, with their upper floors leaning out over the lane so far that they nearly met in the middle, so that walking down it was like walking down a tunnel with a thin crack of stars for a roof. Their windows were mostly dark. Some were boarded. Some had no glass at all, only rags stuffed into the frames. Water dripped from somewhere and froze on the cobbles in long gray tongues. The whole lane smelled of soot and old beer and wet ash and the river.
And halfway along, on the right, there was a light.
A lot of light. Yellow light, warm, spilling out of two wide windows of thick greenish glass onto the black cobbles. And noise with it. Voices, a lot of them, all talking at once. A man laughing. Somebody singing, badly. The clink of glass on glass.
Over the door, on an iron bracket, hung a sign. Not painted. Real. A bell. A big old bronze bell, green with age, as big as a bucket, hanging from the bracket on a chain. It had no clapper. Somebody had taken the clapper out long ago, so that it could not ring. It hung there over the door of the tavern, swinging very slightly in the wind, silent.
The Bell.
Corin stopped in the shadow of a doorway across the lane and looked at it.
He had never been here. He was sure of that. It was not in the Ledger, except as a name, in other people's mouths. The Bell, on Cinder Lane. Wren's report. Mother Grue in the arches. A place where an old man had stood at the bar every night for a week, telling his story.
It did not look like a place where bad things happened. That was the strange thing. It looked warm. It looked like the kind of place you would go into on a freezing night to get out of the wind and have a hot drink and sit by the fire and talk to people. Through the thick green glass of the windows, he could see shapes moving. The shapes of men in caps and women in shawls, leaning on a long bar, laughing. A fire roaring in a big stone fireplace at the back. A cat asleep on a barrel.
And under it all, under the floor, under the warm and the laughing and the fire, in the dark, a counting room. With eleven people in it.
"The front," said Moll, low, beside him. "That's for me."
Corin looked at him.
"One on the beer, the boy said," said Moll. "Upstairs. In the tavern. Who comes down when he's called." He took off his round black Watch hat, and turned it round in his hands, and then reached up and pulled it off altogether and stuffed it in his coat pocket. Without it his head looked smaller and balder and more ordinary. He unbuttoned the top of his blue coat and pulled the collar of his shirt up over it, so the blue hardly showed. "I'll go in the front. Like I always do. Like it's any Sunday. Old Moll, off duty, with a stomach ache, wanting a hot gin. And I'll stand at the bar where I can see the cellar stair." His jaw moved. "And if anyone gets called down, or tries to go down, or comes up, I'll know."
"And then what?"
"And then I'll do what I do," said Moll. "I'll get in the way." He looked at the lit windows. "I'm good at getting in the way. Twenty-two years."
He handed the dark lantern to Corin. Then he put both his big hands flat on his own chest, and breathed out, long and slow, and his whole big body seemed to sag, a little, and soften, and go tired and dull and harmless. Like a man at the end of a long day who wants nothing but a drink.
Corin watched him do it and thought, He's done this before. Many times.
Moll walked across the lane, slowly, a little heavily, with his hands in his pockets. He pushed open the door of the Bell. A wave of yellow light and warm air and noise came out round him. Somebody inside shouted, Moll! Evening, Sarge! Where's your hat? And Moll said, in a big, tired, cheerful voice, Lost it, Bert. Lost my hat and my pipe and my temper, all in one day, and somebody laughed, and the door swung shut behind him.
The lane was dark again.
Corin stood in the doorway with the dark lantern in his hand and Wat beside him like a wall, and Gideon in front, small and pale, in his great boots.
"The back," said Gideon. "This way."
And he led them down a gap between two houses so narrow that Wat had to turn sideways, and they went into the dark.
The Ice Door
The alley behind the Bell was a long thin slot of black between two rows of houses, with a gutter down the middle full of frozen slops.
Gideon led them along it without a light. He did not need one. He went the way a cat goes through a house at night, by memory and touch, clumping softly in his huge stuffed boots, with one hand trailing along the wall. Corin followed with the dark lantern shuttered tight, so that only a hair of yellow leaked out at the bottom. Behind him, Wat breathed. Slow and deep and steady. It was the only way Corin knew he was there. For a man so big, he made almost no sound at all.
The backs of the houses went by on the left. Back doors. Back yards with broken fences. Privies. A water butt, frozen. A heap of broken bottles. A window with a candle in it, and a woman inside, sitting at a table with her head in her hands. Nobody looked out.
Then Gideon stopped.
He put his hand back, without turning round, and touched Corin's arm. Just once. Very lightly. And Corin stopped too, and felt Wat stop behind him, like a ship coming to rest against a wall.
Ahead of them, the alley widened.
Not much. A little. Enough to make a small square yard, open on the alley side, with the black back wall of the Bell along the right. There were barrels stacked against the wall. Beer barrels, empty, big and round, three high. A heap of old straw, frozen into a solid block. A cart with no horse, with its shafts on the ground. And a lamp. One. On a bracket on the back wall of the Bell, burning low and yellow behind dirty glass. The only light in the whole long alley.
And in the middle of the yard, in the ground, the ice door.
Corin saw it in the yellow lamplight. Two flat wooden doors, side by side, set into the cobbles at a slant, like the lid of a great box half buried in the earth. Old wood, black and thick, bound with iron straps, with a big iron ring on each door. Frost on them. A long iron bar lay across both, through two iron loops, to hold them shut from the outside.
And standing on them, a man.
He was big. Not as big as Wat. Nobody was as big as Wat. But big. Broad and heavy and thick through the shoulders, in a long coat of some dark, shaggy stuff, like the skin of a bear, belted at the waist with a rope. A knitted cap. A great muffler wound round and round his neck, up to his nose, so that only his eyes showed between the muffler and the cap. Small eyes. Sleepy. Half shut.
He stood with his feet apart, one on each door, on the iron bar, and his hands in the front of his shaggy coat. And at his belt, on the right, in a leather sheath, something long. As long as a child's arm.
Big Harl has a knife as long as my arm.
He was not doing anything. He was just standing there, on the doors, in the yellow light, in the cold. Breathing. His breath came out over the top of his muffler in slow white clouds. Every now and then he stamped one foot, then the other, on the iron bar, and the bar rang, dully.
Gideon drew them back. A few steps. Into the shadow of a broken fence, behind a stack of old crates that leaned against the back of the next house. From there, through a gap between two crates, Corin could see the whole yard. The lamp. The barrels. The ice door. Big Harl.
They crouched.
They waited a long time.
Corin did not know how long. He had no watch, and he would not have dared open the lantern to look at one. He counted the bells instead. Saint Agatha's rang the quarter, far off, on the hill. Then the half. Then the three quarters.
It was the cold that was the worst of it.
It came up out of the frozen ground and into his knees and his feet and his hands. It came down out of the black sky. It came along the alley in long slow breaths of wind that found the gap at his collar where the scarf had been and went straight down his back like water. His fingers went numb inside his gloves. His bad hand, the bandaged one, the one he had burned on the grating, began to throb again, deep and hot, the only warm thing in him.
Beside him, Wat crouched like a boulder. He did not move at all. His breath went up white over the crates, slow and steady, and Corin had to touch his arm once, gently, and point, so that he would breathe down into his beard instead, where it would not show.
Wat nodded. And breathed into his beard.
Gideon crouched on Corin's other side. Small and thin and still, in his torn shirt and his bare arms, with the lost-property boots on the ends of his legs like two loaves of bread. He did not hunch. He did not hug himself. He did not breathe on his fingers. He just crouched there with his hands resting on his knees and his large pale eyes on the yard, as calm as if it were a summer afternoon.
Corin watched him, out of the corner of his eye.
He could not help it. He had seen Gideon's blue feet. He had seen the two puckered stumps where the smallest toes should have been. They just went black and then one morning they weren't there. And he looked at the boy's bare arms now, in the faint yellow glow from the lamp across the yard. At the thin white skin, gone faintly blue at the elbows. At the long raw scrape with the gravel still in it.
Not a goosebump. Not one. Not a single hair standing up.
He sold his cold, thought Corin. When he was ten. Three coppers, in winter. Because the rich ladies on the Hill like to buy a bit of cold to put in their ices at summer parties.
He thought of a lady in a summer garden, in a white dress, laughing, with a little silver dish of ice in front of her. Spooning it up. Shivering, delighted, with a boy's cold in her mouth.
He looked away.
The wind changed.
Corin felt it before he understood it. The long slow breaths that had been coming along the alley from the left, from the river end, stopped. There was a moment of stillness. Perfect stillness. The lamp on the back wall of the Bell stopped flickering. Big Harl's breath went straight up over his muffler like smoke from a chimney.
And then the air moved again. But not along the alley this time.
Up.
Up out of the ground. Out of the yard. Out of the ice door.
Corin saw it. In the yellow light. A breath of white, rising from the crack between the two flat wooden doors under Big Harl's boots. Thin, at first. Then thicker. Like steam from a kettle, but the wrong way round. Not warm air rising into cold. Cold air rising into cold. Colder than the night. So cold that when it met the night air, the night air itself turned white, and curled, and sank again, and went creeping out across the frozen cobbles in a low thin mist, ankle-deep, toward the barrels, toward the cart, toward the crates where they crouched.
Big Harl stamped his feet on the iron bar and swore, under his muffler.
"Gant's got the hatch open again," he said, to nobody. His voice was thick and slow and sleepy. "Letting the north out. Saints, it'll freeze a man's boots to the boards."
The white mist came across the yard.
It came slowly. Very low. Creeping along the cobbles in long thin fingers, the way mist comes up off a river at dawn. It reached the cart. It went under it and round its wheels. It reached the barrels. It reached the stack of old crates.
And came through the gap between them.
Corin felt it on his face. It was not like the cold of the night. It was different. Sharper. Cleaner. Like the air on a high mountain. Like the air in a room where somebody has just scrubbed the floor with soap and left the window open. It went into his nose and his mouth, and it smelled of nothing, nothing at all, and it was so cold that his teeth ached, all at once, every one, as if he had bitten into snow.
And under the nothing, very faint, something he knew.
Brass. Oil. Dust. And under that, fainter still, rain on a summer road.
He had smelled it last night. Through the bars of a grating. On the hem of a long gray coat, an inch from his face.
His whole body went rigid.
Not here, said Malakor, very low and quick. Not them. Be still. That is not them. That is where they have been. The smell is in the cold. It is in the ice. A pause. Be still, boy.
The white mist curled round Corin's knees. Round Wat's great boots. Round Gideon.
It touched the boy's bare arms. His thin bare neck. His pale face.
And Gideon shivered.
It was only once.
One small, sharp, sudden shiver. It went through his whole thin body from the top of his white head to the soles of his stuffed boots, all at once, the way a dog shakes water off. His shoulders jerked up. His bare arms came in against his sides. His teeth clicked together, once, tck, very loud in the silence. And all over the thin blue skin of his arms, in the faint yellow light, every tiny hair stood up on end.
Then it was gone.
His shoulders came down. His arms went back to his knees. The hairs lay flat. He crouched there exactly as before, still and calm, with his large pale eyes on the yard.
But his face had changed.
It was very small, the change. Corin would not have seen it if he had not been looking. Just a crease. Between the pale eyebrows. The tiny puzzled frown of a boy reading a word in a language he did not speak. Only deeper, now. Much deeper. As if the word had suddenly turned out to be his own name.
Gideon looked down at his own arm.
He lifted it, slowly, in front of his face, into the thin yellow light from the lamp across the yard. And he looked at it. At the skin. At the place on his forearm, below the raw scrape, where the hairs had stood up, and lain down again.
He looked at it for a long time.
"What was that?" he said.
His voice was very quiet. Not flat, quite. Not quite the voice that said it's Tuesday. There was something else in it. Something so small and so far away that Corin could hardly hear it. Like a sound from the bottom of a deep well.
"You shivered," said Corin. Hardly breathing.
"I don't," said Gideon. "I don't shiver. I sold it."
He kept looking at his arm.
"I sold it," he said again. "When I was ten. Three coppers. Mister Gant put my hand on the glass and it went cloudy and it went clear and I was never cold again. Not once. Not ever." He turned the arm over, slowly, and looked at the inside of it. "Not ever."
The white mist was thinning. Sinking back into the cobbles, curling away, going pale and then clear, the way breath goes off a window. Across the yard, under Big Harl's boots, the crack between the two wooden doors had stopped breathing. Somebody, down below, had shut the hatch again.
Gideon lowered his arm.
He sat there for a moment, very still, in the cold. Then he looked at the ice door. At the two flat black doors in the ground, bound with iron, with the bar across them and the big man standing on them.
He looked at them the way you look at a house where you used to live.
Beside Corin, very low, like a cart going slowly over a wooden bridge, Wat spoke. For the first time since the barge.
"That's not river ice," he said.
Corin turned his head.
"Down there," said Wat. He did not take his eyes off the ice door. "That cold. That's not river ice. I know river ice. I've cut it. I've hauled it. River ice is gray and it's full of muck and it melts if you look at it." He breathed out slowly into his beard. "That's north ice. From the Reach. Up past the end of the world, where the sea's frozen all the year. It comes down on the big ships in the spring, in straw, in the holds, blue all the way through. You can't cut it with a saw. You have to break it with a hammer. And it doesn't melt." A pause. "Not ever. Not in the summer. Not in the sun. The ice men say it's got something in it. Something that keeps it cold."
He looked down at Gideon.
"It made you shiver, lad," he said. Not unkindly. "Like you'd touched something of your own."
Gideon did not answer.
He sat with his thin bare arms on his knees and his pale eyes on the ice door, and the tiny crease between his eyebrows did not go away.
Corin took out the Ledger. He did not open the lantern. He wrote in the dark, by feel, in big letters, slanting off the edges of the lines.
Gideon shivered. Once. When the cold came up out of the ice cellar under the Bell. He has not shivered in three years. He sold it. "What was that?"
Wat: "That's north ice. From the Reach. Blue all through. It doesn't melt. It's got something in it."
The cold from the cellar smelled of brass and oil and summer rain. Like the gray ones.
Where do they keep what the hollow ones sell?
