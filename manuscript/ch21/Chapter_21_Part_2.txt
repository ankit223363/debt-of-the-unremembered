Chapter 21: The Hollow Urchin, Part 2 of 5: What Gideon Sold
What Gideon Sold
Corin gave him the copper.
He had two left. He took one out of his pocket and held it out on his gloved palm, and Gideon took it with his thin blue fingers, quite politely, and looked at it for a moment, and put it in the pocket of his cut-off trousers. It made no sound going in. There was nothing else in the pocket for it to clink against.
"Thank you," said Gideon.
"Where is he?"
"Wait," said Gideon. "I have to say it properly. Mister Gant says if you sell a thing you have to say it properly, or it isn't sold." He closed his eyes. Not tightly. Lightly, the way a child closes its eyes to say a prayer it has said every night of its life. "The men took Tam down the back stair and through the coal door and along the back way behind the counting houses. Down the Steps. Across Fuller's Yard. Over the little bridge by the dye-works stream. Into Cinder Lane. Along Cinder Lane to the Bell. Not the front of the Bell. The back. The alley. There's a door in the ground there, flat, with two halves, like a cellar door. The ice door. They used to keep ice down there, for the beer, in the summer, in straw. They opened it and they took him down and they shut it. And a man came and stood on it. And I waited till it got light. And nobody came out."
He opened his eyes.
"That's it," he said. "That's sold."
Corin stood very still.
The Bell.
An old rag-and-bone man, called Wick, was talking about it to anyone who would buy him a gin. In the Bell, on Cinder Lane.
Wren's report. Line six. In her small neat upright hand.
And Mother Grue, in the arches, with her poker. Told them at the Bell, on Wednesday, and on Thursday, and on Friday, and last night.
The same tavern. Where Wick had stood at the bar every night, telling his story to anyone who would listen. A harvester knelt in the muck and asked me what color. With a quiet young woman in a gray cloak in the corner, writing it down.
And under it, all the time, a cellar.
"What's under the Bell?" he said. "Gideon. Do you know? What's down there?"
Gideon looked at him.
"That's another copper," he said.
Corin gave him the other copper.
It was his last. He held it out on his glove and thought, very clearly, That's it. That's everything. You have nothing now. Not a farthing. And then he thought, Neither does he. And he let the boy take it.
Gideon put it in his pocket with the other one. This time it clinked. Just once. A very small sound in the frozen dark.
He looked down at his pocket for a moment, with his head on one side, as if the sound had surprised him. Then he looked up again.
"The counting room," he said. "That's what Mister Gant calls it. It's where they keep the people who owe. The ones who borrowed from the Lady and can't pay. Not the Bank. The Lady. The Bank only takes what you've got. The Lady takes what you've got, and then she keeps you, and she takes what you get." He said it quite flatly. "Every day. A little bit. Whatever you've had that day. Whatever you've seen, or thought, or felt. They put you on a glass in the morning and they put you on a glass at night. Until it's paid. And it's never paid. Because the more you owe, the more it costs to owe it."
"How do you know?"
"I was there," said Gideon. "Three years. In the counting room. Under the Bell."
Corin did not say anything.
"My mam borrowed," said Gideon. "When I was little. From the Lady. Not money. A memory. Somebody else's. A good one. A day at the seaside, it was, with sun on the water. She was sad, my mam. She was sad all the time. And somebody told her that if you bought a happy day off the Lady, and put it in you, it would sit in you like a warm stone, and you'd feel better." He paused. "So she borrowed one. And she did feel better. For a while. And then the Lady wanted paying. And my mam couldn't pay. And she had nothing left to pay with, because she'd been sad so long that all her own days were gray, and the Lady doesn't buy gray."
His voice did not change at all. It went on, light and flat and polite, like someone reading out a list of prices.
"So they took me instead," he said. "To pay. I was eight. They took me down under the Bell, into the counting room, and they put me on a glass, every morning and every night. And they took what I'd got. Which wasn't much. Being hungry, mostly. And being cold. And being frightened. There's a lot of being frightened, in the counting room. Mister Gant says it's the best crop. It grows by itself. You don't have to water it."
He looked down at his blue feet.
"And then one day they took the last of it," he said. "The last of the frightened. And the last of the cold. And the last of the sad. All of it. Because I'd grown up a bit, by then, and I'd stopped having very much, and they wanted to be done with me." A pause. "And I sat on the glass, and it went cloudy, and it went clear. And I didn't feel anything. Not anything at all. Not then. Not ever again."
"And they let you go?"
"Mister Gant let me out," said Gideon. "He said, There's nothing left in this one. He's hollow as a bell." Something happened at the corner of the boy's thin mouth. Not a smile. Not anything. Just a small movement, like the twitch of a sleeping dog. "That's what they call us. Hollow ones. There's a few of us. In the city. We don't feel the cold, so we can sit out all night on the stairs and the railings and the roofs and watch. We don't feel frightened, so we can go anywhere. Nobody bothers to hurt us, because we don't mind. So the Lady pays us a copper to watch things. And we watch."
He looked at Corin with his pale calm eyes.
"I watch the back stair," he said. "That's my place. That's why I saw Tam."
The wind came down the lane.
It came from the top, from Exchange Street, a long low cold breath that went past the red door and the lantern and the stacked barrels and the frozen heap of cabbage leaves, and down, and over the railing where the boy sat, and out into the black yard below. It lifted the edge of the gray blanket on Gideon's knees. It moved his short white badly cut hair. It went through Corin's coat as if the coat were made of paper, and he hunched his shoulders against it without thinking, and pulled his collar tighter, and felt his bare throat, where the scarf had been, go raw and numb.
Gideon did not move.
He sat on the frosty rail in his torn shirt, with his bare thin arms and his bare blue feet, in the wind, and he did not hunch, and he did not shiver, and he did not pull the blanket tighter. He sat there like a boy in a painting.
"What about Tam?" said Corin. His teeth wanted to chatter. He would not let them. "Is he—will they put him on the glass?"
"No," said Gideon. "Tam doesn't owe. Tam's not in the counting room. Tam's in the other room."
"What other room?"
"The room at the back. Past the counting room. Behind the beer barrels. Mister Gant's room." Gideon thought about it. "It's where they take people who didn't borrow. People who did something else. Saw something. Said something. Read something they shouldn't." He looked at Corin. "Mister Gant asks them questions. And then, after, the Lady comes down. In the night. In a veil. And she has a little silver cup."
Corin's blood went cold.
A silver siphon cup with a spiral inside.
A tall woman in a white half-mask, with a necklace of tiny glowing vials, and lilies.
No. Not her. Not the Lady of the Bourse. Not Vivienne. Vivienne wore a dome, and colored lights at her throat, and her eyes did not smile when her mouth did. Vivienne did not wear a veil.
Or did she?
Friday afternoon, Sergeant Moll had said, in the arches, with his cold pipe. There was a lady came to the station. In a carriage. To see the captain. She had a veil on.
"Which lady?" said Corin. "Gideon. Which lady comes down with the cup? The Lady of the Bourse? Lady Vivienne?"
Gideon looked at him for a long time with his pale, empty eyes.
"I don't know," he said at last. "I've never seen her face. Nobody has. She has a veil. And she smells." He paused. "Of flowers."
The wind went down the lane again. Long and low and cold.
And Corin, standing in it, with his hands deep in his pockets and his bare throat aching, thought of a lane behind a printing house, and an old man on a crate, and a dying scribe on a heap of rubbish. Like a funeral. Like a whole room full of funeral flowers.
"How long?" he said. "Gideon. How long does Mister Gant ask his questions? Before she comes down?"
"Two nights," said Gideon. "Usually. Sometimes three. It depends on how long it takes them to say everything." He thought about it. "Tam went down last night. So she'll come down tonight. Or tomorrow." He looked up at the sky, at the thin strip of stars between the tall walls. "Late. After midnight. She always comes after midnight."
After midnight.
SUNDAY MIDNIGHT. Vivienne. Ledgers I–VI, or the sister vial is gone.
Corin looked down at the boy on the rail.
"You're telling me this," he said slowly. "All of it. For two coppers. Knowing who I am. Knowing they're looking for me. Knowing they'd pay you more than two coppers to tell them where I am."
"Yes," said Gideon.
"Why?"
Gideon looked at him. And there it was again. The tiny frown. Between his pale eyebrows. The puzzled look of a boy reading a word in a language he does not speak.
"I don't know," he said. "I don't want anything. I don't care what happens to Tam. I don't care what happens to you. I don't care about anything." He looked down at the gray blanket on his knees. At his blue hands folded on top of it. "But Tam used to give me the crusts. Off the Lady's toast. Every morning. He'd come down the back stair at seven with her tray, and stop, and give me the crusts, and say Morning, Gid. Every morning. For two years." A pause. "Nobody else ever called me Gid."
He looked up.
"I don't know why I'm telling you," he said. "I just am."
The Hollow Ones
Corin crouched down in front of the railing.
He did it so that his eyes were level with the boy's. The way he had crouched in front of Wick on his crate, in a lane behind a printing house, on a morning he did not remember. He did not know why he did it. He only knew that it was what he did, with people who were not used to being looked at.
The frozen cobbles were hard and cold under his knees. The wind came down the lane again and found the back of his neck.
"Gideon," he said. "How many people are in the counting room? Now. Tonight."
Gideon thought about it.
"Eleven," he said. "When I was there last. On Friday. Taking down the bread." He paused. "There was a twelfth. An old man with a cough. But he stopped coughing on Friday night, and on Saturday morning they took him out in a sack."
He said it the way he said everything. Flat and polite. Like a list.
"Eleven," said Corin.
"Seven men. Three women. One girl. About my age." Gideon looked at him. "She cries. All the time. Mister Gant likes her. He says she's a good crop."
Corin did not say anything for a moment. He could not.
He looked down at the cobbles between his knees. At the frost. At the faint blue light from over the Bourse wall, lying on the white like spilled milk. He made himself breathe in, and out, and in again.
Then he looked up.
"How many of Mister Gant's men?"
"Down there? At night?" Gideon counted on his fingers, slowly, one blue finger at a time, the way small children count. "Mister Gant. Big Harl, on the ice door. Two on the counting room. One on Mister Gant's room. One on the beer, in the tavern, upstairs, who comes down when he's called." He looked at his fingers. "Six. Sometimes seven. Sometimes the Lady sends more." He paused. "But not tonight."
"Why not tonight?"
"Because tonight they're going out," said Gideon. "Most of them. After midnight."
Corin went still.
"Going where?"
Gideon looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked down at his pocket. At the place where the two coppers were.
"I haven't got any more coppers," said Corin quietly. "That was my last. I haven't got anything."
"I know," said Gideon. "I heard it. When it went in. It went clink." He tilted his head. "It's all right. I'll tell you anyway. I don't want anything. It's only that Mister Gant says—" He stopped. Something moved in his face. Just a flicker. "I don't know what Mister Gant says. It doesn't matter."
He looked up at the strip of stars.
"The Lady told Mister Gant," he said. "This afternoon. On the back stair. I was on my step. She came down in her blue dress with the lights at her throat, and she stood on the stair above him, and she said, If our forgetful friend hasn't come to the kitchen door with my books by the time the bells ring twelve, then at first light I want you to go to the livery stable off Linden Walk. And bring me back the old man in the white room."
The cold went through Corin like a blade.
"And Mister Gant said, The one with the empty head, my lady? What do you want with him?" Gideon went on, in his flat clear voice, word for word, the way a parrot says things. "And the Lady said, Nothing at all, Mister Gant. I don't want him in the least. But his daughter does. And his daughter is a friend of my forgetful friend. And when they come to me to ask for him back, they'll bring my books." A pause. "And then she laughed. And she said, It's so much easier, you know, when people love each other. It gives you such a lot of handles."
Corin was on his feet.
He did not remember standing up. He was just standing, suddenly, in the middle of the lane, with the blood going hard in his ears and his hands out of his pockets, clenched.
First light. Linden Walk. The old man in the white room.
Lord Thorne. In his white silk gown. With his white hair. By his window, looking at the snow. With Mrs. Hobb humming beside him and his cracked blue charm in a shell box by the bed.
And a Ministry carriage at nine. To collect one guest.
And a Fallowfield carriage at noon.
Three carriages. Three hands, reaching for one old man who did not know his own name. Like dogs fighting over a bone.
It gives you such a lot of handles.
"Gideon," he said. His voice was not steady. "Does anyone else know this? Has anyone else heard?"
"The other hollow ones, maybe," said Gideon. "We hear things. It's what we're for."
"How many of you are there?"
"In the city? I don't know. Twenty. Thirty." Gideon shrugged his thin bare shoulders. "We don't talk to each other much. There's nothing to say. But you see them. If you know what to look for. Sitting on things. In the cold. With no coats." He looked at Corin. "There's one on the roof of the church with no roof. At the bottom of the Cut. Since this morning."
Corin's heart stopped.
Saint Bride's.
"A girl," said Gideon. "Called Wisp. She's sat on the old bell tower since the bells this morning, with her feet hanging down. Watching the hole in the wall where people go in and out." He paused. "The Lady wanted to know where the Thorne girl had gone, after the Watch came. So she put Wisp on it. Wisp's good. She doesn't blink much."
Corin stood in the frozen lane with his fists clenched at his sides.
He had come out of Saint Bride's through the crack in the vestry wall, an hour ago. By the old bell tower. He had climbed up into the dark. He had thought nobody was there.
Somebody had been there. Sitting on the bell tower with her feet hanging down, in the cold, with no coat. Not blinking much.
I want to see who he runs to, Kross had said.
And Vivienne, it seemed, wanted the very same thing.
"Will Wisp tell her?" said Corin. "Has she told her already? That the Thorne girl is at Saint Bride's?"
"I don't know," said Gideon. "Probably. That's what she's for." He looked at Corin. "But it doesn't matter, does it. The Lady doesn't want the Thorne girl. Not tonight. She wants the old man. Because she's clever. Because if you take the girl, the girl fights. But if you take the old man, the girl comes to you."
He said it with no feeling at all. Like a child repeating a sum.
Corin looked at him. At his thin, waxy, pointed face. At his large pale eyes.
"You understand people very well," he said slowly. "For someone who doesn't feel anything."
Gideon considered this.
"That's why," he said. "When you don't feel anything, there's nothing in the way. You just see what people do. And what they do it for." He looked down at the gray blanket on his knees. "Mister Gant says hollow ones make the best watchers. He says feeling things just makes you stupid."
"Do you believe him?"
Gideon was quiet for a long time.
"I don't believe anything," he said at last. "Believing is a feeling."
Corin did not answer.
He crouched down again. Slowly. In front of the rail. And he looked at the boy's blue feet, hanging down in the frost, with the two puckered stumps where the smallest toes had been. And at the gray blanket folded so neatly on his knees, unused.
"Gideon," he said. "Will you do something for me? Not for a copper. I haven't got a copper. Just because I asked."
"I don't know," said Gideon. "What is it?"
"Will you show me the way?" said Corin. "The back way. To the Bell. To the ice door. The way they took Tam. And will you wait with me, in the alley, until it's time. And tell me if I'm doing anything stupid."
Gideon looked at him.
"You're going to go down," he said. Not a question. "Under the Bell."
"Yes."
"Tonight."
"After midnight. When most of them have gone out."
"To get Tam."
"To get Tam," said Corin. "And the girl who cries. And the other ten. And anybody else Mister Gant is keeping down there in the dark like a crop." He held the boy's pale eyes. "And then I'm going to Linden Walk. Before first light. Before anyone."
Gideon considered him for a long moment. With his head a little on one side. The way a thrush considers a worm.
"That's stupid," he said.
"I know."
"There's six of them. Seven. And they've got clubs. And Big Harl has a knife as long as my arm. And you're thin. And you've got a bad hand." He looked at the gray bandage. "And you haven't got any coppers."
"I know."
Gideon looked at him a while longer.
Then he unfolded his thin blue hands from the top of the gray blanket. And he picked up the blanket, slowly, and looked at it, and wrapped it round his own feet. Carefully. Once, and twice, and tucked the end in, the way a mother tucks a baby into a cot. He did it without hurrying, with his head bent and his white hair falling forward, and when it was done he looked at it for a moment, at the gray bundle on the ends of his legs, as if it were something strange that he had never seen before.
Then he slid down off the railing onto the cobbles, on his wrapped feet, without a sound.
"All right," he said. "I'll show you. It's not far." He looked up at Corin. He was very small, standing. The top of his head came only to the middle of Corin's chest. "But you have to bring more people. You can't go on your own. That's not stupid. That's dead."
"I'll bring more people."
"Big ones," said Gideon. "With swords."
"One big one," said Corin. "With a sword. And a sergeant of the Watch, if he'll come. And a girl who runs on roofs."
Gideon thought about it.
"That might be enough," he said. "If you're quick." And then, after a moment, in exactly the same flat polite voice: "I don't care if you die. I just want to say that. In case you think I do."
"I know," said Corin. "Thank you for saying it."
