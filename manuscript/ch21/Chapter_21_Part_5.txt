Chapter 21: The Hollow Urchin, Part 5 of 5: Ten O'Clock
Ten O'Clock
The bells began on the hill.
Saint Agatha's first, deep and slow and sure, the way it always was. Then the Customs House, high and cracked and quick. Then the others, near and far, one after another, until the whole black sky over the city was ringing with it. Ten strokes. Corin counted them in his head, crouched behind the crates with his numb hands on his knees.
Eight. Nine. Ten.
And under them, faint and patient and three quarters of an hour behind, across the water, Old Tide still ringing nine. Not minding at all.
In the yard, Big Harl stamped his feet on the iron bar and looked up at the sky, at the thin crack of stars between the houses, as if the bells were up there.
Then, from under his boots, from under the ground, something knocked.
Not three times. Not slow. Two quick hard bangs, from below, on the underside of the ice door. Bang-bang. The kind of knock you give a door when you are inside it, and want to get out, and are in a hurry.
Big Harl grunted. He stepped off the doors, onto the cobbles. He bent, with his shaggy coat falling open, and took hold of the iron bar in both his big hands, and slid it out of its loops, scraping, and dropped it with a clang on the stones. Then he took hold of the iron ring on the right-hand door, and heaved.
The door came up.
It came up slowly, with a long groan of old hinges, and fell back against the cobbles with a heavy thud. And out of the black square hole underneath it came light. Yellow light, from a lantern somewhere below. And air. Warmer air. Warmer than the yard, which was strange, after the white breath from the hatch. It smelled of people. Of sweat and tallow and old straw and wet stone and something sour underneath, like milk left too long.
And voices. Men's voices. Coming up.
Corin pressed his eye to the gap between the crates.
They came up out of the ground one at a time, up a flight of steep stone steps, into the yellow lamplight. Big men, most of them. In long dark coats and caps and mufflers, with their breath going up in clouds as soon as their heads came out into the cold. One. Two. Three.
The first one carried a lantern. The second one had a coil of rope over his shoulder, and a sack. The third had a club. A short, thick stick of dark wood, with a knob on the end, studded with nails. He swung it as he came up the steps, idly, back and forth, the way a man swings a walking stick on a Sunday afternoon.
Four.
The fourth was not big. He was small and quick and thin, with a pointed face like a weasel's and a long red muffler, and he came up the steps two at a time and stood at the top and looked round the yard with small bright darting eyes. He looked at the barrels. He looked at the cart. He looked straight at the stack of crates where Corin crouched.
Corin did not breathe.
The small bright eyes passed over the crates. And on.
"Cold," said the weasel-faced man. His voice was high and thin. "Saints, it's cold. Why's it always us on a cold night, Harl? Why's it never Gant?"
"Gant don't walk," said Big Harl, through his muffler. "Gant sits."
"Gant sits," agreed the weasel. "And we walk. To Linden Walk. In this." He stamped his feet. "For an old loony in a nightgown."
Corin's hands closed on his knees.
"She said gentle," said the man with the rope. He had a slow deep voice, and a beard, and he had put down his sack and was buttoning his coat up to his chin. "She said, Gentle, mind. He's a lord. And he's no use to me broken."
"Gentle," said the weasel. He laughed. A thin, high, unpleasant laugh. "I'm always gentle. I'm the gentlest man you ever met." He looked at the man with the club. "Aren't I, Sully?"
The man with the club did not answer. He swung his club.
"And the big one," said the man with the lantern. "The captain. With the face. What if the big one's there?"
"He won't be," said the weasel. "He's gone off with the girl. Nobody's seen him since Friday. There's only the nurse. And Pruett. And the Watch on the gate, and they're the captain's, and the captain's ours." He grinned. His teeth were small and brown and pointed. "An old man and a fat nurse and a stable-keeper who laughs at his own horse. I could do it on my own."
"Then do it on your own," said Big Harl.
The weasel stopped grinning.
"Funny," he said. "You're funny, Harl. You should be on the stage." He looked round at the others. "Come on, then. If we're going. Before my feet drop off."
They went.
Out of the yard, into the alley. Not toward the crates. The other way. Toward the river end. Four of them, in a loose bunch, with the lantern bobbing in front and the weasel's high voice going on and on, complaining about the cold. Their boots crunched on the frozen slops in the gutter. The lantern light went along the black backs of the houses, and round a corner, and was gone.
The sound of their boots went with it. Getting fainter. Fainter.
Then nothing.
Corin let out his breath.
It came out of him in a long white shaking cloud, into Wat's beard, and he did not care. He crouched behind the crates with his heart going so hard he could feel it in his teeth.
Four, he thought. Four of them. Going to Linden Walk. To Pruett's. To an old man in a white silk gown, by a window, with a cracked blue stone in a shell box by his bed.
Ten o'clock now. An hour, walking. If they don't hurry.
Eleven.
He thought of Juno, running over the roofs, with her brass key bouncing on its string. Of Oren, by the brazier, looking up from his sword. The Lady's men are going to Pruett's tonight. After ten. He thought of Oren standing up. Of Lyra standing up. Of the two of them going out through the crack in the vestry wall into the dark, past a girl called Wisp on the bell tower. Running.
He did not know if Juno had got there. He did not know if Oren had gone. He did not know if they would be in time.
He could not know. Not from here.
There is nothing you can do about that, said Malakor, very quietly. Not from here. You chose. You chose here. So be here.
Corin shut his eyes for a moment.
Then he opened them, and looked at the yard.
Big Harl was alone.
He stood beside the open ice door, in the yellow lamplight, with his hands in the front of his shaggy coat, and looked down into the hole. Into the warm sour yellow light coming up out of it. He seemed to be thinking about something. Slowly. The way a cow thinks.
Then he bent, and took hold of the iron ring, and began to swing the door shut again.
It was heavy. It came up off the cobbles slowly, groaning, and he had to lean into it, with both hands, with his back to the alley.
With his back to the crates.
Corin felt Wat move.
He did not hear him. He felt him. The great boulder of a man beside him simply was not there anymore. One moment Corin's shoulder was pressed against a wall of salt-stiff canvas, warm and solid. The next it was pressed against cold air.
Corin looked up.
Wat was in the yard.
He had come out from behind the crates without a sound. He was crossing the cobbles now, toward Big Harl's bent back, in five long slow steps. Not running. Walking. The way a man walks across a field toward a gate. With the long wooden boat hook in both his spade-like hands, held crossways in front of him, like a bar.
Big Harl had the door halfway up. He was leaning into it, grunting.
Wat reached him.
He did not hit him. Corin had thought he would. He had thought there would be a blow, a crack, a shout, a fight. There was not. Wat simply lifted the boat hook, crossways, over Big Harl's head, and brought it down in front of his throat, and pulled it back. Against the muffler. Against the neck. Hard. And at the same moment he put one great knee into the small of Big Harl's back, and leaned.
Big Harl let go of the door.
It fell back with a crash onto the cobbles, and the sound of it went up and down the alley like a clap of thunder, and Corin's heart stopped.
Big Harl's hands came up and grabbed at the pole across his throat. His boots kicked. His whole shaggy bulk heaved and twisted, like a bull caught in a gate. His hand went down to his belt. To the long leather sheath. To the knife as long as a child's arm.
And a small shape went past Corin, out of the shadows, very fast.
Gideon.
In his huge stuffed boots, clumping, across the cobbles. He reached Big Harl. And he did the only thing a boy of thirteen with no feelings and no fear and thin bare arms could do against a man three times his size. He took hold of Big Harl's wrist, the one going for the knife, with both his small blue hands, and held on.
Not pulled. Not twisted. Just held on. With all his weight. Hanging off it like a child hanging off a branch.
It was enough. For a moment. For long enough.
Wat leaned back. The boat hook pulled tighter. Big Harl's kicking slowed. His heaving slowed. His big hand on the knife went loose under Gideon's small ones. His eyes, above the muffler, rolled up, until only the whites showed in the yellow lamplight.
And he went down. Slowly. Onto his knees on the frozen cobbles. And then forward, onto his face. Like a tree.
Wat let go of the boat hook. He stood over him, breathing hard, for the first time, in great slow clouds. He bent and put two fingers against the big man's neck, under the muffler, and held them there.
"Breathing," he said. In his voice like a cart on a bridge. "He'll sleep a while. He'll wake with a sore throat."
Gideon was still holding the wrist. Crouched on the cobbles beside the fallen man, in his huge boots, with both his small blue hands wrapped round the thick hairy wrist, the way he had been told. He looked up at Wat.
"Can I let go now?" he said.
"You can let go, lad."
Gideon let go.
He stood up. He looked down at Big Harl, lying on his face in the frost, with his shaggy coat spread out round him like a dead bear. He looked at him for a long time, with his head on one side.
"He put me on the glass," he said. "The first time. When I was eight. He held my hand down on it so I couldn't take it away." A pause. "He laughed."
He said it the way he said everything. Flatly. Like a list.
But his hands, Corin saw, coming out of the shadow behind the crates with the dark lantern, were shaking. Just a little. Just the fingers. The way fingers shake when they have been holding on to something very hard, for a long time, and have only just let go.
Gideon looked down at them.
And the tiny crease came back. Between his pale eyebrows.
Down the Ice Steps
Nobody came out of the Bell.
Corin had been sure that someone would. The crash of the ice door falling back onto the cobbles had been so loud. Like thunder. Like the end of the world. He stood in the middle of the yard with the dark lantern in his hand and his heart going like a hammer and waited for the back door of the tavern to fly open, and men to come pouring out, and lights, and shouting.
Nothing happened.
The back door of the Bell stayed shut. The lamp on its bracket burned low and yellow behind its dirty glass. Through the wall, faint and muffled, the noise of the tavern went on. Voices. Laughter. Somebody singing. The clink of glass. Somebody shouted something, and a lot of people laughed.
And then, very clear, through the wall, a big tired cheerful voice that Corin knew.
"Another! Another, Bert, and one for the lady, and one for the gentleman with the dog, and saints help me, one for the dog!"
More laughter. A cheer. The clink of a lot of glasses.
Wat looked at Corin over the top of Big Harl's fallen body.
"That your sergeant?" he said.
"That's my sergeant."
Wat almost smiled. It moved his beard.
"He's buying the house a drink," he said. "So nobody hears."
"Yes."
"With what money?"
Corin did not know. He thought about a little house in Saltings with a garden, and a sister who grew beans. And a pension in three years. And a big tired man at a bar, in a coat with the collar turned up so the blue did not show, emptying his pockets onto the wood.
I'm good at getting in the way, Moll had said.
They tied Big Harl with his own muffler and the rope from his own belt, and dragged him behind the frozen heap of straw, and put a beer barrel on its side in front of him. It took Wat and Corin together, pulling, and even then they could barely move him. Gideon stood and watched them do it with his head on one side and his shaking hands held a little away from his body, as if he did not know whose they were.
Corin took the knife.
He did not want it. He looked at it in the yellow lamplight, in its long leather sheath, as long as a child's arm, and he did not want to touch it. But he took it, and pushed it through his belt on the left side, under his coat, because he did not want Big Harl to have it when he woke.
Then they stood at the top of the steps and looked down.
The hole under the ice door was square and black and deep.
Stone steps went down into it. Steep ones. Old ones. Worn so smooth and so hollow in the middle by a hundred years of feet and barrels that each step was more like a little bowl than a step, and the frost had gathered in the bowls and turned them white. There was no rail. On either side, the walls were old brick, black and slimy and wet, with white crusts of salt or frost in the cracks, and long wet streaks running down.
The steps went down perhaps twenty feet. At the bottom, there was a floor. Stone flags. And a light. Yellow. From a lantern on a hook on the wall, somewhere round a corner, out of sight. It threw a long wavering yellow patch on the bottom steps and the wet floor, and moved, very slightly, as if somebody down there were breathing on it.
The smell came up to meet them.
Corin had smelled it when the door first opened. He smelled it properly now, standing at the top, with nothing between him and it. People. A lot of people, in a closed space, for a long time. Sweat, and unwashed clothes, and old straw, and a bucket that had not been emptied. Tallow. Damp. And under all of it, that sour, thick, sad smell, like milk gone bad.
He knew that smell.
He had smelled it in the slag room, every morning for a week. On the shelves of jars, in the cold blue light, in the dull dirty glow of a hundred broken memories.
Fear.
Not slag. Not exactly. But fear, kept a long time, and gone stale. The smell of a place where people had been frightened, every day, for years, until the fear had soaked into the bricks like damp.
Mister Gant says it's the best crop, Gideon had said. It grows by itself. You don't have to water it.
"Gideon," said Corin. Very low. "At the bottom. Which way?"
Gideon was standing beside him at the top of the steps, looking down. His shaking hands had stopped shaking. They hung at his sides, quite still again. His face was quite still again too. Calm. Pale. Empty.
All except the crease between his eyebrows. That had not gone away.
"Left," he said. "At the bottom. There's a passage. The barrels are on the right. Beer. Big ones. Then the counting room door. It's got bars. Then the passage turns, and there's the hatch. The cold room. Where they keep the ice. And at the end, past the hatch, Mister Gant's room." He paused. "There's a man on the counting room door. Lom. He's old. He sleeps. And a man on Mister Gant's door. Dace. He doesn't sleep."
"And Gant?"
"In his room. With his book." Gideon looked at Corin. "He's always got his book. He writes everything in it. Who owes. How much. What they gave. Every day." A pause. "It's like yours."
Corin's hand went to his coat. To the Ledger, over his heart.
"No," he said. "It's not like mine."
Gideon considered that. Then he nodded, slowly. As if it were a thing he would think about later, if he ever thought about anything.
"Will Tam be there?" said Corin. "In Gant's room?"
"I don't know," said Gideon. "Maybe. Or maybe in the cold room." His voice did not change. "Sometimes, when they've finished asking, they put people in the cold room. To keep. Till the Lady comes." He looked down the steps. "It keeps them quiet. The cold. After a while."
The night was very still. Through the wall of the Bell, Moll's voice went on, big and cheerful, singing now. A song about a girl and a boat and a fisherman. The whole tavern was singing it with him.
Corin shut his eyes.
He thought of a young man with a scar through his left eyebrow, like a tick on a page, breaking his bread in half. Night, Mrs. Pratt. Don't let the gravy catch. Of a footman's coat and a pair of quick brown eyes. Of a hand drawing a small chalk eye on a wall by a red door, every morning, so that people who needed help would know where to knock.
Of a warming oven in a great hot kitchen at the top of Exchange Street, with a plate of supper in it, keeping.
He opened his eyes.
"Wat," he said. "Behind me. Gideon, behind Wat. If anything goes wrong, you run. Back up the steps. Back to the barge. You tell Hessa. You don't wait for us."
"Why?" said Gideon.
"Because I asked."
Gideon thought about it.
"All right," he said.
Boy, said Malakor, in the back of Corin's head. Very quiet. Very dry. The rod. Take it out. Now. Before you go down. Not after.
Corin put his hand inside his coat, on the right. Into the deep pocket. Past the warm bandaged jar, still humming its one low note, the fourth note, the one that came down. His fingers found the long thin cold shape beside it. The silver rod. With its spiral, winding round and round, and the worn word near the thick end, smooth under his thumb.
He drew it out.
In the yellow light from the bottom of the steps, it shone. Cold and pale and thin. The spiral in it caught the light and seemed to turn, slowly, like water going down a drain.
He held it in his right hand, the bandaged one. It hurt. He held it anyway.
Then he opened the shutter of the dark lantern, just a crack. And he put his foot on the first of the worn, frosted, hollowed steps.
And went down.
It was colder on the steps than in the yard. And then, halfway down, it was warmer. The warm sour breath of the cellar came up round his ankles, and then his knees, and then his chest, as he went down into it, like going down into dirty water.
His boots slid in the frosted hollows. He put his left hand flat on the slimy wall to steady himself. It was wet and cold and soft, like the skin of something dead.
Behind him, he heard Wat coming. Slowly. Carefully. The steps groaning faintly under the great weight of him.
And behind Wat, Gideon. Clump. Clump. In his huge stuffed boots.
The yellow light at the bottom got bigger. And brighter. And moved.
And from somewhere below him, round the corner, at the end of the passage, out of the warm sour fear-soaked dark, a voice spoke. A man's voice. Soft. Pleasant. Unhurried. The voice of someone sitting at a desk, writing, who has heard footsteps on the stair and does not look up.
"Is that you, Harl?" it said. "You're early. Did you forget your hat?"
Corin stopped on the last step.
And from further away, behind a wall, behind bars, very faint, so faint he almost did not hear it over the singing upstairs, a girl began to cry.
