Chapter 6: The House That Forgot Itself, Part 2 of 5: The Lord at the Window
The Lord at the Window
The room behind the white door was small and full of light.
That was the first thing Corin noticed. After the long low room with its dark beams, this one seemed almost to glow. It had been whitewashed, like the other, but more carefully, and more often, so that the walls were a clean soft white like the inside of an eggshell. There was one window, bigger than the ones in the long room, looking down over the stable yard, and the afternoon sun came in through it in a great wide slab of gold and lay across the floor and up over the end of the bed.
The bed was narrow and iron and painted white, and it had been made. Very neatly. The sheets turned down exactly a hand's width over the blanket, the pillow plumped and square. At its foot there was a wooden chest with a folded rug on it. Against one wall, a washstand with a white jug and a white bowl, and beside the bowl, in a little china dish, a bar of soap. Pale and smooth and creamy, almost new. On the windowsill there was a cup of water, and a comb, and a small brass bell.
That was all. There was nothing on the walls. Nothing on the floor but the bare scrubbed boards. Nothing anywhere that might be confusing, or sharp, or hard to understand. It was a room that had been made very simple on purpose, Corin thought. The way you make a room simple for a very small child. Or for someone who has to learn it again every time he looks at it.
And by the window, in a high-backed wooden chair with a cushion on the seat, there sat a man.
He was thin. That was the first thing. Not ill thin, not starved. Just worn down, the way a stone step is worn down, by time and by something passing over it again and again. He might have been fifty. He might have been seventy. It was hard to tell. His hair was white, all of it, fine and soft and brushed straight back from a high forehead. His face was long and narrow and fine-boned, with a straight nose and a firm chin, and it had been shaved that morning so carefully that the skin shone. It was a good face. A clever face. The kind of face that belonged on a coin, or over the fireplace in a great hall, painted in oils, looking down at you as you came in.
He wore a dressing gown of dark blue silk. It had been very fine once. It was faded now along the shoulders where the sun had come through the window onto it, day after day, and the cuffs had been turned and mended, in the same tiny careful stitches as Lyra's. Under it there was a clean white nightshirt, buttoned to the throat. His feet were in soft leather slippers, side by side on the floor, very neat.
His hands lay in his lap.
And he was smiling.
It was a small smile. Calm and gentle and pleased. He was looking out of the window, down into the stable yard, at the green water in the stone trough and the swallows going in and out of their mud nests under the eaves, and he was smiling at them the way you might smile at a pleasant piece of music.
He had stopped humming when the door opened. He turned his head.
His eyes were gray. The same dark clear gray as Lyra's. They went from Oren, standing huge and still by the door, to Lyra, and then past her to Corin, and stayed there for a moment, politely interested. Then they came back to Lyra.
"Good afternoon," said Lord Aurelian Thorne.
His voice was beautiful. That was the terrible thing. It was low and warm and clear, a voice that had been trained, long ago, to fill a great chamber and hold it. A voice that could make a whole Council laugh and then make it cry. And it was saying good afternoon to his daughter as if she were a stranger who had knocked at the wrong door.
"Good afternoon, Father," said Lyra.
She said it brightly. Lightly. As if nothing were wrong at all. She crossed the room and knelt down beside his chair, so that her face was a little lower than his, and put her hand very gently on the arm of the chair, not on him. Corin could see that this was something she had done many times. That she had learned exactly how close she could come.
"Did you sleep well?" she said.
"Very well, thank you." Lord Thorne smiled at her kindly. "Are you the new girl?"
Lyra's smile did not move at all. Not even a little.
"No, Father," she said. "I'm Lyra."
"Lyra," he said, in a pleasant voice, the way you repeat the name of someone you have just been introduced to at a party. "What a pretty name." He looked back out of the window. "Do you see the birds? They have been going in and out all morning. I think they must have children in there."
"Swallows," said Lyra. "They come every summer. You used to say they were good luck for a house."
"Did I?" He seemed pleased. "Then I was right. It is a lucky house."
Corin stood just inside the doorway, beside Oren, and could not move.
He had seen this before. He did not know where. And then he did. A round dirty window on a dark landing. A patch of pale light. A thin clean old man in a brown suit too big for him, with his hair combed flat with water and his hands folded in his lap, smiling at the gray sky. Good morning, said politely, to everyone, the way he did.
Old Terrence.
The same smile. The same stillness. The same clean, combed, cared-for emptiness. Two men who had nothing in common at all, a night porter from Tallow Street and a lord from the High Council, sitting in two windows on opposite sides of the city, smiling at nothing, in exactly the same way.
He felt cold.
"Father," said Lyra, "I've brought someone to see you. A friend of mine." She turned on her knees and held out one hand toward Corin, a small polite movement, like a hostess. "This is Mister Vane."
Lord Thorne turned his head again. He looked at Corin properly this time. His gray eyes moved from Corin's face down to his coat. To the long gray wool. To the brass buttons, with the open book and the closed eye.
They stopped there.
And for a moment, just a moment, the smile went away.
It did not change into anything else. It did not become a frown, or fear, or anger. It simply went, the way a lamp goes out, and what was left was the face underneath. Still and fine-boned and very tired, and for one breath, looking at the buttons on Corin's coat, terribly, terribly sad.
Then the smile came back.
"How do you do, Mister Vane," said Lord Thorne pleasantly. "Do come in. Will you sit? I'm afraid there is only the one chair. And I seem to be in it." He laughed a little at his own joke, softly, as if it surprised him. "I don't have many visitors."
"Thank you, my lord," said Corin. His own voice sounded strange in his ears. "I'll stand. I won't stay long."
"As you like." Lord Thorne nodded, as if that were perfectly reasonable, and turned back to the window. "Do you see the birds?"
"I see them."
"They have children in there, I think."
"I think so too."
Lord Thorne seemed to like that answer. He smiled at the swallows. His hands moved in his lap, loosely, the fingers lifting and falling, the way a pianist's fingers move sometimes when he is listening to music he has played.
Corin came a little further into the room. Oren came with him, one step behind and to the side, so close that Corin could feel the warmth coming off the big man's body. He did not mind. He thought he would have done the same.
He looked at Lord Thorne's hands.
They were long and thin and clean, with long fingers and short square nails, and on the side of the second finger of the right hand there was a hard small lump, old and smooth and yellowish. A writer's lump. The place where a pen rests, year after year, for a whole life. Corin looked down at his own right hand, at the same place on the same finger. He had one too.
And on the left wrist, just below the heel of the hand, where the skin was thin and the blue veins showed through, there was a small dark mark. He had to lean a little to see it. Four letters, tiny, neat, in black ink gone soft and faded with twenty years.
FOLD.
He did not need the Ledger to know what it meant. He had read it at Vespera's that morning. In his own hand. Lord Thorne. One word on his left wrist. Twenty years ago. Three days after Oakhaven. "So I know to look for it."
"My lord," he said. He did not know what he was going to say until he said it. "That word. On your wrist. Do you know what it means?"
Lord Thorne looked down at his own wrist with mild surprise, as if he had not noticed it was there. He turned his hand over and looked at the four small letters for a while.
"No," he said at last, quite cheerfully. "I don't believe I do. Isn't that odd? I suppose somebody must have put it there." He looked up at Corin with his gray, clear, empty eyes. "Do you know what it means, Mister Vane?"
Corin opened his mouth.
And beside him, very quietly, Lyra said, "No, Father. Nobody does."
Lord Thorne nodded. "Ah, well," he said. "Perhaps it will come to me."
He put his hand back in his lap. He looked back out of the window.
And then, slowly, without looking at it, he lifted his right hand from his lap, and reached out to his right, to the white wall beside the window frame, at about the height of his shoulder.
There was a mark on the wall there. Corin had not seen it until now, because the sunlight was so bright on the whitewash. But now that he looked, he saw it. A small gray patch, about the size of a coin. Exactly like the one on the outside of the white door, above the handle. A place where the whitewash had been worn away to the bare gray plaster beneath, by something touching it, in the same spot, over and over and over again.
Lord Thorne put the tip of his first finger on the gray patch.
And he began to tap.
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
He was not looking at it. He was looking at the swallows. His face was calm and pleasant and smiling. His finger moved by itself, like the finger of a man drumming idly on a table while he waits for his dinner.
Tap. Tap.
A pause.
Lyra had gone very still, on her knees beside the chair.
"He does it every day," she said, very softly. She did not take her eyes off her father's hand. "Two years. Every day. Sometimes for hours. The nurse says he does it in the night, too, on the wall by his bed, in the dark. I've had the wall painted three times. It always wears through again." She swallowed. "The doctors say it's nothing. They say people like him do things like that. Patting. Rocking. Humming. They say it's the body remembering how to move, after the mind has gone."
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
Corin was counting.
He did not mean to. He did not decide to. But somewhere behind his eyes, in the place where he counted pockets, and stairs, and hatpins on a door frame, and the people in a room before a fight, something had started counting the moment the old man's finger touched the wall. And it did not stop.
Four. Pause.
Two. Pause.
Nine.
Pause.
Tap. Tap. Tap.
Three.
And then a long pause. Much longer than the others. Lord Thorne's finger rested on the gray patch on the wall. He hummed a little, under his breath, at the swallows.
And then it began again.
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
Four, Two, Nine, Three
Four.
Two.
Nine.
Three.
And the long pause. And the hum. And then four again.
Corin stood in the bright small room and counted it through three times, four times, five, while the old man's finger rose and fell on the gray patch of plaster and the swallows went in and out under the eaves. It did not change. Not once. Not by a single tap. Four, and a short pause, as long as a breath. Two, and the same short pause. Nine, and the pause. Three. And then the long pause, as long as five or six breaths, while Lord Thorne smiled at the yard and hummed his little wandering tune. And then the same again, exactly, from the beginning.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
He took out the new Ledger. His hands were not quite steady. He opened it at the back, at the page where he had written Bertram and Captain Oren Vance and Lock. Ice., and under all of it, as small and as clear as he could make the numbers, he wrote:
4 — 2 — 9 — 3. Always the same. Long pause after 3. Then again.
He looked at it. Then he looked up.
Lyra was staring at him.
She was still on her knees beside her father's chair. But she had turned, and she was looking up at Corin with a face so white that the little cut on her chin stood out on it like a line of red ink.
"What did you write?" she said.
Her voice was very quiet. It was not quite steady.
Corin turned the Ledger round and held it out so she could see.
She read it. She read it again. Then she turned her head, very slowly, and looked at her father's finger on the wall.
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
Pause.
Tap. Tap.
Pause.
Corin watched her lips move. Silently. Counting.
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
Pause.
Tap. Tap. Tap.
And the long pause. And the hum.
Lyra put one hand over her mouth.
She stayed like that for a long time. On her knees on the bare scrubbed floor, in the gold slab of sunlight, with one hand pressed hard over her mouth and her eyes very wide, looking at her father's hand. Her shoulders had begun to shake. Not much. Just a little. The way a branch shakes after a bird has flown off it.
"Two years," she said, behind her hand. "Two years."
Nobody said anything.
"Every day. I've sat here every single day. I've read to him. I've brushed his hair. I've held his cup while he drank. I've sat in this room and listened to that tapping until I thought I'd scream, until I wanted to take his hand and hold it still, until I had the wall painted over and over so I wouldn't have to see the mark." Her voice cracked. "And I never counted. Not once. I never thought to count. I thought it was nothing. The doctors said it was nothing."
She took her hand away from her mouth. She looked up at Corin, and her gray eyes were wet and furious.
"You've been in this room for a quarter of an hour," she said. "A quarter of an hour."
"I count things," said Corin. He did not know how to say it better. "I don't know why. I can't help it. Stairs. Pockets. Pins on a door. When you don't remember anything, you count. So you'll know if something's changed tomorrow."
Lyra stared at him.
Then, unexpectedly, she laughed. It was a strange cracked wet sound, more like a sob than a laugh, and she pressed the back of her wrist against her eyes to stop it.
"Of course you do," she said. "Of course you do. Oh, Father."
Lord Thorne turned his head from the window and looked down at her kindly.
"Are you all right, my dear?" he said. "You seem upset. Shall I ring for someone?"
He lifted the little brass bell from the windowsill, politely, and held it ready.
"No," said Lyra. She took her wrist away from her eyes. She smiled at him, the bright sickroom smile, and Corin saw what it cost her. "No, thank you, Father. I'm quite all right. I just had something in my eye."
"Ah," said Lord Thorne sympathetically. "It's the straw, I expect. It gets everywhere in a stable." He put the bell down again, carefully, exactly where it had been. "Do you see the birds?"
"I see them."
He smiled and turned back to them. And his finger went back to the wall.
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
"What is it?" said Lyra, very low, to Corin. She had not stood up. She stayed on her knees by the chair, close to her father, as if being close to him might help her hear. "What does it mean?"
"I don't know."
"It's a number. It must be. Four thousand two hundred and ninety-three."
"Or four numbers. Four. Two. Nine. Three."
"Or a date. The fourth day of the second month. Ninety-three." She shook her head at once. "No. That's nothing. Nothing happened then that I know of." She pressed her fingers to her temples. "A page in a book. A box at the Bank. The number of a door. A lock. We had a strongroom, at the old house, under the library. It had a lock with a brass dial on it, you turned it round and round to numbers, and only Father and Bertram knew the turns." She stopped. "But the strongroom went with the house. Everything in it was sold. I watched them carry it out."
Corin wrote: Strongroom. Old house. Under the library. Dial lock. Only he and Bertram knew. Sold with the house.
And then, because he had a feeling about it that he could not have explained, he drew a small square around the word Bertram. Twice.
Behind him, Oren made a sound.
It was not a word. It was a low rough noise in his chest, like a stone shifting underwater. Corin turned round.
The big man had not moved from his place by the door. But his arms had come unfolded, and his hands were hanging at his sides, and the fingers of his right hand had begun to move. Very slightly. Against the side of his thigh. As if they were trying to do something they had once known how to do, and had forgotten.
He was staring at Lord Thorne's finger on the wall. And his scarred face had gone gray.
"Oren?" said Lyra.
"Drums," said Oren.
They looked at him.
"It's…" The big man's jaw worked. His eyes went to the side, to the empty shelf, and searched it, and searched it. "It's like the… drums. The army. When you… march. The boy at the front, with the… the drum. On a strap. He beats it. And the beats are…" His hand came up and made a shape in the air. A shape like words coming out of a mouth. "Talk. They're talk. Every beat means a thing. Halt. Turn. Make ready. Fall back. Four and two is… four and two is…"
He stopped.
His hand stayed in the air for a moment, with its fingers spread, reaching. Then it came down, slowly, to his side.
"I knew," he said. His deep rough voice had gone very quiet. "I knew it. My brother. My little brother. He was a… drum boy. In the army. When we were… young. He taught me the calls. All of them. On the kitchen table, with two… spoons. For a whole winter. Till our mother threw the spoons out the window." Something moved at the uncut corner of his mouth. Then it went away. "I knew them all. Every one. For thirty years."
"And now?" said Corin quietly.
Oren looked at the floor.
"Last month," he said. "A big fight. Down at the river. Three men at once. I needed a big wall." He touched his temple with one thick finger, the way he had in the other room. "So I burned the drums. All the calls. I thought, I'll never need those again. I'm not a soldier. Nobody marches anymore."
He lifted his head and looked at Lord Thorne's finger, still tapping on the wall. Four. Two.
"It's in the list now," he said. "With lock. And ice."
The room was very quiet.
Nine.
Three.
The long pause. The hum.
Corin wrote it down. Oren says it's like army drum calls. Every beat means something. His brother was a drum boy. Taught him all the calls. He burned them last month in a fight at the river. He looked at the last line for a long moment. His brother. He added: Ask the brother?
"Where is your brother now?" he said.
Oren's face closed. Not like a door. Like a fist.
"Ministry," he said shortly. "Commander. Enforcers. Black coat, now. Not green." He said it the way you would spit out something rotten. "We don't talk."
Corin did not ask anything else. He wrote Commander. Enforcers. They don't talk, and put a line under it, and closed the book.
Then he did something he did not plan.
He went to the wall beside the window. To the left of the frame, opposite the gray patch where Lord Thorne's finger rested. The whitewash was clean there, unmarked. He put the tip of his own first finger on it.
Lord Thorne's finger was in the long pause. Resting. Waiting.
Corin tapped.
Four.
Lord Thorne went still.
Not the calm stillness of before. A different stillness. His finger did not move from the wall. His humming stopped, all at once, in the middle of a note. He did not turn his head. But Corin, standing a step away, saw the muscles of his thin jaw tighten under the shining shaved skin.
Corin tapped again.
Two.
Lord Thorne's finger began to shake.
It was a very small shaking. You would not have seen it from across the room. A tremble, in the very tip of the finger, where it pressed against the gray plaster. As if something were trying to push up through it from very deep down, and could not quite reach.
Lyra had put both hands flat on the floor. She was not breathing.
Corin tapped nine.
He went slowly. He did not want to get it wrong. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine. His fingertip on the cool white wall.
And Lord Thorne turned his head.
He turned it slowly, as if it were very heavy, and looked at Corin's hand on the wall. At the finger. At the place where it touched the plaster. His gray eyes were fixed on it, wide open. And for the space of one breath there was something in them. Something far down, like a light seen from the bottom of a well. Something that knew.
His lips moved.
No sound came out. But his lips moved, once, around a shape. A small round shape, and then a closing. Like the beginning of a word that started with a W.
Corin lifted his finger to tap three.
And the light went out.
It went out the way a candle goes out when a door is opened in another room. All at once, with no warning. The gray eyes went clear again. Clear and pleasant and empty. Lord Thorne blinked at Corin's hand on the wall, politely puzzled, the way you might look at a fly that has landed on a window.
Then he smiled.
"Do you see the birds, Mister Vane?" he said. "I think they must have children in there."
And his finger slid down from the wall, slowly, and lay in his lap with the other one, quite still. And he did not tap again.
Corin took his hand off the wall. His heart was beating so hard he could feel it in his fingertip.
Nobody spoke. Lyra stayed on her knees, with her hands flat on the floorboards, looking up at her father's face. Oren stood by the door like a man carved out of wood.
And Lord Thorne sat in the gold light, with his hands folded, and smiled at the swallows, and began to hum again, softly, the same small wandering tune, round and round, going nowhere at all.
Corin opened the Ledger and wrote, with fingers that did not want to hold the pencil:
I tapped it back. 4. 2. 9. He stopped. His finger shook. He looked at my hand. Something was there. His lips made a W. Before I could tap 3, it went.
And under it:
He knows it. Somewhere. Very deep.
He was talking to someone.
