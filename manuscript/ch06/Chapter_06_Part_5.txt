Chapter 6: The House That Forgot Itself, Part 5 of 5: Plans at the Kitchen Table
Plans at the Kitchen Table
Mrs. Hobb left at four.
She came out of the little white room with her empty basket and her bonnet on, and announced that his lordship had eaten every crumb, and was having his afternoon rest, and would want his tea at six, and she would be back at six to give it to him, and in the meantime nobody was to wake him or give him anything sweet, because sweet things before tea gave him bad dreams. She said all of this in one breath, very fast, while tying her bonnet strings. Then she beamed at Corin, and said it had been ever so nice to meet a friend of the family, and went out. They heard her oops on the fourth step, and her good afternoon, Mr. Pruett in the yard, and then she was gone, and the long room was very quiet.
The smell of lilies went with her, a little. Not all of it. It was in the walls now, Corin thought. In the whitewash. In the old dark beams.
Lyra got up and opened both windows as wide as they would go. The cool air came in off the yard, smelling of hay and horse and the green water in the trough. She stood there for a moment with her hands on the sill, breathing it.
Then she came back and sat down, and put both hands flat on the table, and said:
"Right."
It was such a Juno word that Corin almost smiled.
"Right," said Lyra again. "We know things now. More than we knew this morning. A great deal more." She looked at the new Ledger, lying open at the back between them. "Read them to me. All of them. In a list. And then we'll decide what to do about each one, and who does it. That's how my father ran his Council meetings. A list, and a name beside every line, and nobody leaves till every line has a name."
So Corin read them.
He read them slowly, off the three crowded pages at the back of the new Ledger, and Lyra listened with her eyes half shut, and Oren sat at the end of the table with his great arms folded and listened too, and every now and then, when Corin came to something, he grunted, very softly, as if he were putting it away in a pocket.
The tapping. Four, two, nine, three. Listen to the walls.
The swept grate. Not a grain of ash.
The fold, down deep. Locked. Only our blood can open it.
The black carriage, the morning after. Gray and white. A case like a doctor's bag.
Bertram. Who called the Ministry. Who sent Oren across the city for a doctor who was not there. Who sold the house and the strongroom and the clock. Who went to a sister in the country and stopped writing.
Doctor Aske. Who also went to a sister in the country. A week before.
The Council money, stopping, last autumn. The envelopes, starting, the month after. Cream paper. White wax. Violet ink. For his lordship's comfort. From a friend. Eleven of them in Mrs. Hobb's tea tin.
The soap.
The little thin shape in the dark cloak, at the cookshop door, before dawn.
The silver half-mask on Lady Thorne's collar. Somewhere with a great deal of water. A grave that nobody had seen.
And Oren's brother. A drum boy once. A Commander now, in black.
When he had finished, Lyra was quiet for a while.
"That's a great many lines," she said at last.
"Yes."
"And only three of us."
"Four," said Corin. "There's a girl on your roof."
Lyra's eyes came open.
Oren had already turned his head, sharp, to look out of the open window, up at the long gray slates of the house across the yard. Corin did not look. He did not need to.
"I saw a small… person," said Oren slowly. "Up there. On the… the brick tube. Where the smoke goes. An hour ago. I thought it was a… a bird. A big one."
"She'd like that," said Corin. "Her name is Juno. She's fourteen. She carries messages on the roofs for two coppers, one if it's raining. She caught me this morning when I fell off a gutter over the Cut. She's mine. My runner. I hired her." He paused. "With a promise, not money. I haven't got any money."
Lyra stared at him.
Then she laughed. It was the first time he had heard her really laugh. It was not a polite laugh. It was a sudden, surprised, rather loud laugh, like a window being thrown open, and she put her hand over her mouth to stop it, and it came out round the sides of her hand anyway.
"The girl with the key," she said. "Who came down off my stable roof two nights ago like a cat down a stair. I gave her two coppers for that drop. I thought she'd break her neck." She shook her head, still laughing. "Of course she's yours. Of course she is. Everything that's happened in the last three days has been one long thread, and I keep finding the same people tied to it."
She stopped laughing. She took her hand away from her mouth.
"Then she gets paid," she said. "Properly. Not promises. I'll pay her. I haven't got much. But I've got more than two coppers, and if she's going to be climbing round on my roof keeping an eye on my father, she's going to be paid like a Thorne pays." She looked at Corin sternly. "Don't argue."
"I wasn't going to."
"Good." She pulled the new Ledger toward her across the table. "Then let's give every line a name."
They did it the way her father had done it. Line by line. A name beside each.
The Ministry. That was Corin's. Tomorrow, early, by the side gate on Archive Lane, the way Toby had said. He would look for whatever he could find. The Council Benevolent Fund, and why it had stopped paying. Any file on Bertram, the Thorne steward. Any file on a Doctor Aske. The record of who had gone to the old Thorne house in a black carriage on the morning after the burning, and why. And the night book from Night Door Three, from the night eight years ago when a porter called Terrence went to work and came home smiling.
"That last one's not mine," said Lyra.
"No. It's Juno's. I promised."
Lyra nodded slowly and did not ask anything else.
The clock. That was Lyra's. She would go to the auction house that had sold the contents of the old house. Halloran and Dunn, on Market Row. Every lot they sold was written in their great book, with the name of the buyer and the price. Lot 41 had been chalked on the back of her mother's portrait. If the portrait had a lot number, so had the clock. And if the clock had gone to a pawnbroker, the book would say which.
"And Bertram's address," said Corin. "If he arranged the sale, he'll have given the auctioneers somewhere to send the money. Or the accounts. Or something."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment. Then she wrote it down herself, in the new Ledger, under his writing, in a neat clear slanting hand that was nothing like his. Halloran & Dunn. Lot book. The clock. Bertram's direction.
It looked strange, her writing in his book. It looked like a visitor sitting in his chair. He found that he did not mind.
The envelopes. Lyra's too. She would ask Mrs. Hobb, very kindly, if she might see the other eleven. For thanking the friend. She would look at every one. The paper. The ink. The wax. The dates. Anybody can be careful eleven times, she said. Nobody can be careful twelve.
The soap. Nobody's. It stayed. Every day. In the little china dish by the white jug. Lyra's mouth went thin when they came to that line. But she wrote stays beside it, in her own hand, and underlined it.
The walls. That was the hardest line. They sat and looked at it for a long time.
"I'll do it," said Lyra at last. "Every day. When I sit with him. When he taps, I'll tap back. Slowly. A little at a time."
"Be careful," said Corin.
She looked at him.
"When I did it," he said, "something came up. From very deep. And it pushed. Like somebody pushing up against a lid. And then it went." He did not know how to say what he meant. "I don't know what's under there. I don't know what happens if it pushes too hard and the lid comes off. He might…" He stopped.
"Break," said Lyra quietly.
"I don't know. Maybe."
She looked down at the page. Then she wrote, beside the walls, very small: Slowly. One number a day. Stop if he shakes.
"Rule two," she said. "Nobody burns for the other one without asking first. That counts for fathers, too, I think."
Lady Thorne. The brooch. The grave. Lyra looked at that line for the longest of all. Then she wrote, beside it: Later. And nothing else.
Corin did not ask.
And last of all: The brother. Commander. Enforcers.
They both looked at Oren.
The big man did not move. He sat at the end of the table with his arms folded and his scarred face turned toward the open window, and for a long time he did not say anything at all.
"He knew the calls," said Corin, carefully. "Better than you. He taught you."
"Yes."
"If anybody alive knows what four and two and nine and three means on a drum…"
"I know." Oren's voice was very flat. "I know what you're saying, Mister Vane." He did not turn his head. "He wears black now. He carries a… stick. A rod. Like yours, but for hitting. He takes people out of their houses at night for the Ministry. Old people. Ink-Binders. Children who… who see things they shouldn't." His jaw tightened. "He was a good boy once. With his drum. He used to…" He stopped.
Nobody spoke.
"Twenty years ago," said Oren slowly, "he went away with the army. Drum boy. Sixteen. Proud as a… rooster. And he came back. One of the only ones who came back, from wherever they went. And he never said where. Never. Not to me. Not to our mother. He came back different. Quiet. Hard. He joined the Ministry the next year. He's been climbing ever since." A pause. "We don't talk."
Corin's hand had gone very still on the table.
Twenty years ago. An army. A drum boy, sixteen, who came back from somewhere he would never name.
He did not know why the back of his neck had gone cold. But he wrote it down, slowly, under Oren's line. Brother went with the army twenty years ago. Drum boy. Sixteen. One of the few who came back. Never said from where. Joined the Ministry the next year.
And then, very small, so small that only he would be able to read it:
Twenty years. Oakhaven?
Lyra was watching him. She could not have read the small line upside down. But she saw his face, and she did not ask.
"I won't make you, Oren," she said gently. "Not now. Maybe not ever."
"Thank you, my lady."
"But if the day comes when there's nobody else to ask…"
Oren was quiet for a long moment.
"Then I'll go," he said. "If the day comes." He unfolded his arms and put his huge hands flat on the table. "And I'll take a big wall with me. Just in case."
Lyra wrote beside the last line: If the day comes. And put down the pencil.
They looked at the page. Every line had a name. Most of the names were Lyra or Corin. One was Juno. One was Oren, with a question mark. One was nobody, and said stays.
"Rule five," said Lyra.
"There are only four."
"There are five now." She picked the pencil up again and wrote it at the bottom of the page, under everything, in her neat slanting hand: 5. Whoever finds something first tells the other one the same day. Even if it's small. Even if it's bad. She looked up. "Agreed?"
"Agreed."
"Oren?"
The big man looked at the page for a long time. Then he reached out one thick finger and put it on the number 5, very gently, the way you might touch a sleeping bird.
"Agreed," he said.
And then, last of all, before she gave the pencil back, Lyra turned to the very first blank page at the front of the new Ledger. The page Corin had not yet filled. The page that, tonight, at the end of Bram's counter, he would fill with READ THIS FIRST and Your name is Corin and all the rest.
She did not write on that page. She turned one more. And on the next page, near the top, where it would be the first thing he saw after the rules, she wrote, carefully, in her own hand:
Lyra Thorne. Linden Walk, the gate with the stone pineapple, up the outside stair, over the stable. She is your partner. You chose it. She will tell you the rest.
She closed the book and pushed it back across the table to him.
"So you don't have to wait for Juno to tell you," she said. "About me."
Corin looked at the closed book for a long moment. At the stiff new leather. At VII.
Then he put it inside his coat, over his heart, next to the old one.
Bright
He stayed until six.
He had not meant to. He had meant to leave as soon as the list was done, and go down to Quill Row, and sit at the end of Bram's counter with a pot of tea and copy the front pages into the new Ledger before the light went. But Lyra made more tea. And Oren, without a word, went down the stone stairs and came back with a loaf and a lump of hard yellow cheese and three small brown apples from somewhere, and put them on the table, and sat. And they ate. And nobody said very much. And the light in the two small windows went from gold to amber to a soft deep orange, and the swallows came home to their mud nests under the eaves, one by one, and went quiet.
It was the first meal Corin could remember eating at a table with other people.
He did not say so. But he wrote it down, afterward, very small, at the bottom of the third page at the back of the new Ledger: Bread and cheese and apples at Lyra's table. Oren brought them. Nobody talked much. It was good.
At six, there was the oops on the fourth step, and Mrs. Hobb's voice in the yard, and the smell of hot broth coming up the stairs ahead of her. And Corin stood up, and put on his hat, and knew it was time to go.
"I'll say goodbye to your father," he said.
Lyra looked at him. Then she nodded.
The little white room was dim now. The great slab of gold had gone from the floor, and the light that came through the window was soft and orange and low, and it lay across the white wall in a long warm stripe, right across the small gray patch beside the window frame. Lord Thorne had woken from his rest. He was sitting in his high-backed chair again, with the rug from the foot of the bed over his knees, looking out at the yard, where the shadows had grown long and blue and the green water in the trough had gone dark as ink.
He was not tapping. His hands lay folded in his lap.
He looked round when they came in, and smiled the pleasant smile.
"Good evening," he said.
"Good evening, my lord," said Corin. "I've come to say goodbye. Thank you for having me."
"Oh, are you going?" Lord Thorne seemed sorry. "What a shame. I don't have many visitors." He looked at Corin with mild interest, as if seeing him for the first time. "Have we met?"
"This afternoon, my lord. I'm Mister Vane."
"Mister Vane," said Lord Thorne politely. "Of course." He nodded. "Do come again."
"I will."
Corin stood there a moment longer, in the soft orange dimness. He did not know why. Lyra was in the doorway behind him, and Oren behind her, and Mrs. Hobb was clattering a pan in the long room. Lord Thorne had turned back to the window. His long thin hands were folded. His white hair was lit orange by the low sun.
Corin looked at him, and felt the three notes come into his throat.
They had been there all day. On and off. In the loft, waiting for Juno. On the roofs. On the stairs at No. 14. He had stopped noticing them. Three notes, going up, and up, and up, and stopping, as if they were asking a question. A song he did not know. A song that had come to him out of a dead man's last minutes in a dark lane, and would not go away. He hummed, said a label on a jar, in his own hand. Gran's song, said a page in the old Ledger. The same one the busker plays on Lantern Bridge. The one that makes your chest hurt.
He did not decide to hum them. They came out by themselves, very softly, through his nose, the way they always did. Up. And up. And up.
And stopped.
Lord Thorne's head came round.
It came round fast. Faster than Corin had seen him move all day. His gray eyes went to Corin's face, and fixed there, and they were not pleasant and polite and empty. They were wide open, and very dark in the orange dimness, and there was something in them again. The thing from the bottom of the well. Close now. Close to the top. Looking up.
He was staring at Corin. Not at the coat this time. Not at the buttons. At his face. As if he were trying very hard to see something in it. Something small and far away. A child's face, perhaps, under a man's.
His lips moved.
And this time a sound came out.
"Bright," said Lord Aurelian Thorne.
He said it very quietly. Hoarsely. As if his throat were not used to the word, as if it had been shut away down in the dark for a very long time and had rusted there. But it was clear. It was not a mumble. It was not a sound. It was a word.
The room went absolutely still.
In the doorway, Lyra made a small sound, like a breath caught on a hook.
Corin did not move. He could not have moved if the roof had come down. He stood in the orange light and looked into the old man's eyes, and the old man looked back at him, and for the space of three heartbeats, four, five, there was somebody there. Somebody who knew something. Somebody who had seen something, a long time ago, that had made his pen stop on the page.
"My lord," said Corin. His voice was hardly there. "What's bright?"
Lord Thorne's lips moved again. Slowly. As if they were trying to find the next word, on a shelf just out of reach.
And then the light went out.
It went the way it had gone in the afternoon. All at once. A lamp blown out in another room. The gray eyes cleared, and softened, and became pleasant and polite and empty again, and the old man blinked at Corin, puzzled, as if he had just woken up and found a stranger standing by his chair.
Then he smiled.
"Good evening," he said. "Have we met?"
Corin could not answer.
Lyra came past him. She went to her father's chair and knelt down beside it, the way she had in the afternoon, close but not too close, with one hand on the wooden arm. Her face was very white. Her eyes were very bright.
"Father," she said. "Father. What did you say? Just now. What's bright?"
Lord Thorne looked down at her kindly.
"I'm sorry, my dear," he said. "I don't think I said anything. Did I?" He patted her hand on the chair arm, gently, absently, the way you might pat a stranger's dog. "Are you the new girl?"
Lyra closed her eyes.
She stayed like that for a moment. Then she opened them again, and smiled at him, the bright sickroom smile, and said, "Yes, Father. I'm the new girl," and stood up, and walked out of the room past Corin and Oren without looking at either of them.
Corin found her in her own narrow room, standing in front of the portrait of the woman in the blue dress. She was not crying. She was just standing there, very straight, with her arms wrapped round herself, looking at the half-finished crimson letters on the painted hoop.
"He spoke," she said, without turning. "To you. A real word. He hasn't said a real word in two years. Not one. Not to me. Not to Rowan, before Rowan went. Not to anybody. Only good morning and thank you and are you the new girl and do you see the birds." Her voice was perfectly steady. "And you hum three notes, and he looks at you as if he's seen a ghost, and says a word."
"I'm sorry."
"Don't be sorry." She turned round. Her face was white and fierce. "Don't you dare be sorry. Write it down. That's what you do. Write it down so it's somewhere."
He wrote it down. Standing in her narrow gray room, in the last of the light, with his hands shaking.
Six in the evening. Saying goodbye. I hummed the three notes without meaning to. Lord Thorne turned fast and looked at my face. Not my coat. My face. Like he was looking for someone. He said "Bright." Out loud. Clear. Then it went. He doesn't remember saying it. Lyra says it's the first real word in two years.
And under it, after a long time:
Bright. What's bright? Who's bright?
And then, because it came to him and would not go away, a line from two nights ago in the old Ledger, from the torn registration list he had found in the hollow cover of a green book. And the two lines near the bottom that he could not read. A B. And a longer word.
He did not write anything else. He did not know what to write.
Lyra walked him down the stone stairs. Oren came behind them. At the bottom, in the dark yard, under the swallows' nests, by the black water of the trough, the big man held out his hand, and in it was Vespera's bone-handled knife.
"Yours," he said.
Corin took it.
Oren did not let go of his hand at once. He held it for a moment, in his own huge one, hard and warm and rough as a tree. He looked down at Corin in the dusk.
"Mister Vane," he said slowly. "The word. In your book. The white flowers. For the dead."
"Yes?"
"Read it to me."
Corin did not need to open the book. "Lilies," he said.
Oren nodded, once.
"Lilies," he said. Carefully. Like a man setting a stone on top of a wall. "Lilies." Then he let go of Corin's hand. "I'll be at the door. Every night. You can tell your little bird on the roof. Nobody in white gets up those stairs while I'm standing."
He turned and went back up into the dark.
Lyra walked with Corin to the gate. The stone pineapple stood on its post above them, a black shape against the darkening sky, with the moss in its crack. Linden Walk beyond the gate was empty and blue with dusk, and down at the far end, a lamplighter was going slowly from post to post with his long pole, and at each post a gemstone woke up, cold and blue, and began to hum.
"Tomorrow," said Lyra. "The Ministry. You'll go?"
"Early. By the side gate."
"And you'll come back and tell me. The same day."
"Rule five."
"Rule five." She looked at him in the blue dark. "You won't remember any of this."
"No."
"Not my father. Not the tapping. Not Oren's word. Not the painting." She swallowed. "Not bright."
"No," said Corin. He touched his coat. "But the book will."
Lyra looked at his hand on his coat, over the two Ledgers, the old one and the new.
Then she did a strange thing. She reached out and put two fingers on the back of his hand, very lightly, just for a moment. The way she had nearly touched the painted needle. A breath away. And then not a breath away.
"Then make sure it does," she said.
And she went back through the gate, and up the stone stairs, and the green door opened, and a little warm light came out, and then it closed.
Corin stood under the stone pineapple in the dusk.
Something dropped out of the sky beside him and landed on the cobbles with a soft thump and a scuff of boots.
"You took ages," said Juno.
She was smudged with soot from her cap to her boots. She had a bit of moss in her hair. The brass key swung on its string against her coat. She looked cold, and cross, and enormously pleased with herself.
"I sat on that chimney the whole afternoon," she said. "The whole afternoon. I saw the big one see me. I waved. He didn't wave back." She sniffed. "And I saw the nurse lady come and go, twice. And I saw nobody in white. Not once. Not a thread." She fell into step beside him, without being asked, as he started down Linden Walk under the yellowing lime trees. "And I saw something else."
"What?"
"The old man. In the window. The posh one, with the white hair." She looked up at Corin sideways. "When you went in to see him, just now, at the end. I could see in. Through the window. From the chimney. He turned round, quick, and looked at you." She hesitated. "Like Grandpa does sometimes. When Gran sings. Just for a second. Like somebody's come to the door."
Corin did not say anything.
At the end of Linden Walk, where the street turned, he stopped, and looked back.
The stable stood at the far end of the street in the blue dusk, behind its tall brick wall. Above the wall, he could see the upper windows. Two small ones, with a warm yellow light in them now, where Lyra had lit the silver candlestick. And the bigger one, to the right. The little white room. It was dark. But not quite dark. There was the last of the orange light in the sky, and it lay on the glass, faintly.
And there was a shape in the window. A thin shape, in a high-backed chair. With white hair.
And beside it, kneeling, a smaller shape. Dark-haired. With one hand raised, flat against the wall beside the window frame.
He could not hear anything from here. He was much too far away. But he did not need to hear.
He watched the smaller hand lift from the wall, and touch it, and lift, and touch it. Slowly. Carefully. Four times.
And then stop. And wait.
Juno had seen it too. She was standing very still beside him, with her hand on her key.
"What's she doing?" she whispered.
"Listening to the walls," said Corin.
They stood there a while longer, in the dusk, under the last lime tree on Linden Walk, while the lamplighter went on down the street behind them and the gemstones woke one by one, cold and blue and humming.
In the dark window, nothing answered.
Then Juno tugged his sleeve, and they turned the corner, and went down the hill toward Quill Row, and Bram's lamp, and the end of Bram's counter, and a pot of tea, and a new book waiting to be told who he was.
