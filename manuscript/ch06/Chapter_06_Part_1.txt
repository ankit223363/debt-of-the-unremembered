Chapter 6: The House That Forgot Itself, Part 1 of 5: Over the Horse
Debt of the Unremembered. Arc 1: The Siphon's Ledger.
Over the Horse
She did not come down the stairs to meet him.
She stayed where she was, on the top step, with her arms around her knees, and looked at him for a long moment across the stable yard. As if she were checking something. As if she were reading him the way he read his Ledger in the morning, line by line, to see if he was still the person she had left at Vespera's at the sixth bell.
"Do you know me?" she said at last.
It was a plain question. She asked it plainly, without any hurt in it, the way a doctor might ask does this hurt?
"No," said Corin. "I know your name. I read it this morning. I know you were at Vespera's. I know we shook hands." He touched his coat, over the place where the old Ledger sat against the new one. "I wrote partner in capitals."
"You did." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. "Then that will have to do. Come up. Mind the fourth step. It's loose."
He crossed the yard. It was a small yard, cobbled, with a stone trough in the middle full of green water and a pump beside it with a dented iron handle. Straw lay in the gaps between the cobbles. There were swallows' nests under the eaves, little cups of dried mud, and the swallows were going in and out of them like arrows, fast and black and white. The tall stable doors on the ground floor stood half open, and from inside came the warm deep smell of horse and hay and leather, and a slow heavy sound of something big shifting its weight from one foot to another.
He looked in as he passed.
The stable was long and dim and cool, with a row of stalls down one side, and most of the stalls were empty. Swept clean. The mangers dry. Only at the far end, in the last stall, there was a horse. An old gray mare, very tall, with a long white face and a sagging back and a coat gone the color of ashes. She turned her head and looked at him over the half door with one large dark patient eye. There was a brass plate on the door of her stall, polished bright, with a name engraved on it in fancy curling letters.
PATIENCE.
"She's ours," said Lyra from above him. "The last one. We had fourteen once. My father kept a carriage and four and two riding horses for each of us and a pony for every birthday. Now there's Patience." Her voice was dry. "Mr. Pruett who owns the livery lets her stay for nothing, because she's too old to sell and too sweet to shoot, and because my father once lent him money when nobody else would. He doesn't remember the money. But Mr. Pruett does."
Corin looked at the old mare. The mare looked at him. Then she sighed, a long gusting horse sigh that blew bits of hay out of her manger, and turned away.
He went up the stairs.
They were worn stone, hollowed in the middle by a hundred years of grooms' boots going up and down, and they had no rail. The fourth one rocked under his foot. He stepped over it. At the top there was a narrow stone landing, and the green door, standing open now, and Lyra on her feet beside it. She had picked up the sword in its scabbard and was holding it by the middle, loosely, the way someone else might carry an umbrella.
She was not tall. He saw that now, close to. The top of her head came to his chin. She was slim and straight, with a pale face that had a little color in it today from the sun, and short dark hair, still ragged from the knife, pushed back off her forehead and held flat with two plain pins in a way that was neat but not fashionable. Her dress was plain gray wool, with a high collar and long sleeves and no lace at all. It had been mended at the cuffs. Very well mended, with stitches so small he could hardly see them. Someone else's stitches, he thought, remembering a line in his Ledger. She can't thread a needle anymore.
There was a small cut on her chin. Healing.
"You look tired," she said.
"Mrs. Marta said I looked like I'd slept in a ditch."
"You slept in Vespera's chair. It's worse." She stood aside. "Come in. Quickly. Before anyone on Linden Walk sees a Ministry coat on my stairs and starts a story."
He went in.
The door opened straight into a room. It was long and low, with a ceiling of old dark beams that he could have touched with his hand, and two small square windows looking down over the yard. The walls were whitewashed. The floor was bare boards, scrubbed pale. Once, he thought, this had been a hayloft, or the place where the grooms slept, and you could still see where the great hay door had been, in the far wall, bricked up now with newer, redder bricks than the rest.
But it was not a hayloft now. Someone had made it into a home. Carefully. With a great deal of trouble. And with things that did not belong in a room over a stable at all.
There was a rug on the floor. Only one, in the middle, but it was a beautiful rug, thick and soft and deep red, with a pattern of birds and vines in blue and gold, and a fringe of silk. It was worn in a path across the middle, where feet went from the door to the stove and back. There was a stove, small and black and iron, with a kettle on it. There was a plain deal table with four plain chairs. And on the table, in the middle, there was a candlestick. One. Silver, heavy, tall, beautifully made, with a pattern of roses and thorns twisting round its stem. It shone. Someone polished it every day.
Against one wall there was a little bookshelf, made of planks laid across bricks. It held perhaps twenty books, old ones, with good leather spines. Above it, on two iron hooks, there was a rack for a sword. Empty now, because the sword was in Lyra's hand.
And there were two doors. One at each end of the long room. Both of them closed.
Corin stood on the red rug and turned slowly round, the way he had in Room 9, the way he had in Juno's loft. His eyes went from thing to thing and counted them. The rug. The candlestick. The good books. The mended cuffs. The scrubbed floor. The bricked-up hay door.
A house, he thought. A whole great house, with fourteen horses and a carriage and four. Folded up small. Very small. And put somewhere it would fit.
"It was the steward's idea," said Lyra. She had been watching him look. She put the sword back on its rack, carefully, the way you would hang up a coat you were fond of. "Bertram. Our old steward. When the money went and the house had to be sold, he found this place. He said, my lady, a Thorne can live anywhere, so long as she lives like a Thorne. He chose the rug. He chose the candlestick. He said one good thing in a room is worth ten poor ones." She touched the candlestick with one finger, lightly. "Then he arranged the sale of everything else, and he paid off the servants, and he found my father a nurse, and he left. He went to his sister in the country. He was very old. He cried when he went."
"Where in the country?"
Lyra looked at him. "You're asking about the clock."
"I'm asking who knows where the clock went."
"Bertram does. Or did." She shook her head. "He sent one letter, the first winter, to say he had arrived safely. Then nothing. I wrote three times. Nothing came back." Her mouth went tight. "I told myself he was old and his hands shook and he couldn't write anymore. That's probably all it is."
Corin did not say anything. He took out the new Ledger, the stiff one, the one with VII on the cover, and opened it at the very back, at the last page, where it would not be in the way when he copied the front pages over tonight. He wrote, small: Bertram. Thorne steward. Arranged the sale. Went to a sister in the country. One letter, the first winter. Then nothing.
Lyra watched him write.
"A new one," she said.
"The old one's full. I wrote the last line in a pigeon loft an hour ago."
"A pigeon loft?"
"It's been a long morning."
She looked at him for a moment more. Then she went to the stove and put the kettle on, and got two cups down from a shelf, plain white ones, and a teapot that did not match them, and a little tin of tea. She moved about the stove quickly and neatly, the way someone does who has had to learn how and has not had very long to learn it.
"Tell me," she said, with her back to him. "The long morning. All of it. Before he wakes up."
"Before who wakes up?"
She nodded at the door at the far end of the room. The one on the right. It was closed. It was painted white, like the walls, and it had a brass handle, polished, and above the handle, at about the height of a man sitting down, there was a small worn patch in the paint. A patch about the size of a coin, where the white had been rubbed away to the bare gray wood beneath.
"My father," said Lyra. "He sleeps after his dinner. He'll wake when the nurse comes back. Or when he hears my voice." She poured the boiling water into the pot. "You can tell me before he does."
So Corin told her.
He sat at the plain table, in front of the silver candlestick, with his hat on his knee, and told her about the white coat in the cellar door on Hook Lane and the white thread on the splinter. About Mrs. Marta and the polite young woman with gloves. About the bird cut fresh into the wall under WE REMEMBER. About the cut paper on his window, and the jars turned to face the wall, and the bow on the box under the bed, and the note in a hand very nearly his own. STOP LOOKING. About Juno, and the dark cloak, and the smell of lilies on the chimney pot, and the gray gloves in Rope Street. About the Cut, and the gutter, and the bosun's boy. About the lily on the Dye-Works chimney, there and then not there. About Toby, and Brack. About Bram's note, and the small woman in the dark cloak who had come asking for his old books.
He read most of it from the old Ledger. He did not mind that she saw him read it. It was easier than pretending.
Lyra did not interrupt him once. She sat across the table with her tea going cold in front of her and her hands folded on the wood, and she listened, and her face grew paler and stiller as he went on. When he got to the part about the lily on the chimney, she shut her eyes for a moment.
When he had finished, she was quiet for a long time.
"She was in your room," she said at last. "Or someone of hers."
"Yes."
"While we were sitting at Vespera's table writing down what we knew."
"Yes."
"And she knows the girl. Your Juno. She looked right at her." Lyra opened her eyes. They were gray, he saw now, a dark clear gray like rain on slate. "Corin. If she knows your room, and your runner, and your stationer, then she knows Vespera. And she knows me. She knows exactly where I live. Everyone does. Thorne Heiress Living Over a Horse. It was in the broadsheets."
"I know."
"Then why hasn't she come here?"
Corin did not have an answer for that. He looked at the white door at the far end of the room, with its worn gray patch in the paint, and thought about it. Then he thought about Pell, on the rubbish heap in Gutter Lane, emptied. And about a voice in a dark lane, low and warm and beautiful, saying Lord Thorne is not well, I'm afraid. He has not been well for some time.
"Maybe," he said slowly, "she doesn't need to. Maybe there's nothing here she wants."
Lyra's face did something he could not read.
"Or maybe," she said very quietly, "she already took it."
And behind the white door at the end of the room, as if it had been waiting for her to say it, something began to tap.
Soft. Slow. Patient. A single fingertip on a wooden wall.
Tap. Tap. Tap. Tap.
Captain Oren
Lyra was already on her feet. But before she could take one step toward the white door, there was another sound. Behind them. Outside.
Boots on the stone stairs.
Heavy boots, coming up fast. They did not stop at the fourth step. They did not stumble on it either. They stepped clean over it, the way only someone who has gone up those stairs a thousand times would know to, and came on, and the green door, which Lyra had pushed to but not shut, swung open so hard that it banged against the wall.
A man filled the doorway.
He filled it in every direction. He was very tall, and very broad, with a chest like a barrel and shoulders that nearly brushed both sides of the frame. He had to duck his head to look into the room. He was perhaps forty-five, with a short beard more gray than brown, cut close to his jaw, and short gray hair, and a face that had been handsome once, before someone had opened it with a blade from the left temple down across the cheek to the corner of the mouth. The scar was old and white and pulled his mouth a little crooked on that side, so that he always looked as if he were about to say something and had decided not to.
He wore a coat that had once been a uniform. Dark green wool, faded nearly to gray at the shoulders, with tarnished silver buttons, and a badge on the left breast, embroidered in thread that had once been red and gold and was now the color of rust and straw. A rose. Inside a ring of thorns. At his hip there was a short heavy sword in a plain scabbard, and his right hand was on the hilt of it. Not drawing it. Just resting there. The way a hand rests on the head of a big dog that has not yet decided whether to bite.
His eyes went round the room once. Lyra. The table. The cups. The silver candlestick.
Corin.
They stopped on Corin's coat. On the long gray wool. On the brass buttons, with the open book and the closed eye.
Something happened to his face. It did not move much. It was not the kind of face that moved much. But it went still in a different way. A harder way. The way water goes still just before it freezes.
"Ministry," he said.
His voice was deep and slow and rough, like stones rolling in a river.
"Oren," said Lyra quickly. "It's all right. He's—"
"Ministry," said the big man again. He took one step into the room. The boards groaned under him. His hand did not leave the hilt. "In the…" He stopped. His eyes moved, very slightly, to the side, as if he were looking for something on a shelf just out of sight. "In the… place. The home place. Where we…" He stopped again. His jaw worked. "Where my lord sleeps."
"I know where he sleeps, Oren. I asked him here."
"You asked."
"I asked." Lyra's chin came up. Her voice did too. It was not loud, but it was the kind of voice that is used to being obeyed, even in a room over a stable. "Captain Vance. This is Corin Vane. He is a harvester at the Ministry of Records. He is not here for the Ministry. He is here for me. He is my—" She hesitated, very briefly. "My partner."
The big man looked at her. Then at Corin. Then at her again. His crooked mouth did not move.
"Partner," he said slowly. As if it were a word in a foreign language.
"Yes."
"In what?"
"In finding out what happened to my father."
The man called Oren stood very still for a long moment. Then, slowly, his hand came away from the hilt of his sword. It did not go far. It hung at his side, close, the fingers half curled. But it came away.
"You were to be at the farrier's," said Lyra. "Till after one."
"Farrier was…" The pause again. The eyes going to the side, looking for the word on its shelf, and not finding it. "Shut. The door. The… the thing on the door. With the…" He made a small shape in the air with his thick fingers. A loop. A hook.
"A lock," said Lyra gently.
"Lock." He said it as if she had handed him something he had dropped. "Lock. On the door. So I came back." He looked at Corin. "And found a Ministry man at the table. Drinking tea. From my lord's cups."
"They're my cups, Oren."
"Your lord father's cups. Everything in this house is…" He stopped. He shut his mouth. He did not try to find the word that time. He only looked at Corin, and Corin looked back.
Corin did not know what to say. He did not know this man. He did not know anything about him except what he could see, and what he could see was a big, slow, scarred man in a faded green coat, standing in the doorway of a room over a stable, guarding a lord who could not remember his own name, and running out of words.
So he stood up. He did it slowly, keeping his hands where the big man could see them. He put his hat on the table, beside the silver candlestick.
"Captain," he said. "I'm carrying a rod. Silver. In the long pocket on my right side. It's for harvesting. It's not a weapon. And a knife, in my left pocket, that an old woman gave me this morning for luck. I don't know how to use it. You can take them both, if you like, while I'm here."
Oren looked at him for a long time.
Then he crossed the room in three strides, and the boards groaned, and he stood in front of Corin, close, so close that Corin had to tip his head back to see his face. He smelled of leather and horse and pipe smoke and, under it, very faint, of something sharp and clean, like the air after a storm.
He held out one huge hand, palm up.
Corin gave him Vespera's knife. Oren turned it over once, looked at the yellowed bone handle, and put it in his own coat without a word.
Then he held out his hand again.
Corin reached into the long pocket and drew out the rod. It was cold, as it always was. It lay across his palm, silver and as long as his forearm, with the word worn almost smooth along its side. He held it out.
Oren reached for it.
His fingers touched the silver. And jerked back.
Not far. Not fast. He was not a man who did anything fast. But his hand came away from the rod as if it had touched a stove, and he stood there with his fingers spread, looking at them, and then at the rod, and his eyes had gone narrow.
"Cold," he said. "It's… cold like the…" His fingers closed on nothing. "Like the… thing. In winter. On the water. When you can walk on the water." His face twisted, very slightly, with frustration, and then went still again. "White. Hard. You can stand on it."
"Ice," said Corin quietly.
Oren looked at him.
"Ice," he said. Slowly. Carefully. Like a man setting down something heavy that he had been carrying a long way. "Yes. Cold like ice." He nodded once, short. "Keep it. Your… rod. I don't want it." He stepped back. "But I'll be in the room. When you see him. The whole time."
"Oren—" said Lyra.
"The whole time, my lady," said Oren. He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. "Or he doesn't go in."
Lyra opened her mouth. Then she looked at the big man's face, and shut it again, and something in her own face went soft and tired and sad.
"All right," she said. "The whole time."
Oren nodded. He went to the window, the one nearest the white door, and stood beside it with his back to the wall and his arms folded across his great chest, where he could see the room and the door and the yard below all at once. He did not take his eyes off Corin.
Corin put the rod back in its pocket. He sat down again, slowly. His tea was cold. He drank it anyway.
Behind the white door, the tapping had stopped.
Lyra came and sat across from him again. She did not look at Oren. She spoke to Corin quietly, but not so quietly that the big man could not hear. Corin had the feeling that she did not want to talk about Oren behind his back, even here, even three steps away.
"He was my grandfather's man first," she said. "Then my father's. Thirty years in the Thorne green. He taught me to ride. He taught me to hold a sword before my fencing master ever did. When the money went, and the house went, and everyone else went, he stayed." She turned her cup round on the table, slowly. "I can't pay him. I haven't paid him in a year. He won't take it anyway. He finds money. I don't ask where."
"Duels," said Oren from the window.
Lyra closed her eyes.
"Duels," said Oren again. His voice was flat. "Down by the… the river. The boats. The place where they… fight for coins. Men pay. To watch. And bet." He shrugged one huge shoulder. "I don't lose."
"You don't lose," said Lyra, without opening her eyes, "because you burn."
The big man did not answer that.
Corin looked from one of them to the other. Then he looked at the big man properly. At the way he stood. At the faded green coat. At the scar. At the eyes that went to the side, every few sentences, looking for something that was not there anymore.
"Semantic," he said slowly. The word came out of the place where the Ledger's lists lived. Sensory. Semantic. Procedural. Episodic. The four floors of a burning house. "You burn what you know. Words. Facts. Names of things."
Oren looked at him across the room. For a long moment the scarred face was very still.
"To make a…" he said at last. He held up one hand, flat, palm out, the way you would hold up a hand to stop a door from swinging shut. "A… wall. Out of air. Nothing gets through. Not a sword. Not a spell. Nothing." He lowered the hand. "Costs words. Big fights, many words. Small fights, few." A pause. "I had a lot of words. Once."
"What did you lose today?"
Oren looked at the floor.
"The thing on the door," he said. "And… the cold thing. On the water. Last week." He was quiet. Then he said, very low, almost to himself: "I keep a list. In my head. Like you keep yours in the book. The ones that are gone." He tapped his temple with one thick finger. "Lock. Ice. Fourteen. The name of the color of my lady's mother's eyes. The word for the long boat with the many oars. The words to the song my mother…" He stopped. "It gets long, the list."
Nobody said anything.
Lyra had opened her eyes. She was looking at the old soldier by the window with an expression that Corin had seen on Mrs. Marta's face that morning, in the dark onion-smelling passage, when she had said she's fourteen, and she thinks she can't fall.
Corin took out the new Ledger again. He turned to the back. And under Bertram, he wrote: Captain Oren Vance. Thorne guard, thirty years. Burns words to make walls. Fights for money at the river. Doesn't lose. Lost "lock" today. "Ice" last week. Keeps a list in his head of the words that are gone. And then, after a moment, because it seemed like the kind of thing that ought to be written down by someone: Lock. Ice.
He looked up. Oren was watching him write.
"What are you…" The big man frowned. "What's that. In the book."
"Your two words," said Corin. "The ones from this week. So somebody has them."
Oren stared at him. For a long time he did not move at all. His crooked mouth did not move. His arms stayed folded. His eyes did not leave Corin's face.
Then, very slowly, he unfolded his arms.
And behind the white door, someone began to hum.
It was a thin, high, wandering sound, an old man's voice, gentle and tuneless and pleased with itself, like a child humming over its supper. It was not a song. It did not go anywhere. It just went on, round and round, soft and contented.
Lyra stood up.
"He's awake," she said. Her voice had changed. It had gone careful and light and bright, the voice you use in a sickroom. She smoothed down the front of her gray dress with both hands, quickly, the way you might before you walk into a ballroom. "Come on, then. Come and meet my father."
Oren pushed himself off the wall. He went to the white door before either of them could, and put his huge hand on the brass handle, and looked back at Corin.
"The whole time," he said.
"The whole time," said Corin.
Oren opened the door.
