Chapter 6: The House That Forgot Itself, Part 4 of 5: The Portrait Turned to the Wall
The Portrait Turned to the Wall
They left Lord Thorne with his pie.
Mrs. Hobb had cut it into squares so small that he could eat them with his fingers, and she sat beside him on the end of the bed and handed them to him one at a time, and he ate each one slowly and politely and said thank you after each, as if each one were a gift. She talked to him the whole time. About the cookshop, and the dogs and the tarts, and Mr. Pruett's knee. He nodded and smiled at the swallows.
Lyra stood in the doorway and watched them for a moment. Then she turned away and walked straight across the long room, past the table and the silver candlestick and the red rug, to the other door, the one at the far end, and opened it, and went in, and left it open behind her.
Corin looked at Oren.
The big man had come out of the little white room after them. He stood by the stove now, with his arms folded again, looking at the open door at the far end of the room. His face did not tell Corin anything at all. But after a moment he jerked his chin at the door. Just once. Go.
Corin went.
It was a small room. Smaller than her father's. It had one window, a narrow one, looking out over the roofs of Linden Walk and the tops of the yellowing lime trees, and the light that came through it was thinner and grayer than the gold light in the other room. There was a narrow bed, neatly made, with a plain gray blanket. There was a chair. There was a row of hooks on the wall with a few dresses hanging from them, all of them plain, all of them gray or brown or dark blue, all of them mended at the cuffs. There was a pair of riding boots, very old and very well polished, standing side by side under the hooks. There was a small washstand, like her father's, but with no soap on it at all. Only a jug and a bowl and a folded cloth.
And on the floor, at the foot of the bed, there was a wooden box.
It was beautiful. Rosewood, dark and red and glowing, with brass corners and a brass handle on top and a little brass keyhole in the front. Its lid was inlaid with a pattern of tiny pale flowers made of mother-of-pearl, so fine that they seemed to float on the dark wood. It was the most beautiful thing in the room. More beautiful even than the silver candlestick. And it was pushed right up against the wall, at the foot of the bed, half under the blanket where it hung down, as if someone had put it there so that they would not have to look at it.
Lyra was standing at the window with her back to him.
"That was my mother's sewing box," she said, without turning round. "I kept it. When everything else was sold. Bertram wanted to sell it. He said it was worth more than the candlestick. I wouldn't let him." A pause. "I used to sit by the window in the evenings and sew. Mend things. Embroider. It was the only thing I still had that was hers. The needles. The silks. The little silver thimble she wore on her middle finger."
Corin did not say anything.
"I can't open it now," said Lyra. "Not that I can't open the lid. I can. But I opened it once, the day after the Inkwell, and looked at all the needles and the threads in their little rows, and I didn't know what any of them were for." Her voice was very flat. "I knew they were needles. I knew they were silks. I just didn't know what you did with them. It was like looking at the tools in a surgeon's case. Pretty and sharp and nothing to do with me." She shrugged, very slightly. "So I closed it, and pushed it under the bed."
She turned round.
She was not crying. Her face was quite dry. But it looked older than it had at noon. Older than it had in the gold light of her father's room. Tired all the way down.
"And that," she said, "is why I don't look at that."
She nodded at the wall beside the door. At the wall Corin had his back to.
He turned.
There was a painting leaning against the wall there. A big one, in a heavy gilded frame, chipped at the corners. It was taller than his chest. It must have been very heavy. Somebody had carried it up the worn stone stairs from the yard and in through the green door and across the long room, and set it down here, against the wall of this narrow little room. And then they had turned it round.
Its face was to the wall. All Corin could see was the back of the canvas, gray and dusty, stretched over its wooden frame, with a few words chalked on the wood in a careless hand. Lot 41. Portrait, Lady T. Withdrawn.
"Bertram put it in the sale," said Lyra. "With everything else. I took it out again. I paid the auctioneer two silver not to sell it. It was the last two silver I had that month." She folded her arms. "And then I carried it up here myself, one step at a time, and I put it there. And I turned it round. And I haven't turned it back since the Inkwell."
"Because of what you burned," said Corin slowly.
She looked at him.
"You remember?"
"No." He touched his coat. "But I wrote it down. In the old Ledger. At dawn, on the river steps, after the fight. You told me what you'd burned. And I wrote it down for you, so it would be somewhere."
Lyra's arms tightened across her chest.
"Then you know," she said. "I burned the embroidery. Every stitch. And when it went, it took her with it. The room at the top of the house. The gold light. Her hands over mine. Her face." She swallowed. "I can't remember her face, Corin. I can't remember my own mother's face. I can remember that she had one. I can remember people telling me I have her chin. But when I try to see it, there's nothing. Just a sort of… smudge. Like a window with rain on it." She looked at the back of the painting. "And she's in there. Her face. Painted. Exactly. By the best painter in Veridia, the year I was born. And if I turn it round, I'll see her. And I won't know her. She'll be a stranger in a blue dress. A stranger with my chin."
She stopped.
"I'd rather not see her at all," she said, "than see her and not know her."
Corin stood for a long time looking at the dusty back of the canvas.
Then he took out the old Ledger. The full one. The soft worn one with no room left. He turned the pages carefully, back past the pigeon loft and the roofs and Room 9 and Vespera's chair, back past PARTNER and the mess of arrows, back to a page near the end where the writing was shaky and fast and spattered with something that might have been river water.
He found it.
"Can I read it to you?" he said.
Lyra did not answer. But she did not say no.
So he read it. Slowly. In a low voice, in the narrow gray room, with the sound of Mrs. Hobb's chatter coming faintly through the open door from the other end of the long room.
"Lyra Thorne," he read. "Her mother taught her to embroider, at six, in a room at the top of the house, over the rose garden. Gold light. A blue dress. In, through, pull. Every great thing is only patience. Fold it small: when something is too big to carry, fold it small and put it somewhere safe and carry it anyway. Lyra burned it tonight in the Inkwell to save my life. Remember it for her."
He stopped.
The room was very quiet.
Lyra had not moved. She was standing by the window with her arms still folded tight across her chest. But her eyes had gone very bright. And her mouth was pressed together hard, the way a child's mouth goes when it is trying not to cry in front of grown-ups.
"To save my life," she said. Her voice was barely there. "You wrote that."
"I must have."
"I don't remember you writing it."
"No. But I did."
Lyra looked at the back of the painting. For a long, long time.
Then she unfolded her arms. She crossed the narrow room in three steps. She put both hands on the gilded frame, and lifted, and turned it.
It was heavy. He saw her strain. He did not help her. He had the feeling that this was something she had to do on her own, the same way she had carried it up the stairs on her own, one step at a time. The heavy frame scraped on the floorboards. The dusty canvas swung round, and the gray light from the narrow window fell across it.
And there was a woman.
She was sitting in a chair by a window. A tall window, with a garden behind it, full of roses, pink and red and white, painted so finely that you could almost smell them. The light in the painting was gold. Late afternoon light, slanting in across a wooden floor. The woman was wearing a blue dress. A deep clear blue, like a summer sky an hour before dark, with a high lace collar. She had a wooden embroidery hoop in her lap, and a needle in her hand, lifted, with a long thread of crimson silk hanging from it, caught in the moment between one stitch and the next.
Her hair was dark. It was pinned up at the back of her head.
And one piece of it had come down. By her left ear. A single dark curl, lying against her pale neck.
Her face was turned a little toward the window. Toward the roses. She was not quite smiling. She looked as if she had just thought of something that was going to make her smile, in a moment, when she got to the end of the stitch. Her eyes were gray. A dark clear gray, like rain on slate. And she had a small, firm, stubborn chin.
Lyra stood in front of the painting with her hands still on the frame.
She did not say anything for a very long time.
Corin did not either. He stood back by the door, and watched her look, and did not move. He watched her eyes go over the painting. Slowly. The blue dress. The hoop. The crimson thread. The dark hair. The one loose curl. The chin. The gray eyes. Back to the curl. Back to the hands.
"In, through, pull," said Lyra.
She said it so softly that he only just heard.
"I don't know her," she said. "I don't know her face. You were right. I was right. It's a stranger." She swallowed. Her voice shook. "But look. Corin. Look at the hoop."
He came closer and looked.
The painter had been very careful. On the white linen stretched tight inside the wooden hoop in the woman's lap, there was embroidery. Half finished. A line of small neat letters in crimson silk, so tiny that you had to stand close to read them, and the last letter only begun, with the needle lifted above it and the long red thread hanging down, caught between one stitch and the next.

Lyra had put her hand inside the collar of her gray dress. She brought out a small square of white linen, folded, worn soft. She opened it. In one corner, in tiny stitches the color of roses, there were three words.
Fold it small.
"I made this," she said. "The last one I didn't sell. I don't remember making it. I can't even tell how I did it." She held the handkerchief up beside the painted hoop. The same words. The same small crimson letters. "But I know these. I've carried them every day. And she was sewing them." Her fingers stopped a breath away from the canvas, beside the painted needle. She did not touch it. "She's a stranger," she said. "But she's a stranger I know one true thing about. And that's…"
She stopped.
"That's more than nothing," said Corin quietly.
"Yes," said Lyra. "That's more than nothing."
She did cry then. Not much. Not loudly. Two tears, one from each eye, that ran down her cheeks and dripped off her stubborn chin onto the gray wool of her dress. She did not wipe them away. She did not seem to notice them. She just stood there with her hand held out a breath from her mother's painted needle, and let them fall.
Corin looked away, to give her a moment. He looked at the rest of the painting instead.
And saw the brooch.
It was small. You would not have noticed it unless you were the kind of person who counted things. It was pinned to the high lace collar of the blue dress, just at the hollow of the throat. Silver. Painted with tiny white touches of light on its edges, so that it seemed to shine. And it was a strange shape for a brooch. Not a flower. Not a bird. Not a jewel.
A mask.
A little silver half-mask, the kind people wear at a masked ball. It covered the eyes and the nose of a face that was not there, and it had two small empty holes where the eyes would be. The painter had put one tiny dot of blue in each hole, so that the mask seemed to be looking out of the painting. Watching.
"Lyra," he said. "The brooch."
She blinked. She looked down at the collar of the painted dress, at the little silver mask, as if she had never seen it before. And then her face changed.
"Oh," she said slowly. "Oh. I'd forgotten. Not burned. Just forgotten." She wiped her face at last, with the back of her wrist. "She always wore it. Always. Every day of her life. She said it came from the place where she was born. She said every girl there was given one, on the day she was named."
"Where was she born?"
Lyra opened her mouth to answer. And stopped.
Corin watched her reach for it. The way she had reached for Nurse's name at Vespera's. The little flicker behind the eyes. The hand going out to the shelf where something ought to be.
"She never said," said Lyra. Very slowly. As if she were only now, for the first time in her life, noticing it. "She never once said. Not the name of the town. Not the country. Nothing. When I asked, she'd laugh and say somewhere with a great deal of water and change the subject." Her eyes came up to his. "I spoke her language, Corin. The second language. The one I burned. I learned it from her when I was small, and I spoke it with her in the garden, so that the servants couldn't understand us, and we laughed about it. And I never once asked her what country it came from."
"Where is she now?"
The question was out before he could stop it.
Lyra looked at the painting. At the woman in the blue dress, caught forever between one stitch and the next.
"She went away," she said. "When I was twelve. Rowan was eight. She had a weakness in her chest, the doctors said, and she went to take the sea air, at a place on the coast. For the winter. She kissed us both in the hall, and Father put her in the carriage himself, and she waved from the window." A pause. "And in the spring a letter came to say she'd died. A fever. And she'd been buried there. By the sea." Lyra's voice was very even. "Father went. Alone. He was gone a month. When he came back, he never said her name again. Not once. Not in eight years."
"Did you ever see her grave?"
"No," said Lyra. And then, after a long moment, in a different voice: "No. I never did."
Corin took out the new Ledger. He wrote, small, at the back:
Lady Thorne. Portrait. Blue dress, crimson thread, one curl by the left ear. Brooch: a little silver half-mask. Every girl gets one where she was born, "on the day she was named." Lyra doesn't know where. "Somewhere with a great deal of water." Went to the coast for her chest when Lyra was 12. Died there, they said. Buried there. Lyra never saw the grave. Lord Thorne never said her name again.
He looked up from the page. Lyra was still standing in front of the painting. But she was not looking at the brooch anymore. She was looking at the face. At the gray eyes, and the chin, and the curl.
She did not turn the painting back round to the wall.
She left it facing the room.
What the House Remembers
When they came back into the long room, Oren was sitting at the table.
Corin had not seen him sit before. He did it badly, the way very big men often do, as if chairs had been made for somebody else and he was only borrowing one. He sat on the edge of it, with his knees wide and his elbows on the table and his huge scarred hands folded in front of him, and he was looking at the silver candlestick. Just looking at it. At the roses and the thorns twisting round its stem.
He did not look up when they came in. But he said, in his slow deep stone-in-a-river voice: "Sit. Both of you. I have a thing to say."
Lyra looked at him in surprise. Then she sat. Corin sat beside her.
From the little white room at the other end, they could hear Mrs. Hobb, still talking, and Lord Thorne, every now and then, saying thank you in his beautiful empty voice.
Oren was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Corin began to wonder if he had lost the thing he wanted to say, the way he had lost lock and ice. Then the big man spoke.
"The tapping," he said. "On the wall. When you… did it back." He looked at Corin. "And my lord stopped. And looked."
"Yes."
"I've seen him look like that one time before." Oren's thick fingers tightened on each other. "Two years ago. The night before. The last night."
Lyra went very still beside Corin.
"You never told me," she said.
"No, my lady."
"Why not?"
Oren looked at her. His crooked mouth worked.
"Because I… lost it," he said at last. "Not burned. Lost. Pushed it down. Like a…" He made a flat pushing shape with one hand, down, against the table. "Like a man pushes a bad thing down in the water and holds it there so he doesn't have to see. For two years. And today, the tapping. And it came up." He breathed out. "So I'll say it now. Before it goes down again. Or before I… burn it."
He looked at the candlestick again. It seemed easier for him to talk to the candlestick.
"I was on the night watch," he said. "At the old house. Same as always. I did the doors at midnight. All of them. Front, back, the… the garden one. The one with the glass."
"The conservatory," said Lyra softly.
"That." Oren nodded, grateful. "And when I came round to the library, my lord was there. In the dark. No candle. Standing at the window. Looking out at the garden." He paused. "I said, my lord, you'll catch cold. And he turned round. And he looked at me like that. Like he looked at Mister Vane's finger on the wall. Like there was somebody very far down inside him, at the bottom of a… a hole. A deep hole, with water in it."
"A well," said Corin.
"Well." Oren took the word and held it. "Yes. Like that. And he said, Oren. Come here." The big man's voice changed, very slightly. It became more careful. As if he were saying words that were not his, and did not want to drop any. "So I went. And he put his hand on my shoulder. Here." He touched his own left shoulder with his right hand. "He'd never done that. Not in thirty years. And he said: Oren, I am going to do something tonight that I cannot undo. And tomorrow I will not know you. I will not know any of you. I am sorry for that. More sorry than I can say."
Lyra had put her hand over her mouth again.
"I said, my lord, what thing?" Oren went on. "He didn't tell me. He said, Keep them safe. Lyra and Rowan. Whatever it costs. And if anyone in white ever comes to this house, Oren, don't let them near my daughter. Not for one moment. Not for anything."
The room was very quiet.
"And then he said one more thing," said Oren. "He said, And when she's old enough, and if she ever asks, tell her to listen to the walls."
Nobody moved.
"I didn't know what it meant," said Oren. "I thought he was… tired. Strange. Talking like a man in a fever. I said, yes, my lord. He took his hand off my shoulder. He went up the stairs to his study. And I never saw him again." A pause. "Not him. Not the one who knew me."
Lyra took her hand away from her mouth.
"Listen to the walls," she said.
"Yes, my lady."
"And he's been tapping on the wall for two years." Her voice was shaking. "Every day. And I painted over it. Three times."
Oren looked at the table.
"I should have told you," he said. "The first day. When the tapping started. I should have… put it together. I didn't. I pushed it down in the water." His jaw worked. "I'm a stupid man, my lady. I'm getting more stupid every year. Every fight. I can't keep things the way I used to."
"You're not stupid," said Lyra fiercely.
"I can't remember the word for the thing on the door."
"That isn't stupid. That's burned. It's different." She reached across the table and put her small hand on top of his huge folded ones. He did not move. "You kept us. All of us. When everybody else went. You kept us, Oren."
Oren looked at her hand on his.
"Not all of it," he said quietly. "There's more."
Lyra's hand tightened.
"In the morning," said Oren. "After. Before it was light. Bertram came and found me at the gate. He said my lord was taken ill in the night. He said I must ride to the other side of the city and fetch Doctor Aske, my lord's own doctor, at once, and nobody else would do, and I must not stop for anything." His face had gone hard. "So I rode. Through the rain. All the way to the river, to the doctor's house. And the doctor wasn't there. His girl said he'd gone to the country a week since. To his sister."
Corin's pencil had stopped over the Ledger.
"So I rode back," said Oren. "Fast as I could. It took two hours, there and back, in the rain. And when I came up the drive, there was a carriage in front of the house. Black. With the eye on the door. Just leaving. And I saw them get in. Two of them. One in gray." He looked at Lyra. "And one in white."
"I saw them too," said Lyra. Her voice had gone very small. "From my window."
"I know, my lady. I saw you at the window." Oren nodded slowly. "The white one. When she got into the carriage. She stopped, with one foot on the step. And she turned. And she looked up. At your window. At you."
Lyra's face went white.
"For a long time," said Oren. "A long time. Then she got in. And they drove away. Past me. Close. So close I could have reached out and… and opened the door." His huge hands had come apart under Lyra's. They had closed into fists on the table. "I didn't. I didn't know. I didn't put it together. Anyone in white, Oren. I didn't remember it till they were gone."
"It wasn't your fault."
"It was my job." Oren's voice was flat. "And when the carriage went past me, the smell came out of the window. Thick. Sweet. Like a…" He stopped. His eyes went to the side. To the empty shelf. He searched it. He searched it for a long time. "Like the white flowers. The big ones. With the gold in the middle. The ones you put by the dead. In the chapel. The ones…" His fists tightened. His voice cracked, just a little. "I can't. I can't say it. I had it. I had it this morning."
"Lilies," said Corin gently.
Oren closed his eyes.
"Lilies," he said. "Yes." He opened them again. "I burned it. Just now. When I was standing in the door and you were in there with the rod, and I thought you might… hurt him. I was ready. I made a wall, ready, inside, not out. Just in case. And to make it, I needed a word. And it took lilies." He laughed. A short, rough, terrible sound. "Of all the words. It took that one."
Nobody said anything for a long time.
Corin wrote it all down. Slowly. In the new Ledger, at the back, on the third page now. He wrote it the way Oren had said it, as near as he could, with the gaps left in.

Two sisters in the country.
He turned the Ledger round and showed it to Lyra.
She read it. She read the last line twice. Then she sat back in her chair and looked at the ceiling for a while, at the old dark beams, with her hands lying open on the table.
"Bertram," she said. "Bertram taught me to ride a pony. Bertram used to put sugar mice in my shoes at midwinter. Bertram cried when he left." Her voice was very steady. "I can't believe it."
"I'm not saying it's true," said Corin. "I'm saying it's written down."
"That's what frightens me," said Lyra.
Oren had not looked up from the table. His fists were still closed. When he spoke again, his voice had gone very low.
"Mister Vane."
"Yes."
"In your book. The word. The white flowers." A pause. "Write it big. Somewhere I'll see it. When I come back to it, I'll want to know what it was."
Corin turned to a clean page. Not at the back. Near the front, after the pages he would copy over tonight, where there was space. He wrote it big, in capitals, in the middle of the page, so that it filled the line from edge to edge.
LILIES.
And under it, smaller: Oren's word. He gave it to keep Lord Thorne safe. It's the smell of the woman in white.
He turned the book round on the table and pushed it across to the big man.
Oren looked at it for a long time. His lips moved. He did not say it aloud. But Corin saw his mouth make the shape of it, once, slowly, the way a child shapes a word it is learning.
Then Oren unfolded his fists and laid one huge hand flat on the open page, very gently, as if the paper were warm.
"Thank you," he said.
