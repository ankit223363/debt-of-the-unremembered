Chapter 6: The House That Forgot Itself, Part 3 of 5: Too Clean
Too Clean
Lyra stood up at last.
She did it slowly, one hand on the arm of her father's chair, the way an old woman gets up. Her knees had left two pale marks in the dust of the sunlight on the floor. She did not look at Corin. She looked at her father, smiling at the swallows, and then she turned and went to the washstand and poured a little water from the white jug into the white bowl and dipped her fingers in it and pressed them to her eyes. She stood like that for a moment with her back to the room.
When she turned round, her face was calm again. Pale, but calm.
"You said," she said, "at Vespera's. That if there was anything left in him, any ash, any little bit of what he burned, you'd be able to find it. With your rod."
"I said I might."
"Then look." Her voice was steady. "Please. Now. While I'm brave enough to let you."
Corin looked at Oren.
The big man had not moved from the door. His scarred face was still gray. He looked at Corin, and then at the long narrow pocket on the right of Corin's coat, where the cold silver lay, and then at his lord in the chair. For a long moment he did not do anything.
Then, very slightly, he nodded. Once.
Corin drew out the rod.
It was cold in his hand. Colder than it had been all day. He held it loosely, the way his hands seemed to want to, the silver lying along the inside of his forearm with the end of it resting just past his fingertips. He did not know what to do with it. And then, the way he had known how to climb the slates and catch the gutter and burn the bosun's boy, he did.
He went and crouched down beside Lord Thorne's chair, on the other side from where Lyra had knelt. Not too close. An arm's length. He held the rod out, low, level, so that its tip hung in the air a hand's breadth from the old man's sleeve, from the faded blue silk over his thin arm.
Lord Thorne looked down at it with polite interest.
"What a pretty thing," he said. "Is it a flute?"
"Something like that, my lord."
"I used to play the flute," said Lord Thorne happily. "I think." He looked back at the swallows.
Malakor, thought Corin.
I'm here. The dry voice was very quiet. Very careful. I've been here the whole time. Hold it still, boy. Don't push. Just let it sit, and let it listen.
Corin held it still.
And the rod listened.
He felt it. Not with his hand. Through his hand. Up his arm, into his chest, behind his eyes. The way he had felt the crowded whispering room inside the rod on the roof above the Cut, all those voices, all those fragments. But this time the rod was not listening to itself. It was listening outward. Reaching. Like a hand reaching into a dark cupboard, feeling along the shelves, for anything at all.
It reached into Lord Aurelian Thorne.
And found nothing.
Corin had known it would be empty. A man who has burned everything is empty. That was what a Slate was. But he had thought, without knowing he thought it, that empty would feel like an empty room. Dusty. Dim. With marks on the floor where furniture had been, and pale squares on the walls where pictures had hung. With a little ash in the grate.
This was not like that.
This was like a room that had been scrubbed.
The floor scrubbed with sand till the grain of the boards stood up. The walls washed and washed again. The grate swept so clean that the black iron shone. Not a speck of dust. Not a mark. Not a thread caught on a nail. Nothing. Clean as a plate that has been licked and then washed and then dried and put away on a shelf.
Oh, said Malakor, very softly.
It was not a word Corin had heard the old voice use before. It did not sound clever or sour or bored. It sounded like a man who has opened a door and found something on the other side that he very much did not want to find.
What is it?
Feel it, boy. Feel how clean. A pause. I've been in a thousand heads. I've been in men who burned themselves to the bone. I've been in a woman who spent every single thing she had, to the last scrap, to hold a bridge for an hour while her children got across. You know what they all had? Every one?
What?
Ash. The voice was flat and quiet. A burned man is a fireplace after the fire. Black. Gray. Cold. But there's always ash. Always. A film of it, on the bottom of the grate. Bits that didn't catch. Little curls of what used to be. The smell of smoke. You can't burn a life without leaving ash, any more than you can burn a log without it. That's what we harvest. That's what's in the rod. That's what you are. Another pause. This man has none.
Corin did not move.
None?
Not a grain. Not a curl. Not a smell. The rod was very cold in his hand now. Cold enough to ache. He burned himself, maybe. Your Lyra says so, and I'll believe her. But after he burned, somebody came. Somebody came and knelt by the grate with a little brush and a little pan and swept it out. Every grain. Every speck. And took it all away.
Corin's hand had begun to shake.
Who would do that?
Somebody who didn't want anybody else to find what he'd left behind. The dry voice went lower still. Somebody who knew that a man like you, with a rod like me, could kneel by that grate one day and sift through the ash, and find a word. A name. A page. The edge of what he burned. So they took it. All of it. A pause. That's what a silver cup is for, boy. Not just for drinking a man alive. For sweeping up after.
Corin saw, as clearly as if it were in front of him, a page in the old Ledger. A dark lane. A tall woman in a white shell mask. A silver cup with a spiral inside it. And Pell Dorran, going into it, one thing after another, until there was nothing left but a song.
Is there anything? Anything at all?
The rod reached again. Deeper. He felt it go down, past the clean scrubbed floor, into the dark beneath, the cellar under the room, the place behind the walls.
And stop.
Wait, said Malakor.
There was something there.
It was very small. It was very deep. So deep that the rod could only just touch the edge of it, the way your fingertips can only just touch something that has rolled to the very back of a high shelf. It was hard. It was tight. It was not ash, not loose, not soft, not drifting. It was folded. Folded over and over and over on itself, small and smaller, like a letter folded into a tiny square and then pressed into a crack in a stone wall so hard that you would need a knife to get it out.
And it was locked.
Corin did not know how he knew that. But he knew it. When the rod touched the fold, the fold did not open. It did not give. It sat there in the dark, small and hard and tight, and it pushed back against the rod, very gently, the way a door pushes back when it is bolted from the other side.
Well, well, said Malakor. His voice had changed again. It had something in it now that Corin had not heard there all day. It sounded almost like respect. The clever old devil. He folded it.
Folded what?
Whatever he wanted to keep. Before he burned everything else. He took one thing, the one thing, and folded it up small and tight and hid it down at the very bottom, under the floor, where the fire couldn't reach it and the sweeper's little brush couldn't find it. A dry sound, like a laugh. And they missed it. Whoever swept him out. They took every grain of ash, and they never knew there was a cellar.
Can you open it?
No. Flat. Certain. It's not mine to open. It's locked from the inside, boy. It's locked with him. With what he is. His blood. His name. His family. A pause. A thing like that opens for one kind of key only. And I'm not it, and you're not it.
Corin did not need to ask what kind of key. He had read it in the Ledger that morning. In his own copy of words written in sleeping ink, words that a dying stone had shown him for a moment and then taken away. I have made one copy, and hidden it where only our blood can open it.
He drew the rod back.
He did it slowly. Gently. The way you would draw your hand back from a sleeping animal. And the cold went out of the silver, a little at a time, until it was only the ordinary cold it always was.
He was still crouching by the chair. His legs had gone stiff. His hand was aching. When he stood up, the room swung a little, and Oren's huge hand came out from nowhere and closed round his arm above the elbow and held him up, without a word, until it stopped swinging.
Lord Thorne had not noticed anything at all. He was humming to the swallows.
Lyra was looking at Corin's face.
"Tell me," she said.
So he told her. In the small white room, in a low voice, with Oren's hand still on his arm. About the scrubbed room. The swept grate. Not a grain of ash. And then about the fold, at the very bottom, small and hard and tight and locked, that the sweeper had never found.
He did not soften it. He did not know how. He told it the way Malakor had told it to him.
Lyra listened without moving. When he got to the part about the brush and the pan, her hands closed at her sides. When he got to the fold, they opened again.
"Someone took it," she said. "After."
"Yes."
"After he burned. Someone came and took what was left." She was speaking very slowly, as if she were reading the words off a page that was a long way away. "So nobody could ever find it. Not even a harvester."
"Yes."
"When?"
Corin looked at her.
"It would have to be soon," he said. "Very soon after. Ash doesn't last. It drifts. It fades. In a day, two days, a lot of it would have gone on its own. If someone wanted all of it, every grain, they'd have to come at once. The same night. The next morning at the latest."
Lyra's face did not change. But something behind it went very still.
"The next morning," she said. "They found him in the morning. In his study, at the old house. In his chair by the cold fire with his hands in his lap. One of the maids found him. She screamed. I heard her from my room." She swallowed. "I came running. Rowan came running. And we stood in the doorway and he looked at us and said good morning, and smiled, and I knew. I knew at once." She shut her eyes. "And then Bertram came, and sent us out. Both of us. Sent us back to our rooms and locked the study door. And he said the Ministry had to be told. That it was the law. When a Councillor burns, the Ministry has to be told at once."
"And they came."
"Within the hour." Her eyes opened. "I saw them from my window. A carriage. Black. With the eye on the door. Two people got out. I couldn't see their faces, it was raining and they had their hoods up. They went into the study with Bertram, and they were in there a long time. And when they came out, one of them was carrying something. A little case. Like a doctor's bag." She stopped. "I thought they were doctors. Bertram said they were doctors. Ministry doctors. Come to see if anything could be done."
Oren's hand had tightened on Corin's arm. It hurt. Corin did not say anything.
"What were they wearing?" he asked. "Under the hoods. Could you see?"
Lyra thought. He watched her think. Her eyes went away, to the side, the way Oren's did when he looked for a word. But Lyra's eyes found what they were looking for.
"One was in gray," she said slowly. "Like yours. Long gray coat. A man, I think. Tall." A pause. "The other one I only saw for a moment. Getting back into the carriage. The hood slipped. The wind caught it."
"And?"
"White," said Lyra. "Her coat was white."
Nobody spoke.
In the chair by the window, Lord Aurelian Thorne smiled at the swallows and hummed. His hands lay in his lap. And on the left wrist, below the heel of the hand, four small faded letters lay in the gold sunlight, where they had lain for twenty years.
FOLD.
So I know to look for it.
Corin took out the new Ledger and wrote, while his hand still shook:
No ash in him. None. Swept out. Malakor says someone came after he burned and took every grain. Silver cup? But one thing was folded down deep, under everything, and they missed it. Locked. Only his blood can open it. — The morning after: Bertram called the Ministry. Black carriage. Two came. One in gray. One in white. A little case like a doctor's bag. Bertram said they were doctors.
He drew a square round Bertram again. Three times now.
Then, under everything, he wrote a question. He did not know why it came to him. But it did, and once it had come, he could not make it go away.
Old Terrence. Is he swept too?
The Nurse
She arrived the way weather arrives.
First there was a voice in the yard below, high and cheerful and out of breath, saying good afternoon, Mr. Pruett, lovely day for it, mind the trough, Patience my girl, how's your poor old back. Then a slow heavy tread on the stone stairs, with a pause and a grunt on every step, and a loud oops at the fourth one. Then the green door in the long room, opening. Then the smell, before anything else: onion gravy and hot pastry and boiled cabbage and something sweet, like stewed apples, filling the whole of the long low room in one great warm wave.
And then the nurse herself, in the doorway of the little white room, round as a cottage loaf and red in the face, with a basket on her arm and her bonnet pushed back and her hand pressed to her chest.
"Oh!" she said. "Oh, my goodness. Company."
She was perhaps fifty-five. She was not tall, but she was very wide, in a brown wool dress with a white apron over it and a white cap under her bonnet. Her face was broad and pink and shiny, with small bright blue eyes and three chins and a smile that seemed to go almost all the way round her head. She looked at Lyra, and at Oren, and at Corin, and at Corin's gray coat, and her smile wobbled a little at the coat, but it held.
"Miss Lyra," she said. "You never said we was having company. I'd have brought up a bit more of the pie." She patted the basket. "There's only the one slice of the pork and the one of the apple, and they're both for his lordship."
"It's all right, Mrs. Hobb," said Lyra. "Mister Vane isn't staying for dinner. He's a friend. He came to sit with Father for a little."
"Well, isn't that kind." Mrs. Hobb beamed at Corin. Her eyes went to the coat again, and then away, quickly, as if it were something not quite polite to look at. "He does like a visitor, his lordship. He doesn't say so. But you can tell." She bustled past them into the room, and the whole floor shook a little under her. "And how's my lamb, then? Did you have a nice sleep? Did you see your birds?"
Lord Thorne turned his head from the window and smiled at her. It was the same smile he had given Lyra. Exactly the same. Pleasant and polite and empty.
"Good afternoon," he said. "Are you the new girl?"
"That's right, my lamb," said Mrs. Hobb comfortably. "I'm the new girl. I've been the new girl for two years." She winked at Corin over her shoulder. "He asks every day. I always say yes. It's easier."
She put her basket down on the end of the bed and began to unpack it. Two slices of pie, wrapped in cloths. A small jug with a lid. A clean napkin. She did it all quickly and neatly and with a great deal of humming, and while she did it she talked, not to anyone in particular, in a steady comfortable stream, the way some people let a kettle sing.
"The cookshop's ever so busy today. I had to wait for the pork. And Mrs. Lister's girl dropped a whole tray of tarts in the street, you never saw such a thing, the dogs had them before she could pick a single one up. And Mr. Pruett says it's going to rain by Friday, his knee's been telling him, and his knee's never wrong." She shook out the napkin and tucked it into the collar of Lord Thorne's nightshirt, gently, under his chin, the way you would for a child. "There. Now. Hands first, my lamb. Before your dinner. You know the rule."
She went to the washstand. She poured fresh water from the white jug into the white bowl. And she picked up the bar of soap from its little china dish.
Corin had noticed it when he came in. Pale and smooth and creamy, almost new. He had not thought about it.
Mrs. Hobb carried the bowl to the chair, and knelt down, with a great creaking of knees and a puff of breath, and took Lord Thorne's thin left hand in her big red one. She dipped it in the water. And she began to rub the soap over it, slowly, gently, between the long fingers, over the knuckles, round the wrist. Over the four small faded letters below the heel of the hand.
And the smell came.
It came up from the warm water, very soft at first, so that Corin could almost have thought he imagined it. Then stronger. Rising up over the onion gravy and the pastry and the stewed apples, slow and sweet and heavy, filling the small white room. A thick, waxy, white smell. Like a room with the windows shut and a great vase of flowers on the table and someone lying very still on a bed.
He knew it before he knew he knew it.
The skin on the back of his neck went cold. His right hand closed by itself on nothing.
Beside him, he felt Lyra go stiff.
She had smelled it too. Of course she had. She had smelled it every day for a year, perhaps, in this room, on her father's hands, on his pillow, on his collar, and never thought about it once. Why would she? It was only soap.
Corin looked at her. She was staring at the bar of soap in Mrs. Hobb's red hand, and her face was the color of the whitewash.
"Mrs. Hobb," said Corin.
His voice came out very calm. He did not know how.
"Yes, sir?" Mrs. Hobb looked up from where she knelt, beaming, with the soap in one hand and Lord Thorne's wet fingers in the other.
"That's a lovely soap."
"Isn't it, though?" She lifted it and sniffed it, delighted, and held it out a little toward him so that he could smell it too. He did not lean closer. He did not need to. "Ever so fancy. Not like the yellow bars you get at the chandler's. It comes from abroad, I shouldn't wonder. It lathers up lovely and it never cracks his poor skin, and it makes the whole room smell like a chapel on a feast day." She went back to washing. "His lordship likes it. Don't you, my lamb?"
"It's very nice," said Lord Thorne politely.
"Where does it come from?" said Corin.
"Why, from the friend, sir."
"The friend?"
"The friend of the family." Mrs. Hobb seemed surprised that he did not know. She looked up at Lyra. "Didn't you tell him, miss? About the friend? Every first of the month, regular as the rain. My wages, in an envelope. And a new bar of the soap, wrapped in paper. For his lordship's comfort, it says. Ever so kind."
Lyra did not move.
When she spoke, her voice was very quiet and very level. The voice of someone walking very slowly across a frozen pond and listening for the ice.
"Mrs. Hobb," she said. "Your wages come from the Council. The Benevolent Fund. For the families of Councillors who have… who are unwell. Bertram arranged it. Before he left."
"Oh, they did, miss. At first." Mrs. Hobb rinsed Lord Thorne's left hand and dried it carefully on the napkin, and picked up the right. "The first year. A man came from the Council every quarter with a paper for me to sign. But then it stopped. A year past, about. Last autumn." She frowned a little, remembering. "I was in a terrible fret. I thought, well, that's me out, then, and who's going to look after my lamb? And I went to Mr. Bertram's old address to ask, and the people there said he'd gone to the country and left no direction. And I didn't like to trouble you, miss, you had enough on your plate." She smiled. "But then, the very first of the next month, there it was. An envelope, waiting for me under the door at the cookshop when I came in for his breakfast. My wages, all correct, to the copper. And the soap. And the note. And it's come every month since."
"From whom?"
"It just says a friend, miss. Look. I've got this month's here. I keep them, in case." She dried Lord Thorne's right hand, and patted it, and put it back in his lap with the other. Then she reached into the deep pocket of her white apron and brought out a folded square of paper and held it up to Lyra. "See?"
Lyra did not take it.
Corin did.
It was an envelope. Thick, cream-colored paper, the heavy expensive kind with a soft rough edge, like the paper he had seen in the High Quarter shop windows on his way up the hill. It had been opened carefully along the top. There was no name on the front. No direction. No mark at all. On the back, where the flap folded down, there was a round seal of wax, broken now in two halves. The wax was white. Pure white, with no crest pressed into it. Just a plain round smooth white disk, like a coin made of milk.
Inside there was a single small card, the same cream color. On it, in a neat sloping hand, in violet ink, it said:
For his lordship's comfort.
From a friend.
And the envelope smelled.
Faintly. Very faintly. You would have had to hold it close to your face, the way Corin was holding it now. But it was there, soaked deep into the thick soft paper, sweet and heavy and white.
Lilies.
He lowered the envelope. He looked at Lyra.
She was looking at the soap. At the pale creamy bar in its little china dish, where Mrs. Hobb had put it down again on the washstand, still wet, still smelling. At her father's hands lying folded in his lap, clean and soft and pale and sweet-smelling, smelling of lilies.
For a year.
Every day.
For a year, someone had been washing her father's hands with the smell of the woman who had stood in Gutter Lane with a silver cup. Who had smelled of lilies in Pell's last minutes. Who had worn white at Oakhaven. And she had come up these stairs every evening and sat in this room and held his clean sweet hands, and never once noticed.
Lyra took one step toward the washstand.
Corin did not know what she was going to do. Throw the soap out of the window. Throw the bowl. Scream. He saw her hand come up, and her face, and he moved before he had decided to, and put himself between her and the washstand, and caught her wrist.
Not hard. Just enough.
She stopped. She looked at his hand on her wrist, and then up at his face. Her gray eyes were blazing.
"Don't," he said, very quietly. So quietly that Mrs. Hobb, humming over the pie, did not hear.
"Let go of me."
"If it's gone," said Corin, "she'll know."
Lyra went still.
"Whoever sends it," he said. "If the soap goes in the river. If Mrs. Hobb stops using it. If the envelopes stop being opened. She'll know we found her. She'll know we know." He let go of Lyra's wrist. "Right now, she doesn't. Right now she thinks you're still the girl who never noticed. That's the only thing we've got that she doesn't know about."
Lyra stared at him. Her breath was coming fast and short.
Then, slowly, her hand came down.
"Rule one," she said, through her teeth. "No lies."
"I'm not lying to you."
"No. You're asking me to lie to her." She turned her head and looked at the soap again, and her mouth twisted. "Every day. In my father's room. With my father's hands."
"Yes," said Corin. "I am."
They looked at each other for a long moment. Behind them, Oren had not moved. But Corin could feel him there, by the door, very still and very big, listening to every word.
"Mrs. Hobb," said Lyra at last. Her voice had gone light and bright again. The sickroom voice. "May Mister Vane keep the envelope, do you think? Just this one. He's a clerk, at the Ministry. He thinks he might be able to find out who the friend is, so that I can write and thank them properly."
"Oh, I'm sure that's no trouble, miss." Mrs. Hobb looked pleased. "It's only right to say thank you. I always said so. I said to Mr. Pruett, it's not right, a kindness like that and nobody to thank for it." She was cutting the pork pie into small neat squares on a plate. "You keep it, sir. I've got eleven more at home in my tea tin."
"Thank you," said Corin. He folded the envelope and put it inside the back cover of the new Ledger. "Mrs. Hobb. Did you ever see who leaves them? Under the cookshop door?"
Mrs. Hobb stopped cutting. She looked up at the ceiling and thought, with her knife held in the air and her tongue between her teeth.
"Once," she said slowly. "Just the once. In the spring. I came early, it was still dark, because his lordship had a cough and I wanted to get his broth on. And as I came round the corner there was somebody bending down at the cookshop door. Just a little shape. Little and thin. In a long dark cloak, with a hood." She shrugged. "I called out good morning, and they stood up and went off round the corner, quick as you like. Didn't say a word. And when I got to the door, there was the envelope." She went back to cutting. "I thought, well, that's a servant. That's the friend's servant. A great lady wouldn't come herself, would she, at that hour."
"No," said Corin. "I don't suppose she would."
He wrote it down. All of it. In the new Ledger, at the back, under everything else, with Mrs. Hobb humming and cutting pie, and Lord Thorne smiling at the swallows with his clean sweet hands in his lap, and the smell of lilies hanging in the small white room like smoke.
Mrs. Hobb. Nurse. Council stopped paying a year ago, last autumn. Since then: envelope every first of the month, under the cookshop door. Wages exact. A bar of lily soap. "For his lordship's comfort. From a friend." Cream paper. White wax seal, no crest. Violet ink. Smells of lilies. She has eleven more. Once, in spring: a little thin shape in a dark hooded cloak, before dawn.
And, under it, pressing so hard that the pencil nearly went through the new stiff paper:
She's been paying for him. For a year. Keeping him clean. Keeping him alive.
