Chapter 40: Drum Calls, Part 4 of 5: Witness, Hold, Fold, Home
Witness, Hold, Fold, Home
They did not move him for a long time.
They could not have, Oren thought. Not without hurting him. Moll lay on his belly on the top of the hay with his face over the hole and his thick hands gripping the stalks, and did not move, and did not speak again, and did not look away from the face below. His breath went out in white clouds into the gold light. Slow, and then quick, and then slow again.
Hessa knelt on the other side of the hole and waited. She did not ask anything. Oren had never seen her wait for anything before. She did it now like a woman sitting by a sickbed.
The stars came out. More and more of them, over the canal, over the black willows and the dark barn and the empty landing stage. Hard and white and very many, the way they are on the coldest nights. There was no moon. Down on the deck, Wat had lit the lantern on the stern pole, and its small yellow light lay on the frost round the tiller. Up at the top of the ramp, the red coal of Abner Cole's pipe glowed and faded.
The tapping went on below them. Four, two, nine, three. And the rest. And again.
At last Moll spoke.
"I need to sit up," he said. "Captain. Help me sit up. I can't say it lying down. Not looking at him."
Oren helped him.
They got him round, slowly, and sat him up on the top of the bales with his back to the hole and his bad leg out in front of him, and his stick across his knees. He sat with his head bowed and his big hands hanging between his knees, like a man in a tavern at the end of a long night. Oren sat down beside him on the frosty hay. Hessa came round and sat on his other side.
Three of them, in a row, on the top of a haystack, under the stars. With the gold light coming up out of the hole behind them.
"How much do you know," said Moll, without lifting his head, "about the drums?"
"Nothing," said Oren. "I knew them all. I burned them. Last month."
Moll turned his head and looked at him.
"All of them?"
"All of them."
"Saints," said Moll softly. "Saints, man. That's thirty calls. And the beats under them. And the speeds." He shook his heavy head. "You must have needed a big wall."
"I did."
Moll looked at him a moment longer. Then he looked away, out over the dark canal.
"Then I'll tell it plain," he said. "Like to a recruit. You'll forgive me."
"Tell it."
"The drum talks," said Moll. "You know that much. You've not burned that. A regiment on the march, or in the field, can't hear a man shout. Not over the noise. But they can hear the drum. So the drum talks for the colonel. Every call is a word. Or a few words. Halt. Advance. Make ready. Fall back. Form on the colors. And every call has a number to it, in the drill book. So a drummer can learn them by heart, and a sergeant can write them on a slate."
He lifted his thick right hand and laid it on his knee.
"Four," he said.
And he tapped. Not four plain taps, the way Lord Thorne did them on the floor-board. Four taps with a shape to them. Two quick, and a space, and two slow. Tap-tap. Tap. Tap. A rhythm. A word.
"That's the long way to do it. On a drum, with the roll under it. But that's four. That's Witness." He let his hand lie still. "You beat it when the whole regiment's to stand and see a thing done. When a man's to be flogged. Or hanged. Or when the colors are given into a new man's keeping. Or when the dead are buried. Witness. All eyes. Every man. You saw it. You'll remember it. You'll swear to it after."
Oren said nothing. Something in his chest had gone very tight.
"Two," said Moll. Tap. Tap. Two long slow beats. "Hold. Stand fast. Don't move. Don't break. Whatever comes at you. Hold."
"Nine." Tap-tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap. Three threes, quick. "That's the one they use in a rout. When the line's breaking. Fold. Close up. Close in. Fold the line in round the colors, round the wounded, round the guns, whatever it is you've got that you can't lose. Make yourself small and hard and close, like a fist round a coin, and don't let them get at it." He paused. "Fold it small, the old sergeants used to say. When they taught it. Fold it small, lads, and hold it close."
Hessa had gone very still beside him.
"And three." Tap. Tap-tap. "Home. March back to camp. It's over. Go home."
He stopped.
"Witness," said Moll. "Hold. Fold. Home."
Below them, through the hole, the old man's finger went on. Four. Two. Nine. Three. Oren heard it now with Moll's words lying over it, like a cloth laid over a table. Witness. Hold. Fold. Home. And it was terrible, how well they fitted. How they had been there all along, for two years, in the study at Crown Walk and the gold room in the hay, under the plain flat taps. Waiting for someone to lay the cloth over them.
"But it's not a call," said Moll.
Oren looked at him.
"Not in the book," said Moll. "Not like that. Not those four, together, in that order. There's no call in the drill book of the Commonwealth Foot that goes four, two, nine, three. I'd stake my pension on it. I was Foot for twelve years. I marched to every call there is. I know them the way I know my own name." He stopped. "And I only heard that one once."
"Where?"
Moll did not answer.
He sat on the hay with his big hands hanging between his knees and looked out at the dark canal. The stars were reflected in it, here and there, where the new ice was thinnest. Little white points of light, very still, in the black.
"On a march," he said at last. "In the north. In the autumn. A long time ago. I was a corporal. Twenty-four years old, with a good leg and a loud voice and no sense." His jaw moved. "Nearly twenty years. A valley. Up in the hills. Past where the roads stop being roads. I can't tell you its name. I don't know its name any more. I burned it."
"Why?"
"Because I dreamed about it. Every night. For ten years." Moll's voice was flat. "So I went to the Bank, one winter, with my last month's pay, and I sat on a glass in a little room, and a harvester in a gray coat took the name of the valley out of my head. And the road up to it. And the faces. And the dreams stopped." He was quiet for a moment. "Mostly."
He lifted his right hand and looked at it in the starlight. At the thick forefinger.
"But they didn't take this," he said. "They couldn't. It wasn't in my head. It was in my finger. And since the summer it's been coming back. At night. On the bench. Four, two, nine, three. And I didn't know why, Captain. I swear to you I didn't know. Not till I looked down that hole."
"What happened," said Hessa quietly, "in the valley?"
Moll shut his eyes.
"Wagons," he said.
He said it as if the word hurt his mouth.
"We came down out of the valley at night with a line of wagons. Farm wagons. Blue ones, painted, the way the hill farmers paint them. Six or seven. With canvas over the tops. I was on the last one, with my squad, walking behind. Guard. That's all we were told. Guard the wagons. Don't look in them. Don't talk to what's in them." His voice dropped. "What was in them was children."
Nobody moved on the top of the hay.
"I don't know how many. A lot. Small ones, mostly. You could hear them in there, under the canvas, in the dark. Crying. Some of them. Calling for their mothers. And the officers said, keep them quiet. And we didn't know how. We were soldiers. We didn't know anything about children."
He opened his eyes.
"And there was a drummer," he said. "On the last wagon. The one I was guarding. A boy. Sitting up on the tailboard, at the back, with his legs hanging down and his drum on its strap beside him. Not playing it. They'd told him not to play it. Too loud. So he had his two sticks in his hands, and he was tapping. On the edge of the tailboard. Soft. With the tips. To the children in the wagon behind him, under the canvas. Over and over. All night long. All the way down the valley road in the dark."
He laid his finger on his knee.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
"That," said Moll. "He tapped that. All night. And the children stopped crying." His voice cracked. "Not all at once. But one by one. They went quiet. As if they understood it. As if somebody had told them what it meant."
Witness. Hold. Fold. Home.
You saw it. Hold still. Make yourself small. You're going home.
Oren sat on the hay and felt the cold go into him. Not the night's cold. Another kind. From the inside.
"Were they?" he said. "Going home?"
Moll looked at him for a long time.
"I don't know," he said. "I never found out. Near dawn, at a ford, the last wagon went over in the mud. The near wheel went into a hole under the water, and the whole thing tipped, slow, like a tree going over. And I was beside it, pushing. And it came down on me." He touched his bad leg. "On this. I heard the bone go. And I heard the children in the canvas screaming. And then I didn't hear anything."
He was silent for a moment.
"When I woke up, I was in a cart going south, with three other men with broken bones, and a surgeon's mate giving us something bitter out of a bottle. And the wagons were gone. And nobody would say where. And when I asked, a captain came and stood by the cart and said, Corporal, there were no wagons. You were hurt in a fall on the march. You'll go home with a good paper and a bit of silver, and you'll remember that." Moll's mouth twisted. "And I did. For twenty years. I remembered it the way they told me. Till my finger started tapping."
Below them, through the square hole in the hay, Lord Thorne's finger stopped.
The Answer Call
All three of them heard it stop.
It was strange, Oren thought afterwards, how loud a thing could be when it stopped. For two years the tapping had been the sound under every other sound in his life. Like the sea to a man who lives by the sea. He had stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heart. And now it was not there, and the silence where it had been was so big and so sudden that it rang.
They turned round on the hay, all three of them, and looked down through the hole.
Lord Thorne had not moved. He lay on his back on the mattress, under the blankets, with his head on Hessa's rolled skirt, and his face turned up toward the square of stars. The candle lit one side of it gold.
But his eyes had moved.
They were not looking at the sky any more. They were looking at the edge of the hole. At the place where, a minute ago, a big gray face had hung over it in the gold light. They were looking for it. Oren could see that they were looking for it. They went along the edge of the hole, slowly, from one corner to the other, the way a man's eyes go along a crowd on a quay, searching for one face.
And his right hand had come up off the floor-board.
It was lifted. A little way. A hand's width. It hung in the air above the blanket, the long thin fingers spread and trembling, the way Moll's hand had hung over the hatch cover. Waiting.
"He's waiting," whispered Hessa. "Saints. Look at him. He's waiting for something."
Moll did not say anything.
He put his hands on the edge of the hole and pulled himself forward on the hay, on his belly, with a grunt of pain, until his face was over the gold square again and the light came up into it. Lord Thorne's eyes found him. Oren saw them find him. Saw them stop, and fix, and hold.
And Moll reached down.
He could not reach far. The room was a body-length below. But he stretched his right arm down through the hole as far as it would go, with his shoulder jammed against the edge of the trap and his face pressed into the hay, until his thick fingers were hanging in the gold air, an arm's length above the old man's lifted hand.
There was nothing to tap on. No wood. No drum.
So Moll tapped on the air.
Oren saw him do it. One finger. Coming down, slowly, onto nothing. And stopping. And lifting. A single beat, in the gold light, with nothing under it.
One.
And then a second shape after it, quick. A double beat, so fast it was almost one. Ta-tap. And then nothing.
Oren did not know it. The meaning was gone with the rest. But his body knew it. He felt it in his own right hand, lying on the hay. A twitch in the forefinger. A small cold shock up the inside of the arm, as if his hand remembered a kitchen table that his head had forgotten.
"Heard," said Moll, hoarsely, into the hole. "That's heard. That's the answer. That's what you beat back to the colonel's drummer, so he knows it got to you. One, and the double. Heard. Understood." His voice was shaking. "I heard you, sir. I heard you. I'm here."
Below them, Lord Thorne's hand came down.
Slowly. All the way. Onto the blanket over his own chest. And lay there, flat, over his heart, the fingers spread.
And tapped. Once.
One. And the double.
Heard.
Hessa put her hand over her mouth.
"That's what he did for Lyra," said Oren. His own voice came out of him strange and rough. "On Friday. When she tapped it back to him. He tapped once. Just once. And then nothing, for ten breaths." He swallowed. "And for me. This morning. When I said I was sorry."
"He was answering," said Moll. He did not take his eyes from the old man's face. "He was telling you he heard. That's all. For two years he's been sending, and nobody's beat back. And then the girl tapped it back to him, and he thought—" He stopped. "He thought somebody had heard. At last. And he said so. And then he waited for the next call. And it didn't come. Because she didn't know there was a next call."
"What's the next call?" said Hessa.
Moll did not answer.
Because below them, on the mattress in the gold room, Lord Thorne was moving.
Not his hand. His mouth.
Oren saw the lips move in the candlelight. Dry and thin and gray, in the gray face. They opened. Shut. Opened. As if they were trying to remember a shape. As if it had been so long since they made one that they had forgotten how it went, and had to feel for it. The way Oren felt for spoon, and found the gray place.
They made a shape.
Oren knew it. He had seen it before. In the study at Crown Walk, in the autumn, when Corin had tapped the four numbers back on the wall. He had seen the old man's lips make that same shape then, and had not understood it. A pushing out. A closing. Like a man about to whistle.
W.
"Witness," said Moll softly. "Yes. Yes, sir. Witness."
The lips moved again.
And this time a sound came out.
It was very small. Smaller than a breath. Oren would not have heard it if the night had not been so still, and the hay all round them so thick and quiet, like a room padded with wool. A dry, thin, cracked sound. A voice that had not been used in two years, except for a single word on Friday, to Lyra: Bourse.
"Drum," said Lord Thorne.
Nobody moved.
"Drummer," said Lord Thorne.
His eyes were on Moll's face. Fixed. Holding. And they were not empty. Oren had looked into those eyes every day for two years, and he had known for two years that they were empty. Like windows in a house where nobody lives. They were not empty now. There was somebody behind them. Far back. A long way back, in the dark of the house. But there. Standing at the window. Looking out.
"The drummer," said Lord Thorne. "Find—"
And the face changed.
Oren saw it go. He saw the somebody at the window turn away. Saw the eyes slide off Moll's face and go back up to the square of stars, slowly, and stop there, and go still. Saw the light go out of them the way a lamp goes out when someone carries it away into another room.
The lips closed.
The hand on the blanket lay still for a long time. Ten breaths. Twenty. Oren counted them. Thirty.
Then it slid down off his chest onto the blanket. And off the blanket onto the floor-board. And the long thin forefinger lifted, and came down.
Four.
Two.
Nine.
Three.
Moll stayed where he was, hanging half into the hole with his arm down in the gold light and his face pressed into the hay, for a long time after that. Oren did not try to move him. He saw the big shoulders shaking under the dark coat. He did not look at the sergeant's face. He did not think the sergeant would want him to.
At last Hessa reached over and put her hand on Moll's back.
"Come on," she said. "Come on now, Sergeant. You'll freeze up here. Come down by the stove."
Moll drew his arm up out of the hole. Slowly. He rolled onto his side on the hay. His face was wet. He did not wipe it.
"Find the drummer," he said. "That's what he meant. Find the drummer. The boy on the wagon." He looked at Oren. "That's the next call, Captain. That's the one the girl didn't know to send. He doesn't want somebody who's heard. He wants somebody who was there."
The Boy on the Wagon
They got him down by the stove.
That took nearly as long as getting him up, and Moll did not swear this time. He came down the plank in silence, with his face set and wet and gray, and Oren and Wat lowered him onto the end of Hessa's bunk in the cabin, and Hessa put a blanket round his shoulders and a fresh mug of tea in his hands, and he sat there under the low black roof, bent over, holding the mug, and stared at the little iron stove.
The cabin was very warm. After the top of the hay, it felt like an oven. The lamp swung on its chain. Robin was asleep again in his box at the foot of the bunk, flung out on his back with his fists up by his ears. Juno was sitting up in her bunk across the narrow space, with her bound feet stuck out on the blanket and her eyes enormous, looking from one face to another. She had heard it all, through the boards and the open hatch. Oren could see that she had.
Nobody said anything for a while.
Then Hessa said, in her short flat voice: "The drummer."
Moll looked up from the stove.
"The boy on your wagon," said Hessa. "The one who tapped it. All night, down the valley. Who taught it to him?"
"I don't know." Moll turned the mug in his hands. "Somebody did. It wasn't in the book, I told you. A drum boy doesn't make up a call. He's not let to. He learns what he's given." He frowned at the stove. "I saw him being given it. I think. At the top of the valley, before we came down. In the dark, by the first wagon. A man in a gray cloak, with the hood up. Not a soldier. A gentleman. From the city. He had the drum boy by the shoulder, and he was bent down, and he was tapping something on the boy's drum with his fingers. Over and over. Teaching it. I was too far off to hear." He was silent for a moment. "Long hands, he had. Thin. White, in the lantern light. I remember the hands."
Oren did not look at the boards of the cabin wall. At the hay beyond them. At the small gold room in the middle of the hay, and the long thin white hand on the floor-board, tapping.
He did not have to look. He could feel Hessa not looking too.
"And the boy," said Hessa. "The drummer. What happened to him?"
"He lived," said Moll.
He said it at once, as if it were the one thing in the whole business he was sure of.
"He lived. I asked. In the cart, going south, with my leg, I asked the surgeon's mate. The drum boy on the last wagon. Did he get out? When it went over? And the mate said, The little drummer? He jumped clear. He's walking back with the rear guard. Don't you worry about him." Moll looked at his tea. "And I remember thinking, good. Good. Because I'd watched him all night. Tapping. Ten years old, maybe. Eleven. Little thin lad, with a back as straight as a ramrod, the way they teach them. Sitting on the tailboard in the dark with his legs hanging down, tapping to the children in the wagon, all night long, so they'd stop crying." His voice cracked. "He never stopped once. Not once, all night. I thought, that lad's the bravest man on this march."
The lamp swung.
Oren sat on the end of the other bunk, bent over under the low roof, with his sword across his knees. He was looking at the floor. At the boards between his boots. He was not seeing them.
He was seeing a scrubbed kitchen table, with a pear-shaped burn at one end.
"How many drummers," he said slowly, "were on that march?"
Moll looked at him.
"Four," he said. "Four drum boys and a drum major, for a column that size. The drum major was killed the next spring, on the border. Two of the boys died of the camp fever that winter, at Highmoor. I heard about it after. The third one went for a sailor and drowned." A pause. "And the one on the last wagon was the only one who learned that call. The gentleman only taught it to him. The others weren't near."
He put down his mug on the floor.
"So there's only one man alive," said Moll, "who learned that call from the hands that made it. The boy on the wagon." He looked round the cabin. At Hessa. At Wat. At Juno. At Oren, last. "If Lord Thorne wants the drummer found, Captain, that's who he means. That's the only drummer there is."
Oren did not say anything.
He could not have. He sat on the end of the bunk and felt it come up through him, slowly, from the floor. Like water coming up through the floor of a cellar when the river rises. Cold. Silent. Filling everything.
His little brother, sitting at the scrubbed kitchen table in an army coat too big at the shoulders. Ten years old, or eleven. With his back straight as a ramrod, the way they had taught him. Home that autumn from a march in the north. Somewhere. He would not say where. He would not say anything about it. Not to their mother. Not to their father. Not to Oren.
He had sat at the table for weeks and said nothing.
And then one night, by the fire, he had picked up two spoons and started tapping.
That's halt. That's make ready. That's form on the colors. You want to learn them, Oren? I'll teach you. All of them.
All of them.
Oren tried. He went down into the cellar of his head with his candle again, desperately, the way a man goes back into a burning house. Down the stairs. Along the shelves. To the kitchen table, and the spoons, and his brother's thin hands coming down on the wood. And he tried to hear them. Not the sound. The shape. He tried to hear if there had been a night, that winter, when Harlan had brought the spoons down four times, and then two, and then nine, and then three, and looked up at him across the table with his pale closed face, and said nothing.
And there was nothing there.
The gray place. The smooth stone. The calls were gone. If his brother had tapped that call to him across the kitchen table, every night for a whole winter, he would not know it now. He had thrown it in the river, with the rest, to make a wall.
"Captain," said Juno, very quietly, from across the cabin.
He looked up.
She was looking at him the way she had looked at him that morning, when he had picked up Robin and found himself humming. Her face was white.
"Your brother," she said. "You told Corin. In the house on Crown Walk. I wasn't there but he wrote it in the Ledger and he read it to me after, on the roof. His brother was a drum boy. Taught him all the calls." She swallowed. "Commander H. Vance."
The cabin was very still.
Moll turned his head and looked at Oren. Slowly. His small sharp eyes, deep in the heavy gray face, went over Oren's face as if they were reading it. The scar. The jaw. The set of the shoulders. The way he sat, even now, even bent under a roof that was too low for him, with his back straight.
"I knew he was your brother," said Moll. "Everybody at the Watch knows that. Commander Vance has a brother who guards a house that's fallen down. They laugh about it on the desk." He drew a long breath. "I said his name to you on the deck tonight, Captain, and I looked at your face when I said it. I thought that was the worst thing I'd brought you. The battalion. Your own brother, holding the locks against you."
He stopped.
"I didn't know he was the boy," he said. "Saints. Saints, man. I didn't know he was the boy on the wagon."
Oren looked at the stove.
"Harlan," he said.
It was the first time he had said his brother's name out loud in six years. It came out of him rough and strange. Like a word in a language he had not spoken since he was a child.
"His name is Harlan," said Oren. "He came home from a march in the north when he was eleven years old. He wouldn't say where. He wouldn't say anything. And then he taught me the drums."
