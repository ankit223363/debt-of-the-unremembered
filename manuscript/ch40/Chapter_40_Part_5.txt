Chapter 40: Drum Calls, Part 5 of 5: Hold
Hold
It was Hessa who said it. Of course it was Hessa.
"You're going," she said.
She was standing at the foot of the three steep steps, with the rudder pin still in her hand, wrapped in its rag. She had not put it down since she brought it up from under the bunk. She held it the way a man holds a cudgel.
Oren looked at her.
"You're going to the city," said Hessa. "Tonight. With the sergeant. On the coal cart. To find your brother." It was not a question. "I can see it on you. You've got a face like a shut door, Captain Vance, but you've been sitting on that bunk for a quarter bell trying to work out how to tell me, and you can't, and it's making you sweat."
"I've served Lord Thorne for twenty-two years," said Oren.
"I know."
"I've never left his door. Not once. Not since he burned. Not to sleep. Not to eat. I took the Inquisition's men on the stairs at Crown Walk in the autumn with a broken hand, so they wouldn't get to his door." His voice was rough. "I don't leave. That's what I'm for."
"I know."
"And you heard him." Oren looked at the boards of the cabin wall. "Two. Hold. That's what he's been saying. For two years. Hold. Stand fast. Don't break. Whatever comes."
"I heard him," said Hessa. "I heard the rest of it too."
She came across the cabin. She did not have to bend under the roof. She stood in front of him, square and short, with the rudder pin in her fist, and looked him in the face.
"He didn't say hold to you," she said. "He said it to a wagon full of children, twenty years ago, in the dark, on a road you weren't on. Witness. Hold. Fold. Home. That's not an order to a guard, Captain. That's what you say to frightened children to make them stop crying." Her pale eyes did not move. "And then tonight he looked up out of that hole and said a new thing. For the first time in two years. Find the drummer. That's an order. That's the only order he's given you since he burned."
Oren said nothing.
"I'll hold," said Hessa. "That's what I'm for. I'll put this pin back in my rudder tonight and take him north, past Withy Reach, to the old turf barns by the peat cuttings, where nobody goes in winter, not even the peat men. And I'll sit on his hay with a boat hook across my knees, and anybody who comes up that canal after him will have to go through me. And through Wat. And through Juno, who bites."
"I do," said Juno, from her bunk, in a small fierce voice. "I bit a gray coat once. On the leg."
Hessa did not turn round.
"You can't hold him better than that, Captain," she said. "Not one big man with a sword, on a barge, against four hundred. And your brother's the one with the four hundred. And your brother's the one who can tell the battalion which locks to search and which to leave. And your brother's the one who sat on a tailboard all night when he was eleven years old, tapping to children in the dark, so they'd stop crying." She paused. "Maybe he still remembers how."
Oren looked at her.
He looked at her for a long time. The small square red-faced woman in the man's coat, with the knitted cap over her ears and the rudder pin in her fist. Who had pulled Old Jory's son out of a lock when she was nine years old. Who had hit Wat with an oar at Saltgate and then pulled him out of the water with it. Who had kept a lord alive in a box in the middle of her hay for five days, and fed him with a spoon, and brushed his hair every morning.
"He might not," said Oren. "Harlan. He might not remember. He might not want to. He told me six years ago I'd thrown the family away. He might hand me to Kross on the Lantern Bridge."
"He might," said Hessa. "Then you'll know. And so will we."
Oren looked down at his own hands. At the scars on them. At the sword lying across his knees.
Then he looked at Moll.
The sergeant was sitting on the end of Hessa's bunk with the blanket round his shoulders and the mug in his hands, watching him. He had not said anything. He did not say anything now. He only lifted one thick finger off the side of the mug, and let it fall. Ta-tap. The double.
Heard.
Oren stood up.
He remembered the roof this time, and bent. He buckled his sword belt tighter. He felt inside his coat for the oilcloth packet with the cream paper in it, and found it, and left it where it was. He looked round the cabin once. At Juno in her bunk, with her bound feet. At Wat on the steps, with his arm in its sling. At Robin, flung out asleep in his box with his fists up. At Hessa, with the pin.
"The turf barns," he said. "Past Withy Reach. By the peat cuttings."
"The third barn," said Hessa. "The one with the broken roof. There's a cut that runs right up to the door. Deep enough for the Grace if you go slow. I'll put her in under the eaves, and pile turf on the deck, and you'll not see her from the canal." She paused. "Tell the boy. When you find him. Tell Corin. The third barn."
"I'll tell him."
"And tell him," said Hessa, "he owes me a goat."
Oren nearly smiled. The uncut side of his mouth moved.
"That was Big Abel," he said. "On the Tanners' Ditch. You owe him a goat."
"Then he can owe it to me," said Hessa, "and I'll owe it to Abel, and that's fair."
Oren went up the steps.
He did not go to the cart. Not yet. He went along the deck in the dark, past the hatch, to the great stack of hay. He climbed it again, the way Hessa had shown him, with his toes in the gaps and his fingers hooked over the edges of the bales. At the top he walked out across the frosty hay under the stars to the middle, where the two bales were still pulled aside and the square of gold light still came up out of the hole.
He knelt at the edge of it and looked down.
Lord Thorne lay on the mattress with his face turned up toward the sky. His eyes were open. Empty again. Looking at the stars. His finger was tapping on the floor-board.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
"Sir," said Oren.
His voice did not come out right. He stopped. He tried again.
"Sir. I'm going away for a while. To the city. To find the drummer." He swallowed. "Hessa will hold your door. She's better at it than I am. She'll take you north tonight, to a barn with a broken roof, and I'll come for you there." He put his scarred hand flat on the frosty hay at the edge of the hole. "I'll find him, sir. I swear it. I'll find Harlan, and I'll bring him to you, and you can tell him whatever it is you've been waiting two years to tell. And then—" He stopped. "And then we'll go home. Whatever home is."
The finger tapped.
Four.
Two.
Oren waited for the nine.
It did not come.
The finger stopped after the two. And lay still on the floor-board. And then it lifted, slowly, and came down, once more.
Two.
And again.
Two.
Three times. Long and slow. Tap. Tap. And a rest. Tap. Tap. And a rest. Tap. Tap.
Oren did not know what it meant. He had no drums left in him. But he did not need Moll to tell him. He did not need the burned winter on the kitchen table. Some things you know without the words for them. Some things go in through the skin.
Hold.
Hold.
Hold.
He knelt there on the top of the hay with his hand flat on the frost, and let it go into him.
Then the finger slid back into its old place on the board, and began again. Four. Two. Nine. Three. As if nothing had happened.
Oren put the two bales back over the hole. Carefully, the way Hessa had pulled them. He stood up. He climbed down.
On the deck, Wat was already at the stern, with the rudder pin in his good hand, kneeling by the rudder post. Hessa was at the bow, with a long pole, breaking the ice along the landing stage with short hard blows. Crack. And crack. The ice went in black lines across the water, out toward the middle of the canal. Juno had come up the steps on her hands and knees and was sitting at the top of them, wrapped in a blanket, with Robin in her arms.
Oren went to the rail.
He did not say goodbye. Soldiers do not say goodbye. He touched Juno once on the top of her head, as he went past, the way you would touch a dog you were fond of. He nodded to Wat. Wat nodded back. And at the bow, as he stepped over the rail onto the landing stage, Hessa looked up from her pole and said, without stopping:
"Mind your brother, Captain."
"Mind your hay," said Oren.
And went up the frosty planks toward the ramp, and the coal cart, and the red coal of Abner Cole's pipe glowing in the dark.
The Red Lantern
Abner Cole did not ask anything either.
He took his pipe out of his mouth when they came up the ramp, Oren with Moll's arm over his shoulders and Moll's stick in his free hand, and looked at the two of them in the starlight. At the sword. At the size of them both. Then he put the pipe back in, and moved over on the box, and jerked his head at the back of the cart.
"Sacks," he said round the stem. "Both of you. There's a Ministry toll at the Long Pond bridge, and the toll man's a cousin of nobody."
They got Moll in first. It was easier going in than it had been coming out, he said, because now he knew which sacks had the lumps in. He lay down on his back in the bed of the cart, among the black sacks, with his bad leg out straight, and Oren piled sacks over him. Best house coal, from the Lower Docks. It smelled of tar and of wet stone and of the deep places under the city. The dust came off it in clouds and went up Oren's nose and down his throat.
Then Oren got in beside him.
He was too big for it. He was too big for everything, these days. He lay on his side with his knees drawn up and his sword along his body, pressed against the high wooden side of the cart, and pulled sacks over himself until there was only a small gap left at the back, by his face, a crack between two sacks and the tailboard, where the cold air came in and he could see out.
Abner clicked his tongue. The big brown horse leaned into the shafts. The cart lurched, and creaked, and rolled.
Oren lay in the coal and watched through the crack.
He saw the farmer's barn go past, big and black. He saw the top of the ramp. And then, as the cart turned onto the canal road, he saw the wharf below. The long gray landing stage, white with frost. The black water beside it, cracked across with Hessa's lines of broken ice.
And the Patient Grace.
She was moving.
Out from the landing stage, slowly. Very slowly. Her long black hull sliding sideways into the middle of the canal, with Hessa at the bow pushing off with the pole, and the ice cracking and tinkling round her. The great stack of hay on her deck, white with frost, glimmering in the starlight like a hill under snow. The small yellow lantern on the stern pole, and Wat's dark shape by the tiller under it. No sail. There was no wind for a sail. Hessa would pole her, and Wat would steer, and where the ice was thick they would break it, foot by foot, all the way to Withy Reach.
Oren watched the lantern swing.
She turned her bow north. Away from him. Away from the city. Toward the bend by the black willows, where the skiff had gone that afternoon.
And in the middle of the hay, in the small gold room, an old man lay on his back looking up at a ceiling of boards, and tapped. Oren could not hear it, over the creak of the cart and the clop of the horse's hooves and the grinding of the iron wheels on the frozen ruts. But he knew it was there. He would know it was there for the rest of his life, he thought. Wherever he was. Like a heartbeat.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
The cart went on, along the canal road, south, between the black willows.
It was perhaps a quarter of a mile to the Brindle toll bridge. An old hump-backed bridge of gray stone, where the canal road crossed from the east bank to the west, over the water, with a little toll house at the far end and a gate across the road that a man had to come out and open. Oren had seen it from the barge every day since Thursday. It was the only way south from Brindle on wheels.
Abner slowed the horse to a walk on the slope of the bridge. Oren felt the cart tilt under him. Up. The iron wheels ground on the stone. At the top, the hump, the cart levelled out for a moment, and from the crack between the sacks Oren could see, for that moment, back along the canal behind them.
North.
The long straight black line of the water, between the two lines of willows. White with frost on both banks. Under the stars. Going away, straight as a rule, for a long way. Half a mile. More. Toward the bend.
And on the water, far up it, a small yellow light. Swinging. Moving away. The Patient Grace.
And beyond it, further up, at the bend itself, where the willows came down thick to the edge of the water and the canal turned out of sight, another light.
It was not yellow.
It was red.
Oren saw it come. It came on all at once, a small hard square of red in the black at the foot of the willows, like a coal blown on. It hung there. A breath. Two. And then it went out.
And came on again.
And went out.
And came on again.
Three times.
Then it went out, and stayed out, and there was nothing at the bend but the black of the willows and the stars.
Oren did not move.
He lay in the coal on the top of the Brindle toll bridge with his face in the crack between two sacks and his heart going like a drum, and stared at the place where the red light had been. Beside him, under the sacks, Moll had gone rigid.
"Captain." Moll's voice, very low, muffled by the coal. "Captain. Did you see that?"
"Yes."
"Red. A red lantern. Shuttered." A breath. "Opened three times and shut."
"Yes."
"I know that light," said Moll.
Oren turned his head in the dark.
"I know that light," said Moll again. His voice had gone flat and dead. "I've seen it on the river from the Watch house window. Going up the Cut at night. A black skiff, with a red lantern on a pole at the bow, shuttered on all four sides. Two men in it. One rowing." He stopped. "And one in the stern in a round hat. Who came into my Watch house on Friday and sat by my stove for an hour, with gray mud on his boots from the Coldwater lock, and didn't say a word, and warmed his hands, and went away."
"Who is he?" said Oren.
"Vell," said Moll. "Mister Vell, the constables call him. When he comes through the front office, the whole desk goes quiet and looks at its boots. Nobody knows what he does. Everybody knows who he does it for." A breath. "He folds his notes small. Like his master."
"Kross," said Oren.
"Kross's gray cape," said Moll. "Kross's best."
The cart had stopped. They were on the far side of the hump now, rolling down toward the toll house, and Abner had pulled the horse up at the gate. Oren heard the toll house door open. A man's voice, yawning, cross. Abner. You again. Saints, it's cold. What've you got? And Abner's voice, round his pipe. What I always have. Coal.
Oren lay in the coal.
He could get out. He could push the sacks off and roll over the tailboard and drop into the road and run. Back over the bridge. Back along the canal road, a quarter mile, a half, a whole mile in the dark on the frozen ruts, to the bend by the willows. With his sword. Against two men in a skiff who would be gone before he got there. Who might never have been there at all. Who might be a pike fisherman and his boy, and a lantern, and a habit of signalling home.
And if he ran, and they were there, and they saw him coming, then they would know there was something on the Patient Grace worth a big man running a mile in the dark to guard.
And if he ran, he would not be in the city tonight, when the battalion marched in. He would not be on the Lantern Bridge in the morning, when his brother rode across it at the head of four hundred men with twelve-foot hooks. He would not be there to say Harlan. Harlan, it's me. I need to ask you about a wagon.
And tomorrow the hooks would come up the canal. Past the Long Pond. Past Brindle. To Withy Reach, and the peat cuttings, and the old turf barns.
And there would be nobody to stop them but Hessa Doon and a boat hook.
Hold, said the old man's finger, on a board, in a box, in the middle of the hay. Hold. Hold.
Oren did not know which way was holding.
The toll gate creaked open. The toll man said something about the cold, and went back inside, and shut his door. Abner clicked his tongue. The cart rolled.
Oren lay in the coal and did not get out.
He pressed his face to the crack between the sacks and watched the toll bridge go away behind them, gray in the starlight. And the canal beyond it, black, going north. And somewhere up it, too far now to see, a small yellow light, swinging. And somewhere beyond that, nothing. No red. Only willows, and stars, and the dark.
It began to snow.
He saw the first flakes come down past the crack. Big and soft and slow, out of the black, like ash from a fire. They landed on the black sacks and did not melt. They landed on the tailboard. They landed on the frozen ruts of the road behind the cart, and filled them, a little at a time. By the time the cart had gone a mile, the road behind it was white, and the toll bridge was gone, and the canal was gone, and the world was only the snow coming down, and the creak of the cart, and the clop of the horse, and the warm hard bulk of the sergeant beside him under the coal, breathing.
"Captain," said Moll, after a long time.
"Yes."
"You did right."
Oren did not answer.
"You'd not have caught them," said Moll. "And if you had, you'd have told them everything by running." A pause. "And Hessa Doon's no fool. She'll have seen it too. From the deck. She'll know."
"Will she?"
"She's a bargewoman. On a dark canal, with a lord in her hay." Moll's voice was very tired. "She'll have seen it before you did."
Oren hoped so.
He lay in the coal and hoped so, and went through his list in his head, the way he did every morning. Lock. Ice. Drums. Chair. Spoon. Lilies. Six. He held them, one by one, like a man counting coins in a pocket in the dark. Six words that were gone.
And he made a new list, under it. A short one. Of things he still had.
Harlan.
The wagon.
The third barn, with the broken roof.
Find the drummer.
He held those too.
And then, somewhere far ahead, through the snow, so far and so faint that at first he thought it was his own heart, he heard it.
Drums.
Coming through the snow from somewhere ahead and to the west. From where the canal road met the great high road that came down out of the north from Highmoor. Over the dark fields. Not one drum. Many. Rolling, and stopping, and rolling again. A long hard rattle, and then beats. Beats with a shape to them. Beats that talked.
The battalion. The rest of it. Marching down the high road through the snow, toward the city.
Oren lay in the coal and listened to the drums of his brother's battalion beating out calls across the snow. Calls he had learned at a scrubbed kitchen table, a whole winter long, with two small round things on thin handles that his mother had thrown out into the snow.
He listened as hard as he had ever listened to anything in his life.
And he could not understand a single word.
