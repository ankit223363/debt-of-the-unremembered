Chapter 40: Drum Calls, Part 1 of 5: The Last Barge
The Last Barge
Oren Vance kept a list in his head.
He had kept it for a month now. Since the fight at the river, when he had burned the drums. He did not write it down. He was a soldier, not a clerk, and he did not like to write things down. Things that were written down could be found, and read, and used. So he kept it in his head, and every morning, before he did anything else, before he opened his eyes, he went through it. The way he had once gone through his kit before a march. Boots. Belt. Sword. Water.
Lock.
Ice.
Drums.
Chair.
Spoon.
Lilies.
Six. Six words that were gone. Not the things. The things were still there in the world. He could see a lock, and use it. He could walk on ice, and feel it cold through his boots. He had sat on a chair last night, in the cabin, by the stove. He had eaten Hessa's porridge with a spoon. But when he looked at them, there was a hole where the word should be. A smooth gray place in his mind, like a stone worn smooth by water. And he had to go round it. The thing you turn the key in. The hard water. The thing you sit on. The thing you eat with.
It made him slow. It made him stop in the middle of saying something, with his mouth open, and his hand in the air, reaching for a word that was not there. He hated it. He hated the look on people's faces when he did it. Not unkind. Patient. The way you look at an old man.
He was forty-six years old. He had been the Captain of the Thorne household guard for twenty-two years. He had once held the gate of the Crown Walk house for an hour against nine men with a broken arm.
And he could not say spoon.
He opened his eyes.
It was not yet light. He was lying on his back on the deck of the Patient Grace, in the narrow space between the edge of the hay and the low rail, wrapped in two army blankets and Wat's old oilskin, with his sword beside him under the blankets where his hand could find it. He had slept here every night since Thursday. Not in the cabin. Not in the hay. Out here, on the deck, in the cold, where he could see the wharf and the water and the road. A guard sleeps where he can see.
Above him the sky was the color of a gray slate, going faintly blue at the eastern edge. No stars left. No moon. A few small high clouds, very still, like smudges on a window.
He sat up.
The cold came in at once, under the blankets, round his neck, down his back. A hard dry cold, without any wind in it. The kind that comes before snow. His breath went up in a thick white cloud and hung in front of his face. The oilskin was stiff with frost and crackled when he moved, and there was frost on the blankets too, a fine white fur of it, and on the rail, and on every stalk of hay that stuck out of the great stack beside him.
He looked at the wharf.
It was empty.
On Thursday night, when Corin came, there had been seven barges at the Brindle hay wharf. Seven, all in a row along the landing stage, so close that their sides nearly touched, all loading hay at the same slow pace for the city stables. All the same. Black hulls, red sails furled, stacks of hay as high as a house. Hessa had said that was the best place in the world to hide. In a crowd of things that look the same.
On Saturday afternoon, the last of the farmer's stacks had come down the ramp, and the last forkful had gone up onto the last barge, and the bargemen had cheered and thrown their caps in the air.
On Sunday morning, at first light, six of them had gone.
Oren had watched them go. One after another, out from the landing stage into the middle of the canal, their sails filling slowly in the little cold wind, turning south. Toward the locks, and the Long Pond, and the city. The bargemen had shouted to Hessa as they went past. Coming, Hessa? And Hessa had shouted back, cheerful, with her fists on her hips: Rudder pin's split! I'm waiting on the smith! Save me a drink at the Bell! And they had laughed, and waved, and gone.
Now there was only one barge at the Brindle hay wharf.
The Patient Grace. Black hull. Red sail, furled. A stack of hay as high as a house, with a small gold room hidden in the middle of it, and an old man in the gold room, tapping.
No crowd now.
Oren looked along the landing stage. The long gray planks, white with frost. The iron rings for the mooring ropes, empty. The black water beside it, with a skin of new ice on it from the night, thin and gray, crazed like old china where the current moved underneath. Further along, the ramp going up to the farmer's yard, with the frozen ruts of the hay carts in it. And the farmer's barn, big and dark, with its doors shut. And beyond that, the road. The canal road, going north toward Withy Reach and south toward the city, between two rows of bare black willows, with the frost white on the ground under them.
Nobody on it. Not yet. It was too early for anybody. Even the farmer's men did not get up before the light, in winter, with no hay to move.
It was very quiet.
He could hear the canal. The small sounds of it. A drip, somewhere. A creak of the hull against the planks of the landing stage as the barge moved a little in the water, the way a big animal moves in its sleep. A crackle of ice. Far off, very far, a cock crowing in some farmyard up the road. And under all of it, so quiet he had to hold his breath to hear it, from the middle of the great stack of hay beside him, a sound.
Tap.
Tap.
Tap.
Tap.
A pause.
Tap. Tap.
It came through the hay the way a heartbeat comes through a man's chest when you put your ear to it. Not loud. Steady. Patient. A finger on wood. Oren knew it the way he knew his own breathing. He had been hearing it every day for two years. In the house on Crown Walk, through the wall of the study. On the barge, through the hay. In his sleep. Some nights he woke up and did not know if he had heard it or dreamed it.
Four. Two.
He waited.
Nine.
Three.
Then the long pause, while the old man rested. And then it would start again. Four. Two. Nine. Three. All day. All night. For two years.
Once, Oren had known what it meant.
That was the worst thing on the list. Not spoon. Not chair. Not even lilies, which had been something to do with Lady Cressida, something important, something he had needed to remember and could not. The worst was drums. Because he had known. He was sure of it. For two years he had heard the old man tap on the study wall and had not truly listened, the way you stop hearing a clock. And then one night last month, sitting up late outside the study door, he had listened. Really listened. And he had felt something in him sit up, the way an old dog sits up when it hears its master's step. Something that said I know that. I know that one. And he had meant to sit down with it in the morning and work it out. And then the next night he had gone down to the river, and there had been three men, and he had needed a wall.
And he had burned the drums.
All of them. Every call. The whole winter on the kitchen table, with his little brother. He had thought, I'll never need those again.
He had been wrong about a great many things in his life. He did not think he had ever been as wrong as that.
He got up.
He folded the blankets, and the oilskin, and put them in a neat square by the rail, the way he had folded his kit every morning for thirty years. He buckled on his sword. He stood for a moment at the rail, looking at the empty wharf and the empty road, with his breath going up white in the gray light.
Then he went to the hatch and knelt and put his scarred face close to the boards, and said, quietly, the way he said it every morning:
"All's well, sir."
Inside the hay, the tapping did not stop. It never did.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
What Juno Heard
The cabin of the Patient Grace was the warmest place in the world, and the smallest.
It was at the back of the barge, under the tiller, down three steep wooden steps that were more like a ladder than a stair. Oren had to go down them sideways, with his head bent, because the doorway was cut for Hessa and not for a man of his size. And once he was down, he could not stand up straight. The roof was made of the curved planks of the deck, black with a hundred years of smoke, and it came down so low at the sides that only Hessa and Juno could stand under it without stooping.
But it was warm.
There was a stove at the far end, a little black iron stove no bigger than a bread bin, with a crooked chimney pipe going up through the roof, and it was always lit. Hessa fed it with coal and with dried peat from a sack, and it ate both, and gave back a dry red heat that filled every corner of the cabin and made the air thick and drowsy. There was a kettle on it, always. There was a smell of peat smoke, and wet wool drying, and tea, and Robin.
There were two bunks, one on each side, built into the curve of the hull like shelves, with faded patchwork curtains that could be drawn across. There was a narrow table between them, folded up against the wall when it was not needed. There was a shelf with tin plates and tin mugs on hooks, and a row of jars with tea and sugar and salt and lard, and a lamp hanging from a beam on a chain, swinging very slightly with the barge, so that the shadows in the cabin swung too.
And on every surface, every hook, every nail, there were things drying. Socks. Shawls. A shirt of Wat's. Robin's small clothes, in a row along the pipe of the stove, like flags.
Juno was in the left-hand bunk. Lying on her back, with the curtain pulled half across, and her feet sticking out at the end on a folded blanket, wrapped up as big as two loaves in goose grease and flannel.
She was awake. Oren could see the gleam of her eyes in the red light of the stove.
"Morning, Captain," she said.
Her voice was flat and tired. She had walked fourteen miles through the frost on Friday to bring them the news from the city. Twenty-three bridges and three locks, and the last six miles with her boots full of ice. When she had come down the ramp onto the wharf at dusk her feet had been white, and then, when Hessa got her boots off by the stove, they had gone a color Oren did not like to remember. Hessa had sat up with her all that night, rubbing them, slowly, in a bowl of water that was only a little warm, while Juno held onto the edge of the bunk with both hands and did not cry out once. Fourteen years old.
"Morning," said Oren. "Your feet?"
"Still on." She wiggled something, under the flannel. He could not see if it was toes. "Hessa says I'll keep them all. She looked this morning. Says the little one on the left was touch and go but it's pinking up." A pause. "It hurts worse than it did. Hessa says that's good. That means it's alive."
"Hessa's right."
"Hessa's always right. It's horrible."
Oren nearly smiled. The uncut side of his mouth moved a little.
He sat down on the end of the other bunk, carefully, bent over under the low roof, with his sword across his knees. The other bunk was Hessa's. She was not in it. She was never in it after the fifth bell. He could hear her somewhere outside, on the deck, moving about, and the soft slap of a bucket going over the side on a rope to break the ice and fetch water.
In a wooden box at the foot of Hessa's bunk, lined with an old fur coat, Robin was asleep. He was lying on his back with his arms flung up over his head, the way babies sleep, with his fists closed and his mouth open. He was eleven months old. He had a round red face and a round head covered in pale fuzz, and a small wet sound came out of him each time he breathed. Oren looked at him for a while. He found that he looked at Robin a great deal, these days, without meaning to.
"Captain," said Juno.
"Yes."
"I've been listening. All night." She turned her head on the pillow to look at him. "To him. Lord Thorne. You can hear it in here, if you put your ear to the boards. Through the hay. Tap, tap, tap." She paused. "I couldn't sleep. My feet. So I lay here and listened, and counted. Four, two, nine, three. Over and over. And I kept thinking I'd heard it somewhere else. And I couldn't think where. And it was driving me mad."
Oren did not say anything.
"And then it came to me," said Juno. "About the fourth bell. It came all at once, the way things do when you stop looking for them." She pushed herself up on her elbows. "It's Moll."
Oren looked at her.
"Sergeant Moll," said Juno. "At the Watch house. Mrs. Marta told me. Friday morning, before I left. In the yard, when she was giving me the letter and the bread and telling me which way to go. She said, And tell the captain this, Juno. Tell him the sergeant taps it in his sleep. Those numbers. The same ones." Juno's voice dropped. "And I forgot. I'm sorry, Captain. I forgot all of it. I got here with my feet like that, and I could only think about my feet, and Hessa put them in the water, and I told you everything else, about the catalogue and the card and the step women and Mr. Linus, and I forgot that. I never forget things. Never. I'm a runner. It's the only thing I'm good at."
She was nearly crying. Oren saw her face go tight, and her mouth press shut, and her chin set, so that she would not.
"You walked fourteen miles on ice," he said. "You're allowed one."
"Mrs. Marta said it was important."
"It is." He looked at the boards of the cabin wall, beside the bunk. Black and smooth and curved. Beyond them, the hold. And the hay. And the small gold room. "Tell me exactly what she said. Every word. Slowly."
Juno shut her eyes. He watched her go back for it. Runners did that, he had noticed. They went back along a road in their heads, the way they had come, and picked up the words where they had dropped them.
"Tell him the sergeant taps it in his sleep," she said. "Those numbers. The same ones. Four and two and nine and three. On his knee, on the bench by the oven, with his eyes shut. And he did it awake too, once, at the table, four and two and four, and didn't know he was doing it. And when Terrence said three at the fourth bell, he tapped once back. Just once. Like an answer." Juno opened her eyes. "Tell the captain. He'll know what it means. He was a soldier too."
The cabin was quiet.
The stove ticked. Robin breathed his small wet breath. Outside, on the deck, the bucket came up out of the canal and was set down, slopping, and Hessa's boots moved away along the planks toward the bow.
He'll know what it means.
Oren sat on the end of the bunk with his sword across his knees and looked at the black curved boards, and felt the hole in his head where the drums had been. Smooth. Gray. A stone worn by water.
"I don't," he said.
Juno looked at him.
"I don't know what it means," said Oren. "Not any more. I burned it. Last month." He touched his temple with one thick finger. "But Moll does."
He stood up. Too fast. His head hit the curved beam of the roof with a sound like a mallet on a post, and the lamp on its chain swung wildly, and the shadows in the cabin swung with it. Robin woke up and began to cry.
Oren did not notice.
"Twelve years in the Commonwealth Foot," he said. "Before the Watch. He told Corin. He told Marta." He was speaking to himself now, not to Juno. "And a bad leg. From a wagon. On a march." He stopped. "And he taps it in his sleep."
"Captain, your head—"
"Juno." He turned to her. "Can you write?"
"Course I can write." She looked offended through her tears. "Mrs. Marta taught me. On the back of the rent book."
"Then write me a letter. To Moll. Now." He was already looking round the cabin for paper. For a pencil. "Short. So nobody else would know what it meant. The old man taps what you tap. Come if you can. Ask Ellis at the yard. No names." He found the stub of pencil Hessa kept for her tallies, on the shelf by the tea, and held it out. "Can you do that?"
Juno took it.
"Who's going to take it?" she said. "I can't walk. Wat can't leave. Hessa won't leave Robin, and you won't leave him." She tipped her head at the boards. At the hay. At the tapping.
Oren did not answer for a moment.
Then, from up on the deck, from the top of the ramp, there came the sound of hooves on frozen ground. A lot of them. And wheels. And a man's voice, shouting cheerfully to a horse.
"Ellis," said Oren.
Juno stared at him.
"Monday," said Oren. "He brings the empty wain back from the city on a Friday night and goes out to the high farms on Monday for straw. Hessa said. He goes past the canal road and back by the north road." He put his hand flat on the curved roof, to keep his head off it, and bent toward her. "And on Tuesday he goes down to the city again with the straw. Write it, Juno. Small. Fold it small. I'll catch him at the top of the ramp."
Robin was crying properly now, with his whole round red face, his fists going. Juno held the pencil in one hand and pulled a sheet of brown paper off the sugar jar with the other, and smoothed it on her knee, and began to write. Slowly. In big careful round letters, the way Mrs. Marta had taught her, on the back of the rent book.
Oren picked Robin up.
He did not think about it. He just did it. The baby was warm and heavy and wet, and furious, and smelled of milk and peat smoke. Oren held him against his chest with one big scarred hand under his bottom and the other on his back, the way Hessa did it, and stood bent over under the low black roof, rocking a little, from foot to foot.
Robin stopped crying. Not all at once. He hiccupped, and grizzled, and pushed his hot face into Oren's coat, and grizzled again. And then was quiet.
Oren stood very still.
He did not know any songs. He had never known any songs. But he found that he was humming something, under his breath, very low. Not a tune. Just a beat. Hm. Hm. Hm. Hm. And a pause. Hm. Hm.
He stopped.
Juno had looked up from the paper.
They looked at each other in the swinging red light. Neither of them said anything.
Then Juno went back to writing. And Oren went on rocking the baby. And did not hum again.
