Chapter 40: Drum Calls, Part 2 of 5: The Hay Room
The Hay Room
Ellis took the note.
He was a broad, slow, red-faced man in a sheepskin coat, sitting up on the box of a great empty wain behind six steaming horses, with a pipe in his teeth that he did not light. He did not ask what was in it. He took the small hard square of brown paper from Oren's fingers, and looked at it, and put it inside his coat, in the pocket over his heart, and buttoned the coat over it.
"Watch house on Rope Street," he said round the pipe. "The sergeant. Tomorrow, when I'm down with the straw." He looked at Oren from under the brim of his hat. "Hessa's lot."
It was not a question. Oren nodded.
"Hessa pulled my brother out of the Long Pond when he went through the ice," said Ellis. "Twenty year ago. He's still alive. Got seven children." He gathered up the reins. "Tomorrow, then."
And he clicked his tongue to the horses, and the great wain lurched and rolled away up the road between the black willows, north, toward the high farms. Oren stood at the top of the ramp and watched it go until the frost-smoke from the horses' backs was gone among the trees.
Then he went down to the hay room.
You got into it from the hold. There was a hatch in the deck, forward of the cabin, and under the hatch a short ladder, and at the foot of the ladder the hold of the barge, long and dark and low, packed to the beams with hay. Hessa's load for the city stables, that she was not taking to the city. Pressed hay, in tight square bales, stacked like bricks from one side of the hull to the other, so close that a cat could not have got between them. It smelled of summer. Sweet and dusty and warm, even in the cold. When Oren breathed it in, he always thought of something, and never knew what.
In the middle of the bales, low down, there was a gap.
You would not have seen it unless you knew. It was behind a loose bale at the foot of the ladder, that you pulled out and set aside. And behind it there was a tunnel. A square hole through the hay, no wider than a man's shoulders, going in. Lined with old boards, so it would not fall in. Hessa said her grandfather had built it, in the days when the Patient Grace had carried brandy from the coast that nobody had paid the Ministry's tax on. Hay on the outside, and a little room in the middle. The gray coats would come aboard and stick their pikes in the hay, and never hit anything but hay.
Oren got down on his hands and knees and went in.
He hated this part. He was too big for it. The boards scraped his shoulders on both sides, and the hay came in at the gaps and down his collar, and he had to go flat on his belly for the last part, pushing his sword ahead of him. It was black in the tunnel, black as the inside of a boot. Six body-lengths. He had counted them, the first night. Then a turn. Then two more.
And then, ahead of him, a glow.
The hay room was a box. Not much more than that. Square, with walls of old boards, the height of a man sitting, the length of a man lying. The brandy room. Hessa had hung the walls with what she had: an old red shawl, a patched blanket, the yellow curtain from the cabin window that she said she never drew anyway. And on the floor she had laid a mattress of hay, with sacking over it, and blankets over that. And at the far end, on a little shelf nailed to the wall, a stub of candle stood in a tin, burning.
That was why it glowed gold. The candle, and the hay all round it, and the red and yellow of the cloth. When Lyra had first seen it, on Thursday night, she had stood in the mouth of the tunnel and put her hand over her mouth and said, It's like the inside of a lantern.
Lord Thorne lay on the mattress, under the blankets, with his head on a pillow made of Hessa's spare skirt rolled up.
He was awake. He was always awake, or seemed to be. His eyes were open, looking up at the boards of the low roof, a hand's width above his face. His gray hair was brushed back off his forehead; Lyra had brushed it, before she went, and Hessa had brushed it every morning since. His face was long and thin and very still, with the bones showing in it, and the skin over the bones gray and fine like paper. A good face. A lord's face. Oren had served it for twenty-two years.
His right hand lay outside the blankets, on the wood of the floor-board beside the mattress.
And the long thin forefinger was tapping.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
Oren came out of the tunnel on his knees and sat back on his heels, with his head bent under the roof, and laid his sword along the wall.
"Morning, sir," he said.
The finger did not stop. The eyes did not move.
Oren had not expected them to. He had stopped expecting it a long time ago. In the first months, at Crown Walk, he had talked to Lord Thorne the whole day long, every day. He had told him the news from the city. He had read him the letters. He had read him the old books from the library that Lord Thorne used to like. Battles, mostly. Sieges. He had thought, if I keep talking, he will come back up. Like a man pulling a bucket out of a well, hand over hand on the rope. And Lord Thorne had lain in the big bed and looked at the ceiling and tapped.
After a year, Oren had stopped reading. After the second year, he mostly stopped talking. Now he just said morning, sir, and all's well, sir, and good night, sir, and sat.
He sat now.
He sat on his heels in the little gold room, with the hay all round them and the candle burning, and listened to the finger on the wood.
Four.
He tried.
He did it every day, since the fight. He did not tell anyone. He sat and listened to the tapping and he went into his own head, the way you go down into a cellar with a candle, looking for something you put there years ago. Down the stairs. Along the shelves. Past the things that were still there.
The kitchen table at home. In the house where he grew up. The house by the barracks, in the Lower Town, that was gone now, pulled down for a warehouse. He could see it. The long scrubbed table, white with scrubbing, with a burn mark at one end in the shape of a pear, where his mother had once put down a hot pan. The window over the sink. The cold coming in round it in winter.
And his brother, sitting at the table.
Harlan. Ten years old, or eleven. Just home. In that same winter. Thin, so thin, in a boy's army coat that was too big for him at the shoulders, with the brass buttons all polished. Sitting very straight on the bench with his back like a stick, the way they had taught him. His hair cut short as a brush. His face pale and closed.
He had come home from the army in the autumn. From a march. Somewhere in the north. He would not say where. He would not say anything about it, not to their mother, not to their father, not to Oren, who had been a grown man already, twenty-six, a guard at the Thorne gate with his first scar. Harlan had come home and sat in the kitchen and said nothing for weeks.
And then one night, by the fire, he had picked up two spoons.
That was not the word in Oren's head. The word was gone; it was on the list. In his head there was only the shape of them. Two small round bowls on two thin handles, the things you eat with. He had to borrow the word from other people's mouths now, from Hessa's, from Juno's, the way you borrow a coat. But the shape was there. The shape was very clear.
That was what Oren could see. He could see it as clear as the candle in front of him. His little brother at the scrubbed table, with a spoon in each hand. Holding them the way you hold drumsticks. Loosely. Between the thumb and the first two fingers. And bringing them down on the wood.
Tap.
Tap-tap.
Tap, tap, tap.
And saying, without looking up: That's halt. That's make ready. That's form on the colors. You want to learn them, Oren? I'll teach you. All of them. Then you'll know what the drum says.
And Oren had sat down across the table from him. And learned.
All winter. Every night. Harlan tapping, with the two spoons, and Oren tapping back, with his fingers, until his fingers knew them better than his head did. Until their mother came in from the yard one night in February and said If I hear one more tap from that table I shall go mad, and took both spoons out of Harlan's hands and opened the window and threw them out into the snow.
And Harlan had laughed.
The first time since he came home. Oren remembered that. He remembered his little brother's face cracking open at the scrubbed table, and the laugh coming out of it, high and surprised, as if it had been stuck in there for months. And their mother standing at the open window with the snow blowing in, laughing too, with her hand over her mouth.
He could see all of that.
He could see the spoons, going out of the window, turning over and over in the lamplight, and falling into the snow.
And he could see his brother's hands, coming down on the table. Tap. Tap-tap.
And he did not know what they said.
That was the hole. Right there. In the middle of everything else. He could see Harlan's hands on the table, and he could hear the sound, and there was nothing behind it. No meaning. Like a man speaking a language you have never learned. Like watching somebody's lips move through a window.
The calls had gone. The whole winter of them. Burned out of him in one night by the river, to raise a wall against three men with knives. And what was left was the kitchen, and the spoons, and his brother's laugh. All the parts that did not matter. All the parts he could not use.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
Oren sat on his heels in the hay room and listened to Lord Thorne's finger on the wood, and pressed the heels of his scarred hands into his eyes, hard, until he saw colors.
"I'm sorry, sir," he said. "I had it. I had it in my hand. And I threw it away."
The finger stopped.
Oren took his hands from his eyes.
Lord Thorne had not moved. His eyes were still on the boards of the roof. But his finger was still. It lay on the wood beside the mattress, quiet, for the first time Oren could remember in daylight. One breath. Two. Three.
And then it lifted, very slowly. And came down once.
Tap.
One.
The way it had come down once for Lyra, on Friday, when she had tapped the numbers back to him on his palm. Oren had been there. He had seen it. One tap, and then ten breaths of silence, and then the old count again.
He did not know what one meant either.
He sat and waited, and counted the silence, the way Lyra had. Four breaths. Seven. Ten.
And the finger began again.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
A Day of Waiting
The day went by the way days go by on a barge in winter. Slowly, and all at once.
The light came up gray and stayed gray. It never got brighter than the light inside a pewter mug. The sky lay low over the canal like a lid, white-gray and heavy and still, and there was no wind, not a breath, so that the smoke from the cabin chimney went straight up in a thin blue thread and stood there. The frost did not melt. It stayed on everything, all day. On the rail, on the hay, on the landing stage, on the black willows along the road. When Oren touched the rail with his bare hand at noon, it was as cold as it had been at dawn.
"Snow," said Hessa, looking at the sky. "Tonight or tomorrow. A lot of it."
She said it the way she said everything, short and flat, with her fists on her hips. She was a small square woman, Hessa Doon, not much taller than Juno, in a man's coat with the sleeves turned back and a man's knitted cap pulled down over her ears and a skirt hitched up into her belt at one side so that she could climb. She had a broad red face, chapped across the cheekbones, and very pale blue eyes, the color of ice on a pond. Oren had never seen her sit down in daylight. She did not seem to need to.
"Is that bad?" said Oren.
"It's good and bad." She spat over the rail onto the ice. "Good, because nobody comes up a canal road in deep snow if they don't have to. Not even gray coats. Bad, because if it comes deep, the canal freezes hard, and then we're not going anywhere either." She looked at the empty landing stage. "Not with a split rudder pin."
The rudder pin was not split.
Oren knew that. He had watched her take it out on Sunday morning, before the others left, and wrap it in a rag, and put it under her bunk. A long iron pin as thick as a man's thumb, perfectly good, with nothing wrong with it at all. Without it the rudder hung loose, and the Patient Grace could not be steered, and so she could not go. And so, if any bargeman or farmer or gray coat asked why the Patient Grace was still at Brindle when the other six had gone, there was a reason. A good one. Everyone on the canal knew a split rudder pin when they heard of one. And everyone knew that the smith at Withy Reach was slow.
"When the boy comes back," Hessa had said, putting the rag under the bunk, "and the girl, and we know where we stand, the pin goes back in. And we go north. Withy Reach, and the peat cuttings, and the old turf barns past them. Four days. Nobody goes there in winter. Not even the peat men."
When the boy comes back.
That had been on Sunday.
They had heard nothing since Friday night, when the hay wain went up the ramp with Corin and Lyra hidden under its canvas. Nothing at all. Not a word. Not a note. Ellis had brought the wain back from the city on Saturday afternoon, empty, and come down the ramp, and stood on the landing stage, and said only: Got them to the Bridewell. Dropped them at the stable yard before the light. They went off on foot. And that was all. That was the last anybody had heard.
Three days.
Oren did not like it. He had not liked it on Saturday, and he liked it a great deal less on Monday. Three days was a long time for a man with a hole in his leg and a WANTED notice on every wall in the Lower Town, and a girl with the face of the Thorne house, to be in a city full of gray coats, and not send word.
But there was nothing to do about it. That was the worst part. A guard can stand at a door. A guard can stand in front of a man with a sword. A guard cannot stand in front of somebody fourteen miles away.
So he stood on the deck of the Patient Grace and looked at the road.
Wat came up at noon.
He came slowly, up the three steep steps from the cabin, one at a time, with his left hand on the rail and his right arm held tight against his chest in a sling. He was a long, rawboned, quiet man with a gray-brown beard and a face like a closed door. He had taken a boat hook in the shoulder four days ago, under the deck of a dead ship on the Saltmarsh, putting his own body between Corin and a gray coat with a pike. Hessa had sewn him up with seven stitches in the front and five in the back, by the light of a candle, on the cabin table, with Wat lying across it face down and not saying a word.
He came and stood beside Oren at the rail, and looked at the road too.
They did not talk. They did not need to. They had stood like this for an hour every day since Thursday. Two big quiet men at a rail, looking at a road. It was, Oren thought, the most restful company he had kept in years.
"Juno says you sent a note," said Wat, after a long while.
"To Moll. With Ellis. Tomorrow."
"About the tapping."
"Yes."
Wat thought about that.
"He was in the army," he said. "Moll. He told Corin. Twelve years. The Commonwealth Foot." He was quiet for a moment. "So was I. Four years. Before I went on the river."
Oren turned his head and looked at him.
"I didn't know that."
"Nobody does. Hessa does." Wat looked at the road. "I was a sapper. Digging. Trenches and mines and that. I never had much to do with the drums. You only heard them far off, over the top of the trench." He moved his good shoulder. "I knew halt, and fall back, and make ready. Everybody knew those. Four and two isn't any of them."
"No."
"Moll was Foot. Twelve years. He'd have marched to it. Every day." Wat paused. "He'd know."
"That's what I'm hoping."
They stood.
After a while, Wat said, without turning his head: "There's a boat."
Oren looked.
It was coming up the canal from the south. From the city end. A small black skiff, low in the water, with one man rowing and one man sitting in the stern. It came slowly. The man rowing was taking his time, long easy strokes, breaking the thin skin of new ice at the bow so that it cracked and tinkled and slid away on both sides like broken glass. The man in the stern had a fishing rod across his knees, and a round hat pulled down over his eyes, and a wooden box beside him, the kind fishermen keep their bait in. There was a lantern on a pole at the bow, not lit. A small square lantern with shutters on all four sides, painted red.
Oren watched it come.
It came up level with the Patient Grace. Not close. Out in the middle of the canal, where the ice was thinnest. The man in the stern did not look up. He sat with his hat over his eyes and his rod across his knees, as if he were asleep. The man rowing did not look up either. He pulled his long easy strokes, and the skiff slid past, and went on up the canal toward the north, toward Withy Reach. Breaking the ice.
Oren watched it until it went round the bend by the willows and out of sight.
"Fishing," said Wat. "In this."
"Pike," said Oren. "They say pike bite in the cold."
"Do they."
Neither of them said anything else about it. But Oren went on looking at the bend in the canal where the skiff had gone, for a long time after there was nothing there to see. And he did not know why. There had been nothing wrong with it. A man rowing, and a man fishing. A red lantern. He had seen a hundred skiffs like it on the city canals.
He thought it was something to do with the hat.
In the afternoon he went down into the cabin, to sit by the stove for a while and get the cold out of his hands, and he took out the paper.
He did not know why he did this either. He did it every day. It lived in the inside pocket of his coat, wrapped in a square of oilcloth to keep it dry. A single sheet of thick cream-colored paper, folded small, creased and smudged with soot, with one corner scorched brown where a spark had caught it. Corin had given it to him, in Vespera's room, before the sewers. That, Corin had said, I'm giving to you. And Oren had put it inside his coat and kept it there ever since, through the sewers and the Saltmarsh and the long cold days on the barge.
He unfolded it on his knee by the stove.
He could read it. He could still read; that was not on the list. I, Aurelian Thorne, being of sound mind. And then the rest of it, in a clerk's hand, line after line, giving away everything. All my papers, all my lands, all my rights in the Thorne name, and the keeping of my children. And near the bottom, the line that had made Lyra's hands shake when she read it: I remember that I have two children only. A son and a daughter. I remember there were never any others.
He read it through, slowly. Then he held it up close to his face, the way he did every day, and breathed in.
The smell was still there. Fainter now, after all the soot and the river and the cold. But there. Thick and sweet and heavy, like a room full of flowers with the windows shut. A white smell. A smell that went with violet ink and cream envelopes and a lady's gloved hand on a banister.
He reached for the name of it.
And found the gray place.
The smooth worn stone, where the word should be. He knew it was on the list. He knew that this was the one, the sixth, the last he had lost. He could feel the shape of the hole with his mind, the way you feel with your tongue for a tooth that has been pulled. He knew that the smell was important. That it meant a person. That Corin had gone quiet when he smelled it. And he could not say what it was.
He sat by the stove with the paper under his nose and his eyes shut.
"What is it?" said Juno, from her bunk.
"Nothing," said Oren.
He folded the paper in three again, along its old folds, and wrapped it in its oilcloth, and put it back inside his coat, against his chest.
He went back up on deck. The light was going. It went early, in the deep of winter; by the third bell after noon it was already blue in the hollows, and by the fourth the willows along the road were only black shapes against a sky like slate. The frost came down harder. Hessa lit the lantern on the stern pole. Robin was crying in the cabin, the tired crying of a baby at the end of the day, and Juno was singing to him, badly, out of tune, a song about a fox. Wat was splitting kindling on the deck by the hatch, one-handed, with a small axe, slowly. Chock. And chock.
And Oren stood at the rail and watched the road go dark.
He heard it before he saw it.
Wheels. Heavy ones. A cart, coming up the canal road from the south. From the city. Not fast. The slow grinding roll of a loaded cart on frozen ruts, and the clop of a single big horse, and the jingle of harness. And a voice. A man's voice, deep and hoarse, swearing at the horse with great tenderness.
Oren put his hand on his sword.
The cart came out of the dusk between the willows. A coal cart. Low and black and square, with high sides, piled with black sacks. A big brown horse in the shafts, with feathered feet and a blanket on its back. A man up on the box, wrapped to the eyes in a muffler, with a whip he was not using. And beside him, on the box, a second man.
Big. Broad. In a long dark coat with the collar up and a hat crammed down. With a heavy stick held upright between his knees. And one leg stuck out stiffly in front of him onto the footboard, at an angle a leg should not go.
The cart stopped at the top of the ramp.
The second man climbed down. Slowly. Badly. With the stick, and a grunt, and a word Oren had not heard since the barracks. He stood for a moment at the top of the ramp in the blue dusk, leaning on the stick, getting his breath.
Then he looked down at the barge. At Oren, standing at the rail.
And even in that light, even from there, Oren could see that the big boiled-ham face under the hat was gray.
