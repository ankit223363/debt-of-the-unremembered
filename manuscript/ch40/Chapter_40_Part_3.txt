Chapter 40: Drum Calls, Part 3 of 5: The Coal Cart
The Coal Cart
Moll came down the ramp like a man coming down a ladder in the dark. One step, and the stick, and a stop. One step, and the stick, and a stop. His bad leg went out stiff in front of him at each step, and the foot at the end of it turned out at its wrong angle, and slid a little on the frost, and he caught it with the stick and swore at it under his breath.
Oren went up the landing stage to meet him.
He did not hurry. He had learned that, in thirty years of guarding doors. A man who hurries toward a stranger at dusk makes the stranger afraid, and a frightened man is a dangerous one. He walked up the frosty planks at the pace of a man going to see who has come, with his hand resting on the hilt of his sword, not gripping it. And he stopped three paces off.
He had never met Moll. He had heard of him. From Corin, a great deal. From Juno, more. The sergeant. Big. Face like a boiled ham. Bad leg. Never runs. He knew him at once.
They looked at each other.
Moll was as tall as Oren, or nearly, and broader in the body, with a great barrel of a chest under the dark coat and a thick red neck under the muffler. His face under the crammed-down hat was wide and heavy and square, the kind of face that is usually red, with small sharp eyes set deep in it and a big nose broken long ago. It was not red now. It was gray. A bad gray, like old dough. There was sweat on his upper lip, in the frost.
"Captain Vance," said Moll.
"Sergeant."
"They said you'd be big." Moll looked him up and down. "They didn't say you'd be this big."
"They didn't say you'd be this gray."
Moll made a sound that was nearly a laugh.
"No," he said. "I don't suppose they did."
On the box of the coal cart, at the top of the ramp, the driver unwound his muffler from his face to spit. He was a little old man, very black, as black as a sweep, with coal dust in every line of his face and a white stubble on his chin, so that he looked like a lump of coal that had grown a beard. He spat over the side of the cart onto the frost and wound the muffler back on.
"That's Abner," said Moll, without turning round. "Abner Cole. He does the Brindle farms on a Monday. Coal for the farmhouse, and the bothy, and the smithy. Every Monday for twenty years. Nobody looks at Abner's cart." He drew a long breath. "I came out of the city under six sacks of best house coal, Captain. Lying on my back. With the coal dust going up my nose. Through two Watch posts and a Ministry toll. With Abner singing." A pause. "I'm too old for it."
"Why?"
"Why am I too old?"
"Why did you come?"
Moll looked past him. At the barge. At the black hull and the furled red sail and the great stack of hay, with the frost on it, and the thin blue thread of smoke going straight up from the cabin chimney into the dusk. At Wat, standing by the hatch with his axe in his good hand, watching. At the small square shape of Hessa coming up out of the cabin, wiping her hands on her skirt.
"Is that her?" he said. "Hessa Doon?"
"Yes."
"Then I'll tell it once," said Moll, "in front of all of you. Because there's no time to tell it twice." He shifted his weight on the stick. "But I'll tell it sitting down, if it's all the same to you, Captain. Before this leg of mine decides to go and sit down on its own in that canal."
They got him aboard.
It took Oren and Wat together, one on each side, and Wat had only one arm. They walked him along the landing stage and lifted him over the rail, and Moll swore the whole way, steadily, inventively, in a low hoarse voice, without once repeating himself. Hessa listened to it with her head on one side. When they had got him down onto the hatch cover and sat him on it, with his bad leg out straight in front of him on the deck and his stick across his knees, she looked at him for a long moment.
"Commonwealth Foot," she said.
Moll looked up at her.
"My father swore like that," said Hessa. "He was Foot. Twenty years. Same words. Same order." She folded her arms. "Tea?"
"Saints, yes."
She went below.
Abner Cole did not come down. He sat up on the box of his cart at the top of the ramp, with his big brown horse blowing white clouds in the shafts, and lit a short clay pipe, and smoked it, and looked at the sky. Oren saw the red coal of the pipe glow and fade, glow and fade, in the blue dusk. Watching the road. He had been told to, Oren thought. Or he did not need to be.
Hessa came back up with a tin mug. Moll took it in both his thick hands and drank half of it at once, though it was so hot it steamed in the frost like a kettle, and shut his eyes.
Wat sat down on an upturned bucket by the hatch. Hessa stood. Oren stood. Down in the cabin, through the open hatch, Robin had stopped crying, and Juno had stopped singing. The barge was very quiet.
From the middle of the hay, very faint, the tapping went on.
Moll opened his eyes.
"Three things," he said. "I'll give you the worst first. Then you'll know why I came under the coal." He put the mug down on the hatch beside him. "The Enforcer battalion marches in tonight."
Nobody moved.
"Four hundred men," said Moll. "From the barracks at Highmoor. For Midwinter. They come every year, a company or two, to stand on the bridges and look grand for the Archon's parade. Not this year. This year it's the whole battalion. With carts. And a commander." He looked at Oren. "The first two companies came in by the river road this morning. The rest come down the Highmoor road tonight, with the carts and the commander. The first lot were drilling on the Parade when I came out of the Inquisition at noon. In the snow-light. I stood at the top of the Inquisition steps with my stick, and watched them wheel. Four hundred men in black, wheeling like one man. You could hear the drums from the river."
Oren said nothing. He found that his hand had closed on the hilt of his sword.
"And there's an order," said Moll. "Posted on the Watch house board this morning, before I went up the Hill. With the Archon's seal. From tomorrow, the battalion holds every lock on every canal in and out of the city. Every one. From Saltgate to the Long Pond. Every barge, every skiff, every wherry and punt to be stopped and searched. Every hold to be opened. Every load to be turned out on the bank." A pause. "By order of the Commander of the Enforcer Battalion of the Ministry. Commander H. Vance."
The tapping went on in the hay.
"Hay," said Hessa. Her voice was flat. "Every load turned out on the bank."
"Every load."
"With pikes."
"Long ones," said Moll. "I saw them in the carts. Twelve foot. With hooks. For searching hay and straw and wool." He looked at the great stack beside him, white with frost, with its small gold room in the middle. "They've done this before, Hessa Doon. Somebody's told them what to look for."
Hessa did not say anything for a moment.
Then she turned round, and went down the three steep steps into the cabin, and Oren heard her drop to her knees by her bunk. He heard the scrape of something heavy being pulled out from underneath it. The clink of iron in a rag.
She came back up with the rudder pin in her hand.
"Wat," she said.
Wat was already getting up off the bucket.
"Tonight," said Hessa. "North. Withy Reach, and past it, and the peat cuttings. Before the snow. Before the locks." She looked at Moll. "How far do they come? North?"
"I don't know. The order said every canal in and out of the city." Moll shook his heavy head. "The Long Pond was the furthest it named. Brindle's past the Long Pond. Withy Reach is past Brindle. Maybe they don't come so far. Maybe not at first."
"Maybe," said Hessa. "Maybe's a good word. I'll take maybe over a pike." She hefted the rudder pin. "That's one. What's two?"
Moll looked at Oren.
"Two is about the boy," he said. "Corin."
Oren felt his chest go tight.
"He's alive," said Moll quickly. "He's alive. I'll tell you that first. I had it from Mrs. Marta, who had it from a girl called Nell, who came to the Watch house back door at the fifth bell this morning with a message from Mother Grue in the arches. He's alive, and the girl's with him, and she's not hurt." He paused. "But he's hurt bad. His leg. There was a doctor sent for. And there was something about the Bourse cellars, and a boy called Gideon who got bit by a dog, and I couldn't make head or tail of the rest of it, Nell was crying so hard." Another pause. "She said, Tell the barge he saw a face. Tell them he knows who it is now. And that was all she'd say."
Oren let out his breath.
Alive.
He had not known, until that moment, how much he had been waiting to hear that word. He stood on the deck of the barge with his hand on his sword and felt it go through him, from his chest down to his boots. Like warm water.
"And three?" he said.
Moll did not answer at once.
He picked up the tin mug from the hatch cover and looked into it. It was empty. He held it anyway, in both his big hands, and turned it round. Oren saw that his hands were shaking. Not much. The small hard shake of a man who has been very cold and very frightened, and is not yet either one any less.
"Three's about me," said Moll. "And I don't understand it. That's why I came, really. If I'm honest. Not the battalion. Hessa'd have found out about the battalion from the next bargeman up the canal tomorrow. Not the boy. You'd have had that by Wednesday." He looked up at Oren. "Three I came for. Because I've been an old soldier for a long time, Captain, and a Watch sergeant for a long time after that, and I've been frightened a few times in both. But I've never been frightened like I was this morning."
He put the mug down.
"This morning," said Moll, "at the tenth bell, in the Office of the Inquisition, a man sat across a desk from me and tapped his finger on the wood."
The tapping went on in the hay.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
Moll's head came up. Slowly. Like a dog's when it hears a step on the stair.
What the Inquisitor Tapped
But Moll did not ask about it. Not yet. He sat on the hatch cover with his head up, listening, for the space of perhaps three breaths. Then he shook himself, the way a big dog shakes off water, and looked down at his hands, and went on. As if he had decided that he had not heard it. Or that he would not let himself hear it until he had finished.
Oren let him.
"The Office of the Inquisition," said Moll, "is on the Hill. Behind the Ministry of Records. A gray building. No windows on the ground floor. Just a door, with two men on it, and a brass plate, and a lot of steps." He moved his bad leg on the deck. "Forty-one steps. I counted them. With this leg, you count every one."
He had gone up them at a quarter to the tenth bell, he said, in his best coat, with his hat brushed and his boots blacked and his papers of service from the Commonwealth Foot in an oilcloth packet inside his coat. The men on the door had looked at the summons with the red seal and let him in without a word. Inside, a long hall, tiled in gray, very cold. A bench along one wall. Men on the bench, and women, waiting, with summonses in their hands, not looking at each other. A clerk at a high desk at the end, writing. A clock. Nothing else. No fire.
"I sat on the bench for an hour," said Moll. "Next to a woman who sold pies on Comb Street. She'd been summoned for selling a pie to a man on a broadsheet. She didn't know he was on a broadsheet. She cried the whole hour. Quiet. Into her shawl." He was silent for a moment. "They called her in before me. She didn't come out by the same door."
Then they had called him.
Up a stair. Along a corridor. Into a room at the end. Small and plain, with whitewashed walls and a tall narrow window, and a table, and two chairs, one on each side. No fire here either. A man sitting at the table, behind a single sheet of paper, with his hands folded on it.
"Tall," said Moll. "Even sitting. Thin. Gray coat, very plain, very good. Hair going gray. A pleasant face. That's the thing I can't get out of my head, Captain. How pleasant it was. Like a schoolmaster who likes boys. Pale eyes. Washed-out, like a sky after rain." He stopped. "He didn't give his name. He didn't need to. I've been at the Rope Street desk for twenty years. I know who sends the cloth back folded."
"Kross," said Hessa.
Moll nodded.
"He asked me to sit," he said. "He asked me if I'd like tea. I said no. He asked me about my leg, very kind. He asked me how long I'd been at Rope Street, and whether I liked it, and whether Mrs. Marta's bread was really as good as everybody said." Moll's mouth went hard. "He knows she's staying with me. He knows all of it. He wanted me to know he knew."
"And the papers?" said Oren.
"He asked for them. I gave them to him. My papers of service. Twelve years in the Commonwealth Foot, and an honorable discharge, and the colonel's seal at the bottom, and a bit about the leg." Moll looked at his hands. "He read them. Slowly. All of it. Every line. Twice. And while he was reading them—"
He stopped.
Oren waited.
"While he was reading them," said Moll, "he tapped."
He lifted his own thick right hand from his knee and laid it flat on the hatch cover beside him. And with his forefinger, slowly, softly, on the frosty wood, he showed them.
Four.
Two.
Nine.
Three.
"Like that," said Moll. "On the table. Just with the tip of one finger. While he read. Not looking at me. Not looking at his hand. The way a man does when he's thinking, and doesn't know he's doing it." His voice was very low. "Except he did know. He knew exactly. Because when he got to the end of the line, he stopped reading. And he didn't look up. And he did it again. Four, two, nine, three. And waited."
Nobody on the deck moved.
In the hay, very faint, the old man's finger went on. Four. Two. Nine. Three. Moll did not turn his head toward it. Oren saw the effort it cost him not to.
"I sat still," said Moll. "I've been sitting still in front of officers for thirty years. I sat as still as a post. I looked at the window. I thought about Mrs. Marta's bread. I thought, It's nothing. It's a man tapping on a table. I didn't move my face. Not a muscle. I know I didn't."
He looked down at his right hand on the hatch cover.
"But my finger moved," he said.
Oren did not breathe.
"On my knee," said Moll. "Under the table. Where he couldn't see. I didn't tell it to. I didn't even know until I felt it. It just came down. Once." He lifted the finger from the wood, and let it fall. Tap. "Like that. Once. The way it's been doing in my sleep since the summer. The way it did for old Terrence on Thursday night, when he said three at the fourth bell." He shut his eyes. "And he heard it."
"Through the table?" said Wat.
"Through the floor, maybe. Through the wood. I don't know. I don't know how. But he heard it." Moll opened his eyes. "He didn't look up. He didn't change his face. He just smiled. A little. At the paper. The way you'd smile at a letter from someone you were fond of. And he folded up my papers of service. Small. Very small. In half, and in half again, and corner to corner. And put them in his own pocket."
Hessa made a sound in her throat.
"And he said," said Moll, "Thank you, Sergeant. That will be all for today. We'll keep these. You'll come back on Thursday, at the same hour. And then, when I was at the door, with my hand on it, he said one more thing. Very pleasant. Not loud." Moll's voice changed. Went smooth and quiet and a little amused. It was a good imitation. It made the back of Oren's neck go cold. "And Sergeant. Do bring your memory with you."
The deck was silent.
Up at the top of the ramp, the red coal of Abner Cole's pipe glowed and faded in the dark.
"I walked down forty-one steps," said Moll. "With my leg. And I stood at the bottom and watched four hundred men in black wheel on the Parade to the drums. And I thought, he let me go. Like he let the boy go, on Wednesday night, in the sewers. Corin told Mrs. Marta. Kross let me run, to see where I'd run to." He looked at Oren. "And I thought, I mustn't go anywhere. Not to the arches. Not to the barge. Not anywhere I'd lead him. I should go back to the Watch house and sit at my desk and drink my tea and wait for Thursday."
"But you came," said Oren.
"I came under six sacks of coal, through two Watch posts and a Ministry toll, on a cart that's been doing the same round every Monday for twenty years." Moll's jaw set. "I'm not a fool, Captain. Nobody followed Abner's cart. I lay under the coal and listened to the road behind us the whole way. Nothing. No hooves. No wheels. Nobody."
Oren thought of a black skiff, sliding up the middle of the canal in the afternoon, breaking the ice. A man in the stern with a round hat over his eyes and a fishing rod across his knees. A small square red lantern on a pole, not lit.
He did not say anything.
He did not know why he did not say anything. Only that there was nothing to say. A man fishing. A red lantern. Pike bite in the cold.
"I came," said Moll, "because of the battalion, and the locks, and the pikes. Because if I sat at my desk and drank my tea while they turned out Hessa Doon's hay on the bank with twelve-foot hooks, I'd never sleep again." He paused. "And because of what my finger did. Because I've been tapping those numbers in my sleep for six months, since the summer, since before any of this, and I don't know why. And this morning a man in the Office of the Inquisition tapped them at me across a table, and I answered him. And I don't know why. And I wanted—"
He stopped.
In the hay, the tapping had not stopped. It went on. Four, two, nine, three. And the long rest. And again.
And Moll could not keep his head turned away from it any longer.
He turned it. Slowly. Toward the great frosted stack of hay beside the hatch. And sat there on the hatch cover with his bad leg out on the deck and his stick across his knees, and listened. Oren watched his face while he did it. He watched it go from gray to something that was not a color at all. The color of a candle. The color of a man who has seen something walk out of his own grave.
"Who's that?" said Moll.
His voice had gone to almost nothing.
"Who's that, Captain? In the hay? Who's tapping?"
"Lord Thorne," said Oren.
Moll stared at him.
"Lord Aurelian Thorne," said Oren. "Of Crown Walk. Who burned his own mind two years ago in his study, on a winter night, and has tapped those four numbers on the wall every day since. Every day. All day. For two years." He held Moll's eyes. "He's in a room in the middle of the hay. He doesn't speak. He doesn't know us. He doesn't know his own daughter. He only taps."
Moll did not say anything.
He sat on the hatch cover and stared at the hay. His lips moved. No sound came out. His thick right hand lay on the frosty wood beside him, and Oren saw the forefinger lift. And hang there in the air, trembling, over the wood. Waiting. The way a man's hand hangs over a door he is afraid to knock on.
"Take me to him," said Moll.
Four, Two, Nine, Three
He could not go through the tunnel. That was clear at once.
Oren took him to the hatch and showed him the ladder, and the hold, and the dark square mouth behind the loose bale at the bottom, and Moll looked at it for a long moment with his stick in his hand and his bad leg stuck out in front of him at its wrong angle. Then he looked at Oren.
"Captain," he said, "I couldn't get through that if you greased me and pushed."
"No."
"Is there another way?"
Oren opened his mouth to say no. And Hessa, behind him, said, "Yes."
They turned.
She was standing by the great stack of hay, amidships, with her fists on her hips, looking up at it. Not at the side. At the top.
"My grandfather," she said, "was a careful man. He knew the gray coats might come one day and stick pikes in his hay. But he knew something else could happen too. Something worse." She looked at Moll. "He knew the tunnel might fall in. With somebody in the room. And then you'd have a man shut in a box in the middle of thirty ton of hay, with no way out but to dig, and no air." She jerked her chin up at the stack. "So he made a lid."
She climbed.
She went up the side of the stack the way Juno went up a wall. Fast and sure, with her toes in the gaps between the bales and her fingers hooked over the edges, and her skirt hitched up into her belt. At the top she stood up, small and square against the dark sky, with the frost glittering round her boots, and walked out across the top of the hay to the middle. And knelt. And began to pull bales.
Oren went up after her. It was not as easy for him. By the time he got to the top his hands were full of hay and his breath was loud, and the cold up there, out of the shelter of the rail, bit at his face like teeth. Hessa had already dragged two bales aside. She was kneeling over a square of black between them, no bigger than the top of a barrel.
No. Not black. Gold.
The candlelight came up out of the hole. Faint and warm and yellow, out of the middle of the hay, into the blue dark. And with it, a breath of warm air, smelling of hay and tallow and old man.
Oren knelt beside her and looked down.
There was a trap. A square of old boards, set into the roof of the little room, with an iron ring in it. Hessa had already lifted it back on its leather hinges. And through the square hole, a body-length below, he could see the inside of the hay room, the way you see the inside of a lantern from the top. The red shawl and the patched blanket on the walls. The candle on its shelf. The mattress, and the blankets on it.
And Lord Thorne's face, looking up.
Looking straight up through the trap, at the sky. At the two dark shapes kneeling at the edge of the hole against the stars. His eyes were open. The candle lit one side of his face gold and left the other in shadow. His lips were a little parted.
His finger was still tapping on the floor-board.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
"Get the sergeant," said Hessa.
It took a long time.
Oren and Wat and Hessa did it in the end with a rope, and a plank, and Moll's language, and a great deal of patience. They made a sort of ramp out of the plank up the side of the stack, and tied the rope round Moll under his arms, and Oren went up first and pulled, and Wat pushed from below with his one good arm, and Hessa went up beside Moll the whole way, putting his good foot into the gaps between the bales for him, one gap at a time, the way you would teach a child to climb a ladder. Moll did not stop swearing once. It was low and steady and almost tender, the way he had sworn at the horse. Oren did not think he knew he was doing it.
And then he was at the top.
He lay on his belly on the frosty hay with his bad leg out straight behind him, gasping, his face gray and running with sweat in the cold. Oren and Hessa got him by the shoulders and turned him, and drew him forward, slowly, across the top of the bales, until his head and shoulders were at the edge of the square hole. And he could look down.
He looked down.
Oren knelt beside him and watched him do it. He would remember that for the rest of his life, he thought. Whatever else he lost. Whatever else he had to burn. He would try to keep that.
The big gray face, hanging over the edge of the hole in the dark, with the candlelight coming up into it from below and turning it gold. The small sharp eyes, deep in the heavy face, looking down. The thick hands, flat on the hay on either side of the hole, gripping the stalks. The breath going out of him in white clouds into the gold light, and the gold light coming up through the white.
And below him, a body-length below, Lord Aurelian Thorne, looking up.
They looked at each other. The sergeant from Rope Street and the lord from Crown Walk. Through a square hole in a stack of hay, on a frozen canal, at the beginning of a winter night.
The finger tapped.
Four.
Moll's whole body went rigid on the hay.
Oren felt it. He was kneeling so close that his knee touched the sergeant's shoulder. He felt the big body go stiff under the coat, all at once, the way a man goes stiff when he is struck by a bolt from a crossbow. Every muscle. Every joint.
Two.
Moll made a sound.
It was not a word. It came out of him low and deep, from the bottom of his barrel chest. A sound like a bullock makes in a field at night, when something is wrong and it does not know what.
Nine.
His face. Oren looked at his face, in the gold light coming up out of the hole. And he saw what Mrs. Marta had seen, Juno said, in the Watch house kitchen on the day the gray man came to Rope Street and went away again, when the sergeant stood in the doorway with his hands shaking at his sides. The face of a man who had been told something that could not be untold.
It went white.
Not gray. Not pale. White. The way a face goes white in the snow. The way a face goes white in the moment just before a man faints. All the blood went out of it at once, out of the heavy cheeks and the broken nose and the thick lips, and left it the color of tallow, of the candle in the hay room. Even his lips went white. Even the tip of his nose.
Three.
And the long pause.
In the pause, nobody moved. Not Oren. Not Hessa, kneeling on the other side of the hole with her hand on the lifted trap. Not Moll. Not the old man below, looking up. The candle flame stood quite still on its shelf. A single stalk of hay drifted down from the edge of the hole, turning over and over in the gold light, and settled on the blanket beside Lord Thorne's tapping hand.
And from Moll's white lips, in a whisper so faint that Oren had to bend close to hear it, there came a word.
Not a name. Not a question.
A word Oren had not heard in twenty years. Not since a scrubbed kitchen table, and a boy in an army coat too big at the shoulders, and two spoons.
He did not know what it meant. The meaning was gone. Burned out of him by the river. But he knew the sound of it. He knew it the way you know a voice you have not heard since you were a child, calling you in from the yard. And something in him sat up, the way an old dog sits up when it hears its master's step.
"Witness," whispered Moll.
