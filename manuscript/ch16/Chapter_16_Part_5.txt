Chapter 16: The Girl with Wrong Tattoos, Part 5 of 5: The Other Two
The Other Two
"The red-haired one," said Vespera. "And the one asleep."
Nobody had said anything for a long time. Hester had stopped talking. She sat curled in the big leather chair with the purple shawl pulled up to her chin, and her eyes had begun to close, slowly, the way a child's eyes close after a long cry, and open again, and close. Juno had sat down on the floor by the chair, close, with her back against the side of it, without being asked. Bet Mallow stood by the stove with her arms folded. The jar on the table glowed.
And Vespera sat on her stool and looked at the wall, and said it.
"The one asleep had writing on her too. Hester said so. All over her arms, like hers. Only hers wasn't moving." She was quiet a moment. "Because it had taken. Because there was no little tree in hers. Because it went down into her all night and grew its roots and wrapped round her and she swallowed it whole."
She turned her head and looked at Corin.
"That girl woke up this morning," she said, "with clean arms. Clean as milk. And a clear face. And she knows, the way she knows her own name, that she was on the steps under the Lantern Bridge on the night the doctor drowned. Looking for her cat. And she saw a girl with short black hair and a sword. And she heard her laugh."
Corin did not answer.
"And she's frightened," said Vespera. "Because she never told anyone. Because she was too scared. But now she must. Because it's right." Her voice was quite flat. "That's the last line. That's what he wrote on her. But now I must, because it is right. It's not just a story, Corin. It's an order. It tells her what to do with the story. Where to take it."
"The Watch," said Corin.
"The Watch. Or the Ministry. Or a broadsheet, if she can't find either." Vespera looked at the window, at the line of gray light round the edges of the closed shutter. It had gone pale and yellow. The afternoon was ending. "She's had all day. Since sunrise. If they put her out on a street corner at dawn and let her walk."
"And the red-haired one?"
Vespera did not answer that.
She did not need to. Corin saw it in her face. A little one, Hester had said. Crying for her mam. With nothing on her arms yet.
Waiting her turn.
Bet Mallow moved first.
She uncrossed her enormous red arms and went to the leather chair and bent down and looked at Hester's face. The girl's eyes had closed. Her head had fallen sideways against the cracked leather wing of the chair, and her mouth had come a little open, and she was breathing slowly and deeply, the way you breathe when you have not slept for a night and a day and have finally been allowed to.
"She's coming with me," said Bet.
Vespera looked up.
"To the Saltings," said Bet. "Tonight. To my sister's. Over the smoke-house on Herring Row." She said it the way you would say the sun will come up tomorrow. "She can't go back to Lye Street. Not tonight. Not any night. That big onion-eater knows where she lives. He said her name in a doorway." Her small blue eyes were very hard. "So she's going where nobody's ever going to come and look for her. Among forty fishwives and their husbands and their sons, on a street where everyone knows everyone, and a stranger can't walk ten yards without six women asking him his business with a gutting knife in their hands."
"Her aunt—" said Corin.
"I'll send to her aunt. My girl's boy'll take a message. Hester's safe. Hester's working for Bet Mallow now, at the smoke-house, for better money than Murdock's." Bet sniffed. "Which she will be, if she wants it. I could do with arms like a bargeman."
She bent and gathered up the sleeping girl out of the leather chair, purple shawl and all, as easily as Gorse had lifted her out of the doorway on Lye Street. Hester did not wake. She murmured something, and turned her face into Bet's shoulder, against the stiff fish-scaly canvas of the apron, and went on sleeping.
Bet looked down at her for a moment. Then she looked at Vespera, over the girl's head.
"My barrow's in the side passage," she said. "Under the sacks again. Like this morning. I'll go down the lane past the same forty people, and they'll see Bet Mallow with her barrow full of sacks, same as always, and not one of them'll look twice."
"Bet," said Vespera.
The big fishwife waited.
"Thank you," said Vespera.
Bet Mallow snorted. It was a huge snort, like a horse.
"Don't thank me, Ink-Witch," she said. "You can do me a tattoo. On the house. My Jem's name. He went out with the herring fleet in the autumn of the bad storm, and he never came back, and some mornings I can't remember if his eyes were brown or green." Her voice did not change at all. "I'd like to have that back. Somewhere I can see it."
Vespera looked at her for a long moment.
"Green," she said. "Come on Sunday. I'll find it."
And Bet Mallow went out by the little low door into the narrow passage, carrying the sleeping girl, and they heard the creak of the barrow, and the rustle of the sacks, and the slow heavy squeak of the wheel going away down the side of the shop toward the lane.
Vespera got up off her stool.
She took the jar from the table. The jar of glowing purple dust. She held it in both hands for a moment, against her chest, the way Corin had held the first Ledger on Bram's rag rug. Then she went to the shelf at the back of the room, the one with the old dark bottles on it, and reached up, high, to the very top, and moved three bottles aside, and set the jar behind them, in the shadow, where its glow could only just be seen. And moved the bottles back.
"He'll know," she said, with her back to them. "Hollis. Sooner or later. That somebody drew his ink out of that girl. There's only one Binder in this city who could." She did not turn round. "And then he'll know I've seen what he fed it with."
"Will he come?" said Corin.
"I don't know." Vespera turned round. Her face was gray and tired and very old. "I don't know anything about him anymore. I thought I did. Ten years, I've been thinking I knew exactly what he was." She looked at the shelf, at the faint purple glow behind the bottles. "And it turns out he's been carrying me round inside him the whole time. And burning me. A piece at a time. Every time he did something he knew I'd hate."
She was quiet for a moment.
"That's not a man who's forgotten," she said. "That's a man who can't."
Corin wrote it down.
He sat on the floor by the empty leather chair, in the last of the yellow light from the burned-down lamps, and wrote it as fast as he could, before any of it could go. The girl in the chair. The moving letters, like ants. Mary Coyle, who was not Mary Coyle. The lines on her arms, every one. The hook on the g. Hollis Marsh, called Needle. Twelve years at Vespera's table. A purse of gold. Who says what's true, Vespera? The ruler, and the soup, and the ladder. Night, Hollis. Night, Vespera. A jar of purple dust, glowing, behind three bottles on a high shelf.
The little tree. He drew it again, bigger, so he would know it anywhere.
A white room smelling of wet plaster and cloves. A silk screen with herons. A lady with a kind voice and the smell of lilies. Don't be frightened, darling. A pencil drawing of Lyra, so good it was as if she were there.
A paper on a stand, in big black letters, all the same size, like soldiers.
A man's voice behind the screen that went up and down like a hymn. Copy it exactly, Needle. Every mark.
Gorse. Take her down to the river. The tide weighs it, and it comes up light.
Two girls on a bench. One asleep, with writing that did not move. One crying for her mam.
And at the bottom, in big letters, so hard that the pencil went through:
THEY ARE MAKING WITNESSES. TO SAY LYRA KILLED DOCTOR ASKE.
ONE OF THEM IS ALREADY WALKING.
He stopped, with the pencil on the paper.
Somebody wrote the paper, he thought. The paper Hollis copied. In letters all the same size. And put a northern truth-mark in it, in the last line. And either they didn't know what it would do, or…
Or they did.
He sat very still.
Malakor, he said. Not aloud. Inside. In the place where the voice in the rod could hear him, the way it always could.
The rod was silent for a moment.
Then, very dry, very thin: Yes.
The man behind the screen. The one whose voice went up and down.
An Ostmark voice, said Malakor. I have heard them. Long ago. They cannot help it. In the mountains they teach the children songs before they teach them words, so that when they learn to speak at last, every word comes out with a tune still in it. They say you can hear an Ostmarker's voice sing even when he is cursing you. A pause. Or giving orders behind a silk screen.
Would an Ostmarker put a truth-mark in a lie by mistake?
The rod was silent for a long time.
No, said Malakor at last. Not by mistake. Never by mistake. That letter is the first thing their children learn to write. Before their own names. They would sooner cut off their hand than put it on a lie.
Corin looked down at his drawing of the little tree.
Then he meant it to fail, he said.
Then, said Malakor softly, somebody in that white room does not want your Thorne girl hanged. And is very, very careful about how they say so.
"Juno," said Corin.
She was up before he had finished the word. She had been sitting by the empty chair with her knees drawn up, watching him write. She was on her feet now with her cap on and her coat buttoned, and her sharp face was set.
"Linden Walk," she said.
"Linden Walk. As fast as you can. Over the Dye-Works. Tell Lyra—" He stopped. What did you tell somebody? Somebody is making girls believe they saw you kill a man? "Tell her I'm coming. Tell her not to go out. Not anywhere. Not for anyone. And tell the captain to bar the gate."
"Bar the gate," said Juno. "Don't go out. You're coming." She was already at the little low door. She stopped with her hand on the latch and looked back at him. "Mister V. Is it true? What they wrote on her? About Miss Lyra?"
"No," said Corin. "Not one word."
Juno looked at him for a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, short and sharp, and was gone. He heard her boots in the side passage, fast, and then nothing.
Corin got up off the floor. His legs were stiff. His arm, from holding the lamp, ached from the shoulder to the fingers. He put the Ledger inside his coat.
Vespera was standing by the stove with her stained hands held a little away from her body, looking at the empty leather chair.
"Go," she said, without looking at him. "I'll be here." A pause. "I'm always here."
"Vespera—"
"Go, Corin." She turned her head. Her small dark eyes in her gray face were wet again, but quite steady. "Go and look after your Thorne girl. Somebody should look after somebody, in this city. Somebody should get there in time."
He went.
The Broadsheet
The sun was going down when he came out at the top of Hook Lane.
It went down red. A deep, dull, smoky red, like a coal in a grate, sinking slowly into the haze over the far end of the river, where the masts of the ships stood up against it in black lines like the teeth of a comb. The snow on the roofs had gone pink. The slush in the streets had gone the color of weak tea. And the cold was coming back. He could feel it, as he walked, coming up out of the ground and down out of the sky at the same time, closing on the city like two hands. The puddles along the gutters had begun to skin over with ice. The drips off the icicles had slowed, and stopped. By midnight, he thought, everything that had melted today would be frozen again, harder than before.
He walked fast. He did not run. A man running in a Ministry coat made people look. He walked as fast as he could without running, with his collar up and his hands in his pockets and his head down, along the riverfront road, toward the Lantern Bridge.
The lanterns on the bridge were being lit. A man was going along it with a long pole, from post to post, and as he went the little blue lights came on one after another behind him, across the river, in the red dusk, like a row of cold stars coming out.
At the foot of the bridge, on the corner, there was a boy.
He was small and thin and very loud, in a cap too big for him and a sack over his shoulders against the cold, standing on an upturned crate with a great bundle of printed papers under one arm and another one held up in his free hand, flapping, over his head. Broadsheets. Corin had seen boys like him on every corner of the city, in his Ledger. He had never stopped for one. Not once, the Ledger said. You don't buy broadsheets. They are all lies, and they cost a copper.
The boy was shouting.
"Doctor's drowning no accident!" he yelled, at the top of his thin cracked voice, to the people hurrying past him in the red dusk. "Doctor Aske MURDERED! Witness comes forward! Young woman says I SAW HER FACE! Read all about it! Watch seek lady of the High Quarter! Read all about it! One copper!"
Corin stopped.
He stopped in the middle of the pavement, so suddenly that a man behind him walked into his back and swore at him and went round. He did not notice. He stood in the slush at the foot of the Lantern Bridge with the blue lanterns coming on over his head, and looked at the boy on the crate, and at the paper flapping in the boy's hand, and the cold that was closing on the city closed on him too, all at once, from the inside.
One of them is already walking.
He went to the crate. He took a copper out of his pocket, one of his last five, and held it up. The boy snatched it, and bit it, and stuffed a broadsheet into his hand without looking at him, and went on shouting.
"Doctor Aske MURDERED! I SAW HER FACE!"
Corin walked away from the noise. Along the river wall, a little way, to where a lamp-post stood alone in the dusk with its blue light just coming on, and he stood under it, and unfolded the paper, and read.
It was cheap paper. Gray and thin and still damp from the press, so that the ink came off on his gloves. The letters were big and black and smudged, and they went across the top of the page in a great shout.
THE DOCTOR WAS MURDERED.
WITNESS: "I SAW HER FACE."
And under that, smaller, in columns, the way broadsheets always were:
A young woman of the Cut came forward this afternoon at the Watch house on Rope Walk, to tell the Sergeant of the Watch that the late Doctor Matthias Aske, physician, whose body was taken from the river under the Lantern Bridge two autumns ago, and whose death was at that time ruled an accident, did not die by misadventure, but was struck down and drowned by a young lady.
The witness, Miss Agnes Fell, sixteen years of age, a laundress, gave her account to the Sergeant with great feeling and in some distress. She said that she had been on the landing beneath the bridge on the night in question, looking for her cat, and had seen the deed with her own eyes by the light of a lantern. She said she had told no one, for two years, from fear. "But now I must," she said, "because it is right."
Corin's hand had begun to shake. He held the paper with both hands.
Miss Fell described the young lady as of middle height, slender, with short black hair cut close like a boy's, and gray eyes, and said that she carried a sword. She said that the young lady struck the doctor with the hilt of the sword and he fell into the water, and that the young lady laughed, and said: "That is for my father."
The Sergeant of the Watch, having heard Miss Fell, is understood to be seeking for questioning a young lady of the High Quarter, of a family lately fallen into misfortune, whose father is known to have been under the care of the late doctor at the time of his death.
The Watch has not named the young lady.
It is not thought that they need to.
And at the bottom of the column, in a little box of its own, with a line round it:
Councillor Cressida Thorne, of No. 11 Crown Walk, on hearing the news this afternoon, was said to be deeply distressed. "My poor girl," the Councillor is reported to have said. "Whatever she has done, she has been so very unhappy." Councillor Thorne has offered the Watch every assistance.
Corin read it twice.
Then he folded it, very carefully, in half, and in half again, and put it inside his coat, against the Ledger.
And then he ran.
He did not care who looked. He ran along the riverfront road in the red dusk with his gray coat flapping round his legs and his boots slipping in the freezing slush, and people jumped out of his way, and a carter shouted at him, and a dog ran after him barking for half a street and then gave up. He ran up the hill from the river. He ran along Linden Walk, with its tall quiet houses and its bare trees in a row, black against the dying sky. He ran so hard that his chest hurt and the cold air tore at the inside of his throat like a knife and his eyes streamed.
At the top of the crooked lane that went down to Pruett's yard, he stopped.
Not because he wanted to. Because there was a carriage there.
The same carriage. Black, with its blinds down, standing at the top of the lane by the curb, where it had stood yesterday morning, with its two black horses blowing white clouds into the cold. There was no one on the box. No big man eating pies. The driver was gone. The carriage stood there empty, waiting, with its lamps unlit.
Corin looked at it for a long moment, with his chest heaving.
Then he went down the lane.
The tall green gate at the bottom was shut. He had never seen it shut. It had always stood open, with the path shoveled through the snow. Now it was closed, and barred. He could hear the bar. He put his hand on the gate and pushed, and it did not move, and from the other side something heavy shifted against it, and a deep slow gravel voice said, "Who."
"Vane."
The bar came off. The gate opened the width of a man. And Captain Oren Vance stood in the gap with a lantern in one hand and his short heavy sword naked in the other, filling the gap from side to side, with his scarred face red in the lantern light and his gray eyes very cold.
He looked past Corin, up the lane, at the black carriage at the top. Then he stood aside.
"In," he said. "Quick."
Corin went in, and Oren shut the gate behind him and dropped the bar across it with a crash.
Lyra was in the long room over the stable.
She was standing at the plain wooden table under the dark beams, with the lamp lit, and the stove going, and Juno beside her. Juno's face was white and furious. Lyra's was not anything. It was quite calm. Quite still. The face of someone standing very straight in a room where the floor has just begun to tilt.
On the table in front of her, there was a broadsheet.
The same one. Gray and smudged and damp. THE DOCTOR WAS MURDERED. WITNESS: "I SAW HER FACE."
She looked up when he came in.
"Mister Pruett brought it," she said. "Half an hour ago. He bought it from a boy on Linden Walk on his way home. He came up the stairs with it in his hand and he couldn't speak. He just gave it to me and went down again." She looked down at it. "He's been crying in the hay store ever since. Patience is with him."
Corin did not say anything. He came to the table and stood across it from her.
"Juno told me," said Lyra. "About the girl. Hester. About the ink. About what was written on her." She put one finger on the broadsheet, on the words short black hair cut close like a boy's. "About this."
"Lyra—"
"I've never been on those steps," said Lyra. Very quietly. "Not in my life. Not until yesterday. Not for one moment." She did not look up. "I thought Doctor Aske had gone to the country, like Bertram, like everyone, until you found him in the Ministry's death book. And I didn't know how he died until two days ago, when you told me what Bertram saw." Her finger moved on the paper. That is for my father. "And now a girl I've never met has stood in the Watch house on Rope Walk and told them she saw me do it. And she believes it. Doesn't she. Juno says she'll believe it till the day she dies."
"Yes," said Corin.
"So it doesn't matter what I say."
He did not answer that.
Lyra looked at the broadsheet for a long time. Then she put her finger on the little box at the bottom. On the words inside it.
My poor girl. Whatever she has done, she has been so very unhappy.
"She'll come and see me," said Lyra. "In the cells. Won't she. She'll come down with a basket. Cake, and a clean blanket, and a cup of chocolate. And she'll sit on the other side of the bars and hold my hand through them and call me darling." Her voice was perfectly level. "And on Monday, while I'm in a cell, the carriage will come for Father. And there'll be nobody here but Oren and Mrs. Hobb to stand in the yard and watch it go." She paused. "And she'll say, Of course, darling, I could speak to the Sergeant. It's all a terrible mistake. I could make it all go away. If only you'd let me have that little blue stone."
She looked up at Corin, at last.
Her gray eyes were dry. Quite dry. That was the worst of it.
"That's what it's for," she said. "Isn't it. Not to hang me. She doesn't want me hanged. A hanged girl can't give her anything." Her mouth moved, very slightly. "It's to make me ask."
At the window, Oren had come back up the stone steps from the yard. He was standing at the small square window that looked out over the stable roof and up toward the crooked lane, with his back to the wall beside it and the lantern turned down low at his feet. He had been standing there, quite still, since Corin came in.
He spoke now. Without turning round.
"Lights," he said.
They all looked at him.
"At the top of the lane." Oren's deep slow voice did not change. "By the carriage. Lanterns. Six. No. Eight." A pause. "Coming down."
Lyra did not move.
"Blue coats," said Oren. "With the… the brass. On the hats. The…" He stopped. He was reaching. Corin could see it, even from across the room, in the set of his great shoulders. Reaching down into the dark for a word, and closing his hand, and finding nothing.
"The Watch," said Juno. Very small.
"The Watch," said Oren. "Yes."
And from the bottom of the lane, through the cold still air, faint at first and then louder, there came a sound. Not a knock. A hammering. A fist on the wood of the tall green gate, hard, and again, and again. And a voice. Loud, and rough, and bored, the voice of a man who has done this a hundred times and expects to do it a hundred more.
"Open the gate! Open in the name of the Watch! We are seeking Miss Lyra Thorne!"
Nobody moved.
In the white room at the end of the long room, behind the white door with the worn gray patch in its paint, Corin heard Mrs. Hobb stop humming.
And then Lyra moved.
She did it quite calmly. She folded the broadsheet in half, and put it inside her dress. She went to the hook by the stove and took down her filthy brown coat and put it on, and buttoned it to the chin. She took down the sword in its scabbard of worn black leather and buckled it on under the coat, with hands that did not need to look at what they were doing. She put her hand to her collar, once, to the bootlace, to the small hard shape of the blue stone under the gray wool. Then, from the windowsill, she picked up something small and green and shining, and put it in her pocket.
The beetle. Leaf.
Then she walked to the white door at the end of the room, and opened it, and went in.
Corin did not follow her. Nobody did. Through the open door he saw her kneel down beside the white chair by the window, where her father sat in his faded blue silk dressing gown with the red blanket over his knees, looking out at the dark. He saw her take one of his long thin hands in both of hers. He saw her put her forehead against it, for a moment. Just a moment.
He did not hear what she said. He did not try to.
The hammering on the gate went on. Open in the name of the Watch. Something heavy hit it. The bar groaned.
Lyra came out of the white room. She shut the door behind her, softly, the way you shut a door on someone asleep. Her face was quite white and quite calm.
"Oren," she said. "Stay with him. Whatever happens. Don't fight them. Don't let them hurt you. You're no good to him hurt." She looked at the big man. "Tell them I've gone. Tell them you don't know where. It'll be true."
Oren looked at her for a long moment, with his scarred face and his cold gray eyes.
Then he nodded. Once.
"Juno," said Lyra.
"The hay store," said Juno. She was already at the door. "Over the wall. The dye-yard. Same as yesterday. They'll be at the gate for five minutes before they think of the back. Watchmen never think of the back." Her sharp face was fierce. "And then the Cut. And then I don't know. But somewhere."
The gate groaned again. Something cracked.
Lyra looked at Corin.
She did not ask him anything. She did not need to. She stood in the long low room over the stable, under the old dark beams, with her filthy coat buttoned to her chin and her sword under it and her short black hair sticking up at the back, and her gray eyes looked at him, steady and dry and waiting.
Somebody should look after somebody, in this city, Vespera had said. Somebody should get there in time.
"Come on," said Corin.
And they went out of the green door and down the worn stone steps with no rail into the dark yard, the three of them, quickly, quietly, while the Watch hammered on the tall green gate at the bottom of the lane and shouted her name into the freezing night, over and over, as if a name, said loudly enough, could make a person come.
