Chapter 16: The Girl with Wrong Tattoos, Part 4 of 5: Undoing
Undoing
The girl could not say her name.
She tried. They all watched her try. She sat in the middle of the ring of lamps with her raw hands twisting in her lap and her mouth opening and shutting, and every time she reached for it, a letter on her left wrist shivered, Mary, and her face went flat, and she said, "My name is Mary Coyle," in the lesson voice. And then her eyes filled with tears, and she said, "No. No, it isn't," in the real one. And reached again. And the letter shivered again.
After the fifth time, Vespera sat back.
"It's sitting on top of it," she said. "The lie. Right on top of her name, where I'd put it if I were him. She can't get past it from inside." She looked round the room. "Somebody has to go in from outside. Somebody has to give her a road back."
Nobody moved.
Then Juno got up off the floor by the stove.
She came across the boards in her big coat and her boots and stood in front of the leather chair, inside the ring of lamps, and looked at the girl. She was smaller than her. Thinner. Two years younger, perhaps. But she stood there with her chin up and her sharp sooty face very serious, the way her grandmother stood in the doorway of the purple door.
"You said Murdock's," said Juno. "Before. You said I was at Murdock's, at the tubs."
The girl looked at her.
"Murdock's on Lye Street," said Juno. "In the Cut. Big red door, with the paint coming off. Steam coming out the windows all day long, even in winter, so the whole street smells of soap and wet sheets. And old Mother Murdock sits on a stool in the yard with her wooden leg up on a bucket and a stick in her hand, and if you're slow with the mangle she hits you with it." Juno's mouth twisted. "She hit me once. When I was little. For running through her sheets."
The girl's lips moved.
"She hits the backs of your legs," she said. Very small. In the real voice. "Not hard. She can't reach hard. But it stings."
"It stings," agreed Juno.
"And she calls you girl. All of us. Girl, the coppers. Girl, the starch. She never learns anybody's name." The girl's eyes were fixed on Juno's face. "Except mine. She learned mine. Because I'm the fastest at the mangle. She says I've got arms like a bargeman." A breath. "She says, Hes, you've got arms like a bargeman."
On her left wrist, the word Mary shivered.
And did not settle.
It went on shivering. Harder. The M loosened, and the a slid, and the r and the y crawled away from each other, sideways, as if they did not want to be near each other anymore. And for the first time, they did not crawl back.
"Hes," said the girl. In a whisper. Her face crumpled. "Hes. Hester. Hester Dunn." She looked up at Vespera with tears running down her freckled face. "My name's Hester Dunn. I'm sixteen. I live with my aunt on Lye Street, over the pie shop, and I work at Murdock's, and I've never had a cat, I'm frightened of cats, I've never been on the bridge at night, I've never—" Her voice broke. "Please. Please. It's coming up. I can feel it coming up. Please."
"I know, lamb," said Vespera. "I've got you. Hold still now."
She already had the needle in her hand.
It was not the needle Corin remembered from his Ledger. Not one of the fine ones that hung from the beams. It was bigger. Hollow, like a reed, made of silver, as long as a finger, and at the top of it, where the thread should have gone, there was a tiny bulb of glass, round and clear, no bigger than a pea. And in the bulb there was dust. A pinch of something gray and fine and faintly glittering, like the dust on a moth's wing.
"Empty crystal," said Vespera, without looking up. "Hungry. Starving. It'll drink anything I put it near." She held the needle over the girl's left wrist, over the first line, over the shivering, crawling, coming-loose word Mary. "Corin. The lamp. Closer. Don't let it shake."
He held the lamp. It did not shake. He did not know how.
Vespera put the point of the hollow needle into the girl's skin. Not deep. Just the tip. Into the first loop of the M.
And the ink came up.
It came up out of the skin like water coming up through sand when you press it with your thumb. A tiny bead of it, dark, shining purple, oily, at the tip of the needle. And then it went up the needle. Up the hollow silver reed, slowly, the way water goes up a straw, and into the little glass bulb at the top. And in the bulb, the gray crystal dust drank it. Corin saw it happen. The dust darkened, and swelled, and glowed, very faintly, violet, like the last of a sunset.
And on the girl's wrist, the M was gone.
Not faded. Gone. There was only clean skin, red and a little raw, where it had been. And a single tiny dot of blood, bright as a berry.
Hester Dunn let out a breath. A long shaking breath, like someone who has been holding their head underwater.
"Oh," she said. "Oh. That one's gone. I felt it go."
"That one's gone," said Vespera. "Four hundred and twelve to go. Hold still."
She worked all afternoon.
Letter by letter. Word by word. The hollow needle going in, just the tip, and the purple bead coming up, and climbing the reed, and the crystal in the bulb drinking it and glowing. When the bulb was full, too dark and too bright to drink any more, she tapped the purple dust out of it into a little white china dish on the black velvet tray, and filled it again from a jar of gray, and went on. The dish filled up slowly with purple. It glowed. It sat on the tray among the needles and the cloths and glowed with a soft, shifting, uneasy violet light, like something breathing in its sleep.
The lamps burned down. Juno trimmed them. The room got hotter. Bet Mallow went out, without a word, and came back an hour later with a loaf and a hunk of cheese and a jug of milk, and put them on the table, and sat down on her stool again and folded her arms. Nobody ate.
The girl sat still. She was very good. She held her arms out on the arms of the leather chair, one and then the other, and kept them there, steady, and did not pull away, though Corin could see from her face how much it hurt. Every few words she would let out a breath, and say "that one," very softly, and once she laughed, a small shaky astonished laugh, and said "cat, that was cat, it's gone, I'm not frightened of it anymore." And Vespera said "good girl" and went on.
Corin held the lamp.
He held it for so long that his arm went numb from the shoulder down, and then began to burn, and then went numb again. He did not put it down. He watched the needle go in and out. He watched the purple climb the reed. He watched the lines on the girl's thin arms grow shorter, and shorter, from the wrist up, as if somebody were rubbing out a page of writing very slowly from the bottom.
And so he was watching, the first time it happened.
Vespera had just drawn the word doctor. The bulb at the top of the needle was nearly full, dark and shining. And as she lifted the needle away from the girl's arm, the lamplight caught the little glass bulb, and shone through it.
And in the glass, there was a picture.
It was very small. No bigger than the bulb itself. But it was there, inside the glowing purple dust, the way a picture is there in a drop of water when you hold it up to the light. Moving. A table. A long scarred table, dark wood, with a lamp on it, and pots of ink. And a boy's hand on the table. Thin. Dirty. Nails bitten to nothing. Holding a needle.
And over the boy's hand, another hand. A woman's. Broad and brown and strong, and covered all over in fine black writing. Closing round the boy's fingers. Gently. Guiding them. Making a letter, very slowly, on a scrap of leather.
A g.
And at the bottom of the g, the boy's fingers twisted, just a little, against the woman's. And made a hook.
And the woman's hand let go, and picked up a ruler, and smacked the back of the boy's hand. Not hard. And in the picture, very faint, so faint it might have been only the hiss of the lamp, there was a sound. A woman's voice. Dry and cross and fond.
Again, Hollis. Properly, this time.
The picture went dark. The bulb was only full of glowing purple dust.
Vespera had not moved.
She sat on her stool with the hollow needle held up in front of her face, in the lamplight, and looked at the little glass bulb at the top of it. And her face did not change at all. But her hand, Corin saw, had begun to shake. Very slightly. The way Rook's had shaken.
"Vespera," he said.
She did not answer. She tapped the bulb, very carefully, over the white china dish. The purple dust fell out of it in a soft glowing heap, and lay there, breathing.
She filled the bulb again from the jar of gray. She bent over the girl's arm again. She drew the next word. With. And lifted the needle. And held it up to the light.
A kitchen. A stove. A bowl of soup on a table, steaming. And a woman's voice, from somewhere out of the picture. Eat that, and then bed. You look like a drowned rat.
She drew the next. A black bag.
A ladder. A little room at the top of it, with a straw mattress and a window. A woman's broad back, going down the ladder with a candle. Night, Hollis. And a boy's voice, very small, from the mattress. Night, Vespera.
She drew the next.
And the next.
And every bulb that came up full had a picture in it. Every one. Small and faint and moving in the glowing purple. A woman's hands, mixing ink. A woman's laugh, once, sudden and loud, at something a boy had said. A woman asleep in a leather chair by a stove, with her mouth open, snoring, and a boy putting a blanket over her, very carefully, so as not to wake her. A woman's face, younger, much younger, with less writing on her arms, bending close over a boy's work with a lens, and saying That's good. That's very good. That's the best I've ever seen.
Vespera's face.
Every one.
She stopped, after the twentieth.
She put the needle down on the black velvet. She sat on her stool with her stained hands in her lap, and her face, in the light of the burned-down lamps, was the color of the ash in the stove.
"He's been feeding it with me," she said.
She said it very quietly. To nobody. To the needles hanging on their threads.
"False ink has to be fed. With something true. Something of the writer's. Each lie he writes, he burns one real thing to make it go." Her voice was quite steady. Quite flat. "I told you that. I told you that an hour ago. I didn't think—" She stopped. "Ten years. Ten years of lies. Hundreds of them. Little ones, and then big ones. And every single one, he's fed with a piece of me. The table. The soup. The ladder. The ruler." She looked at the white china dish, glowing purple on the tray. "He's been burning me out of himself. One lie at a time. So he'd have nothing left of me. So it wouldn't hurt anymore when he did it."
The girl in the chair was looking at her. So was Juno. So was Bet Mallow, on her stool, with her big red arms gone slack.
Corin put the lamp down. Carefully. On the floor. His arm screamed. He did not care.
He got down on his knees beside her stool. He did not touch her. He did not know if he should. He just knelt there, close, the way Bram had stood close behind him the night before, solid, like a wall.
"Or so it would," he said.
Vespera turned her head and looked at him.
"He could have burned anything," said Corin. "Anything at all. His mother. His father. The Cut. The first time he saw snow. He's had ten years. He could have used anything." He looked at the white dish. At the soft purple glow. "He used you. Every time. For every lie. And every time, he'd have seen it. Just before it went. The table, and the soup, and you asleep in this chair." He swallowed. "I don't think he was trying to stop it hurting, Vespera. I think he was trying to make sure it did."
Vespera looked at him for a long, long moment.
Then she made a sound. Not a word. Something low and rough and broken, deep in her chest, like a stone grinding on a stone. And she put her stained hands over her face. And she sat on her stool in the burned-down lamplight, with fifty years of true things written on her arms, and wept.
Nobody moved.
The needles turned, very slowly, on their threads overhead, and chimed. Just once. Very softly. As if a draft had come into the room from somewhere, though the door was shut.
After a long time Vespera took her hands away from her face. She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, hard. She reached out, and picked up the hollow silver needle from the black velvet tray, and looked at it.
"Get me a jar," she said. Her voice was hoarse. "Juno. A clean one. With a lid. From the shelf."
Juno got her one.
Vespera took the white china dish of glowing purple dust and tipped it, very carefully, into the jar. Every grain of it. And screwed on the lid. And set the jar on the table, beside the lamp, where she could see it.
"They're mine," she said. "He burned them. But they're mine as well as his. I'll keep them." She filled the bulb of the needle again from the jar of gray, and bent once more over the girl's thin arm, and her hand, Corin saw, was not shaking anymore. "Every one. Hold still, lamb. Three hundred and ninety to go."
What Hester Remembered
The last letter came out at a quarter to four.
It was the little tree. The Ostmark sign, on the back of her right shoulder, in the middle of the last line. Vespera left it till the very end. She drew all the rest of the line first, I saw her face and it was her, a letter at a time, until the little tree stood alone on the girl's freckled skin with nothing round it. Just itself. Upright, with its three spreading roots and its tiny round head. And all by itself, with nothing to fight, it had gone quite still.
Vespera looked at it for a long moment through the lens.
Then she put the hollow needle into the root of it, very gently, and drew it out.
The bulb at the top of the needle filled with purple, the same as all the others. She held it up to the light, the same as all the others.
There was no picture in it.
Not the table, not the soup, not the ladder. Nothing. Only the soft glowing purple dust, and in the middle of it, very faint, so faint that Corin was not sure he saw it at all, a sort of shimmer. Like heat over a road in summer. Like the air above a candle.
And a sound.
Not a voice. A note. A single long low note, like someone humming with their mouth shut, very far away. It went on for as long as it takes to breathe out. And then it stopped.
Vespera tapped the bulb over the jar, and the purple dust fell in with all the rest, and the jar glowed.
"That one wasn't his," she said quietly. "Whatever it was fed with. It wasn't him."
She put the needle down.
The girl's arms were clean.
Red, and sore, and pricked all over with tiny dots like the marks of a hundred flea bites, from the wrists to the shoulders. But clean. Not a letter left on them. Not a line. Vespera washed them in warm water with clove oil, very slowly, and the girl sat in the leather chair and let her, with her eyes shut and tears running down her face, and did not make a sound.
Then Vespera took a fine needle. One of the small ones, from a thread over the table. And a little pot of ink. Silver. And on the inside of the girl's left wrist, just below the heel of the hand, where the lie had been, she wrote.
It did not take long. Three lines. Small and neat and clear. And when she had finished, the silver lay in the red skin quietly, like ink on a page, and did not move at all.
Your name is Hester Dunn. You work at Murdock's, at the tubs.
You were never on the bridge.
You are frightened of cats.
The girl opened her eyes and looked at it for a long time.
Then she laughed. A small, cracked, astonished laugh, with the tears still on her face.
"Frightened of cats," she said. "You put that."
"It's true," said Vespera. "Isn't it?"
"It's true." Hester looked at her wrist. "They're horrible. With their eyes." She touched the silver letters, very lightly, with the tip of one raw red finger. "It feels… It feels like mine. It feels like it was always there."
"That's how you know," said Vespera. And she put the needle away.
Bet Mallow made her eat.
She tore the loaf in half with her enormous red hands and broke the cheese into lumps with her thumbs and poured the milk into a mug and put it all in the girl's lap, and stood over her with her arms folded until she had eaten every crumb. Hester ate like a starving dog. Fast, and with both hands, and without looking up. She had not eaten, she said, with her mouth full, since her dinner at Murdock's the day before. Bread and dripping, at noon. And then nothing.
When she had finished, she sat back in the big leather chair with the empty mug in her hands and the purple shawl round her shoulders, and she looked round the room. At the seven burned-down lamps. At the needles on their threads. At the jar on the table, glowing purple. At Juno, and Bet, and Vespera, and Corin, kneeling on the floor beside her. As if she were seeing it all for the first time. As if she had just woken up.
"Where am I?" she said.
"Hook Lane," said Juno. "Vespera's. The Ink-Binder."
"The witch?" Hester's eyes went wide. "The writing witch? Mother Murdock says you sell your soul if you go in her shop."
"Mother Murdock," said Vespera dryly, "owes me for a tattoo of a sailor's name on her left hip, which she has never paid for, and has never shown to Mister Murdock." She sat down on her stool. "Now then, Hester Dunn. You're safe. You're fed. Your arms are your own. And I'm very sorry, lamb, but I need you to remember."
Hester's face went still.
"Remember what?"
"Last night," said Vespera gently. "Whatever you can. However it comes. It doesn't have to be in order. Just tell us."
It came in pieces.
She had left Murdock's late. Ten o'clock, about. There had been a great pile of sheets from one of the big houses on the Hill, to be done by morning, and Mother Murdock had kept four of them back to finish. She was the last to go. She had walked up Lye Street toward home in the snow, with her shawl over her head and her hands tucked into her armpits, because they were raw from the lye and the cold hurt them.
"And a man said my name," said Hester.
She said it very quietly. Looking at the empty mug in her hands.
"From a doorway. Behind me. Hester Dunn? Like that. Like a question. Like he was making sure." She swallowed. "And I turned round. Because you do. And he was big. So big. He filled the whole doorway. And he smelled." Her nose wrinkled. "Beer. And onions. Like the pie shop under my aunt's, only worse."
Corin did not move.
"And he put a sack over my head," said Hester. "And picked me up. Like I was a sheet off the line. Just like that. And put me in a carriage. And I screamed and screamed and nobody came." Her voice was quite flat. "Nobody ever comes, on Lye Street. Not at night."
"Where did they take you?"
"I don't know. I couldn't see. It was a long way. Up. The horses were pulling hard, like it was uphill." She frowned. "And then stairs. Down. A lot of stairs. And a room."
"Tell me the room," said Vespera.
"It was white." Hester's eyes had gone far away. "White walls. Whitewash, new, still wet in places, you could smell it. Wet plaster. And no windows. Lamps. A lot of lamps, like here." She looked round at the burned-down lamps on the floor. "And it smelled of something else. Something sharp. Like when you've got toothache and your aunt puts a—"
"Cloves," said Vespera.
"Cloves." Hester looked at her. "Like this room. Like you."
Vespera's face did not change. But her hands, in her lap, closed slowly into fists.
"He keeps his needles in clove oil," she said. Very low. "I taught him that."
"There were girls," said Hester.
The room went very quiet.
"Two of them. On a bench. Against the wall. Sitting side by side, in their shifts, like me." Her raw fingers tightened on the mug. "One was crying. A little one, with red hair. She was crying and crying, and saying I want my mam, over and over. And the other one was asleep. Or she looked asleep. With her head against the wall and her mouth open." She swallowed. "Her arms were all over writing. Like mine were. Only hers wasn't moving."
Corin looked at Vespera. Vespera did not look back.
"And there was a screen," said Hester. "At the end of the room. A big painted one, silk, with birds on it. Herons. Standing in water. And there was a lady behind it."
"A lady."
"I never saw her. Only her shape, a bit, through the silk, when the lamps were behind her. Tall. And her voice." Hester's face changed. Something came into it. Something frightened, and puzzled, and nearly ashamed. "She had the nicest voice. That's the thing. Warm. Kind. Like someone's mam in a story. She called me darling. She said, Don't be frightened, darling. Nobody's going to hurt you. You're going to help me tell the truth about something very sad." Her mouth twisted. "And the smell came over the screen. Every time she talked. Flowers. White ones. Sweet. Like a funeral."
Nobody said anything.
Nobody needed to.
"And she said, Show her the picture," said Hester. "And the big man held up a picture. A drawing. In pencil. Of a girl. A lady. With short black hair, cut like a boy's, with a bit falling over her forehead. And a sword." Her eyes were very wide. "It was so good. It was like she was there. And I had to look at it. For ages. And every time I looked away, the big man turned my head back."
"And then?"
"And then the needle man came."
She was quiet for a long time after she said it. Turning the empty mug round and round in her hands.
"He was thin," she said at last. "So thin. Like a stick. Not old. But his hair was going gray already, at the sides. And his face—" She stopped. "He had a kind face. That's what I can't… He had a kind face. Sad. Like a dog that's been kicked." She looked at Vespera. "And he was crying. The whole time. Not loud. Just tears, running down. And he kept saying I'm sorry. I'm sorry. It'll stop hurting once you believe it. I promise. It'll stop."
Vespera's fists had gone white in her lap.
"And he talked," said Hester. "While he was writing. Every word. He whispered every word as he put it on me, very quiet, under his breath. My name is Mary Coyle. I have a cat. Like he was reading me a story." She frowned. "Only he wasn't making it up. He was reading. There was a paper. On a little stand, beside him, where he could see it. And he kept looking at it. Every few words. And then back at my arm."
"A paper," said Corin. "What was it like?"
"Big letters. Black. Very straight. Very neat. All the same size, like soldiers." Hester shook her head. "Not his writing. His was small and pretty and curly. This was different. Like a schoolmaster's. Like the sign over the Ministry door."
All the same size.
Corin's hand went to his coat. To the back of the Ledger, behind the marbled paper, where a small square of cream-colored paper lay folded. SELL THEM. YOU NEVER READ THEM ANYWAY. In letters that were all exactly the same size.
He did not take it out. He kept his hand there, flat, on the outside of his coat.
"And at the end," said Hester. "The last line. On my shoulder, at the back. He looked at the paper, and he stopped. And he said, What's this? This isn't a letter. What is this?" She swallowed. "And there was another voice. Behind the screen. With the lady. Not hers. A man's."
Vespera looked up sharply.
"I hadn't heard him before," said Hester. "He hadn't said anything. He was very quiet. And his voice was…" She frowned, hunting for the word. "It went up and down. Not like talking. Like singing, nearly. Like a hymn. Like he couldn't help it." She shivered. "And he said, Copy it exactly, Needle. Exactly as it is written. Every mark."
The needles on their threads overhead turned, very slightly, and chimed.
"And the needle man did," said Hester. "He copied it. The funny mark. Like a little tree." She touched her own right shoulder, through the shawl. "And as soon as he lifted the needle away, it started. The ants. All over. And it hurt, it hurt so much, and I screamed, and the writing started moving, and the needle man looked at it and went white as milk and dropped his needle and said It's not taking. It's not taking. Something's wrong. I didn't—I never—"
She stopped. She was shaking all over, under the purple shawl.
"And the lady said…" Her voice had gone very small. "The lady said, very kind, very gentle, like she was sorry, Oh dear. Then she's no use to us, is she, darling? What a shame." Hester's eyes were full of tears. "Gorse. Take her down to the river."
Nobody breathed.
"Take her down to the river," said Hester. "The tide weighs it. And it comes up light."
Corin did not hear the rest.
He heard it. He wrote it down, later, every word. The carriage again, and the sack, and the cold. The big man carrying her down a long flight of slimy stone steps in the dark, humming to himself, three notes, over and over. The water. The cold of it, so cold that it stopped her breath. The big man's hands letting go. And then nothing. And then the gray light, and the bottom step of the Salt Stairs under her cheek, and her feet in the river, and a big woman in a canvas apron bending over her saying Saints and fishes, girl, what've they done to you.
She had not drowned. She did not know why. She thought perhaps the big man had not thrown her far enough. She thought perhaps the tide had been going out, and had left her on the step. She thought, she said, in a very small voice, that perhaps the ants had kept her awake. That she had been fighting so hard inside, against the writing, that there had not been room for drowning.
Corin heard all of it. But he heard it the way you hear rain on a roof when you are thinking of something else.
The tide weighs it, and it comes up light.
He had heard those words before. Not in his Ledger. In his own head. In a dead man's voice, in a dead man's memory, on a landing under the Lantern Bridge, by the light of a low yellow lantern. A woman in a white shell mask, with a necklace of tiny vials, standing over a little round man on his knees. A splash. The tide weighs it, and it comes up light.
And before that, further back, in the very first pages of the Ledger in his coat. A dead scribe on a rubbish heap in the Ink Quarter, and a jar, and a voice in the Ash. The same words.
The same lady.
She said it every time.
It was, he thought, the nearest thing she had to a name.
