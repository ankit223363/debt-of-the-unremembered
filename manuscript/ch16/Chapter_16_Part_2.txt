Chapter 16: The Girl with Wrong Tattoos, Part 2 of 5: The Girl in the Chair
The Girl in the Chair
They went the quick way. Juno's way. Out through the boiler room, past the deaf man shoveling coal, up the paper stairs into Archive Lane, and then not along the lane at all but straight over the wall at the end of it, and down through the coal yard, and across the tannery drying ground where the hides hung frozen stiff on their frames like great brown boards, and down, and down, through the alleys and the yards and the backs of houses, toward the river.
The snow was melting everywhere. The alleys ran with it. Every gutter was a stream and every drainpipe was a waterfall and every step was a sheet of wet slush with ice underneath, and Corin fell twice and Juno did not wait for him either time. She ran ahead, always just in sight, a small dark shape in a big coat with her cap pulled down, and at every corner she stopped, and looked back, and jerked her head, and was gone again.
She did not talk. Not once. That was how Corin knew how frightened she was.
Hook Lane, when they came out at the top of it, was a river of slush. The snow had gone off the cobbles in the sun and run down the steep lane toward the quay at the bottom in a brown rushing stream, and the icicles along the gutters were dripping so fast that it was like walking down under a fringe of rain. Corin went down holding on to the wall. Juno went down the middle, splashing.
Faber's shop was open. Through the cracked window Corin saw him at his bench under the lamp, bent over a little watch with his jeweler's glass in his eye. He did not look up as they went past. But his right hand, on the bench, lifted, just an inch, and fell again. I see you. And then, very slightly, his head turned toward the shop next door.
Vespera's shutters were closed.
All of them. The front ones, and the little ones on the side, and the door had a board across it, wedged under the handle, and on the board somebody had chalked one word in big angry letters.
SHUT.
Juno did not go to the door. She went down the side of the shop, into the narrow gap between Vespera's wall and the next house, where there was hardly room to turn round, and she knocked on a little low door there with the side of her fist. Three times quick. Twice slow.
The door opened at once.
The smell came out first.
It was not the smell of Vespera's back room, the smell Corin's body knew, of hot ink and cloves and herbs and the stove. That was there. But there was something else on top of it. Something sharp and sour and wrong, like the smell of a coin that has been in someone's mouth. Like the smell of the air after a pan has been left too long on the fire, and burned dry, and cracked.
"In," said Vespera. "Shut it. Don't let the light in. And don't speak loud."
She was standing in the doorway with her sleeves rolled up past her elbows, and her arms were covered in their fine writing, black and blue and here and there red, so close that they looked gray. But her hands were not. Her hands were stained. The fingers of both of them, to the second knuckle, were a dark, deep, oily purple, almost black, the color of a bruise four days old. And she held them a little away from herself, the way a cook holds her hands when they are covered in raw meat and she does not want to touch anything else.
Her wide brown weather-beaten face was gray. There were lines on it that Corin did not think had been there yesterday.
He went in. Juno went in after him and shut the little low door and put her back against it.
The back room was dark. The shutters were closed and the curtains drawn and only one lamp was lit, turned low, on the long table among the pots of ink. The needles hung from the ceiling on their threads, hundreds of them, very still. Nobody had brushed against them. Nobody was moving fast enough in this room to set them chiming.
There were two people in it. Three, with Vespera.
One of them was a woman sitting on a stool by the stove. A big woman, broad as a door, in a long canvas apron gone stiff with fish scales and salt, and a man's boots, and a shawl, with her sleeves rolled up over enormous red raw forearms. She had a wide red face and small blue eyes and her gray hair was tied up in a cloth. She had a mug of tea in her hands and she was not drinking it. She was looking at the leather chair by the stove with her mouth set very hard.
Corin knew her. Not from his Ledger. From yesterday. From a long covered market full of fish, with a glass roof white with snow. Did you hear it, Bet? It struck. Old Tide struck.
The other one was in the leather chair.
His chair. The big old cracked leather chair with one arm worn through to the stuffing. The chair that knew the shape of his back.
There was a girl in it.
She was young. That was the first thing. Sixteen, perhaps. No older. She was small and thin, and she was sitting in the big chair the way a child sits in a chair that is much too big for it, right at the back, with her knees drawn up and her bare feet tucked under her, wrapped in Vespera's purple shawl. Her hair was brown and long and had been tied back in a plait once, but the plait had come half undone, and wisps of it stuck to her face. Her face was round and plain and pale, with a snub nose and freckles, and it was wet. She had been crying. She was not crying now. She was looking at nothing, at the dark wall across the room, with her eyes very wide and her lips moving.
Her hands lay in her lap. They were red. Raw red, and cracked across the knuckles, and puckered and white at the fingertips, the way hands go when they have been in hot water and soap for years. A washerwoman's hands.
She was wearing a shift. A plain, thin, gray cotton shift, with no sleeves, the kind a girl sleeps in. And her arms were bare.
And her arms were covered in writing.
Both of them. From the wrists, all the way up, past the elbows, to the shoulders, and over the shoulders, and down under the edge of the shift at the back. Line after line after line of it. Small, close, neat writing, tattooed into the skin, every word perfectly formed, every line perfectly straight, the way the lines on a page of a fine book are straight.
But it was not Vespera's writing. Corin knew that at once, even in the dim light from the one low lamp. Vespera's ink was silver, or blue, or black, and it lay in the skin quietly, like ink on paper, and stayed where it was put.
This ink was purple. A deep, dark, shining purple, almost black, the color of the stains on Vespera's fingers. It had an oily shine to it, like the shine on the top of a puddle where a cart has dripped grease. And it was not quiet.
It moved.
Corin stood in the middle of the dark room and watched it, and felt his skin go cold all over, from his scalp to his heels.
It was not much. It was not the whole of the writing. Most of it lay still on the girl's thin arms, line under line. But here and there, all over, a letter would move. A single letter. It would shiver, very slightly, the way a leaf shivers before the wind gets to it. And then it would change. Slowly. Like a drop of ink spreading in water. An s would loosen and uncurl and become a c. A t would lose its cross. The tail of a y would slide away sideways and join itself to the letter beside it. And for a moment the word would be wrong, a word that was not a word at all, and then, very slowly, the letter would shiver again, and crawl back, and settle into what it had been before.
All over her arms. A letter here. A letter there. Shivering and crawling and settling and shivering again. Like ants on a wall. Like something trying to get out.
And every time a letter moved, the girl's lips moved too.
Corin went closer. He did not mean to. His feet took him, slowly, across the boards, until he was standing beside the chair, and he could hear what she was saying. She was saying it very softly. Over and over. In a small flat voice, like a child saying a lesson she has learned by heart without understanding a word of it.
"I was on the steps. Under the bridge. The Lantern Bridge. At night. I was looking for my cat." A breath. "There was a lantern. There was a man on his knees. A little round man. A doctor." A breath. "And there was a girl. A lady. With short black hair. Cut short, like a boy's. And a sword. A thin sword, very bright." A breath. "She hit him. With the sword. Not the sharp part. The handle. On the head. And he fell in the water." A breath. "And she laughed. She laughed and she said, That's for my father. And she went up the steps. And I saw her face. In the lantern. Gray eyes. Gray eyes. Thorne gray."
She stopped.
She stopped, and her face changed. All at once. The flat blank lesson-look went out of it, and something else came in. Something frightened. Something very young. Her wide eyes filled up with tears and her mouth crumpled.
"No," she said. In a different voice. A real voice. Small and hoarse and shaking. "No. No, I wasn't. I wasn't there. I was at the tubs. I was at Murdock's, at the tubs, I'm always at the tubs, I don't go out at night, I've never been on the bridge at night, I haven't got a cat—" Her hands came up out of her lap and clutched at each other, the raw red fingers twisting. "I haven't got a cat. Why would I say I've got a cat? I haven't got a—"
And on her left arm, just above the wrist, a letter shivered. The c of cat. It loosened, and crawled, and settled again.
The frightened look went out of her face.
"I was on the steps," she said, in the flat small lesson voice. "Under the bridge. The Lantern Bridge. At night. I was looking for my cat."
Corin found that he had sat down. On the floor, by the chair, on the bare boards. His legs had simply stopped holding him up. He sat there with his back against the stove-warm leather and his hands on his knees, and he did not know what to do with them.
A girl with short black hair. Cut short, like a boy's. And a sword.
Gray eyes. Thorne gray.
That's for my father.
"Bet Mallow found her," said Vespera behind him. Her voice was very low and very flat. "On the Salt Stairs. At first light. Lying on the bottom step, by the water, in nothing but that shift, with her feet in the river." She nodded at the big fishwife on the stool, who did not look round. "Bet thought she was drowned at first. Then she sat up and started saying that. Over and over. And Bet's a fool, but she's not a stupid fool. She looked at the girl's arms. And she put her in her barrow under a pile of sacks and wheeled her up Hook Lane past forty people and brought her to me."
"I didn't know where else," said the fishwife. Her voice was a deep hoarse growl, like a dock crane. She was still looking at the girl. "You don't take a thing like that to the Watch. Not round here. The Watch'd take one look at what's on her arms and send her straight up the Hill. And then she'd say it to whoever they sent her to. All of it. About the lady with the sword." She took a long breath through her nose. "And somebody'd hang for it."
Corin looked up at her.
Bet Mallow looked back at him with her small blue eyes. Hard. Like a woman who has gutted ten thousand fish and knows exactly where the knife goes.
"I was in the market yesterday," she said. "When Old Tide struck. Everybody was looking up at the tower. But I saw three white coats come running down the middle row after somebody, and knock over a whole barrel of my sister's oysters, and never stop to pay." Her mouth went hard. "And by teatime the whole of the Saltings was saying it was a Ministry man and a girl with short black hair and a sword, and they'd gone over the Lantern Bridge like hares." She drank her tea, at last, in one swallow. "So when this one sits up on my steps at dawn saying short black hair and a sword and the Lantern Bridge, over and over, like a parrot…" She put the mug down on the floor. "I'm not stupid, Ministry. This one never saw anybody on any steps. Somebody wrote it on her. Somebody wanted her to say it. And I'd like to know who, so I can go round with my gutting knife and have a word."
The girl in the chair had stopped talking. She was staring at the wall again. On her right arm, near the elbow, a letter shivered, and crawled, and settled.
Corin looked up at Vespera.
The Ink-Binder was standing at the long table with her stained purple hands held a little away from her body, looking down at the girl. And her face, in the low light of the lamp, was the face of someone looking at a very old wound that has opened again.
"What is it?" said Corin. "Vespera. What's on her?"
Vespera did not look at him.
"False ink," she said.
She said it very quietly. As if it were a word that should not be said aloud in this room. As if saying it might make the needles on their threads begin to turn.
"It's false ink," said Vespera. "And I know whose."
False Ink
Vespera washed her hands first.
She did it at the basin in the corner, slowly, with a stiff brush and a cake of yellow soap and water so hot that it steamed. She scrubbed for a long time. The purple did not come off. It faded a little, to the color of a plum, and then stopped fading, and she looked at it and dried her hands on a rag and threw the rag in the stove, where it burned with a thin, sour, violet flame.
Then she pulled up her stool, the one with the worn seat, and sat down on it in front of the leather chair. Close. So close that her knees almost touched the girl's bare feet under the shawl. And she looked at the writing on the girl's arms for a long time without touching it.
"You know what I do," she said. Not to the girl. To Corin, on the floor beside the chair. "You've read it. In your book."
"You write things into people," said Corin. "True things. Things they've lost."
"True things." Vespera nodded slowly. "That's the whole of it. That's the law. The first law of the Binding, and the only one that matters. Old Haddon taught it me when I was younger than this child, and he had it from his master, and his master had it from his. Write only what is true." She held up one of her own arms, turning it in the lamplight, so that the thousand fine lines on it caught the light. "Every word on me is true. Every word I ever put on you is true. You are not Jory Kestle. That was true. He was surprised at the end. That was true too. I found it in you, and I wrote it down, so you could keep it. That's all the Binding is. Finding what's true and giving it somewhere to live."
She lowered her arm.
"The ink knows," she said. "That's the thing nobody understands who isn't one of us. The ink knows. There's crystal in it. Ground crystal, from the same stones they put in the lamps, so fine you could breathe it. Empty crystal. Hungry. When you write a true thing into a body with it, the crystal reaches into the mind, the way a root reaches down into wet ground, and finds the memory that goes with the words, and holds on. And the mind says yes. Yes, that's right. That's mine. And the ink goes quiet. And stays."
She looked at the girl's left arm. Above the wrist, a letter shivered, and crawled, and settled.
"False ink is the same crystal," she said. "Ground the same. Mixed the same. Only it's been fed first."
"Fed?"
"Fed." Her wide brown face was very still. "You take a memory. A true one. Your own. And you burn it into the crystal before you ever put the needle to anybody. So the crystal's not hungry anymore. It's full. Full of something real. And then you write a lie with it." She was quiet for a moment. "And when the ink goes into the body, it doesn't reach for a memory to hold. It doesn't need to. It's already got one. It brings one. It carries your real memory in with it, and wraps the lie up inside it, like a pill inside a bit of bread. And the mind reaches out and takes it, the way it takes any memory. Because it feels real. Because it is real, underneath. Only the words on top are a lie."
Corin sat on the floor and looked up at the girl's thin arms, and felt sick.
"And by morning," said Vespera, "it's done. The ink goes in deeper and deeper, all night, and wraps itself round everything, and grows its roots, and by the time the sun's up the mind has swallowed the whole of it. Every word. And believes it. Not like you believe a story. Like you believe your own name." She tilted her head at the girl's arm. "And then the ink goes quiet. And then it fades. By noon, there's nothing on the skin at all. Not a mark. Clean as milk. You could look at her arms all day with a lens and never see a thing."
"Nothing?"
"Nothing. That's what it's for, Corin. That's the whole cleverness of it." Her voice had gone very hard. "A girl walks into the Watch house with clean arms and a clear face and tells them what she saw on the steps under the Lantern Bridge. And she's not lying. She's never lied in her life. She believes every word, down to the bottom of her heart. You could put her in front of the Archon himself, and he could read her with his own hand, and he'd find it there. In her. Real as her mother's face." She breathed out. "And nobody will ever know it was written."
The stove ticked. Bet Mallow, on her stool, had gone very still.
"Then why—" Corin stopped. He looked at the girl's arms. At the purple letters, still there, still shining with their oily shine, still shivering and crawling here and there like ants. "Why is it still on her? It's morning. It's past noon nearly."
"Because it didn't take."
Vespera leaned forward on her stool. Not touching. Looking.
"Something's wrong with it," she said. "I don't know what yet. Something in the writing. A word, a letter, a stroke out of place. The ink went in and started to grow its roots, the way it should. And then it hit something, down in her, that it couldn't wrap round. Something it couldn't swallow." She watched a letter on the girl's right shoulder shiver and loosen and crawl. "And it's been fighting ever since. All night. All morning. The lie trying to go down. And her own self, underneath, trying to push it back up." Her face twisted. "That's what the moving is. That's what you're watching. It's two things trying to be true in the same place at the same time."
The girl in the chair spoke again. In the flat small voice.
"There was a girl. A lady. With short black hair."
And then, in the other voice, the real one, hoarse and frightened, tears running down her freckled face: "No. No. I've never. I've never seen her. I don't know her. Please. Please make it stop. My arms hurt. My arms hurt, it's like ants, it's like ants under my skin, please—"
She was looking at Vespera. Straight at her. For the first time.
Vespera put out one stained hand, very slowly, and laid it on the girl's knee, over the purple shawl. Just laid it there. Heavy and warm and still.
"I know, lamb," she said. Very low. Her hard voice had gone soft as wool. "I know it does. I'm going to make it stop. I promise. It's going to take a while, and it's going to hurt worse before it's better, and I'm sorry for that. But I'm going to make it stop." She did not take her hand away. "Can you tell me your name? Your real one?"
The girl's mouth opened.
On her left arm, three letters shivered at once, and crawled, and settled.
The girl's mouth closed again. Her face went flat.
"I was on the steps," she said. "Under the bridge."
Vespera took her hand off the girl's knee. She sat back on her stool. She did not say anything for a long moment.
"Not yet," she said quietly, to nobody. "Not yet. All right."
"Who would do this?" said Corin.
He heard his own voice, and it did not sound like his. It sounded like somebody else's. Somebody much angrier.
"To her? To a girl from a laundry who's never done anything?" He looked up at the purple writing on the thin shoulders. A girl with short black hair. And a sword. "To Lyra?"
Vespera turned her head and looked at him.
"You know who'd do it to Lyra," she said. "You wrote it in your book. In letters so big the pencil tore. I read it yesterday, over your shoulder, while you sat in that chair and wrote." Her voice was very level. "Cressida Thorne killed Doctor Aske. Gorse put him in the river. Bertram saw."
Corin said nothing.
"And now Bertram's dead," said Vespera. "And Bertram's Ash is in a jar by your bed, and it remembers. And Lyra knows. You said so. She knows Lyra knows." She nodded at the girl in the chair. "So. A lady who's killed a man, and knows her own brother's girl knows it, and knows there's a harvester running round the city with the only witness in a pickle jar. What does she do?"
Corin saw it. All at once. The way you see the whole of a room when someone strikes a match.
"She gets her own witness first," he said.
"She gets her own witness first." Vespera's mouth was a hard line. "Before anybody can go to the Watch about her, she goes to the Watch about you. About Lyra. A young woman came forward. She saw it all. A girl with short black hair, cut like a boy's, and a sword, on the steps under the Lantern Bridge. Gray eyes. Thorne gray. She said, that's for my father." She shook her head slowly. "And then it doesn't matter what's in Bertram's jar. It doesn't matter what you say you saw in a dead man's Ash. Who's going to believe a harvester who harvests? Who's going to believe a Thorne girl who lives over a stable and climbs roofs and knocks white coats down on bridges? Against a laundry girl with clean arms and a clear face and nothing to gain?"
The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, far off, a gull cried.
"And Monday," said Corin. Very low. "Fallowfield. If Lyra's in a cell, she can't—"
"If Lyra's in a cell, she can't do anything at all." Vespera looked at him. "Can she. Not about Monday. Not about her father. Not about the brooch, or the portrait, or the little blue stone round her neck that her aunt wants so badly." She paused. "She'd give her aunt anything. To get out. To get him back."
Corin put his face in his hands.
He sat on the floor of Vespera's back room with his back against the stove-warm leather of his own chair and his gloved hands over his face, and he did not move for a long time. The girl above him in the chair had started again, softly, in her flat lesson voice. I was on the steps. Under the bridge. The Lantern Bridge. And he listened to it, and did not move, and thought about a girl with short black hair kneeling on the scrubbed boards of a white room by her father's chair, holding two blue stones together in front of his chest.
I'm not giving it to her. I don't know yet how I'm going to stop her taking him. But I'm not giving it to her.
He took his hands away from his face.
"You said you know whose it is," he said. "The ink."
Vespera did not answer at once.
She had turned back to the girl. She was looking at the writing on her arms again. Not at the words now. At the letters. The shapes of them. The way they were made. Leaning close, closer, with her own face only a hand's width from the girl's thin shoulder, her small dark eyes moving along the lines, slowly, from one end to the other, the way Rook's eyes had moved along the edge of a blue stone.
She stopped. On one letter. A g. In the word laughed. On the inside of the girl's right arm, near the elbow.
At the bottom of the g, where its tail curled round, it did not curl the way a g's tail usually curls. It went down, and round, and then, at the very end, it turned back up again, sharp, in a tiny hook. Like the barb on the end of a fishhook.
Vespera looked at it for a long time.
"I know whose it is," she said. "Because I spent four years trying to make him stop doing that."
She straightened up on her stool. Her face in the lamplight was gray, and old, and very tired.
"I taught that hand," said Vespera. "Every letter of it. I sat beside it at that table for twelve years, and held it, and showed it how." She looked down at her own stained fingers. "And I'd know it anywhere."
