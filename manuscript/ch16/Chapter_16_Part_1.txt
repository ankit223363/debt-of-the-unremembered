Chapter 16: The Girl with Wrong Tattoos, Part 1 of 5: Friday
Friday
He woke in his coat, with his gloves on, holding a book.
For a moment that was all he knew. A gray ceiling with a crack in it like a river. Cold. A weight against his chest, stiff and square, held in both arms the way a child holds a doll. Light at a small window, pale and white and very bright, brighter than any light had a right to be at this hour.
Then his hands opened the book, the way they always did, and the first page told him who he was.
READ THIS FIRST. Your name is Corin. You were born in Oakhaven. You had a younger sister named Elara. You used to fear the dark.
He read it. Then he read the rest. It took a long time, the way it always did, and today it took longer, because the last pages were so full. The Bourse. A boy at a wheel. A woman by a fire under a dome of glass. A footman in an ice house. A stationer with a broken nose and a chalk mark drawn fresh every morning for thirty years. Six books on a rag rug. A blot in the middle of his sister's name.
And at the very bottom, pressed so hard that the pencil had gone through the paper:
I read them tonight.
I'm not selling them.
He lay on the narrow iron bed with the book open on his chest and looked at those two lines for a long time.
Then he turned his head and looked at the crate beside the bed.
The jar was there. BERTRAM ASHCOMBE. STEWARD. FORGIVE ME. In the middle of the crate, not on the left. And beside it, lying flat, a small square of thick cream-colored paper, with a long thin hatpin laid across it. A hatpin with a single round white bead on the end, like a pearl.
He did not pick the note up. He did not need to. He could read it from where he lay, in the white light from the window.
SELL THEM. YOU NEVER READ THEM ANYWAY.
In his own hand. Or very nearly.
He got up.
The snow had not gone. That was the first thing he saw, at the window, with the blanket round his shoulders. It had not snowed again in the night, but it had not melted either. The city lay under it the same as yesterday, white and soft and strange, only it was not clean anymore. The street below was trodden into a brown slush down the middle, full of wheel ruts and hoofprints and the marks of a thousand boots. The snow along the edges had gone gray with soot. On the roofs opposite, round every chimney pot, there was a dark ring where the warm smoke had melted it, so that the white roofs were spotted all over with black, like the coat of a dog.
But the sky was blue. Clear, pale, washed blue, from one side to the other, without a cloud in it. And the sun was out, low and white over the roofs, and where it touched the snow the snow blazed so brightly that it hurt his eyes.
He stood at the window and let it hurt them for a while.
Then he went to the washstand and broke the ice on the jug, and washed, and looked at his face in the cracked mirror until it looked back at him. He took off yesterday's collar, the clean Hill collar that Vespera had found him, and folded it, and put it in the drawer. He put on his own. He brushed his long gray Ministry coat with his hand. He put the Ledger in the inside pocket, on the left, over his heart. He put the rod in the long pocket on the right. The little brass lamp. The white gloves, in his side pocket, rolled up small.
Then he picked up the cream-colored note, and the white-beaded hatpin, and looked at them both in the white light from the window.
He thought about throwing them on the fire. There was no fire. He thought about throwing them out of the window. He did not.
He folded the note in half, and in half again, and put it in the back of the Ledger, behind the marbled paper, next to the scorched blue corner of a drawing that he had put there the day before. And he stuck the hatpin through the lapel of his coat, on the inside, where nobody would see it.
He did not know why. It seemed better to have it where he could find it. Things you could find were less frightening than things you could not.
Mrs. Marta was in the passage at the bottom of the stairs, sweeping slush out of the front door with a broom twice as tall as she was. She stopped when she saw him coming down. She looked at him with her small sharp black eyes, up and down, the way she always did.
"You ate it," she said.
It was not a question. He had left the bowl outside her purple door, washed, with the plate on top and the dishcloth folded over it, and a note on the dishcloth in pencil. He did not remember writing the note. The Ledger said he had. Thank you. It was the best thing I ever tasted. C.
"Yes," said Corin. "Thank you."
Mrs. Marta sniffed.
"The best thing you ever tasted," she said. "Cold stew. On the floor. At midnight." She shook her head and went back to her sweeping, hard, so that the slush flew out of the door in a brown spray. "You need feeding, that's what you need. You need a wife. Somebody to make sure you eat when it's hot." Sweep. "Not that you'd remember her." Sweep. "Poor girl."
Corin stood at the bottom of the stairs.
"Mrs. Marta," he said. "Did anyone come to the house yesterday? While I was out? Up the stairs?"
The broom stopped.
Mrs. Marta turned round slowly and looked at him. She did not look surprised. She looked, he thought, like a woman who had been waiting all morning for somebody to ask her exactly that question, and was very glad that someone finally had.
"No," she said. "Nobody came up those stairs. Not by the door. Not while I was in. And I was in all day, except for an hour at the market, and Juno was on the roof that hour, and she says nobody came in the door." Her mouth went thin. "Why?"
"Somebody was in my room."
Mrs. Marta's small face went very still.
"By the window," said Corin. "Over the roofs. They moved a jar. And they left me a note on the door frame. Pinned up with the others. With a white hatpin."
He opened his coat and showed her. The long thin pin, stuck through the inside of the lapel. The little white bead, glinting.
Mrs. Marta looked at it for a long time.
Then she leaned her broom against the wall. And she came up to him, close, the top of her white head no higher than the middle of his chest, and she put out one small brown wrinkled finger and touched the white bead, very lightly, the way you would touch something you thought might bite.
"That's not mine," she said.
"I know."
"I don't have white ones. I never have. White's for weddings and the dead." She took her finger away. "I've seen one like it before, though. On your door frame. Not two weeks back. Holding up a note that said STOP LOOKING, in a hand that wasn't yours." Her small black eyes came up to his face. "You remember that?"
"I've read it."
"Same thing." She sniffed. "Lilies," she said. "Isn't it. It's always lilies."
She picked up her broom again. She swept, once, hard, at the slush on the step. Then she stopped, and stood with both hands on the handle, looking out of the front door at the white street and the blue sky.
"I'll get a bar put on your window," she said, without turning round. "An iron one. Today. My cousin's boy does them. And Juno can sleep on the roof." Sweep. "She'll like that. She says she sleeps better on roofs. Daft girl." Sweep. "Go to work, Mister Vane. And eat something on the way. And mind the slush. It's worse than the snow."
Corin went to work.
The Ledger said the Ministry of Records was a long way. Up the hill from the Lower Docks, along the riverfront road, past the Lantern Bridge, up Archive Lane to the Hill. It said that most days he walked it in an hour, and some days in two, because he stopped to look at things he did not remember having seen before, which was everything.
Today it took him an hour and a half, and he looked at everything.
The city was different in the sun. Yesterday it had been white and silent and secret, like a city under a sheet. Today the sheet was coming off. The snow was melting off the roofs in long, slow, glittering drips, so that every street sounded like rain, though the sky was blue. It ran down the gutters in little brown streams, gurgling, and down the drainpipes, and off the ends of the icicles, drip, drip, drip, and the icicles got shorter and sharper and clearer, and every now and then one of them broke off and fell and smashed on the cobbles below like a dropped glass. Children were out. Dozens of them. Throwing snowballs at each other across the lanes, and at the carts, and at the windows, and at Corin, once, from behind a wall, so that one hit him on the back of the neck and went down inside his collar, cold as a fish, and a whole crowd of small faces popped up over the wall and shrieked with laughter and ducked down again.
He stood in the slush and laughed too. He did not know why. He could not stop.
He walked on with the snow melting down his back.
On the riverfront road the wind came off the water, sharp and cold, and he turned his collar up against it. Below the wall, the river was not black anymore. It was brown again, and busy, and full of ice. Great broken plates of it, gray and white, turning and bumping and grinding against each other as the current carried them down toward the sea, so that the whole river seemed to be moving at once, groaning, like a herd of slow animals. The boats were out among them, pushing through. Barges. Ferries. Men with long poles, shoving the ice away from their bows and shouting.
And across the water, on the far bank, the Tide Hall. Gray and grand and square, with its row of pillars, and its tall tower, and its great pale face shining in the sun.
The hands said a quarter past eight.
Every other clock in the city said nearly nine.
Corin stopped at the wall and looked at it for a long time. Old Tide, three quarters of an hour behind the world, going on going, not minding. Somewhere inside it, perhaps, among the turning wheels, an old man with white hair and a brass dog. Or perhaps not. Perhaps he had gone, by his way of his own, to some other clock, in some other tower, and was sitting in the dark behind its face, listening to it tick, waiting for it to be safe.
When it's safe, I'll stop a clock at twelve. Somewhere you can see it.
He looked at all the clocks he could see from the riverfront road. On the church on the hill. On the corn exchange. On the front of the bank. On the little round tower over the ferry stairs. Nine, nearly nine, ten to nine, five to.
None of them said twelve.
He walked on, toward the Lantern Bridge, and Archive Lane, and the Hill.
Wards and Persons
He went in by the front.
The Ledger said he always did. Every day for six years, old Wilkin on the side gate had said once. You never once come round the back. So he went up the great stone steps at the front of the Ministry of Records with all the other gray coats, in the sun, with the slush running down the steps in little brown rivers and the clerks hurrying past him with their heads down and their papers held against their chests.
The building went up over him like a cliff. Black stone, wet and shining in the sunlight, with the melting snow running off every ledge and carving in long dark streaks. Over the great doors at the top of the steps, cut into the stone, there was an eye. A huge one, as tall as a man, carved deep, with a thick heavy lid.
Closed.
The Ministry's eye was always closed. He knew that. He had read it a hundred times, he supposed, and forgotten it a hundred times. It was closed because the Ministry did not need to look. It already knew.
Under it, as he went through the doors, he thought about another eye. A tiny one, in chalk, in the corner of a board in a shop that smelled of paper. Drawn fresh every morning for thirty years.
Open.
The Great Hall was full of noise. Clerks at long desks, row after row, hundreds of them, scratching with their pens. Brass tubes running up the walls and across the ceiling like the pipes of an organ, and every now and then a thunk and a whoosh, as a little leather case full of papers shot up one of them to somewhere high above. Messengers in gray running between the desks. A man at the end of the hall reading out names from a list in a high flat voice that nobody seemed to be listening to.
Nobody looked at Corin. He went down the side of the hall, past the desks, to a narrow door at the end, and down.
The First Basement smelled of ink, and the sound of a hundred pens came through its door like rain on a roof. The Second smelled of mice and mold. And the Third had a door of black iron with no handle, and a small square window of thick cloudy glass, and a brass plate.
HARVESTING SECTION. DANGER. DO NOT ENTER WITHOUT A HARVESTER PRESENT.
He was a harvester. He went in.
The Harvesting Section was one long, low room, cut out of the rock under the hill, with a curved close ceiling and iron lamp-cages hanging from it that he had to duck under. The walls were bare damp stone with little white crusts of salt where the water had come through. Down both sides, from the floor to the ceiling, there were shelves, and on the shelves there were jars. Hundreds of them. Plain glass jars, like the one on the crate beside his bed, with wax seals and paper labels, and in the bottom of each one a thin gray layer of something soft and faintly shining. They glowed, very slightly, in the dim light from the lamp-cages. The whole room had a faint gray shine, like the inside of a shell.
At the far end, under a green-shaded lamp, there was a desk. A small, crowded, very tidy desk, piled with papers in neat square stacks, with a pen tray and an inkwell and a blotter, and a plate with half a bun on it.
And behind the desk, a young man jumped up so fast that he knocked his chair over.
"Inspector!"
He was thin and pale and narrow, with a long neck and a big nose and ears that stuck out, and thick round spectacles that made his eyes look very large and very bright, like the eyes of a startled owl. His fingers were black with ink to the second knuckle. His collar was crooked. His hair stood up at the back as if he had slept on it, which he probably had.
The Ledger said his name was Toby. The Ledger said a great many things about Toby. Junior Clerk. Very nervous. Very loyal. Saved fifteen forms from the pulp because he couldn't bear to throw them away. He gave me half a bun on Mrs. Marta's step in the rain. DO NOT LEND TOBY MONEY.
"Toby," said Corin.
"You came in!" Toby picked up his chair and set it straight and then knocked over a stack of papers and caught them and put them back. "You came in. I didn't know if you would. After—everything. Yesterday. The bridge." His voice dropped to a loud, excited whisper. "Everyone's talking about it, Inspector. Up in the Hall. They say a harvester lit up the whole middle of the Lantern Bridge like a lightning strike. They say it was you. They say there was a Thorne girl with a sword and a chimney sweep and—" He stopped. He looked at Corin. His owl eyes went very wide behind the thick glass. "It was you."
"Yes."
"Saints." Toby sat down in his chair, hard. Then he got up again. "Saints. Inspector. Lucian came down yesterday afternoon. Himself. Down the stairs. Into here." He pointed at the stone floor as if Lucian's footprints might still be on it. "He's never been down here. Not once in two years. He stood just there, by the door, and he wouldn't come any further because of the jars, and he asked me whether you'd been in."
"What did you say?"
"I said no." Toby swallowed. "Because you hadn't. It was true. And he looked at me. For a long time. With his eyes all red round the edges the way they get when he hasn't slept. And then he said, When he does come in, Clerk, you might remind him that the Pell file is closed. And he went away." Toby's ears had gone pink. "He said remind. He said it very carefully. As if he wanted me to hear it was a warning and not an order. I think." He looked miserable. "Lucian's all right, really. I think he's frightened too."
Corin did not say anything. He took the Ledger out of his coat and wrote it down, small, at the bottom of the page. Lucian came down. Asked if I'd been in. "Remind him the Pell file is closed." A warning, Toby thinks. Not an order.
Then he closed the book and looked at Toby.
"I need to find a file," he said. "Not ours. Not a Harvesting file. A file from upstairs. And I need you not to ask me why."
Toby's face did a great many things at once. It went white, and then pink, and then white again. His mouth opened and shut. His big owl eyes went from Corin's face to the plate with the half-bun on it, and back, as if the bun might have some advice.
Then he pushed his spectacles up his nose with one inky finger, and sat down at his desk, and pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward him, and picked up his pen.
"What name?" he said.
The Index was on the Second Basement. Toby said so. It was the only part of the Second Basement that was not mice. It was a long room full of cabinets, wooden cabinets with hundreds of small drawers, and in every drawer there were cards, and on every card there was a name, and next to every name there was a number. Every file in the Ministry had a card. Every card had a home. You could find anything, Toby said, his voice getting faster and faster as they went up the stairs, anything at all, if you knew how the cards were sorted, and almost nobody did, because the cards had been sorted by six different supervisors in sixty years and every one of them had had his own ideas.
"But I know," said Toby, at the door, a little breathless. "I learned. In my first year. On my own time. Because I thought, if you're going to work in a building made of paper, Toby, you'd better know where the paper is."
He went to a cabinet near the end, without looking, the way Juno went over roofs. He ran his finger down the little brass labels on the drawers. He stopped. He pulled one out. It came out a long way, two feet or more, packed tight with gray cards standing on their edges, and he walked his inky fingers along them, very fast, flick, flick, flick, his lips moving.
"Persons," he muttered. "Persons, Wards of the Council. Not Persons, Deceased. Not Persons, Indebted. Wards. Ta, Te, Th—Thackeray, Thane, Thistlewood, Thorne." His fingers stopped. "Thorne."
There were three cards. Toby lifted them out one at a time and laid them on the top of the cabinet in a row, and he and Corin bent over them together.
The first one said: THORNE, Edmund. Lord. Deceased. See Persons, Deceased. And a number.
The second one said: THORNE, Isolde. Lady. See Persons, Deceased. See also SEALED. And no number at all. Just the word SEALED, stamped across the card in red, so hard that the ink had bled.
Corin looked at it for a long moment.
Then at the third.
THORNE, Aurelian. Lord. Councillor (lapsed). Ward of the Council.
Order of Removal to the House of Rest, Fallowfield. Moved by Cllr. C. Thorne (nearest family of age). Signed Cllr. Ld. Pembrook.
To be executed: Monday, noon. Carriage from Fallowfield. Escort of two.
File: 2 — 7 — 14 — 6.
Toby read it. He read it twice. His lips moved on Monday, noon. And then he looked up at Corin, and his big owl eyes behind the thick spectacles had gone very still.
"That's Miss Thorne's father," he said. "Isn't it. The Thorne girl. With the sword."
"Yes."
"They're taking him away. On Monday."
"Yes."
Toby looked down at the card again. Moved by Cllr. C. Thorne. He did not say anything for a long time.
Then, very quietly, so quietly that Corin almost did not hear it over the scratching of pens through the floor above, he said, "Level Two. Row Seven. Stack Fourteen. Box Six. That's the Wards room. Second floor, east side. They keep the live orders in the front, by the door, in a cabinet with a lock, so they're handy on the day." He swallowed. "I know where it is. I've been in there. Twice. Taking down Harvesting reports when a ward died with Ash in him."
"Thank you," said Corin.
He took out the Ledger and wrote it down. 2-7-14-6. Wards room. Second floor, east. Live orders in a locked cabinet by the door. And under it: Lady Thorne's card: SEALED. In red. No number.
When he looked up again, Toby was watching him.
Not the way Toby usually watched him. Not with his eyes going everywhere and his hands moving and his mouth half open to say something. Toby was standing quite still by the cabinet, with his inky hands at his sides, looking at Corin with his big owl eyes, and his thin pale face was very serious.
"Inspector," he said. "I know what you're thinking about."
Corin did not answer.
"I'm not stupid," said Toby. "Everyone thinks I am. Because I talk too much and I knock things over and I'm frightened of the jars. But I'm not." He took a breath. "You're thinking about the back of REGULATIONS, BOOK ONE."
Corin looked at him for a long time.
"Yes," he said.
Toby nodded slowly. He did not look frightened. That was the strange thing. Corin had expected him to go white, and shake, and say Saints, and knock something over. He did not. He stood by the cabinet with his collar crooked and his hair standing up at the back, and he looked, for the first time since Corin had read about him, not like a boy at all.
"Page two hundred and twelve," said Toby. "Regulation Forty, paragraph three. A file closed by Order 88-C may not be reopened by any Section, for any cause, without a fresh order under the same seal." He pushed his spectacles up his nose. "Under the same seal, Inspector. The Archon's. And the Archon doesn't open files. Not ever. He's too busy. That's the whole point of an 88-C. Once it's closed, it's closed, and nobody can touch it." He paused. "Not even a Councillor."
"Toby—"
"I'd fill it in for you," said Toby. "If you wanted. My handwriting's better than yours. Everybody's is. And I know where the pipe is on the second floor, and when the clerk in the Wards room goes for his dinner." He was speaking quite steadily. "It would take ten minutes."
Corin did not say anything for a long moment.
He thought about a stationer's back room at midnight, and an old man with a broken nose leaning across a workbench. You think about that boy, whoever he is, with his ink on his fingers and his book of regulations. Because if it goes wrong, it won't be you they come for first.
It'll be him.
"No," he said.
Toby's face changed. Just for a moment. Something went out of it, and something else came in. Something hurt.
"Not yet," said Corin. "Not no. Not yet. I need to think. I need to—" He stopped. He did not know how to say it. "If it goes wrong, Toby. If anybody upstairs ever found the batch. They'd come here. To your desk. Before they ever came to me."
Toby looked at him.
"I know," he said.
"Then—"
"I know, Inspector. I've known for two years. Since the day I didn't wake Supervisor Brightwater." Toby's ears had gone very pink. "That's why I kept them. I think. I didn't know it then. But I think I kept them for something. For a day like this. For somebody who needed them more than the pulp did."
The pens scratched through the ceiling, faint as rain. Somewhere in the walls, a tube went thunk, and whoosh.
"Promise me," said Corin. "Promise me you won't do anything. Not on your own. Not without me."
Toby looked at him for a long, long moment.
"I promise," he said at last. "Not without you." He put the three gray cards back in the drawer, in their places, very neatly, and slid the long drawer shut, and stood with his inky hand flat on the front of it. "But Monday's very soon, Inspector."
"I know."
"I just wanted you to know that I would." Toby took his hand off the drawer. "That's all. If you needed me to. I'm not brave. I'm not like you. But I would."
They went back down to the Third Basement, past the mice and the mold, without talking.
At the bottom of the stairs, by the black iron door of the Harvesting Section, there was a grating in the wall. Low down, at the height of a man's knee. Iron bars, black and rusted. Cool air came through it, from somewhere outside, and the smell of coal, and the sound, very faint, of a boiler growling in its sleep.
And a voice.
"Mister V! Mister V!"
Corin crouched down by the grating. On the other side of it, pressed against the bars, sooty and sharp and pink with cold, with her cap pushed back and her bright eyes very wide, there was a face.
"Juno," he said.
"Vespera says come," said Juno. She was out of breath. She had been running. "Now. Straight away. She says don't stop for nothing and don't bring nobody and don't tell nobody where you're going." She gulped. "She says it's needle work."
"Needle work?"
"That's what she said. Tell him it's needle work, and it's not mine." Juno's sharp face had gone pale under the soot. "There's a girl in your chair, Mister V. In Vespera's back room. A fishwife brought her in, at first light, off the Salt Stairs. Barefoot. In her shift. In the snow." She swallowed. "And she's all over writing. Both arms. And it's moving."
