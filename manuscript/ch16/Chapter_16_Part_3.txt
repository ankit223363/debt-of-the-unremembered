Chapter 16: The Girl with Wrong Tattoos, Part 3 of 5: Needle
Needle
"His name is Hollis," said Vespera. "Hollis Marsh."
She did not say it the way people say a name. She said it the way people put down something heavy they have been carrying up a long hill. Carefully. All at once. And then stood looking at it.
"They call him Needle now. In the Cut. In the dens on the Lower Docks, and in the places on the Hill where they don't call it a den. Ask for Needle. Needle can do it. Needle can make your wife believe you were home all night." Her mouth twisted. "He was always thin. Even as a boy. Thin as a needle, and sharp as one, and he'd go right through you if you weren't careful. That's where it came from, I think. I don't know who gave it him. It wasn't me."
She got up off her stool. She went to the long table, among the pots of ink, and stood with her back to them all, and her stained hands flat on the wood.
"He came to me when he was twelve," she said. "A boy from the Cut. Nobody's. His mother was dead and his father was a Slate, sitting in a doorway on Soap Lane smiling at the rain. He came in here one winter, in a coat made of a sack, to sell me a bottle of ink he'd stolen off a cart. Very good ink. Oak-gall and iron, the best. And while I was counting out his coppers, he looked at my arms." She was quiet a moment. "He looked at them a long time. And then he said, How do you make it go in so it stays? And I looked at his hands. On my counter. Thin, and dirty, with the nails bitten to nothing. And as still as stone."
"Like Rook's," said Corin. He did not know why he said it.
Vespera turned her head and looked at him over her shoulder.
"Yes," she said. "Like that. Clever hands. The kind you're born with or you're not. I'd waited twenty years for hands like that to walk in at my door." She turned back to the table. "So I took him. I gave him the coppers for the ink, and a bowl of soup, and the little room up the ladder to sleep in. And in the morning I put a needle in his hand."
The lamp hissed. On the stool by the stove, Bet Mallow sat very still with her big red arms folded.
"Twelve years," said Vespera. "He was with me twelve years. From a boy to a man. He swept my floor and boiled my needles and ground my crystal and mixed my ink, and every night at that table, after the shop was shut, I sat beside him and taught him the letters." She touched the edge of the table with one stained finger. "There. Right there. His elbow wore that mark in the wood. You can still see it."
Corin looked. There was a mark. A small pale hollow, worn smooth into the dark old wood at the corner of the table, where an elbow might rest, night after night, for years.
"He was the best I ever had," said Vespera. "The best I ever saw. Better than me, by the end. His lines were finer. His hand was steadier. He could write a whole page on the inside of a man's wrist, so small you'd need a lens to read it, and every letter perfect." She shook her head slowly. "Except the g. He always did that hook on the g. From the very first night. I don't know where he got it. Some sign over some shop in the Cut, I suppose, that he'd looked at when he was little and couldn't read. I tried for four years to break him of it. I'd smack his hand with a ruler every time. He'd say sorry, sorry, and do it again on the next line." Something that was almost a smile moved her mouth, and went. "In the end I let it go. I thought, every Binder has something. Some little mark of their own. That was his."
"And then?" said Corin.
Vespera did not turn round.
"And then a lady's man came into my shop," she said. "Ten years ago. In the spring. A big man, in a good coat, with a gold ring. While I was out. And he talked to Hollis. For a long time. And when I came back, Hollis was standing at that table with a purse in his hand. A fat one. Gold. More gold than I make in a year." Her voice was quite flat. "And he said, Vespera. They want me to write something for them. Just one thing. On just one man. It's only a little thing. It's only a little lie."
The needles hung on their threads over her head, very still.
"I said no," said Vespera. "I said, Write only what is true, Hollis. That's the law. That's the only law. And he laughed at me. Not a nasty laugh. He was never nasty. A sad one. And he said, Who says what's true, Vespera? The Ministry writes lies every day, in every file, and stamps them, and calls them records. Every Slate in this city has a lie on him somewhere, in the Ministry's hand. Why should they be the only ones who get paid for it?"
She was quiet for a long moment.
"And I didn't have an answer," she said. "That's the truth. That's what I've carried for ten years. I stood here at this table with fifty years of true things written on my arms, and a boy I'd raised asked me why the truth should only be for the poor, and I didn't have an answer." She looked down at her stained hands. "So I hit him. Across the face. With the back of my hand. As hard as I could." Her voice did not change. "And I took his indenture out of the drawer, the paper with my name on it and his, and I burned it in that stove. In front of him. And I told him to get out."
"And he went."
"And he went." Vespera nodded slowly. "He took his needles, and the purse, and his coat. And at the door he stopped. And he looked back at me. And he said, I'll only ever write little ones, Vespera. I promise. Only little lies." She breathed out. "And he went up the lane in the rain. And I haven't seen his face since. Not once. Ten years." She paused. "But I've seen his hand. On arms. In the Cut. On the docks. Men who believe they paid debts they never paid. Women who believe their husbands were kind. A man on the Salt Stairs, two winters ago, who believed he'd killed his own brother and threw himself in the river." Her voice was very low. "I've seen that hook on the g more times than I can count. And every time, I think, only little ones, Vespera. I promise."
She turned round at last.
Her face, in the lamplight, was wet. She did not wipe it.
"This isn't little," she said. She looked at the girl in the leather chair. At the purple writing crawling on her thin arms. A girl with short black hair. And a sword. "This is a rope. For a girl of twenty. With his hook on the g." Her mouth shook, once, and went hard. "So now I know what he's worth. Ten years, and a fat purse, and now this."
Corin looked at her.
At her stained hands, held a little away from her body. At the wet on her wide brown face. At her left wrist, where the sleeve of her old wool dress had fallen back a little as she turned. At the inside of it, below the heel of the hand, where the skin was thin and the blue veins showed through, among the hundred tiny lines of names and dates.
At the one line that was not finished.
HOLL—
He did not know he was going to say it until he had.
"Vespera," he said. "Your wrist."
She went very still.
She did not look down at it. She did not pull the sleeve over it, the way the Ledger said she always did. She just stood there by the table with her hands held away from her, and looked at him, and her face did not move at all.
"Is that him?" said Corin. Very quietly. "Hollis? Is that what you started to write?"
The stove ticked. The girl in the chair whispered, I was on the steps. Juno, by the little low door, had stopped breathing.
"No," said Vespera.
She said it quite steadily.
"No. That's older than him. That's much older." She looked down at last, at her own wrist, at the four faded letters and the line. "I wrote that fifty years ago, Corin. When I was a girl, younger than your Lyra, still sweeping Old Haddon's floor. Before Hollis was born. Before his mother was born, likely." She touched it with the tip of one stained finger. "It was a name. I'm nearly sure it was a name. Somebody I loved, very much. And I wrote the first four letters, and then I couldn't remember the rest. And I never have."
"Then—"
"Then it isn't him." Vespera's voice was very low. "It can't be. It was there before him."
She stood looking at it for a long moment.
"But I'll tell you what I've never told anyone," she said. "Not Haddon. Not Bram. Not you, in six years in that chair." She did not look up. "When that boy came in at my door, at twelve, in his coat made of a sack, and put his stolen ink on my counter, and I asked him his name. And he said Hollis." Her finger rested on the four letters. "I stood there with his coppers in my hand, and my heart stopped. Like a clock. Because it was the same. The first four letters. H. O. L. L. The same as the name I'd lost."
She took her finger away.
"And I've wondered," she said. "Every day since. Whether that's why I took him. Not his hands. His name. Because some foolish part of me thought, there, Vespera. There it is. It came back." She pulled her sleeve down over her wrist, slowly, and held it there. "And it wasn't. It was only a boy. A boy who happened to have the same four letters. And I gave him everything I knew, and he took it, and went up the lane in the rain and used it to write ropes."
Nobody said anything.
After a while Vespera let go of her sleeve. She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, once, hard. And she came back to the stool in front of the leather chair, and sat down on it, and looked at the girl's arms.
"That's enough of that," she said. Her voice was the old voice again. Hard and dry and sure. "I can't undo ten years. But I can undo this." She held out one stained hand toward the long table without looking round. "Juno. The black velvet tray. And the lens. The big one, with the brass handle. And light every lamp in this room, child, I don't care who sees. I want to see every letter he put on her."
Juno went.
And Corin got up off the floor, and found the lamp on the end of the table, and turned it up as high as it would go, and held it over the girl's thin purple-written arm, steady, while Vespera bent close with the lens and began, slowly, from the wrist, to read.
The Wrong Letter
It took a long time to read it all.
Juno lit every lamp in the room. There were seven. The one on the long table, and two on the shelves, and one on the hook by the stove, and one in a bracket over the little low door, and two that she found in a cupboard and filled from a can and trimmed with a pair of scissors, her tongue between her teeth. She set them all round the leather chair, on the floor and on stools and on the end of the table, so that the girl sat in the middle of a ring of yellow light like a saint in a church window. The needles on their threads overhead caught the light and glittered, hundreds of them, very still. The room got warm. Then hot. The sour coin-smell of the false ink got stronger in the heat, until Corin could taste it on the back of his tongue.
Vespera did not seem to notice. She sat on her stool with the big brass-handled lens held close to the girl's left wrist, and she read.
She read aloud. Very low. Not to them. To herself, Corin thought, the way a person reads aloud when they are trying to learn something by heart. Line by line, from the wrist up toward the elbow, moving the lens a little at a time. And every few lines she stopped, and went back, and read a line again, more slowly, with her lips moving on each word.
"My name is Mary Coyle."
The first line. On the inside of the wrist, just below the heel of the hand, where Vespera always put a name.
"I am sixteen. I live on Soap Lane, at the top of the Cut, over the chandler's." A pause. The lens moved. "I have a cat. Her name is Smut. She is black with one white foot. She goes out at night."
"That's not her name," said Juno suddenly, from the floor by the stove.
Everybody looked at her.
"Mary Coyle," said Juno. Her sharp face was fierce. "There's a Mary Coyle on Soap Lane. Over the chandler's. Everybody knows her. She's got a cat called Smut, it's always on the chandler's sign, it's black with a white foot. And Mary Coyle's fat, Mister V. She's fat as a bun and she's got red hair and she's thirty if she's a day." She jabbed a finger at the girl in the chair. "That's not her. That's not Mary Coyle."
Vespera did not lower the lens. But her mouth went very thin.
"No," she said. "It's not. He's given her somebody else's name. A real one. With a real cat. On a real street." She was quiet for a moment. "So that if anyone goes to look, there's a Mary Coyle, and a chandler's, and a black cat with a white foot. And they'll never look at the girl's face long enough to see she's the wrong Mary."
She went on reading.
"On the night the doctor drowned, Smut did not come home. I went out to look for her. I went down Soap Lane and along the river to the Lantern Bridge, because she likes the fish heads under the bridge." The lens moved. "It was late. It was dark. There was a lantern on the steps under the bridge. On the landing. I went down to look for Smut."
The girl in the chair had gone quiet. She was not whispering anymore. She was sitting very still in the middle of the ring of lamps, with her raw red hands in her lap, and her eyes on Vespera's face, and as Vespera read each line her lips moved with it. Silently. Word for word. As if she were hearing a song she knew.
"There was a man on his knees on the landing. A little round man. A doctor, with a black bag." The lens moved up toward the elbow. "There was a girl standing over him. A lady. Young. With short black hair, cut short like a boy's, with a lock falling over her forehead. She had a sword. A thin bright sword."
Vespera stopped. She went back. She read that line again. Cut short like a boy's, with a lock falling over her forehead.
"He's seen her," she said. Not looking up. "Hollis. Or somebody's told him very carefully. That's her hair. Exactly. The way she cuts it with a knife."
Corin did not say anything. His hand on the lamp had gone tight.
"She hit the doctor with the handle of the sword. On the head. He fell in the river. He did not come up." The lens moved. "She laughed. She said, That's for my father. And she went up the steps, right past me. And the lantern was on her face. And I saw her eyes. They were gray. Thorne gray. Like the portrait in the window of Niles's shop."
Vespera lowered the lens.
"Niles's shop," she said.
"There's a portrait in Niles's window?" said Corin.
"There's a portrait of every Thorne in the city in somebody's window," said Vespera. "They sold everything, two years ago. It was in all the broadsheets." She raised the lens again. "It doesn't matter if there's one in Niles's or not. A girl from the Cut who's never been up the Hill wouldn't know what Thorne gray is. So he's given her somewhere she might have seen it. Somewhere ordinary. A shop window." Her voice was flat. "He's very careful. He was always careful."
She read on. Up past the elbow. Onto the upper arm. The lines there were the same story again, told a second time, a little differently, with different words, the way a real person tells a thing they saw more than once. The doctor on his knees. The black hair. The sword. That's for my father. Gray eyes. And then the right arm, and the same again, and again, and a fourth time across the shoulders, so that every part of her body, Corin thought, would remember it. So that if anyone ever asked her, she would not have to look for the words. They would simply be there. Everywhere. In her wrists and her elbows and her shoulders and the back of her neck.
And at the very end, high up on the back of the right shoulder, where it ran down under the edge of the shift, the last line.
"I did not tell anyone, because I was frightened. But now I must, because it is right. I saw her face, and it was her."
Vespera stopped.
She had stopped in the middle of the line. On one word. Her lens hung still over the girl's thin freckled shoulder, and her small dark eyes behind it had gone very narrow.
"What?" said Corin.
Vespera did not answer. She leaned closer. Closer still. Her face almost touching the girl's skin. She turned the lens a little, one way, and then the other, catching the light from the seven lamps.
"That's not ours," she said.
She moved aside on her stool, just a little, so that Corin could see. And she held the lens for him, steady, over the girl's shoulder. Over the last line. Over the space between face and it was her.
There should have been a word there. A small word. And. Three letters, small and neat and purple, like all the others.
There was not.
There was a sign.
It was the same purple ink as the rest. The same oily shine. It was not big. No bigger than the letters round it. But it was not a letter. Not any letter Corin knew. It was a single straight stroke, standing upright, like the trunk of a small tree. And from the foot of it, three short lines went down and out, spreading, like roots going into the ground. And at the very top of the upright stroke, there was a tiny closed circle. Like a bud. Or a head.
A tree, with roots, and a round head on top.
Corin stared at it.
And as he stared, it moved. Not like the others. The others shivered, and crawled, and changed, and came back. This one did not change. It stayed exactly what it was. But all round it, the letters of face and it was her were shivering, hard, faster than anywhere else on her arms. Pulling. Pushing. As if they were trying to get away from it. As if it were a stone dropped into a pond, and they were the ripples.
The girl in the chair made a small sound in her throat. Like a hurt animal.
"What is it?" said Corin.
Vespera took the lens away. She sat back on her stool. Her face in the yellow light of the lamps was very strange.
"It's northern," she said. "Ostmark. The old kingdoms in the mountains, past the end of the river, where the snow never melts." She looked at the sign on the girl's shoulder. "It's one of their letters. Their old letters, from before. Old Haddon had a book of them. A little brown book, with the spine gone. He showed it me once, when I was a girl, when he was in a good mood, which wasn't often." She was quiet a moment. "I'd forgotten I knew it. I haven't thought of that book in forty years."
"What does it mean?"
"It doesn't mean and," said Vespera. "That's the first thing. I'll tell you that for nothing." She touched the edge of the lens with one stained finger. "In the north, Haddon said, memory magic is a crime. Worse than murder. They don't burn. They don't harvest. They don't write in skin. They sing. Everything that matters, every name, every law, every king, every war, they make into songs, and they teach the songs to children, so that nothing can ever be taken." She paused. "What is sung cannot be stolen. That's their law. Haddon said it was carved over every door in the mountains."
She looked at the little tree on the girl's shoulder.
"That sign is what their scribes put in front of something that's been sung," she said. "A true thing. A thing they swear on. A thing that's been made into a song so it can never be lost." Her voice was very low. "It means this is true. This was sung. This cannot be taken."
Nobody spoke.
Corin looked at the sign. At the tiny tree with its roots and its round head, standing in the middle of a lie.
"He put a truth-mark," he said slowly, "in the middle of a lie."
"In the very last line," said Vespera. "The line that ties it all off. The line that says it was her. The knot at the end of the thread." She shook her head. "And the crystal went down into her, carrying his lie, and it got all the way to the end, to the knot, and found that. This is true. This cannot be taken. And the crystal didn't know what to do. And her own mind, underneath, heard it too. Heard it say true, and looked at the lie it was holding, and said no. No, that's not true. That's not mine." She looked at the girl's face. "And pushed. And it's been pushing ever since."
The girl in the chair was looking at Vespera. Her eyes were very wide and very wet. Her lips were moving, without sound.
No. No, that's not mine.
"So it saved her," said Juno, from the floor. In a small voice. "The wrong letter. It saved her."
"It saved her," said Vespera. "And it saved your Thorne girl too. Very likely." She set the lens down on the black velvet tray, carefully. "For now."
"Why would he do that?" said Corin. "Hollis. If he's so careful. Why would he make a mistake like that? With a letter he doesn't even know?"
Vespera did not answer at once.
"I don't know," she said at last. Slowly. "That's what frightens me. He doesn't know that letter. I never taught it him. I never knew it myself, not to write. I only saw it once in Haddon's little book, forty years ago, and forgot it." She frowned at the purple sign. "And that's not a mistake a Binder makes. You don't write a letter you don't know by accident. Your hand doesn't just go there. You have to mean to." She was quiet a moment. "Or you have to be copying."
"Copying?"
"From something written down. A page. A paper. Somebody else's words, that you've been told to put on exactly as they're written." Her small dark eyes came up to his face. "Hollis was always a good copier. The best. Give him a page and he'd put it on a man's arm so true you could lay the one over the other and not see a hair between them. Every comma. Every crossed t." She looked back at the little tree. "Every letter, even if he didn't know what it was."
Corin took the Ledger out of his coat. He opened it at the last page. And very carefully, with the stub of pencil from the spine, he drew it. The upright stroke. The three roots spreading from its foot. The tiny round head on top.
Under it he wrote: Ostmark letter. Northern. Their truth-mark: "This is true. This was sung. This cannot be taken." In Hollis's false ink, in the last line, in place of AND. It broke the lie. Hollis doesn't know this letter. Vespera thinks he was COPYING.
And then: Copying from whose page?
He looked at it for a long moment. Then he closed the book.
When he looked up, Vespera had pulled her stool closer to the chair. She had laid her stained hands, palms up, on her knees. And she was looking at the girl. Not at the writing. At the girl's face.
"Now then, lamb," she said, very gently. "Now I know what he wrote. All of it. Every word, and the one that's wrong." She held up one hand, slowly, so as not to startle her. "And now we're going to take it off you. A letter at a time. And it's going to be a long afternoon. And you're going to have to help me."
The girl looked at her.
On her left wrist, the first line shivered. My name is Mary Coyle.
"Because to take a lie out," said Vespera, "I need something true to put in its place. Something real. Something of yours." Her voice was soft as wool. "And the first thing I need, lamb, is your name."
