Chapter 26: What the Dead Remember, Part 3 of 5: The Man Behind the Chair
The Man Behind the Chair
He was standing in the shadow behind Lady Vivienne's chair, between the fire and the nearest of the tall glass cases.
He was tall. Taller than Kross, taller than either of the big men behind Tam. He stood very straight, with his hands clasped behind his back and his feet a little apart, the way soldiers stand when they have been told to wait. He had been standing like that, Corin understood, since before Tam came up in the lift. Quite still. In the shadow. Watching. And Tam had not seen him, because a footman's eyes are always on the wall, and because the man had not wanted to be seen.
Now he had moved. One step. Out of the deepest part of the shadow, into the edge of the colored light from Vivienne's throat. And the light fell on his coat.
It was not a coat from Veridia.
Corin knew that at once, the way you know a word is not in your own language before you know what it means. He had never seen a coat like it on any street in the city. Not on the Hill, not in the Docks, not in the Ministry. It was long, longer than a Veridian coat, almost to the ankles, and it was made of a heavy, close, dull cloth the color of old iron. Not gray. Not black. The color of an iron gate that has stood out in the rain for a hundred years. It had a high stiff collar that came right up under the jaw, so that the man's head seemed to sit on it like a stone on a pillar. Down the front, from the collar to the waist, there were two rows of buttons. Flat round buttons, not brass, not silver. Iron. Each one stamped with a small shape that Corin could not quite make out in the shifting light. And at the cuffs there was a narrow band of dull dark red, like dried blood, like rust.
He had seen that coat once before. Not on a man. On a page.
In a file. In the Ministry. Years ago, in his first months in the Harvesting Section, when Toby had not yet come and he had been given the dull work, the copying, the sorting. A thick old file, tied with black tape, marked BORDER. NORTH-EAST. NOT FOR COPYING. And inside it, among the reports and the maps and the lists of names, a drawing. A careful pen-and-ink drawing of a man in a long coat with a high collar and two rows of flat iron buttons. And under the drawing, in a clerk's neat hand:
Officer of the Iron Guard of Karst. Rank shown by the cuffs. Red: colonel or above. If seen inside the border, report at once to the Office of the Archon. Do not approach.
Corin had copied out the file, every page. And then he had burned something, a few weeks later, for a case, and he had not remembered it again. Not until now. Not until he saw the coat through a dead man's eyes and the page came back to him all at once, whole and clear, like a lamp turned up in a dark room.
Do not approach.
The man in the iron-colored coat was looking at Tam.
He had a long, hard, weathered face, brown from the wind, with deep lines cut down from the sides of the nose to the corners of the mouth. Gray hair, very short, cut close to the skull like a soldier's. A short gray beard, trimmed square. And his eyes were pale. Not pale like Kross's eyes, washed-out and pleasant. Pale like ice on a deep lake. Pale and hard and very, very steady.
And they did not move. Not once. Corin had never seen eyes so still. They looked at Tam the way a man looks at a column of figures he has already added up.
Lady Vivienne did not turn round. She did not look at the man behind her chair. But Corin, through Tam, saw her shoulders change. Just a little. They went stiffer. Straighter. The way a woman's shoulders go when somebody she is afraid of walks into a room behind her.
And Corin understood something then, that he had not understood on Thursday night, sitting in the low chair across the fire from her.
Lady Vivienne was afraid of somebody.
"Colonel," said Silas Kross pleasantly, from his chair on the other side of the fire. He did not get up. "I didn't think you'd want to be seen."
The man in the iron coat said nothing for a moment. Then he spoke.
His voice was deep and slow, and it had a sound in it that Corin had never heard in Veridia. Not an accent, exactly. A kind of weight. As if every word was being carried a long way, over hard ground, and set down very carefully at the end.
"The boy is nothing," said the Colonel. "I do not care who sees me with the boy. The boy will not remember me."
He looked at Tam for another long moment. Then the pale eyes moved. To Lady Vivienne. To the back of her black head, with its jet pins.
"The books," he said. "The six. You have them?"
Lady Vivienne opened her fan. Closed it.
"By Sunday midnight," she said. Her voice was as calm as ever. Perfectly calm. But Corin heard, through Tam's ears, something under the calm. Something very thin and very tight, like a wire. "I have offered the Harvester a price he will not refuse. He will bring them to my kitchen door. And then they are yours."
"And if he refuses?"
"Then Gant will go to the livery stable at first light, with the whole of the downstairs, and take the old man in the white room, and we will have something to trade." A pause. "Either way, Colonel. By Monday you will have your books."
The Colonel said nothing.
He looked at the back of her head for a long time. Then his pale eyes moved again, slowly, round the glass room. Over the tall cases, with their cut-crystal vials glowing on black velvet. Joy. Mercy. Grace. Love (first). Over the great dome of glass and iron overhead, and the frosty stars beyond it. And came to rest, at last, on Silas Kross.
"And the Harvester himself?" he said.
Kross smiled.
"Leave the Harvester to me," he said. "I want to see who he runs to."
"You have said this before."
"And I'll say it again. He's more use to us running than caught. Every door he knocks on is a door we didn't know about. Every friend he goes to is a friend we can find later." Kross moved the barley sugar from one cheek to the other. "Let him run, Colonel. Let him run all night. By the end of it I'll have every name in the city that matters to him, written down in a neat little list. And then we can decide what to do with them."
The Colonel looked at him without any expression at all.
"In Karst," he said slowly, "we do not let a thing run, once we have it in our hand."
"In Karst," said Kross pleasantly, "you remember everything. Every face. Every name. Every word ever said to you. Your famous iron memories." He spread his gray-gloved hands. "Here in Veridia we have to write things down. It makes us patient."
Something happened in the Colonel's hard brown face. It was too small and too quick to be a smile. Corin was not sure it was anything at all.
Then the Colonel turned his head.
Not toward Vivienne. Not toward Kross. Toward the far side of the glass room. Toward the doors of the vial-lift, standing open, with the lamplit cage beyond them.
Someone was coming up.
Corin felt Tam hear it. The creak of the cables. The slow grinding of the great wheel, down in the vault, as the lift-boy turned it. The cage rising, slowly, up the shaft. Somebody else was coming to the glass room. Somebody who had been sent for.
And the Colonel took one step back into the shadow behind Lady Vivienne's chair. Then another. Until the iron-colored coat was only a darker shape against the dark, and the pale eyes were two small cold points of light, and then not even that.
He did not want to be seen by whoever was coming.
Or he did not want whoever was coming to know that he had been seen.
The lift cage came up level with the floor of the glass room, and stopped, with a small soft jolt. The lift-boy, very small, very pale, pulled the cage door open. And stepped back. And looked down at his own feet, and did not look up again.
A man stepped out of the lift.
And Tam Hollin, for the first time in six years as a footman on the Hill, forgot to keep his eyes on the wall.
The Left-Turning Spiral
Corin could not see his face.
That was the first thing, and the strangest. He was looking at the man through Tam's eyes, and Tam was looking straight at him, for the first time in six years forgetting the wall, staring, the way a child stares. And still Corin could not see the face.
It was not that it was hidden. There was no hood. No mask. No shadow over it. The lamplight from the lift cage fell full on it, and the colored light from Vivienne's throat, and the red light from the fire. It was simply that every time Tam's eyes went to the face, they slid away from it. The way your eyes slide off a very bright light. The way your foot slides off a wet stone. Tam looked, and looked again, and each time his eyes came away and found themselves looking at something else. A shoulder. A sleeve. The floor.
There, said Malakor. Very quietly. Very far away, as if he were speaking from the bottom of a well. There it is. You feel that? That sliding? That's a man who doesn't want to be remembered. And knows how not to be.
So Corin stopped trying to see the face. He looked at the rest.
The man was not tall. Not short. Not thin. Not stout. He wore a plain dark coat, very plain, with no buttons showing, of a good dark cloth that did not catch the light. Under it, a plain dark waistcoat and a white shirt. No jewelry. No chain. No pin. He stood very easily on the thick rugs, just outside the lift, with his hands loose at his sides, the way a man stands in his own house.
His hands were bare.
They were long hands. Clean. Pale, with long fingers and smooth, rounded nails. The hands of a man who had never carried anything heavier than a pen. And on the third finger of the left hand, there was a ring.
Tam's eyes found the ring and stayed there, because the ring was easier to look at than the face. A heavy ring. Gold. With a flat seal set into it, the kind men press into wax. And on the seal, small and sharp, cut into the gold, a shape. A dome. With lines going out from it. Like the rays of a sun coming up.
Corin felt his own heart, a long way off, in his own body, kneeling on the stone floor of an arch under the Dye-Works, give one great hard thump.
He had seen that shape on a carriage door. He had seen it, that very afternoon, in red ink, in the margin of an old green ledger. Do not reopen. By order.
And in the man's right hand, held loosely, the way another man might hold a walking stick, there was a rod.
A silver rod. Thin. About the length of a man's forearm. Exactly like Corin's own. Exactly like the one lying across his own knees, right now, in the arch, wrapped in the last of Tam's gray light.
Almost exactly.
The spiral at its tip went the other way.
Corin saw it. Tam did not know what he was seeing. Tam did not know about rods, or spirals, or which way they turned. But Tam's eyes saw it, and so Corin saw it, clear and sharp in the lamplight. A fine spiral cut into the silver tip. Going round. And round. And round. Toward its middle.
To the left. Against the sun. Like water going down a drain on the other side of the world.
I only ever knew one other harvester who cut his spiral to the left, Malakor had said once, in the Harvesting Section, with the jar on Elara's sheet of glass between them.
Malakor did not say anything now. Corin could feel him, at the edge of everything, behind his left ear. Very still. Not breathing, if a voice in a rod could breathe. The way a mouse goes still in the corner of a room when the cat walks in.
"Good evening, Vivienne," said the man.
His voice was gentle. That was the worst of it. Gentle and warm and kind, like the voice of a doctor at a bedside, or a teacher bending over a child's slate. It did not slide away, the way the face did. It went in. It went into Tam's ears and down into his chest and settled there, warm, and Tam's frightened heart, which had been going like a drum, began to slow. Corin felt it slow. Felt Tam's shoulders loosen, and his hands at his sides stop shaking, and something in him lean toward that voice, the way a cold man leans toward a fire.
Corin wanted to scream at him. Don't. Don't. Don't listen to it.
But this had already happened. Two nights ago. And there was nothing he could do.
"Good evening," said Lady Vivienne. She did not get up. But Corin saw her hand close on her fan. "I didn't know you'd come yourself."
"Silas sent for me. He said there was a young man who had stood beside my Harvester." The gentle voice turned, a little. Toward Tam. "Is this him?"
My Harvester.
"This is him," said Kross. He had stood up. Corin had not seen him do it. He was standing by his chair now, very straight, with his gray-gloved hands folded in front of him. Not lounging anymore. Not easy. The way a clerk stands when the head of the department comes into the room.
The man walked across the glass room toward Tam.
He did not hurry. His shoes made no sound on the thick rugs. He came and stood in front of Tam, close, so close that Tam could have reached out and touched his plain dark coat. And Tam did not step back. He could not. He did not want to. The gentle voice had settled in his chest like a warm stone, and he only wanted to stand there, and be looked at, and do whatever he was told.
"Don't be afraid," said the man. "This won't hurt. Not very much."
And he lifted the silver rod, and touched the tip of it, very lightly, to the middle of Tam's forehead.
Just above the small white scar.
Corin felt it.
Not as Tam had felt it. As something else. As something much worse. Because Corin knew this. Corin knew this feeling. He had felt it once before. A long time ago. Six years ago. In the dark. He did not remember it. He had burned it, or it had been burned for him. But his body remembered. His skin remembered. The bones of his skull remembered. A cold point of silver against the forehead, very light, very gentle. And then a pull. A slow, steady, terrible pull, going round and round, to the left, against the sun. Like water going down a drain.
Like a house being emptied. One room at a time.
The man did not empty Tam. He only looked. Corin could feel him looking. It was like Elara's candle, going through the rooms of a house in the dark. But Elara's candle had been careful. Elara's candle had gone past the soft things and not touched them. This was not a candle. This was a hand. A long clean pale hand, going through the rooms of Tam Hollin, opening drawers, lifting lids, turning things over, and putting them back not quite where they had been. Quickly. Lightly. The way a thief goes through a house when he knows exactly what he is looking for.
He was looking for Corin.
Corin felt him find it. Every memory Tam had of him. The servants' hall on the Hill. The bread broken in half. Name's Tam. The white gloves on the end of the bed. The kitchen door at the Bourse. The vial-lift. The vault. Bram's back room, and the glue pot going blup. The hand went through every one of them, quickly, turning them over, looking at them. Looking at Corin's face, in each one. Through Tam's eyes.
And then it stopped.
The rod came away from Tam's forehead.
The man stood back. He looked at Tam for a moment. Then he turned, and looked at Kross. And Corin, through Tam, still could not see his face. But he heard him smile.
That was the only way to say it. He heard it. In the voice. A smile that came into the gentle voice like warmth comes into a room when somebody opens the door of a stove.
"He's grown," said the man softly. "Hasn't he."
"He has," said Kross.
"Taller. Thinner. Tired. Very tired." The man turned the silver rod slowly in his long fingers. "He's burned a great deal since I last saw him. A great deal. Six years of it. I'd hardly know the house." A pause. "But the doors are still marked. Every one. Just as I left them. I could find my way through him in the dark."
Kross said nothing.
"I emptied him myself, you know," said the man. Gently. Almost fondly. The way a man speaks of a garden he once dug. "I don't often do it myself, anymore. But that one I did. Every room. And I wrote on every door, so I would know them again." The smile came into his voice again. "All but one."
"The locked one," said Kross.
"The locked one." The man was quiet for a moment. "I could not open it. Not then. It was locked from the inside, by somebody who loved him very much, and who knew exactly what I was. So I left it. I thought it would starve, in there, alone, in the dark, with all the other rooms empty around it. I thought it would wither away to nothing in a year or two." He turned the rod again. "It hasn't. It's still there. Still locked. And getting stronger. I could feel it, just now, even through the boy. Like a heartbeat behind a wall."
He looked down at the rod in his hand.
"When it opens," he said, very softly, "and it will open, Silas, one day, it is waiting for something, it is waiting for him to find something, and when he does it will open—I want to be there. I want to be the first thing he sees."
And the smile in his voice grew warmer.
And warmer.
And Corin, a long way off, kneeling in the dark under the Dye-Works with the glowing rod across his knees, felt the locked door at the end of his own house give one great hard shudder, as if something on the other side of it had thrown itself against the wood.
