Chapter 26: What the Dead Remember, Part 1 of 5: Under the Dye-Works
Under the Dye-Works
The Dye-Works stood on the edge of the river, at the bottom of a slope of mud and broken bricks, and it never slept.
Corin had passed it a hundred times by day. A great black ugly building, all chimneys and pipes and tanks, with steam coming out of every crack and the river below it running in stripes. Red, near the outflow pipe. Then blue. Then a sickly yellow-green. Then brown again, as the colors faded into the river's own color and were carried away to the sea. By day, the Dye-Works stank of ammonia and boiling cloth and something rotten, and you could smell it three streets away.
By night it was worse. And stranger.
The fires under the great vats were never let out. Their light came through the high barred windows of the Dye-Works in long red bars, and fell across the mud and the bricks and the black water, so that the whole slope looked as if it were lit by a sunset that never ended. The steam went up white into the dark, and the red light caught it from below, and it glowed like the breath of some great sleeping beast. There was a sound, always, a deep slow thudding, like a heart. The pumps. And under it, a hiss, like a kettle the size of a house.
Juno led him down the slope. Not by the road. By a path that was not a path, a crooked line of flat stones and planks laid across the mud, that you could only see if you knew it was there. Corin followed her, stepping where she stepped. The mud on either side of the path was black and soft and shining, and it smelled of dye and rot, and once his foot slipped off a plank and went into it up to the ankle, and it closed round his boot with a cold sucking grip like a hand, and Juno had to pull him out.
At the bottom of the slope, where the Dye-Works stood on its stone feet in the edge of the river, there were the arches.
They ran along under the building like the arches under a bridge. Great round arches of old black brick, a dozen of them, maybe more, going back into the dark under the floor of the Dye-Works above. The river came right up to the front of them at high tide. You could see the line of green weed on the brick where the water reached. But it was low tide now, and the river had drawn back, and there was a strip of stony mud in front of the arches, and on the mud, a fire.
Not a big fire. A brazier. An iron basket on three legs, full of glowing coals, with a battered kettle sitting on top of it. Around it, in a ring, there were people.
Corin knew some of them. He had seen them in the counting room under the Bell, last night, behind the bars, in the dark. He had seen their faces when the door was opened. Now they sat round the brazier on boxes and crates and upturned buckets, wrapped in blankets and sacks and coats that did not fit them, with the red light from the Dye-Works on their faces and the yellow light from the brazier on their hands.
John Rennick, the carter, with his big shoulders and his bandaged head, who had sold his own name to Gant's book for a debt he could not remember. Pegg, the washerwoman, who had cried all the way out of the cellar and had not stopped crying until they reached the arches. Tilly, who was twelve, and had sat in the corner of the counting room with her knees up to her chin and her eyes shut, and had not said a single word to anybody, and still had not. The white-haired old woman, the one Wat had carried. She was sitting closest to the brazier, wrapped in two blankets, with a tin mug in her hands. She was smiling at nothing.
And others. Seven men, three women, and the girl. Eleven. All of them. Every one he had let out.
They looked up as he came into the light.
Nobody said anything. Nobody moved. But Corin saw something go round the ring of faces, from one to the next, like a ripple going round a pond. He did not know what it was. It was not fear. It was not quite hope, either. It was more like the way people look up when a door opens in a cold room and someone comes in out of the night, and they wait to see if he has brought the cold in with him, or a fire.
Then John Rennick got up off his crate.
He was a very big man. Nearly as big as Wat. He stood up slowly, with one hand on his bandaged head, and he looked at Corin across the brazier. And then he did a strange thing. He took off his cap. It was an old cloth cap, greasy and shapeless, and he held it in both his big hands in front of his chest, the way a man holds his cap when he goes into a church.
And one by one, round the ring, the others did the same. The men took off their caps. The women put their hands to their shawls. Even the white-haired old woman, smiling at nothing, lifted her tin mug a little, as if she were drinking to someone.
Only Tilly did not move. She sat on her bucket with her knees up and her arms round them and her eyes open now, very wide, looking at him.
Corin stood there. He did not know what to do. Nobody had ever done that for him before. Nobody had ever taken off their cap.
"He's inside," said a voice. "Don't stand there gawping. He's been waiting all day."
Mother Grue came out of the dark of the nearest arch.
She was just as Corin remembered her. Very small, and very old, and so wrapped in shawls that she was nearly round. A black bonnet tied under her chin with a bit of string. A brown creased face like a walnut. Small black eyes, sharp as two pins. She had her poker with her, the black iron one with the hook at the end. She held it in one hand like a walking stick, and leaned on it, and looked at Corin.
Her eyes were red. She had been crying. Or not sleeping. Or both.
"Mother Grue," Corin said.
"Harvester." She looked him up and down. At the smoke stains on his coat, and the coal dust he had not been able to wash out of the seams, and the borrowed blue shirt, and the cut on the back of his hand. "You look worse than he does. Nearly." She sniffed. "Juno find you at the Bank?"
"On the quay."
"Good girl." Mother Grue put out one small brown hand without looking, and Juno came and took it, the way she always did, as if it were the most natural thing in the world. "Good girl. You ran all the way, I suppose. Over the roofs. You'll break your neck one day and then where will I get my cough drops."
"Off Doyle's," said Juno. "Same as always."
"Don't you be clever with me."
But she held Juno's hand tight. And Corin saw that her own hand was shaking.
"Where is he?" he said.
Mother Grue looked at him for a long moment with her sharp black eyes.
"Back of the third arch," she said. "Where it's driest. We put him by the wall, where the warm comes through from the vats. Wat brought a mattress off a barge. Pegg's been sitting with him. And the big quiet boy. The one who doesn't feel anything." She shook her head slowly. "That one hasn't left him once. Not since they brought him in. Not to eat. Not to sleep. Just sits. I don't understand it. He says he doesn't care. But he won't go."
Gideon, thought Corin.
"I've done what I can," said Mother Grue. "Hot bricks. Blankets. Broth, when he'd take it. He took a bit this morning. Not since." Her voice cracked, just a little, and she stopped, and swallowed, and went on. "It's the cold, Harvester. It's in him. Not on him. In him. I've put my hand on his chest and it's like putting my hand on a stone out of the river. I've seen people come in half-froze off the water, in thirty years under these arches, more than I can count. And I've seen them warm up again. And I've seen them not." She looked at him. "This isn't like either. This is something else. This is a cold that's eating."
Corin thought of the cold room under the Bell. The glowing blue shelves. The ice. North ice, Gant had called it, cut from the Reach, blue and hard and old, that never melted. That smelled of brass and oil and summer rain. And Tam, folded up small on it, with the frost in his hair, for a whole day and a night.
"Can I see him?" he said.
"That's what you're here for, isn't it." Mother Grue let go of Juno's hand. "You stay here, girl. By the fire. Get yourself warm."
"I want to come."
"I know you do. You stay."
Juno looked at her. Then at Corin. Her face was white and set and stubborn. But she did not argue. She went and sat down on an upturned bucket beside Tilly, close to the brazier, and held out her hands to the coals. Tilly looked at her sideways. And then, very slowly, without a word, the younger girl moved her bucket a little closer, until their shoulders touched.
Mother Grue turned, and leaned on her poker, and led Corin into the dark.
The Cold That Eats
The third arch went back a long way.
It was like walking into a cave. The red light from the Dye-Works windows reached in a little way and then gave up, and after that there was only a lantern, hanging from a nail in the brick far ahead, small and yellow and steady. The floor was stone, old flags laid on the mud, uneven, with puddles between them that shone in the lantern light. The walls were black brick, wet near the floor and dry higher up, and the roof curved over Corin's head so low near the sides that he had to walk in the middle.
And it was warm.
That surprised him. He had thought it would be cold under the arches, by the river, in winter. But the back wall of the arch was the wall of the Dye-Works itself, and on the other side of it, Corin knew, there were the great vats with their fires that never went out. The warmth came through the brick. He could feel it on his face as he got closer, a dry, steady, faintly sour warmth, like standing near a bread oven. The bricks of the back wall were dry and pale and dusty, and there was no damp on them at all.
Tam lay against that wall.
They had put him on a mattress, a thin striped mattress off a barge, stained with tar, with straw poking out of the seams. They had laid blankets over him, five or six of them, one on top of another, so many that he looked like a heap of washing. Somebody had put hot bricks wrapped in rags along his sides. Somebody had put a sack folded up under his head for a pillow. Somebody had put a tin cup of broth on the stone beside him, with a skin on it, gone cold.
Pegg the washerwoman sat on one side of the mattress, on a box. She had her red hands in her lap and her eyes were swollen.
On the other side sat Gideon.
He sat cross-legged on the bare stone, in his huge lost-property boots, with his thin blue hands resting on his knees. He did not have a blanket. He did not seem to need one. He was quite still. His pale empty face was turned toward Tam's face on the sack pillow, and he was looking at it the way he looked at everything, flatly, without any expression at all.
He looked up when Corin came in.
"You came," he said. His voice was the same as always. Flat. Clear. Like a list. "He asked for you. Two hundred and eleven times. I counted. Since this morning." He paused. "I don't know why I counted. I don't care. But I counted."
Corin knelt down on the stone beside the mattress.
Tam's face was turned toward the warm wall. It was very white. Not pale. White. White like paper, like chalk, like the frost on a window. His lips were the color of slate. His short hair, which had been brown, had gone dull, and there were still tiny crystals of frost in it, at the roots, near his scalp, that had not melted. Corin stared at them. The arch was warm. The bricks were warm. There was a hot brick against Tam's side, under the blankets, steaming faintly. And there was frost in his hair.
The small white scar through his left eyebrow stood out against the white skin like a tick on a page.
"Tam," said Corin.
The eyelids moved.
Slowly. So slowly. As if they were very heavy, or very cold, or both. They came up a little way and stopped. And under them, the quick brown eyes were not quick anymore. They were slow and dim and filmed over, like a window on a winter morning. But they moved. They found Corin's face.
And the slate-colored lips moved too.
"Vane," said Tam Hollin.
It was hardly a word. It was a breath with a shape in it. But Corin heard it.
"I'm here."
"Took your time."
Something happened in Corin's chest. Something that hurt. He did not know if it was a laugh or the other thing. It came out of him as a short, rough breath.
"Everyone keeps saying that," he said.
The corner of Tam's mouth moved. It might have been a smile. It was very small.
Corin put his hand on the blankets, over Tam's chest. He did it without thinking. And through the six thick blankets, through the wool and the cotton and the sacking, he felt it at once. The cold. It came up through the cloth into his palm, the way it had come up through the glass desk at the Bank, only worse. Much worse. The glass had been cold the way a river is cold. This was cold the way a stone is cold at the bottom of a deep well that the sun has never touched. A cold that did not sit still. A cold that moved. Slowly. Steadily. Like a tide coming in.
Mother Grue had been right. It was eating.
North ice, said Malakor, very quietly, behind his left ear. From the Reach. I've seen it once before, boy. Long ago. They used to keep it in the deep vaults, for storing the worst of what they took. Fear. Shame. The things that rot if they get warm. A pause. It's not water ice. It's memory ice. Thousands of frozen memories, packed together, so cold they burn. And he lay on it for a day and a night. A longer pause. It's in his blood now. All those frozen fears. It's going through him like frost goes through a pond. From the outside in. And when it reaches the middle—
Malakor did not finish.
Corin did not need him to.
"Mother Grue says you've been asking for me," he said.
Tam's dim eyes moved.
"Wanted…" A breath. A long pause. "Wanted to say. Before."
"Say what?"
Tam did not answer at once. His eyes went away from Corin's face, up, to the curved black roof of the arch, as if he were looking at something there. Then they came back.
"Gideon," he said. "Go away a minute."
Gideon looked at him. His pale face did not change.
"I don't want to go away," he said. "I don't want anything. But I don't want to go away."
"I know," said Tam. A breath. "Go anyway. Please."
Gideon sat very still for a long moment. Then he unfolded his thin legs and stood up, in his huge boots, and walked away down the arch toward the red light at the front. He did not look back. But at the edge of the lantern light he stopped, and stood there with his back to them, and did not go any further.
Pegg got up too. She put one red hand on Tam's forehead, gently, the way a mother does, and then took it away quickly, as if the cold had bitten her. She wiped her eyes on her apron. And she went after Gideon, and stood beside him.
Mother Grue did not go. She stood a little way off, leaning on her poker, in the shadow by the side wall. Tam did not ask her to go. Corin did not think anybody had ever asked Mother Grue to go anywhere and been obeyed.
Tam looked at Corin.
"You've got it?" he whispered. "The rod?"
Corin's hand went to the long narrow pocket on the right side of his coat. He did not take the rod out. He only touched it, through the wool. It was cold. Not the deep cold. Not the cellar cold. Just ordinary. Waiting.
"I've got it."
"Good." Tam's eyes closed. Then opened again, slowly. "Then… when it's time…" A long breath, rattling. "Take it. All of it. Everything."
Corin did not move.
"Tam—"
"Don't let it go to nothing." The whisper was very faint now, but very fierce. "Don't let it… just go. Into the air. Into the river. Like my mother's did. She sold it all. A bit at a time. Every good thing she had. To the Bank. And then there was nothing left, and they took her anyway, and when she died there was nothing to… nothing in her to…" He stopped. His slate lips shook. "Nobody harvested her. There wasn't anything. Just… empty. Like a jar."
Corin could not speak.
"I've got things," whispered Tam. "In here. Things I saw. In the glass room. Up top. Before they put me on the ice." His dim eyes held Corin's. "I saw them. All of them. I heard what they said. About you." A breath. "Take it. When I go. Before it gets cold. Take it and use it."
"You're not going—"
"Don't." Tam's voice was hardly there. "Don't lie, Vane. You're bad at it. Your face."
And from the shadow by the wall, Mother Grue made a sound that was nearly a laugh and nearly a sob.
Corin looked down at the white face on the sack pillow. At the frost in the roots of the hair. At the scar through the eyebrow.
He had harvested a great many dead. He had knelt in lanes and alleys and cellars and drawing rooms beside a great many bodies, and put the tip of the silver rod to the cold skin of their foreheads, and drawn out what was left. The last minutes. The last sights. The burnt-out Ash. He had done it so many times that he had stopped counting.
He had hardly ever been asked.
Almost nobody ever asked to be harvested. The dead did not ask. They had no say. The Ministry sent a Harvester, and the Harvester came, and took what he found, and wrote it down, and that was all. Nobody ever asked the dead if they minded.
Tam was asking.
"I'll take it," Corin said. His voice came out rough and low. "All of it. I promise."
Tam's eyes closed.
"Good," he breathed. "Good."
And under Corin's hand, through the six blankets, the cold moved a little further in.
