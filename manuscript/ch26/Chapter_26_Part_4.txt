Chapter 26: What the Dead Remember, Part 4 of 5: On Ice
On Ice
"And the footman?" said Lady Vivienne.
She had not moved from her chair. Her fan lay closed in her lap, and her pale hands lay on top of it, and her heavy-lidded eyes were on the man in the plain dark coat. Calm. Perfectly calm. But the wire was still there, under the calm. Corin could hear it.
The man did not answer at once. He was looking at the silver rod in his hand, turning it slowly, so that the left-turning spiral at its tip caught the firelight.
"He's yours, Vivienne," he said at last. "Do what you like with him." A pause. "Only—"
"Only?"
The man looked at Tam.
Tam was standing quite still on the thick rug, with his white-gloved hands at his sides. His eyes were not on the wall anymore. They were not on anything. They were wide and dull and a little wet, the way a man's eyes are when he has been woken too suddenly from a deep sleep. The warm stone of the gentle voice was still sitting in his chest. He did not know what was happening. He did not want to know. He only wanted to stand there, and be looked at, and do what he was told.
"Only don't burn him," said the man. "Don't sell him. Don't empty him. Keep him whole." He tilted his head a little, the way a man does when he is thinking of something pleasant. "Put him somewhere cold. Somewhere he'll keep. Gant has that room, under the Bell, doesn't he? With the north ice."
"He has."
"Put him there."
Lady Vivienne was silent for a moment.
"He'll die," she said. "In the end. On the north ice. It goes into the blood. A day, perhaps. Two, if he's strong."
"I know," said the man gently. "But he'll die slowly. And he'll die whole. With everything still in him. Every face he's ever seen. Including mine." The warmth came into his voice again. "And if my Harvester is as clever as I think he is, and as kind as I know he is, he'll find this boy before the end. He'll carry him out of the cold. He'll sit with him. He'll hold his hand." A small pause. "And when the boy dies, he'll take the Ash. All of it. Because the boy will ask him to. They always ask him to. He's that sort of man."
He turned the rod in his fingers.
"And then he'll see me," he said. "Standing here. In this room. Talking about him." The smile in his voice was very warm now. Very kind. "I'd like him to see me, Vivienne. I've waited six years. I'd like him to know that I'm still here. That I've never been very far away. That I've been watching him all this time, the way you watch a seed you've planted, to see what it grows into."
Corin, kneeling on the cold stone under the Dye-Works, with his own body a long way off and his own heart going like a hammer, heard the words go into him and lodge there, like splinters of ice.
He knew.
He knew I'd find Tam. He knew Tam would ask. He knew I'd take it.
He put Tam on the ice so that I would see this.
This is a letter. Tam is a letter. And it's addressed to me.
"And Silas," said the man.
"Sir."
"Keep letting him run." The gentle voice did not change. "But not forever. You'll know when. When he's found what he's looking for. When the door starts to open. Then bring him in." A pause. "Gently. I don't want him damaged. Not more than he is."
"And his friends?"
The man was quiet for a moment.
"His friends," he said, "are the doors he's knocked on. You have their names. Keep them. We'll need them later. People always come out of a house when you knock on their friends' doors." A pause. "Especially that one."
And for one moment, one very short moment, Tam's eyes went to the man's face, and did not slide off.
Corin saw a mouth.
That was all. Just a mouth. Not the eyes. Not the nose. Not the shape of the face. Just a mouth, for less than a breath, in the red light from the fire. Thin lips. Pale. And curving. Up at the corners. Slowly. Into a smile.
A smile that came at the very moment the man said especially that one. As if the words were sweet in his mouth. As if he had been waiting a long time to say them.
The man who smiles when he—
The line was in Corin's Ledger. He had written it weeks ago. He did not know where it had come from. He had found it in his own hand one morning, at the bottom of a page, unfinished. The man who smiles when he— And nothing after it. And he had never known what came after it.
Now he thought he knew. Or knew one way it could end.
The man who smiles when he hurts you.
Then Tam's eyes slid away again. And the face was gone. And there was only the plain dark coat, and the long clean hands, and the gold ring with the dome on it, and the silver rod turning to the left.
The man went back to the lift.
He did not say goodbye. He stepped into the lamplit cage, and the small pale lift-boy pulled the door shut, and down in the vault the great wheel began to turn again, slowly, grinding. The cage went down. The man in the plain dark coat went down with it, out of sight, into the shaft. And the gentle voice went with him. And the warm stone in Tam's chest went cold all at once, like a coal dropped into water.
Tam staggered. He nearly fell. One of the big men behind him caught his arm.
And the fear came back. All of it. All at once. Corin felt it come flooding back into Tam the way water floods back into a hole you have dug in the sand by the sea. Cold and black and huge.
Lady Vivienne sat very still in her chair by the fire. She was looking at the empty lift shaft. Her face was calm. Her pale hands lay on her closed fan. But Corin saw, through Tam's eyes, the knuckles of those hands. They were white.
Behind her chair, in the shadow, the man in the iron-colored coat stepped forward again. Into the edge of the light. His pale hard eyes were on the empty lift shaft too.
"That," said the Colonel slowly, in his deep, heavy voice, "is the man you serve?"
Lady Vivienne did not turn round.
"That," she said, "is the man everyone serves, Colonel. Sooner or later. Whether they know it or not."
The Colonel said nothing. But he looked at the empty lift shaft for a long, long time, with his ice-pale eyes, the way a soldier looks at a piece of ground he will one day have to cross.
Then Lady Vivienne lifted one hand.
"Take him down," she said. "To Gant. Tell him the room with the ice. Tell him not to burn him." She did not look at Tam. "Tell him to keep him whole."
The big men took Tam's arms.
The last thing Tam saw in the glass room was Silas Kross, sitting back down in his low chair by the fire, and taking another barley sugar out of his pocket, and unwrapping it, slowly, with his gray-gloved fingers. And folding the paper. In half. And in half again. And again. Smaller and smaller.
Then the lift. Then the shaft. Then a back stair, and a door, and the cold night air of a lane, and a cart, and a long jolting ride through the dark with a sack over his head. Then another stair, going down. Warm air, sour, like dirty bathwater. A small neat man in a cardigan, with gold spectacles, saying mildly, Oh dear. The ice room? Really? Well, if she says so. Then a hatch in a floor. Then cold.
Then cold.
Then cold.
Blue light. Glowing blue shelves. Hundreds of small frozen jars with hundreds of small labels. Cold. Fear. Shame. Cold. Cold. Cold. The ice under him, hard and smooth and burning. The cold going into him. Into his hands, first, through the white gloves. Then his feet. Then his knees. Then his chest. He curled up. He curled up small, smaller, as small as he could, with his knees to his chest and his head bent, the way you curl up in bed on a winter night when the blankets are too thin.
And he thought about rain on a slate roof.
He thought about it for a long time. Hours. He thought about the sound of it, very loud, very close, as if he were lying right under it in a narrow bed. And oranges. And his mother laughing like a kettle. And six plates on one arm, and the little lift of the elbow. And swimming. He had never learned. He would learn. One day. In summer.
And then, a long time later, a hand.
A warm hand. On his face. In the dark. The first warm thing in all that cold. And a voice, saying his name.
Tam.
And then nothing. Then gray. Then the sea again, rushing in, rushing out, and Corin was falling, falling up, out of the cold blue light and the glass room and the dark, up and up through twenty-two years of somebody else's life, toward a small yellow light very far away.
A lantern. Hanging from a nail. In an arch under the Dye-Works.
Coming Back
He came back on his hands and knees.
That was the first thing he knew. The stone under his palms. Cold and wet and gritty, with the edges of the flags pressing into the heels of his hands. He was on all fours on the floor of the arch, with his head hanging down, and his whole body was shaking, and he did not know how he had got there.
The second thing he knew was the sound. A terrible sound. A dry, rasping, heaving sound, over and over, very close. It took him a long time to understand that it was coming from himself. That it was his own breath, going in and out of his own throat, as if he had been running for a very long time, or drowning.
The third thing he knew was that he did not know his own name.
He knelt there on the stone, shaking, and reached for it. The way you reach for a cup on a shelf without looking. And his hand closed on nothing.
There was a name there. He could feel it. It was right there, in the front of his mind, where a name ought to be. Tam. That was the name. Tam Hollin. Born on Carrow Street, by the tanneries. Boot boy at nine. Footman at sixteen. Six plates on one arm. That was who he was. That was the name his hand found when he reached.
But it was wrong. He knew it was wrong. It did not fit. It was like putting on another man's coat in the dark and finding that the sleeves were too short and the shoulders too narrow and it smelled of somebody else.
Boy.
A voice. Dry and old and very close. Behind his left ear.
Boy. Listen to me. You're not him. You're not Tam. Tam's gone. You've got his Ash in you, that's all, and it's very loud right now, because there's so much of it, and it's all new. But it's not you.
He did not know whose voice it was. He did not know what it was talking about. He knelt on the stone and shook.
Your book, said the voice. Your book, boy. In your coat. Over your heart. Take it out. Open it. Read the first page.
His hand moved. He did not tell it to. It went inside his coat, by itself, as if it had done this a thousand times before, and found something there. Something warm. Something flat and square and soft, made of leather, worn smooth at the corners.
He took it out. He could not see it. His eyes were full of water. He held it close to his face, close to the yellow light of the lantern on its nail, and he opened it.
The first page.
Big black letters. Pressed so deep the pen had nearly cut the paper.
READ THIS FIRST.
He read it.
Your name is Corin.
He stopped. He read it again.
Your name is Corin.
And the coat of the other man came off him.
Not all at once. Slowly. The way a wet coat comes off, heavy, clinging, one arm at a time. He felt it go. He felt the name Tam slide back, and back, out of the front of his mind, to somewhere further in, where it belonged. Not gone. He did not think it would ever be gone. But in its place. A thing he had been given to keep. And in the space where it had been, the other name came forward, slowly, like a face coming up out of deep water.
Corin.
Corin Vane.
He read the rest of the page. You were born in Oakhaven. You had a younger sister named Elara. You used to fear the dark. He read it three times. And each time he read it, a little more of himself came back. The way the feeling comes back into a hand that has been asleep. Pins and needles, prickling, burning. But his.
He shut the Ledger. He held it against his chest with both hands.
And he looked up.
Mother Grue was kneeling on the stone in front of him. He had not seen her come. She was very close, her brown walnut face only a little way from his, and her small black eyes were looking into his eyes, very hard, the way you look into a dark well to see if there is water at the bottom.
"Who are you?" she said.
Her voice was sharp. Not unkind. Sharp, like the voice of a woman checking a coin to see if it is good.
"Corin," he said. His voice came out cracked and strange. "Corin Vane."
"What's my name?"
"Mother Grue."
"Where are you?"
"The arches. Under the Dye-Works."
"What did you have for your supper?"
He thought about it. He reached for it. He found pie. Steak and kidney, in a warming oven, with the gravy kept hot. And he knew, at once, that it was not his supper. It was Tam's. Mrs. Pratt's pie.
"I don't know," he said. "I don't think I had any."
Mother Grue looked at him for a long moment more. Then she sat back on her heels, slowly, and let out a long breath, and her face crumpled up all at once, like a paper bag.
"Good," she said. "Good. You're back." She wiped her eyes with the corner of one of her shawls. "You've been gone two hours, Harvester. Two hours, on your knees, with your eyes open, not blinking. Talking."
"Talking?"
"In his voice." She nodded at the mattress. "Tam's voice. Yes, my lady. Over and over. And then you stopped talking and started shivering. I put three blankets on you. You didn't notice." She looked at him. "And then you started saying something else. In your own voice. I don't know what it meant. You said it a lot of times."
"What?"
Mother Grue was quiet for a moment.
"You said, It's a letter. It's a letter. It's addressed to me."
Corin did not say anything. He looked past her. At the mattress against the warm back wall of the arch.
Tam lay there under his six blankets, with his hands folded on top of them, one over the other, where Mother Grue had folded them. His white face was turned up toward the curved black roof of the arch. His eyes were closed. His slate-colored lips were a little parted, as if he were about to say something. The frost was still in his hair.
He looked very young. Younger than he had ever looked alive. Younger than he had looked in the servants' hall, breaking his bread in half. He looked like a boy. A boot boy, nine years old, asleep in a narrow bed under a slate roof, with the rain coming down.
And Corin knew that bed.
That was the strange thing. The thing that would stay with him, afterwards, longer than almost anything else from that night. He knew the bed. Not because he had seen it. Because he had slept in it. A narrow iron bed in an attic room on Carrow Street, with a straw mattress that crackled when you turned over, and a gray blanket with a hole in one corner that you could put your toe through. And the slate roof sloping down so close over your head that you could reach up and touch it. And the rain. The rain on the slates, very loud, very close, drumming, the best sound in the world. And your mother's voice from the room below, singing something with no words while she scrubbed the pans, and laughing at herself, a laugh like a kettle coming to the boil.
He had never been to Carrow Street in his life. He did not know where it was. Somewhere by the tanneries, Tam had said. He had never slept in an attic. He had never had a mother who laughed like a kettle. Not that he could remember.
But he knew that bed. He knew the hole in the blanket. He knew exactly how the rain sounded on that roof, at night, when you were nine and warm and nearly asleep and nothing bad had happened yet.
It was his now. Tam had given it to him. Along with everything else.
He thought he would keep it. Not burn it. Not ever. Whatever else he had to burn, in the days to come, he would keep that bed, and that rain, and that laugh. It was the least he could do. Tam had held still for three hours so that it would not go to nothing.
Corin would hold still too.
Corin looked at him for a long time.
Then he got up off his knees. It was very hard. His legs would not hold him at first, and Mother Grue had to take his arm, and he had to lean on her, small as she was, until the blood came back. Then he went to the mattress and knelt down beside it again.
He did not say anything. There was nothing to say. He had heard everything Tam had to say, now. Every word of twenty-two years. He had it all.
He reached out and took one of the folded white hands in his. It was very cold. Colder than the stone. He held it for a moment.
Then he took off his own coat, the gray coat with the smoke still in it, and he laid it over Tam, on top of the six blankets. Over his chest. Up to his chin.
"He's past being warm, Harvester," said Mother Grue gently.
"I know."
He left it there anyway.
At the front of the arch, in the red light, Gideon had not moved. He was still standing where he had been standing, with his thin blue hands at his sides and his huge boots on the wet stone. His face was the same as always. Pale and flat and empty. But it was wet. And as Corin watched, he lifted one thin hand, slowly, as if he did not know what it was for, and touched his own cheek with his fingertips. And looked at them. At the wet on them.
And went on looking, for a long time, as if it were something he had never seen before.
Gideon
Corin went to him.
He did not know why. He was so tired that every step down the long arch felt like wading through deep mud, and his head was still full of the sea, of twenty-two years of somebody else's life rolling and turning and slowly settling, like silt settling in a pond. He should have sat down. He should have lain down on the stone and slept. But he went to Gideon.
The boy did not look round as he came. He was still looking at the wet on his fingertips. Holding his hand up in front of his face, in the red light from the Dye-Works windows, turning it a little, this way and that, the way you turn a stone you have found on a beach to see if it is worth keeping.
Corin stopped beside him.
They stood there together for a while, at the mouth of the arch, looking out at the river. The tide had come in a little more since he had arrived. The black water was closer to the arches now, and the line of green weed on the brick was nearly covered. The steam from the Dye-Works went drifting low across the water, red underneath, white on top. The pumps thudded through the wall behind them. Somewhere, out on the river, a bell was ringing on a buoy, slow and sad, as the water rocked it.
"It's wet," said Gideon.
"Yes."
"It came out of my eyes." He turned his hand again. "I didn't tell it to. I didn't want it to. I don't want anything." He was quiet. "Is it crying?"
Corin looked at him.
He thought of what he knew about Gideon. Not much. A boy of about thirteen, who had sold everything he felt to the Black Bank, a piece at a time, until there was nothing left. Fear, first, Gideon had told him, the night they met. Then cold. Then hunger. Then shame. Then the rest. All of it. For coppers. And now he could stand in the snow in his shirt and not shiver, and walk into a fire and not flinch, and watch a man die and not care. He had said so himself, over and over, in his flat clear voice like a list. I don't care. I don't want anything. I don't feel anything.
And he was crying.
"Yes," said Corin. "It's crying."
Gideon looked at the wet on his fingers for a long time.
"I sold that," he said. "Crying. I'm nearly sure I did. It was one of the first ones. After fear. Before cold." He frowned, very slightly. It was the first time Corin had ever seen anything move on that pale flat face. "They gave me four coppers. I bought a pie. It was a good pie. I remember the pie." A pause. "I don't remember crying. I don't remember what it was for. I just know I sold it."
"Maybe you didn't sell all of it."
"The Bank always takes all of it. That's what Mister Gant said. The Bank doesn't take half of a thing. It takes the thing."
"Mister Gant," said Corin, "tells a great many lies."
Gideon thought about that. He seemed to take it very seriously, the way he took everything, as if it were a sum he had to check.
"Yes," he said at last. "He does."
He lowered his hand. He let it hang at his side again, with the wet still on the fingertips. He did not wipe it off.
"Why did you stay with him?" said Corin. "All day. Mother Grue said you didn't eat. Didn't sleep. Just sat."
Gideon did not answer at once.
"I don't know," he said. "I don't care about him. I didn't know him. I only saw him once before, on the back stair at the Bourse, when the men took him down. He looked at me. On the stairs. When they went past. He looked right at me. And he said, Run, kid." Gideon was quiet. "Nobody ever told me to run before. Everybody always tells me to stay. To watch the stair. To sit on the fourth step. To wait." He looked out at the river. "I didn't run. I didn't care enough to run. But I remembered it."
"And so you stayed with him."
"I suppose." Gideon's voice did not change. "It doesn't mean anything. I don't care. But I didn't want him to be by himself. In the cold. Like on the ice." He frowned again, the small, faint frown. "That doesn't make sense. You can't not want something if you don't want anything."
"No," said Corin. "It doesn't make sense."
"Then why did I do it?"
Corin looked at him. At the thin blue hands, the right one with its raw red palm from the hot kettle handle in Gant's room. At the huge lost-property boots. At the pale, flat, empty face, with the tear-tracks still drying on it in the red light.
He thought of the house that Elara had walked through, with her candle. Room after empty room. Swept clean. Bare to the boards.
And he thought: But not every room. Never every room.
"I think," he said slowly, "that the Bank is very good at taking things. But it isn't as good as it thinks it is. I think when you sell a thing, they take it out of you, and they put it in a jar, and they think they've got all of it. And they've got most of it. But there's always a bit left behind. In the corners. A crumb. A thread. Something they missed."
Gideon was listening. Very still.
"And if you're very lucky," said Corin, "something comes along and finds that bit. Something like a man on the back stair who looks at you and says run, kid. And the bit wakes up. Just a little. And it starts to grow."
"Like a seed," said Gideon.
"Like a seed."
Gideon looked down at his own hand. At the wet on his fingers, drying now, in the cold air from the river.
"I don't want it to grow," he said. "If it grows, it'll hurt. That's why I sold it. Because it hurt." A pause. "I remember that much. I remember that it hurt."
"I know."
"Will it hurt? If it grows?"
Corin did not lie to him. He had lied once already today. That was enough.
"Yes," he said. "Sometimes. A lot, sometimes."
Gideon was quiet for a long time.
"Tam was good," he said at last. Flatly. Clearly. Like a list. "He said run, kid. He held still for three hours. He gave you everything, so it wouldn't go to nothing. He was good." A pause. "I don't care. But he was good."
"He was."
"Then it can grow," said Gideon. "A bit. If it wants. I don't mind."
And he turned round, without another word, and walked back into the arch. Not to the brazier, where the others were. To the back. To the mattress against the warm wall. And he sat down on the bare stone beside it, cross-legged, in his huge boots, where he had sat all day. Beside the still shape under the six blankets and the gray coat. And he did not move again until they came to sew Tam into the sail.
Corin stood at the mouth of the arch and watched him.
Then he looked down at his own hands.
They were dry. They were shaking, still, a little, from the Ash. But they were dry. He had not cried. Not when Tam died. Not when he came back out of the sea of Tam's life onto the stone. Not when he saw the white face on the sack pillow, looking like a boy of nine asleep under a slate roof.
He did not know if that was because he had burned the part of himself that cried, long ago, for some case he could not remember. Or if he had simply forgotten how.
He thought he would look in the Ledger, later. To see if it was written there. Burned: crying. He thought it might be.
He was afraid to look.
