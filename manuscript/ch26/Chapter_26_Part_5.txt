Chapter 26: What the Dead Remember, Part 5 of 5: The Letter
The Letter
"You're going to do something stupid," said Juno.
She was sitting beside him on an upturned crate at the front of the arches, with her knees up to her chin and her arms wrapped round them. The brazier glowed in front of them. The kettle on top of it had boiled dry long ago and nobody had filled it. Behind them, in the arch, the others were asleep, or pretending to be, rolled up in their blankets on the stone. Tilly was asleep with her head on Juno's knee. Juno had not moved for an hour, so as not to wake her.
Corin did not answer.
"I can see it on your face," said Juno. "You've got the look. The one you get before you go off on your own somewhere and come back with half your head burned out."
"I haven't decided anything."
"Yes, you have. You're just not saying." She looked at him sideways. "What did you see? In the Ash. Mother Grue says you kept saying something about a letter."
Corin looked at the coals.
He thought about not telling her. She was fourteen. She had been running across the roofs of the Lower Docks for three days and three nights, carrying his messages and finding his friends and keeping him alive. She had a key on a string round her neck and a grandmother on Tallow Street with a purple door and a dead father whose name she wore like a badge. She did not need to know about a man with a gentle voice and a gold ring, who had emptied Corin six years ago and written on all his doors.
But she was in it already. Up to her neck. He had put her there himself.
And she had a right to know what was coming.
So he told her.
Not all of it. Not the Colonel. Not the locked door. But the rest. The glass room. Vivienne. Kross, unwrapping his barley sugar, folding the paper smaller and smaller. And the man who came up in the lift, with the face you could not look at, and the gentle voice that went into your chest like a warm stone.
Put him somewhere cold. Somewhere he'll keep. And if my Harvester is as clever as I think he is, and as kind as I know he is, he'll find this boy.
And then he'll see me.
Juno did not interrupt. She listened with her chin on her knees and her eyes on the coals, and her face went whiter and whiter in the red light.
"So it was a trap," she said, when he had finished. "Tam. The whole thing. Leaving him on the ice. So you'd find him and take his Ash and see."
"Not a trap. A letter." Corin's voice was very low. "He wanted me to see him. He wanted me to know he's there. That he's been there all along. Watching." He looked at his hands. "And he wanted me to hear the rest. What he said to Kross. His friends are the doors he's knocked on. You have their names. We'll need them later."
Juno was quiet.
"Us," she said. "He means us."
"Yes."
"Me. And Lyra. And Oren. And Vespera. And Mother Grue." Her voice was small. "And Gran."
"Yes."
"And Hessa and Wat and the baby. And Briggs. And Moll. And Pruett. And everybody in these arches, because you came here. Because you let them out."
Corin did not answer. He did not need to.
Juno looked down at Tilly, asleep on her knee. At the thin face, and the tangled hair, and the small hand curled up under the chin like a baby's.
"So that's the stupid thing," she said slowly. "That's what you're going to do. You're going to go away. On your own. So you don't knock on anybody else's door. So you don't lead them to anybody else."
Corin looked at her. He did not say anything.
"I knew it," said Juno. "I knew it. I could see it on your face." Her voice had gone fierce. "Well, it won't work. It's too late. He's already got the names. You said. Written down in a neat little list. You going away won't make him rub them out."
"No."
"So it's just stupid. That's all. It's just you being on your own, for nothing, and us not knowing where you are, and you burning things you shouldn't, with nobody to tell you to stop."
"Juno—"
"Don't Juno me." She sounded, for a moment, exactly like Mother Grue. "You think you're the only one who gets to decide? You think we're just doors? Just names on his list?" Her eyes were wet, and furious. "We're not doors. We're people. We knew what we were doing. Lyra knew. Oren knew. Vespera knew, she's known you six years. Gran knew when she let you have the top room. I knew. I knew the first day." She swallowed. "Nobody made us. We chose. You don't get to take that away and call it keeping us safe."
Corin sat very still.
On Juno's knee, Tilly stirred in her sleep, and was still again.
"He said one more thing," Corin said at last. Quietly. "The man. When Kross asked about my friends. He said, Especially that one."
Juno looked at him.
"Which one?"
"I don't know." He looked at the coals. "I don't know which one he meant. I couldn't see his face. Only his mouth. Smiling."
Juno was silent for a long time.
Then she reached out, without a word, and took his hand. The way she took Mother Grue's hand. As if it were the most natural thing in the world. Her fingers were small and cold and rough with the iron of drainpipes and the edges of slates.
"Then we'll all be careful," she said. "All of us. Together. Not you on your own." She held on tight. "Promise."
He thought of all the promises he had made. To Briggs. To Lyra. To Juno herself, at the kitchen table at Pruett's, before she ran. I'll come. To Tam. I'll take it. All of it.
Some he had kept. Some he had not.
"I'll try," he said.
Juno looked at him for a long time with her wet, furious eyes.
"That's not a promise," she said.
"I know."
"It'll do," said Juno. "For now."
And she did not let go of his hand.
Those I Owe
They put Tam in the river at the turn of the tide.
It was Mother Grue's idea. Or not her idea, exactly. It was simply what was done, she said, under the arches, by the people who lived there. Nobody under the arches had ever been buried in a churchyard. Churchyards cost money. And nobody under the arches had ever wanted to go to the Ministry pits out on the Flats, where the dead of the Lower Docks went when there was nobody to pay for them, to be tipped into the lime with the slag from the Bank. So when somebody died under the arches, they waited for the tide to turn. And then they gave them to the river.
"It takes them out to sea," said Mother Grue. "All the way. Past the Customs House and the forts and the last of the lights. Out to where it's clean." She sniffed. "He never learned to swim. Well. He won't need to, now. The river'll carry him."
Wat came. Nobody had sent for him. He just came, in the dark, an hour after midnight, rowing a little flat-bottomed boat up along the edge of the river from wherever the Patient Grace was hidden. He tied the boat to an iron ring in the brick at the front of the arches, and came in, huge and slow and silent in his tarred jacket, and stood at the edge of the lantern light, and took off his cap. He did not say anything. He looked at Tam for a long time. Then he looked at Corin, and nodded once.
They wrapped Tam in a sail. An old one, gray canvas, patched and mended, that Wat had brought in the bottom of his boat. Pegg sewed it up, sitting on her box by the lantern, with a big curved sailmaker's needle and tarred thread, very neatly, the way she must once have sewn sheets. Before she did the last part, over his face, Corin took his coat back. He folded it over his arm. And Mother Grue bent down, very slowly, with her poker, and put something into the canvas, by Tam's folded hands.
An orange.
Corin did not know where she had got it. Oranges were dear in winter. More than a day's wage, on the Hill. But there it was, small and round and bright, in the gray canvas, glowing in the lantern light like a little sun.
"He liked them," said Mother Grue. "You said. In his voice, when you were away. You said he liked oranges and the sound of rain." She straightened up, slowly. "I can't give him the rain."
Then Pegg sewed up the last part.
Wat and John Rennick carried him down to the water. The others came after, out of the arches, in a slow straggling line, with blankets round their shoulders. All eleven of them. Even the white-haired old woman, on Juno's arm. Even Tilly, who still had not said a word, walking very close beside Juno on her other side. Gideon came last, in his huge boots, and stood a little apart from the others at the edge of the mud, with his thin blue hands hanging at his sides.
The tide was just on the turn. Corin could see it. The black water had stopped coming in. It lay quite still against the mud, flat and shining, with the red light from the Dye-Works windows on it in long wavering bars, and the steam drifting low across it. And then, very slowly, it began to move the other way. Out. Toward the sea.
They laid Tam in the water.
Wat waded out with him, up to his knees, then up to his waist, holding the gray bundle in his great arms the way you hold a sleeping child. And then he lowered it, gently, onto the surface of the black water. And let go.
For a moment the bundle lay still. Then the tide took it.
It went slowly at first. Turning a little, the way things turn in a slow current. Then faster. Out from the shore, out from the arches, out into the middle of the river, where the red light from the Dye-Works did not reach, and the water was only black. A pale gray shape on the black. Getting smaller. And smaller.
And then it was gone.
Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say. Mother Grue began to hum again, the same old tune as before, three notes up and three notes down, like a wave on a beach. And after a moment, very quietly, so quietly that Corin was not sure at first that he heard it, someone else began to hum it too.
It was Tilly.
She was standing by Juno at the edge of the water, with her arms wrapped round herself, looking out at the black river where the gray shape had gone. Her lips were moving. And the tune was coming out of them, very small and very thin and a little cracked, like a bird singing in the cold. Three notes up. Three notes down.
It was the first sound anybody had heard her make.
Juno looked at her. Then Juno began to hum too. Then Pegg. Then, one by one, all the others, standing in a line along the edge of the mud with their blankets round their shoulders, humming the old slow tune out over the water after Tam. Even Wat, standing up to his waist in the river, with his cap in his hand. A low deep rumble, like a cart on a far road.
Gideon did not hum. He stood apart, at the end of the line, with his thin hands at his sides. But he did not go away.
Corin did not hum either. He did not know the tune. Or he did, somewhere, and could not find it. It was like the name on the door, at the very back of his house. It was in there. He could feel it. He could not get it out.
He stood on the mud with his gray coat over his arm and the river cold around his boots, and he listened. And somewhere very deep in him, behind the locked door at the end of all the empty rooms, something listened too.
When the humming stopped, the others went back up the mud toward the arches, slowly, in ones and twos. Mother Grue went with them, leaning on her poker, with the white-haired old woman on her arm. Juno went with Tilly. Gideon went last, without a word.
Wat did not go.
He came wading back out of the river, slow and heavy, with the black water streaming off his canvas trousers, and stood on the mud beside Corin. He did not say anything for a while. He wrung out the bottom of his tarred jacket with his great hands, and put his cap back on, and looked out at the river where the gray shape had gone.
Then he put his hand inside his jacket, and took out something wrapped in oilcloth, and held it out.
"From the lady," he said. His voice was deep and slow and rumbling, like barrels rolling in a cellar. Corin had heard it perhaps a dozen times in all the days he had known him. "The young one. On the boat. Lyra." He said the name carefully, as if it were a glass he was afraid of breaking. "She said give it you. Nobody else. She said to say she wrote it at sundown."
Corin took it. He unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside there was a folded piece of paper, torn from the back of something, a pattern book perhaps, with a faint printed border of leaves around its edge. He unfolded it, and held it up to the red light from the Dye-Works windows.
Lyra's writing. He knew it at once. Small and quick and fierce, slanting hard to the right, with the letters pressed so close together that they seemed to be shouldering each other out of the way.
Corin —
We are safe. Hessa won't tell me where and I've stopped asking. Mrs. Hobb says his heart is steady. He has tapped all day. Four, two, nine, three. On the cabin wall, on the blanket, on my hand. I thought I would go mad.
At sundown he stopped.
He looked at me. Really looked, the way you said he looked at you in the white room. And he said a word. Not W. Not wait. A whole word, quite clear. I made him say it twice to be sure. Mrs. Hobb heard it too.
He said: LINUS.
Then he shut his eyes and went to sleep and he has not tapped since.
I don't know anyone called Linus. Do you?
— L.
P.S. Juno says you are not eating. Eat something.
Corin read it three times.
Then he folded it up again, very carefully, along its creases. He put it inside his Ledger, between the pages, next to the pale green card from the Fourth Desk and the curl of hard brown orange peel.
Linus.
He thought of a schoolhouse with a bell. Of a man who taught in it, and cut spirals on silver rods in the evenings, by the stove, for the love of it. Of a small girl putting her hand on a glass, and the glass going white, and the master saying one word. Blazing.
He thought of a line in a dead doctor's book, at Crown Walk, that he had copied out with his own hand. Brought to me at Crown Walk by H.L. himself, at night, by the garden door. Female. Aged about two years. He says she was registered. He says she is Bright.
H.L.
Hale, Linus. The way a clerk writes a name in a ledger, the family name first.
Corin stood very still on the mud.
"Is it bad?" said Wat. "The paper. The lady looked like it was bad. Or good. I couldn't tell which."
"I don't know either," said Corin. "Not yet."
Wat nodded slowly, as if that were a perfectly good answer. He looked at the river again. Then he looked down at Corin, from his great height, with his slow kind eyes.
"The boy," he said. "Tam. He was the one held still?"
"Yes. Three hours."
"Hm." Wat thought about it. "I carried him. Out of the cold. In the cellar. He weighed nothing." A long pause. "I'll tell Hessa. She'll want to know his name. She keeps a list."
"A list?"
"In the back of the tide book. Everybody we ever put in the water. Everybody we ever pulled out." Wat touched his cap. "So the river knows we know."
Then he turned, and went back across the mud to his little flat boat, and untied it from the iron ring in the brick, and stepped down into it, so that it rocked and settled under his weight. He took up the oars. He did not look back. He rowed away slowly along the dark edge of the river, the way he had come, toward wherever the Patient Grace was hidden among all the other black barges, and the dark took him, and the steam drifted across where he had been.
Corin watched until he was gone.
Then he went back up the mud to the arches, with Lyra's letter in his Ledger and Lyra's father's one clear word going round and round in his head like a spiral on the tip of a rod.
Later, much later, when the others had gone back into the arches, and the brazier had been built up again, and the kettle put on, Corin sat by himself on an upturned box at the front of the arch with his coat round his shoulders, and took out his Ledger.
He did not open it at once.
First, he put up his hand to the back of his collar. He felt along the seam, with his fingertips, the way he had done a hundred times. And there it was. A small square of something stiff, under the cloth. Sewn in. Madame Hesper had done it for him, with her quick small stitches, so long ago now that it felt like another life. A pocket, in the collar of his coat. And inside it, a piece of paper, folded very small.
He took his knife and cut three stitches, carefully. He drew the paper out. He unfolded it.
It was soft with handling. The creases had gone white and furry. At the top, in his own small hand, in faded ink, it said:
THOSE I OWE.
And under that, a list. Not long. A few names. Some of them with lines through them. Some with ticks beside them. Some with nothing. Pell Dorran. Lyra Thorne. Vespera. Juno. Oren. And near the bottom, in a different ink, with a mark after it that he had made and could not remember making:
Elara. (which one?) (both?)
He looked at the list for a long time.
Then he took out his stub of pencil, and he wrote, under the last name, carefully, pressing hard:
Tam Hollin. Who asked.
He folded the paper up again, very small, the way it had been. He put it back in the pocket in his collar. He did not have a needle. He would ask Pegg for one. Or Mother Grue. In the morning.
Then he opened the Ledger, and began to write down everything he had seen in the glass room. Everything. Vivienne's white knuckles. The barley sugar. The Colonel in the iron-colored coat, and the red at his cuffs, and do not approach. The gentle voice. The gold ring with the dome on it. The silver rod turning to the left. I emptied him myself. I wrote on every door. All but one. And the mouth, curving up, for less than a breath. Especially that one.
It took him a long time. His hand cramped. The pencil went blunt and he sharpened it with his knife and went on. The lantern burned down and Mother Grue came and turned up the wick and went away again without a word. Behind him, in the arch, the brazier crackled, and the kettle sang, and somebody was snoring, and Tilly was still humming, very softly, to herself, over and over, three notes up and three notes down.
When he had finished, he sat and looked at the river.
It was very late. Or very early. The red light from the Dye-Works had not changed. It never changed. But the sky beyond the far bank had begun, very faintly, to go gray.
Mother Grue came and sat down on the box next to his. Slowly, with her poker. She did not say anything for a while. She held out her hands to the warmth that came from the arch behind them.
"There was a man asking for you," she said at last.
Corin looked at her.
"This afternoon," said Mother Grue. "Before Juno went to find you. An old man. Came down the plank path in the daylight, very careful, with a stick. Nobody comes down the plank path in the daylight." She frowned. "Thin. Gray. Round spectacles, with gold rims, mended with thread at one side. A good coat, gone shabby. And his fingers were blue."
"Blue?"
"Stained. Like a man who's handled ink all his life. Blue ink. Right into the skin, round the nails." Mother Grue sniffed. "Polite. Very polite. Talked like a schoolmaster. Asked if a young Harvester called Vane had been down here. Asked if he was well. Asked if he'd been eating." She looked at Corin sideways. "I told him I'd never heard of you. I tell everyone that."
"Did he say who he was?"
"He did." Mother Grue was quiet for a moment. "He said, Tell him, if you see him, that his old teacher would like to see him. Tell him I'll be at the Lamplighter's Rest on Ferrule Street, by the Ink Quarter end of the Lantern Bridge, tomorrow afternoon. From three. And then he said one more thing. He said, Tell him I'm sorry it's taken so long." She looked at Corin. "And then he told me his name."
"What was it?"
Mother Grue looked out at the river. At the gray coming up in the sky beyond the far bank.
"Hale," she said. "Linus Hale."
And behind Corin's left ear, very quietly, so quietly that it was hardly a voice at all, Malakor said one word. Corin had heard him say it once before, in the Harvesting Section, dry and quick, the way you say a fact. Linus cut it that way. Never like this. It came out of the rod in his pocket like a breath out of a man who has been holding it for thirty years.
Linus.
