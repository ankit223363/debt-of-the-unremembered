Chapter 26: What the Dead Remember, Part 2 of 5: Last Words
Last Words
He did not die at once.
Corin had thought he would. He had thought, kneeling there with his hand on the blankets and the cold moving under it, that it would be a matter of minutes. But Tam Hollin had been a footman on the Hill for six years, and a footman on the Hill learns to stand very still for a very long time, through very long dinners, without fainting, without complaining, without anybody noticing he is there at all. And he stood still now. Inside himself. Against the cold.
It took nearly three hours.
Corin stayed. He did not get up from the stone once. His knees went numb, and then they went past numb into a dull deep ache, and then they went numb again, and he did not move. Mother Grue brought him a box to sit on, and he shook his head, and she put it down beside him anyway and went away. Later she brought him a mug of tea, black and very sweet, and he drank it without tasting it. Later still, Juno came, very quietly, and stood at the edge of the lantern light for a long time, and then went away again without saying anything.
Tam talked.
Not all the time. Not much. A few words, and then a long silence, with only the slow rattle of his breath. And then a few more. Sometimes his eyes were open. Mostly they were shut. Sometimes he seemed to know where he was. Sometimes he did not.
"The gloves," he said once, out of a long silence. "Did they fit?"
Corin did not understand at first. Then he did. The white cotton gloves. A footman's gloves, very clean, with a tiny darn on the left thumb. Tam had put them on the end of his bed, the first night, so that he would find them in the morning.
"They fitted," said Corin. "They still do."
"Good. Mother darned the thumb." A breath. "Before."
Another time, much later, he said, "Mrs. Pratt."
"I know. The cook. At the Bourse."
"She kept my supper." Tam's slate lips almost smiled. "In the warming oven. Every night. Even when I was late. Pie, mostly. Steak and kidney." A long pause. "Tell her. Tell her I'm sorry about the gravy."
"What about the gravy?"
"She'll know."
And once, very late, when the lantern had burned down so low that Mother Grue had come and turned up the wick, and the red light from the Dye-Works windows at the front of the arch had gone a deeper, darker red, Tam opened his eyes all the way. Fully. For the first time since Corin had come. And his eyes were quick again, just for a moment. Quick and brown and clear, the way they had been in the servants' hall on the Hill, sliding sideways to look at Corin's gloves.
"Vane," he said. His voice was stronger. "Listen."
"I'm listening."
"I drew a map." The words came quickly, all together, as if he were afraid they would run out. "Of the Bourse. All of it. The vault. The vial-lift. The back stairs. Where the guards stand. Where the doors are. I drew it on the back of the servants' roster, a bit every night, for you. In case you ever had to come in. In case you had to get somebody out." A breath. "I hid it in the lining of my livery coat. The plum one. My second-best. It's on the hook behind the door of the footmen's room. Top of the house, under the dome. Third hook from the left."
Corin stared at him.
"She found out," whispered Tam. "Not the map. She didn't find the map. But she found out I'd been talking to you. And she said—" He stopped. His face twisted. "She said I'd read the wrong books. Talked to the wrong people. Drawn on my—"
He could not finish. His breath rattled.
"Drawn on your what?" Corin said gently.
Tam was quiet for a long time.
"On my credit," he whispered at last. "She said I'd drawn on my credit. With her. And it had run out." His eyes went to the curved black roof. "Everything's credit, with her. Everything's an account. You're worth what you're worth to her, and not a copper more, and when you're not worth it anymore, she closes you."
His eyes came back to Corin.
"The map," he said. "Third hook. Don't forget."
"I won't forget." Corin took out his Ledger and wrote it down, there and then, by the light of the lantern, with the stub of pencil. Tam's map of the Bourse. Lining of his plum livery coat, second-best. Footmen's room, top of the house, under the dome. Third hook from the left. He held up the page so that Tam could see. "There. It's written. I can't forget it now."
Tam looked at the page. His eyes were going dim again. The quickness was going out of them, slowly, like the light going out of a window at dusk.
"You write everything down," he said.
"Everything."
"Write me down."
Corin's hand stopped on the page.
"Write me down," Tam whispered again. "Not the map. Me. So somebody's got it. Somewhere." His eyes closed. "Tam Hollin. Born on Carrow Street, by the tanneries. Mother was Annie. She cleaned the Third Basement. She had a laugh like a kettle. Father was a sailor, went to sea, never came back. I was a boot boy at nine and a footman at sixteen and I could carry six plates on one arm without spilling the soup. I liked oranges and the sound of rain on a slate roof. I never learned to swim." A long rattling breath. "I was going to. One day. In summer."
Corin wrote it. Every word. His hand was shaking so badly that the letters went crooked across the page, but he wrote it.
Tam Hollin. Born on Carrow Street, by the tanneries.
Mother: Annie. Cleaned the Third Basement. A laugh like a kettle.
Father: a sailor. Went to sea. Never came back.
Boot boy at nine. Footman at sixteen. Could carry six plates on one arm without spilling the soup.
Liked oranges, and the sound of rain on a slate roof.
Never learned to swim. Was going to. One day. In summer.
He looked up from the page.
Tam's eyes were shut. His breath was very slow now. Very far apart. Each one rattled in his chest like a pebble in a tin, and then there was a long, long silence, so long that Corin's heart stopped every time, and then another.
Mother Grue came out of the shadows by the wall.
She did not say anything. She lowered herself down onto the box beside the mattress, slowly, with her poker, the way old women sit. And she reached under the six blankets, and found Tam's hand, and took it in both her small brown ones.
And she began to hum.
It was a very old tune. Corin did not know it. It was slow and low and wandering, and it went up three notes and came down again, the way a wave goes up a beach and comes back down, and then up three notes again. A fisherman's tune, he thought. Or a lullaby. Or both.
At the front of the arch, by the red light, Gideon turned round.
He did not come back. He stood where he was, in his huge boots, with his thin blue hands hanging at his sides. But he turned round, and he looked down the long dark arch toward the lantern, toward the mattress and the old woman humming and the man kneeling with the book. And his pale empty face was the same as always.
But he was shivering.
Corin saw it. Very faint. A small, quick tremor going through the thin shoulders, and then another. The way a person shivers when a cold wind comes in through an open door. Gideon, who felt nothing. Who had sold every feeling he had ever had to the Bank, and did not care, and could stand in the snow in his shirt without noticing.
He was shivering.
Tam breathed in.
It was a long breath. Longer than any of the others. It went on and on, slowly, filling his chest under the blankets, as if he were about to dive into deep water.
And then it went out.
And did not come back.
Mother Grue went on humming. She went on humming for a long time, the same three notes, up and down, up and down, holding the cold hand in both of hers. And then she stopped, and the arch was very quiet, and there was only the slow deep thud of the pumps through the wall, and the hiss of the vats, and far away at the front of the arch, by the red light, a sound that Corin had never heard before and would never forget.
Gideon was crying.
Without any sound at all. Without any change in his face. Just tears, running down the pale empty cheeks, one after the other, and dripping off his chin onto his huge lost-property boots.
"I don't care," he said. His voice was flat. Clear. Like a list. "I don't care. I don't feel anything. I don't care."
The tears went on running.
Mother Grue laid Tam's hand down on the blanket. She folded it, gently, over the other one. She looked at Corin.
"Now, Harvester," she said. Her voice was very quiet. "Before it gets cold. You promised him."
The Harvest
Corin took the silver rod out of his pocket.
He did it slowly. He always did it slowly, with the dead. It was one of the few rules he had been taught at the Ministry that he had never once broken, in six years, not even in the worst lanes on the worst nights. Slowly. With respect. They have nothing left to give but this. Do not snatch it.
The rod lay across his palm. Thin and silver and cold, about the length of his forearm. In the lantern light it gleamed softly, and the fine spiral cut into its tip caught the light and seemed to turn, very slowly, to the right. The way a clock turns. So that it's always going forward.
Easy, boy, said Malakor, behind his left ear. Quieter than Corin had ever heard him. Easy. He asked for it. That's a rare thing. Do it well.
Mother Grue had got up off her box. She stood back, by the side wall, leaning on her poker, in the shadow. She did not go away. Corin did not ask her to.
He moved closer to the mattress. He knelt up, so that he was leaning over Tam, the way you lean over a sleeping child to see if he is breathing. The face on the sack pillow was very white and very still and very calm now. All the hurt had gone out of it. The slate-colored lips were a little parted, as if he had been about to say something else. The frost in his hair had not melted. Corin thought now that it never would.
He lifted the rod.
He held it the way he had been taught. Not like a knife. Not like a pen. Like a dowsing rod, lightly, between the thumb and the first two fingers, so that it could move a little on its own. And he brought the tip of it down, very slowly, until it was a finger's width from Tam's forehead. From the place just above and between the eyebrows, where the skin was thinnest and the bone was closest. Just above the small white scar like a tick on a page.
He waited.
It did not come at once. It never did. When a person died, the Ash did not rise up out of them in a great cloud, the way people thought. It came slowly. It gathered. It was like dew, he had told Toby once, in the Harvesting Section, when Toby had asked. Like dew gathering on the grass at night. You could not see it come. You only knew, after a while, that it was there.
He felt it gathering now.
It began as a coolness. A faint coolness in the tip of the rod, that had not been there before. Then a weight. Very slight. As if a moth had landed on the end of the silver. Then the coolness spread, down the rod, toward his fingers, and the weight grew, and the rod began to tremble very faintly in his hand, the way a dowsing rod trembles when it finds water.
And then he saw it.
It came up out of Tam's forehead like breath on a cold morning. Gray. Pale gray, almost silver, faintly glowing. It came up in a thin thread, curling, the way smoke comes up from a candle that has just been put out. It hung in the air between the white skin and the silver tip of the rod. And then, slowly, it found the spiral.
And began to turn.
Round and round, to the right, the way the spiral turned. Following it. Winding itself onto the tip of the rod the way thread winds onto a spool. And as it wound, more came up after it. A thicker thread now. Then a ribbon. Then a slow gray flood, rising up out of Tam Hollin's forehead and winding round and round onto the silver rod in Corin's hand, glowing softly in the lantern light, like the mist on the river at dawn.
There was a great deal of it.
More than Corin had expected. More than he had ever harvested from anyone. Most of the dead he harvested had very little left. They had burned it, or sold it, or lost it, long before they died. A few last minutes, that was all. A few last sights. A thin gray thread that wound onto the rod in a moment and was gone.
Tam had not burned. Tam had not sold. Tam had kept everything. Every good thing and every bad thing, every plate carried and every word overheard, every face on the Hill and every room he had stood in, still as a statue, for six years, with his white gloves on and his eyes on the wall and his ears open.
It kept coming.
The rod grew heavy in Corin's hand. Heavier and heavier. The coolness in it had gone past cool into cold, and past cold into something that burned. His fingers ached with it. His wrist ached. The ache went up his arm, the way the cold of the glass desk had gone up it at the Bank. But he did not let go. He did not move. He held the rod quite still, a finger's width from Tam's forehead, and let it come.
Steady, said Malakor. Steady. Nearly. Nearly. There's the last of it.
The gray flood thinned. It went back to a ribbon. Then a thread. Then a wisp, curling up off the white skin, very faint. And then nothing.
The forehead was just a forehead. White and smooth and cold.
Tam was empty.
Not empty the way his mother had been empty. Not a jar with nothing in it. Empty the way a house is empty when everyone has gone out of it together, carrying everything, to somewhere else. Corin could feel the difference. He did not know how. He only knew that there was nothing left behind. Not a scrap. Not a crumb. Tam had given him all of it.
He lowered the rod.
It was glowing. The whole length of it, from the spiral at the tip to the end in his hand, wrapped round and round in the soft gray light, like a spindle wrapped in wool. It was so heavy now that he had to hold it in both hands. He sat back on his heels on the stone floor, with the glowing rod across his knees, and breathed.
Well done, said Malakor quietly. Well done, boy. That's the cleanest I've ever seen you take one.
Corin did not answer.
He looked down at the rod. At the gray light wound round it. It was beautiful. That was the thing nobody ever told you about Ash, the thing they did not put in the Ministry handbooks. It was beautiful. It glowed like pearl. It moved, very slowly, on the silver, like a living thing breathing in its sleep.
And now he had to take it in.
That was the part that the Ministry did not like to talk about either. The rod could hold the Ash, for a little while. An hour, perhaps. But it could not keep it. Ash did not keep. It faded, the way breath fades on a window, unless somebody took it in. And the only way to take it in, the only way to see what it held, was to put it inside yourself.
That was what a Harvester was. That was what they called him in the Lower Docks, behind his back, in the lanes. Not Harvester. Not Investigator. The man who eats Ash.
He had done it hundreds of times. He knew how it would feel. Like drinking something very cold and very bitter, all at once, too fast. Like a stranger's whole life going down your throat. And then, for a while, you would not be sure which memories were yours and which were theirs. You would see with their eyes. You would remember what they remembered. And then, slowly, it would settle. It would fade. It would become a story you had been told, and you would write it in the Ledger, and that would be all.
But he had never taken in this much before. Never from someone who had kept everything. Never a whole life.
"Harvester," said Mother Grue softly, from the shadows. "Are you sure? That's a lot of a man, on that stick."
"I promised him."
"I know you did. I heard." She was quiet. "I'm only asking if you're sure you'll come back out of it. Afterwards. As yourself."
Corin looked at the glowing rod.
He thought of Hollis, in his loft, with his hand over the hole in his chest. Of Elara, in her glass room, with her little black book of days. Of Oren saying spoon like a word in a foreign language. Of all the empty rooms in his own house, with Oakhaven written on their doors.
He thought: There's plenty of room.
"I'll come back," he said.
He lifted the rod in both hands. He turned it round, so that the spiral end, where the gray light was thickest, was toward his own face. He held it there for a moment, in front of his eyes, so close that the soft gray glow filled everything he could see.
Then he put the tip of it to his own forehead.
And Tam Hollin came into him like the sea.
The Glass Room
For a long time there was only the sea.
That was how it felt. Like falling into deep water, very cold, very fast, and being turned over and over by it, so that he did not know which way was up. Things went past him in the gray. Faces. Rooms. Voices. A woman laughing like a kettle. Rain on a slate roof, very loud, very close, as if he were lying right under it in a narrow bed. Six plates on one arm, the weight of them, the heat of the soup coming up through the china into the skin of his forearm, and the trick of it, the little lift of the elbow that kept them level. A boot, and a brush, and a tin of blacking, and a kitchen at five in the morning, dark and cold, with the fire not yet lit. An orange. The smell of it, sharp and sweet, when his thumbnail broke the peel.
Tam. All of it Tam. Twenty-two years of Tam Hollin, pouring through him like a river through a broken dam.
Corin did not fight it. He had learned, long ago, that you did not fight it. You let it come. You let it turn you over. And you waited, the way a man caught in a flood waits, holding his breath, for the place where the water slows.
And you looked for the last of it. The newest. The part closest to the end. Because that was always the part that mattered.
He found it.
It was like coming up out of the water into a room.
A warm room. Very warm. He knew it at once, though he had only been in it once, on Thursday night, and only for a few minutes. But he knew it now the way Tam had known it, which was much better. Every inch.
The roof was glass.
The whole roof. A great dome of glass and black iron, like the inside of a bubble, curving up and over everything, so high and so wide that it made the room feel as if it had no roof at all. Outside it, the night. Saturday night. Black and clear and frosty, with the stars very small and very bright, and a thin moon low down over the edge of the dome, like a paring cut from a fingernail. And in the angles of the iron, little white drifts of snow left over from the week, that had not melted.
The fire was roaring in the great black marble fireplace. The air smelled of woodsmoke, and of burnt sugar and spice, heavy and dark and sweet. The rugs on the floor were so thick that his boots, Tam's boots, sank into them and made no sound. And all around the walls, under the edge of the glass, the tall narrow cases stood with their little silver locks, and in the cases, on black velvet, the cut-crystal vials glowed. Gold and rose and deep green and violet. And blue. Every blue there was.
Joy. Nerve. Mercy. Grace. Love (first).
Tam was standing in the middle of the room.
Corin could feel it. Tam's feet on the thick rug, side by side, heels together, the way a footman stands. Tam's hands at his sides, in white gloves. Tam's back straight. Tam's eyes on the wall opposite, on a point just above the top of the nearest glass case, the way a footman's eyes are always on the wall, never on the faces. And Tam's heart.
Tam's heart was going like a drum.
Two big men stood behind him. Corin could feel them too, through Tam's back. Close. Too close. One on each side. They had brought him up in the vial-lift from the back stair, one holding each arm, and they had not let go until the lift doors opened. Now they had let go. But they were there.
And there was a voice.
"Tam," said Lady Vivienne. "Dear Tam. Do come a little closer. Into the light. Where I can see you."
She was sitting by the fire.
In her low deep chair of black velvet, very close to the flames, with her feet on a little stool and her closed fan in her lap. Exactly as Corin had seen her on Thursday night. The long pale face. The high cheekbones. The black hair piled up high and held with long pins tipped with jet. The gown of deep, dark, shining violet, like the skin of a plum. And round her throat, lying across her collar and down onto the silk, the long deep loop of heavy gold chain, and on it the dozen cut vials, each one a different color, each one glowing so brightly that it lit her face from below.
And her eyes. Heavy-lidded and dark and perfectly calm.
Tam stepped forward. Two steps. Into the light. And stopped. Heels together. Eyes on the wall.
"Better," said Lady Vivienne. "Thank you."
She did not say anything else for a while. She looked at him. Corin could feel her looking. It was like standing in front of a fire that gave no heat. Tam stood very still under it. He did not let his eyes come down off the wall. But Corin could feel his gloved hands, at his sides, trying very hard not to shake.
And then Tam did something that a footman must never do.
His eyes moved.
Just a little. Just for a moment. Off the wall, and sideways, and down. Because there was someone else in the room. Someone he had not seen when the lift doors opened. Someone sitting on the other side of the fire, in a low chair, in the shadow beyond the reach of the colored light from Vivienne's throat.
A man.
Slim. Not tall, not short. In a long gray coat, very well cut, buttoned to the throat. Gray gloves. No hat. Very pale hair, almost white, cut short and neat. A pale, smooth, pleasant face, the kind of face nobody would look at twice in a crowd.
He was unwrapping a barley sugar.
He did it slowly, with his gray-gloved fingers, as if it were the most interesting thing in the world. The twist of paper at one end. Then the other. Then the sweet itself, gold and clear like amber, lifted out and put into his mouth. And then the paper. Folded in half. And in half again. And again. And again. Smaller and smaller. Until it was a tiny hard square, no bigger than a fingernail. And put away into his coat pocket.
Then he looked up. At Tam.
And smiled. A small, kind, friendly smile.
Tam's eyes went back to the wall so fast that Corin felt the jolt of it, like missing a step in the dark.
"You've met Inquisitor Kross, I think," said Lady Vivienne. "No? Well. You've heard of him. Everybody has." She opened her fan, a little way, and closed it again. "He has been telling me the most interesting things, Tam. About you."
Tam said nothing. A footman says nothing, unless he is asked a question.
"He tells me," said Lady Vivienne, "that on Thursday night, a Harvester of the Ministry came to my kitchen door and asked for you by name. That you took him down to my vault in my own vial-lift. That you stood beside him while he looked at my vials. And that afterwards, you walked with him through my kitchen, and out into the lane, and that you were seen, later that night, in Bram's back room on Quill Row, with the glue pot going, drinking tea with him."
Tam said nothing.
His heart was going so hard that Corin thought it would break through his ribs.
"Is it true, Tam?" said Lady Vivienne gently.
And Tam said, in his quick clipped footman's voice, very steady, to the wall:
"Yes, my lady."
There was a little silence. The fire crackled. One of the vials at Vivienne's throat flickered, a deep rose-red, and steadied.
"Thank you," said Lady Vivienne. "I do so like it when people tell me the truth. It saves such a great deal of time."
She turned her heavy-lidded eyes toward the man in the gray coat.
"There," she said. "You see, Silas. He tells the truth. He always has. That is why I kept him so long. A footman who tells the truth is worth three who don't."
"And now?" said Silas Kross. His voice was pleasant and easy and quite soft. The barley sugar made a little lump in his cheek.
"Now," said Lady Vivienne, "I'm afraid he has drawn on his credit."
She looked back at Tam.
"I'm sorry, Tam," she said. And she sounded, Corin thought, almost as if she meant it. "You read the wrong books. You talked to the wrong people. And you have drawn on your credit with me, all of it, every copper, and it has run out." She opened the fan again. All the way, this time. It was black lace, very fine, painted with small gold domes. "And you know what happens to an account when the credit runs out."
Tam knew. Corin felt him know it. Felt it go through him, from the soles of his feet to the top of his head, cold and sure.
She closes you.
But before Lady Vivienne could say anything else, somebody moved. Behind her chair. In the shadow between the fire and the glass cases, where the light from her throat did not quite reach.
Somebody Tam had not seen at all. Somebody who had been standing there, quite still, the whole time.
Tam's eyes moved again. He could not stop them.
And Corin saw.
