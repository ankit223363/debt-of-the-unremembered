Chapter 45: The Usual Desk, Part 2 of 5: Room Nine
Room Nine
Room nine was on the left side of the Long Office, the fifth door from the end. It had a small brass 9 on it, and a wooden shutter in the middle of the door at the height of a man's eyes, that slid sideways so you could look in without opening it.
Wren did not look in.
She stood outside the door with her hand on the latch and made her face still. She did it again, though it was still already. She did it the way you check a lock you know you have locked. Then she opened the door and went in, and shut it behind her.
The reading rooms were all the same. She had been in every one of them. A small square room with no window, and white walls, and a stone floor. A lamp in an iron cage high up on the wall, burning a steady white Ash-light that never flickered. Two chairs, facing each other, close, with their front legs nearly touching. Wooden, plain, heavy, bolted to the floor. One of them had leather straps on the arms. The other did not. On a small shelf by the door, a row of glass jars with their lids off, waiting. And a towel.
The old man was in the chair with the straps.
The straps were not done up. Somebody had decided, or not bothered. He was sitting in the chair the way he had sat on his sack in the arches: with his back against the wood and his knees drawn up and his arms round them, folded small, as small as a thin old man can fold, so that he hardly took up half the chair. His long ragged coat with the string for buttons hung off him like a sail with no wind in it. His feet were bare. They were blue-white with cold, and filthy, and there was a cut on the top of one of them, across the bones, that had bled and dried.
There was a bruise on the side of his face. A big one. Purple going black, from the cheekbone to the jaw, with the skin split along the top of it. And his lip was swollen.
Gant's men knocked him about a little, I'm afraid, getting him into the cart.
He was looking at the floor.
There was a bowl on the floor beside the chair. A tin bowl, with soup in it, gone cold, with a skin on it. The spoon was in the bowl. It had not been used.
Wren stood by the door and looked at him.
She had read his file. There was not much in it. There never was, for men like him. Wick. No other name known. Rag-and-bone man, Lower Docks. Found the body of the scribe Pell Dorran in a lane in the Ink Quarter (see file Dorran) and told the whole street that a harvester would come for it. Emptied, by order, three days later. And under that, in a different ink, in a hand she knew very well, small and neat: A pity. He was proud.
She had read that line three times. She had wanted to tear it out.
She crossed the room. Two steps. She did not sit down in the other chair. Not yet. She knelt down instead, on the stone floor, beside the strapped chair, so that her face was lower than his. The way you kneel beside a dog that has been kicked.
"Wick," she said.
Nothing.
She picked up the bowl. She held it in her lap, on her white coat. The soup was cold and the skin on it wrinkled when she moved it. She took the spoon out and wiped it on the edge of the bowl.
"You didn't eat your soup," she said.
The old man's eyes moved. Not to her. To the bowl. They rested on it for a moment. Then they went back to the floor.
And his finger, on his knee, where his arms were folded, moved.
Tap. Tap.
Twice. Very small. A finger lifting, and falling, on the rough cloth of his coat, over the bone of his knee.
Wren looked at it.
She had seen men tap before. In this room, and the others. Men with nothing left, who tapped or rocked or hummed or picked at their fingers, the way a wheel goes on turning after the cart has stopped. She had written some of them down, in the black books. Taps his foot. Counts on his fingers to five, then starts again. It did not mean anything. It was the body, going on, when the man had gone.
She had never seen anyone tap twice. Only twice. Always twice. And stop. And wait. And twice again.
As if he were answering something.
She put the spoon back in the bowl.
"Wick," she said again. Very low. "I have to look inside you. I'm sorry. I don't want to. But if I don't, he'll send someone who doesn't care how they do it." She paused. "I'll be gentle. I'll take one thing. Only one. And then I'll go."
The finger tapped. Twice.
She did not know if he had heard her. She did not think so. She said it anyway. She always said it. To every one of the four hundred and eighty-one. Some of them had screamed at her. Some had begged. Some had sat there with their faces closed like doors and said nothing at all. But she had always said it, every time, because somebody should.
She put the bowl down on the floor.
Then she stood up, and sat down in the other chair, the one without the straps, facing him, so close that her knees nearly touched his folded ones.
She took off her right glove.
She laid it on her knee. White kid, fine, tight at the wrist. She looked at her bare hand. Small. Narrow. The nails cut very short, the way the Office liked. There was ink on the side of the middle finger, from the morning's reports. And on the palm, faintly, the mark where she had held the beach stone too hard, a week ago, at the bottom of the Ministry, so that the white ring had bitten into her skin. It had never quite gone.
Forced Extraction. That was the name of it in the Office books. It was not a pleasant name, and it was not a pleasant thing. She had learned it at the Academy, at eighteen, from an old Inquisitor with no eyebrows who said that most people did it like a butcher, and she would be different, because she had small hands.
You put your bare hand on the person. The temple was best, or the back of the neck. And you went in. Not with a stone. Not with Ash. With yourself. With the thing in you that the Office had found when it tested her, at sixteen, in the long white room: the thing that could feel other people's memories the way fingers feel the shape of things in the dark. You went in, and you felt your way, room by room, down the corridors of a person, until you found the thing you were looking for. And then you took hold of it. And you pulled.
And it came out of them, into you. Into your hand. Warm. Living. And from your hand, if you were quick, into a jar.
And it was gone from them for good.
The Office did not care what it cost the person. That was not the Office's business. But it cost you something too. Not much. A little. Every time. A taste of what you had taken, that stayed behind in you, like the smell of smoke in your hair after you have walked past a fire. That was why most Inquisitors drank. That was why the old one with no eyebrows had no eyebrows. He had taken a man's memory of a house fire, once, the first year, and it had stayed with him, and for the rest of his life he could not bear to have anything near his face.
Wren did not drink.
Wren wrote it down.
She held up her bare right hand in the white Ash-light.
"Wick," she said. "Look at me."
He did not look at her.
She leaned forward, and very gently, the way you would touch a sleeping baby to see if it was warm, laid her bare palm against the side of his head. On the side without the bruise. Over the temple, where the thin gray hair grew sparse and soft as a child's, and the bone was very close under the skin.
He did not flinch.
His finger tapped on his knee. Twice.
And Wren Halloway closed her eyes, and went in.
Reading Wick
A person, from the inside, was a house.
That was how Wren had always felt it, from the very first time, at sixteen, in the long white room. Not a house you could draw. Not one with a front door and a roof and a number. But a house all the same. Rooms opening into rooms. Corridors. Stairs that went down. Doors that were shut, and doors that were open, and doors that had been locked so long the keys were lost. And in every room, things. Furniture. Pictures on the walls. A smell. A warmth or a coldness. The things a life had put there, one by one, over the years, and left.
Most people's houses were full. Crowded. Untidy. You went in at the front, and it was like walking into a house in the middle of a party. Voices everywhere. Lamps lit in every room. People going up and down the stairs, carrying things, talking, laughing, quarrelling. You had to push your way through. You had to stand still in the middle of all the noise and feel for the one room you wanted, the way you feel for one cold draught in a warm house, and follow it.
Wick's house was not like that.
Wick's house was empty.
She stood in the front of it, in the dark, and listened, and there was nothing to hear. No voices. No footsteps. No lamps. She went forward, carefully, the way you go into a house where you have been told someone has died, and the first room was empty. Not tidy. Empty. The walls were bare. There were pale squares on them, here and there, where pictures had hung for a long time and kept the paint from fading, and then been taken down. The floor was bare boards. In the middle of the floor, a little heap of dust, very fine, gray, like ash from a grate.
The next room was the same.
And the next.
She went through them one after another. A long string of bare rooms, going back and back into the dark. Some of them had been big rooms, once. She could tell by the size of the pale squares on the walls, and the marks on the floor where heavy things had stood. A bed. A table. Something tall, like a dresser, with a row of hooks above it where cups had hung. In one room there was a fireplace with a cold grate, and on the mantelpiece, a line in the dust where something had stood, a clock perhaps, and been lifted away. In another there were marks on the doorframe, at different heights, low and then higher and then higher still, the way a family marks a child's growing. The names beside the marks had been scraped off.
She did not know what had been in them. She would never know. Somebody had come through this house, long before her, with a cart, and emptied it to the walls. Every room. Every cupboard. Every drawer. The way bailiffs empty a house when a man cannot pay. They had not even left him his own name.
A pity, said a small neat hand, in a file, in her head. He was proud.
She went on.
Far back, deep in the house, where the corridors began to go down, she found the first thing that was still there.
It was a sound.
Very faint. From behind a door at the bottom of a short stair. A door that was not locked, and not open, but stood a little way ajar, so that a thin line of gray light came out round its edge. And from behind it, a sound like rain on a tin roof. Or a stick on a drum. Two beats. A pause. Two beats.
Tap. Tap.
She went down the short stair and pushed the door.
It opened onto snow.
Not a room. A field. A wide gray field under a low gray sky, in a cold so deep it went into her bones at once, the way cold goes into you when you fall through ice. Snow on the ground, trodden into gray slush. Snow in the air, blowing sideways. And across the field, from one side to the other, a line of men.
They were standing very close together, shoulder to shoulder, in long coats gray with snow, with their muskets held across their chests. Not moving. A long thin gray line in the white. And she was in it. She was one of them. She could feel the shoulders of the men on either side of her pressing against her own, hard and cold. She could feel the musket in her hands, heavy, so cold that her fingers had stuck to the iron. She could feel her own feet in the snow, wet through, numb, rooted.
And she could hear the drum.
Behind the line. Somewhere back there, in the blowing snow. A boy with a drum, beating. Two. Two. Two. Over and over, steady as a heart. Hold. Hold. Hold.
And out in front of the line, across the white field, coming toward them through the snow, something she could not see. Something big. Something that made the ground shake under her numb feet. Coming closer.
And every man in the line wanted to run. She could feel it. She could feel it in the shoulders pressing against hers, the way you feel a horse about to bolt. Every one of them wanted to throw down the musket and turn and run back through the snow toward the drum and past it and away. And every one of them stood.
Because the drum said hold.
And so they held.
Wren stood in the line in the snow with the cold in her bones and the musket stuck to her hands and the ground shaking, and understood.
This was what they had left him. Of everything. Of the whole house. They had taken his name and his wife, if he had one, and his children, and the marks on the doorframe, and the clock on the mantelpiece. They had taken the lane, and the dead scribe, and the pride. And they had left this. The worst moment of his life. A field. A line. A drum. The moment when every bone in him had told him to run, and he had not.
They had not taken it because they did not want it. Nobody would buy it. Nobody on the Hill would pay a single copper to stand in a frozen field and wait for something to come out of the snow.
So they had left it to him. And it was all he had. And he stood in it, every day, every hour, and held. And tapped it on his knee, on the floor, on the brick. Two. Two. So that he would remember to hold.
She stepped back out of the field, carefully, and pulled the door to behind her, the way it had been. A little way ajar. So that the line of gray light still came round the edge.
She did not take it.
She stood at the bottom of the short stair for a long moment, in the dark, breathing. Her own breath. Not the cold.
Then she went on.
There was one more room.
She almost missed it. It was not deep in the house. It was near the front, near the way she had come in, tucked away to one side of the first bare room, behind a door so small and low that she would have walked past it if she had not been looking. A cupboard, really. A cupboard under a stair.
There was a light in it.
Not gray light. Yellow. Warm. The light of a fire, coming out under the bottom of the small low door in a thin bright line, and lying on the bare boards of the first room like a ribbon.
Wren knelt in front of the little door, and opened it.
And there they were. The last few things. The things an old man with nothing left had seen in the last few days and weeks, and held onto, the way a drowning man holds onto a plank. Kross had been right. He was always right.
They were very small. A handful. Laid on the floor of the little cupboard, in the firelight, side by side, like the treasures a child keeps in a box under the bed.
A tin bowl, warm, with soup in it, and the barley floating.
A spoon. Being put into his hand. Fingers closing his fingers round it. Gently. A woman's fingers, old and rough, with a ring on one of them. And then, later, the same thing again, but different fingers. Younger. Red with cold, with ink in the cracks of the knuckles.
A red glow on black brick. A brazier. The smell of smoke.
A voice, a woman's, very old, saying Up, now. Time to go. And under it, a high thin sound, like a kettle, that was his own voice, and that he could not stop.
And a face.
It was the last thing in the cupboard. Right at the back, in the warmest part of the light. And he had put it there, Wren could tell, the way you put the best thing at the back where it will be safe. A girl's face. Looking at him. Close. In the red light of a brazier, from under the edge of a flat gray cap that was too big for her, pulled down over her ears.
She was young. Not much more than twenty. Her dark hair was cut short, roughly, so that it stuck out under the cap in uneven ends, as if somebody had done it in a hurry with a knife. Her face was thin. Too thin. With dark marks under the eyes, the way a face gets when it has not slept properly for a long time. But it was not a poor girl's face. Wren had read too many faces to be fooled by a cap and a cut. The bones were too fine. The skin was too clear, under the dirt. And the eyes.
The eyes were dark, and straight, and steady. They looked at Wick as if he were a person. As if there was still somebody there to look at.
Wren knew those eyes.
She had seen them every day for two years. At the end of a bench under the windows of the Long Office. In a pale stiff face that tried very hard to look as if it did not care about anything. Folding a wrapper. Small. Very small.
Rowan Thorne's eyes.
In his sister's face.
Wren knelt in front of the little cupboard in the empty house, in the firelight, and did not move.
She had never seen Lyra Thorne. Not once. Not on the bridge, not on the barge, not anywhere. She had only read about her. In files. In reports. In Watcher Crane's description, that the whole Office had by heart: Female, about twenty, dark, well-spoken, slight build, may be dressed as a boy. She had imagined her a hundred times. Sitting up on a barge deck with a long knife across her knees.
She had not imagined her like this. Kneeling by a brazier in the arches, putting a spoon into an old man's hand, and closing his fingers round it, and looking at him as if he mattered.
Go and find me a face, Silas Kross had said.
And here it was.
Wren looked at it for a long time. The girl's face, at the back of the cupboard, in the warm light. The best thing. The thing he had put at the back, where it would be safe.
Then she reached into the cupboard with her bare hand, and took hold of it.
She did it as gently as she had ever done anything. Gently. Like a nurse. The way the old Inquisitor with no eyebrows had said she would. She took only the face. Not the bowl, or the spoon, or the brazier, or the old woman's voice. Only the face, and the eyes, and the moment of being looked at. She gathered it up in her bare hand, small and warm, the way you would pick up a bird that had fallen out of its nest.
And pulled.
It came out of him into her. Warm. Living. The girl's face, close, in the red light, with the dark straight eyes. And with it, the thing that had made him keep it. The feeling of it. Of being looked at, for the first time in years, by somebody who saw a man.
It went into her like a hot drink on a cold morning. Down through her chest. Into her bones.
And in the little cupboard, where it had been, there was a dark place on the floor. A small one. The size of a face.
The firelight did not reach it.
