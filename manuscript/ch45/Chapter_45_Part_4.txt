Chapter 45: The Usual Desk, Part 4 of 5: The Guard List
The Guard List
At the second bell after noon, the messenger boy from the Health came up the east stair with two men in brown coats, and went to room nine, and came out again with the old man between them.
Wren did not look up from her desk.
She heard them. Everybody heard them. The two men in brown were not rough, the way Gant's men had been rough. They were quick, and quiet, and very used to it. They each took an arm, and lifted, and the old man came up out of the strapped chair folded small, with his knees still drawn up, as light as a child. And then the sound began. The high thin sound, like a kettle. Like a dog shut out in the snow. It came out through the door of room nine and went down the Long Office between the desks and up into the high ceiling, where the lamps floated on their chains, and every clerk in the room bent his head a little lower over his book.
It went down the length of the Long Office, past Wren's desk, past the bench where the cadets sat very straight, past the Chief Clerk's platform, where old Mr. Abernethy did not look up. Out through the dark wood door with the brass plate. Down the east stair. Fainter. Fainter.
And stopped, all at once, somewhere far below. Like a door shutting.
Wren turned a page.
She did not know what was on it. She turned the next one too.
At the third bell, the Inquisitor's door opened, and Kross put his head round it.
"Halloway. A moment."
She went.
He did not ask her to sit, this time. He was standing at the window with his back to her, in his shirtsleeves, with a sheet of paper in his hand, and the gray light of the afternoon coming in round him, so that he was only a shape against it. The river below had gone the color of lead. Snow was coming down again, slow and soft, out of a sky the same color.
"How is your hand, Wren?"
"My hand, sir?"
"Your writing hand." He did not turn round. "I have a copy that needs making. Fair. Three times. One for the Archon's Council, one for the Bank, and one for our own files. And it must be perfect, because the Archon's people read everything twice, and the Bank reads everything three times, and charges for it." He held the paper out behind him, without looking, over his shoulder. "My hand is too small for them. They complain they need a glass."
Wren took it.
It was one sheet, closely written on both sides, in Kross's small neat upright hand, with crossings-out and little arrows and notes squeezed into the margins. A draft. At the top, underlined twice:
THE MIDWINTER MASQUERADE. The Memory Bourse, Lantern Quay. Monday next, from the ninth bell. Order of the Office, at the request of the Archon's Council, for the keeping of the peace.
And under it, a list.
She read it standing there, by the door. She could not help it. Her eyes went down it the way they went down every report, every page, every line, quickly, taking it in whole, the way you take in a room when you walk into it.
Front steps and bronze doors, Exchange Street: six men, watchers, in plain coats. Constable Hurley and five.
Exchange Street, carriage line: four men, mounted. From the battalion. Commander Vance's choice.
Cinder Lane, behind the Bourse. The coal chute and the Bell corner: two men.
Kitchen gate and kitchen yard: two men. With the Lady's porters.
The wet door, river side, and the boat stair: four men, in a boat. Mr. Vell to command. No barges to tie up below the Bourse after the eighth bell.
The roof, and the dome: two men. Climbers.
Inside. The great hall: none. The Lady will not have uniforms on her floor.
The gallery: the Inquisitor, masked. With Inquisitor Halloway, masked.
Wren's eyes stopped on that line.
They stayed on it for a moment. Then they went on, because they had to.
The upstairs doors, one to twelve (servants' and private): one man at each. Watchers. In the Lady's livery, by her leave.
And under that, squeezed in between the lines, in a smaller hand still, with a little arrow:
Excepting door three, the back stair from the kitchen yard. No post. By request of the Treasurer's office (D. S.), who will provide his own.
And at the very bottom, on its own:
Lot 41. The Inquisitor to be present at the opening of the box, at midnight, with the Fourth Desk, as clause eleven requires.
Wren lowered the paper.
Kross had turned round from the window. He was looking at her. He had been looking at her, she understood, the whole time she was reading. Watching her eyes go down the page.
"You'll come with me," he said. "On Monday. In the gallery. You'll need a mask." He smiled. "White, I think. The Lady likes her guests in color. It will make us easy to find."
"Yes, sir."
"You saw door three."
It was not a question.
"Yes, sir."
"The Treasurer asks that we leave door three to his own people." Kross came away from the window and sat down behind his desk. "The Treasurer asks for a great many things. He asks very charmingly, and he always asks the Council first, so that by the time the asking reaches me it has become an order." He picked up his pen. "Copy it as it stands, please. Arrows and all. The Council will want to see that I put it in."
"Yes, sir."
"Three copies. By the fifth bell, if you can."
"Yes, sir."
She turned to go.
"Wren."
She stopped.
Kross was not looking at her. He was looking down at his desk, at the green leather, where his pen lay beside a small folded square of white paper, the size of a fingernail. One of his own.
"Do you know," he said, "why the Treasurer would want one door in the whole of that great house left to him? One door. Out of twelve. The servants' stair. The one the waiters use." He turned the little square of paper over with the tip of his pen. Over. Under. "It isn't the best door. It isn't the grandest. It doesn't go anywhere that a man of his position would want to go, at a party."
"No, sir."
"No." He turned the paper again. "It goes to the little rooms behind the gallery. Where the Lady's private guests sit with their sealed bids. And where, at a quarter to midnight, the waiters go up with the last of the wine, and nobody looks at them at all." He looked up at her, and his pale washed-out eyes were mild and kind. "I have been wondering about it since Monday. I wondered if you might wonder about it too, while you copy. You have such a clear head for doors."
Wren stood by the door with the paper in her hand.
"Yes, sir," she said.
"That's all."
She went out, and shut the door, and walked down the Long Office between the desks with the guard list for the Midwinter Masquerade in her hand, and felt every line of it, every arrow and every crossing-out, burning through the paper into her fingers like the white ring of a beach stone.
She sat down at her usual desk.
She took three clean sheets of the Ministry's best paper out of the drawer, the heavy cream kind with the watermark, and laid them side by side. She trimmed her pen. She filled the inkstand. She laid Kross's draft at the top of the desk, squared to the edge.
And began to copy.
Three Notes
Copying was the best work there was.
Wren had always thought so. Better than reading reports. Much better than the reading rooms. When you copied, you did not have to decide anything. You did not have to judge, or weigh, or write No action at the bottom of somebody's life. You only had to look, and see, and make the same marks again, as exactly as you could, on a clean page. The front of your mind did it. And the back of your mind could go wherever it liked.
She dipped the pen.
THE MIDWINTER MASQUERADE.
Capitals, for the heading. The Ministry's copperplate. Each letter the same height as every other letter, each one leaning at the same angle, each thick stroke and each thin one exactly where it should be. She had learned it at the Academy, from a writing master who rapped your knuckles with a ruler if a single loop was the wrong size. She had not minded. She had liked it. It was the only lesson at the Academy that had ever made her feel calm.
The Memory Bourse, Lantern Quay. Monday next, from the ninth bell.
The pen scratched. The ink went down black and shining and dried dull. Outside the tall window, the snow came down past the glass, slow and soft, and the river below went from lead to iron to slate as the light began, very slowly, to go.
Front steps and bronze doors, Exchange Street: six men.
The back of her mind went where it liked.
It went first to room nine. To the strapped chair, empty now. The tin bowl on the floor, with the cold soup in it, and the spoon. She had seen it when she passed the door, through the little shutter that somebody had left open. The bowl was still there. Nobody had thought to take it away. The spoon was standing up in the soup, where an old man's hand had let go of it, when the two men in brown coats lifted him.
She put that away.
Exchange Street, carriage line: four men, mounted.
It went to the Pantry. To a white plate with a blue line round it, and a slice of pork pie. To a long scraped hand lying flat on the front of a gray coat, over a letter.
She put that away too.
Cinder Lane, behind the Bourse.
It went to the cupboard.
She had not meant to let it go there. But it went anyway, the way your tongue goes to a sore tooth. To the small dark cupboard at the back of herself, that she had not known she had until this morning. And to what was in it now, in the warmest part of the light. Looking out.
A girl's face. Dark straight eyes. A gray cap too big for her.
She let it stay there. Just for a moment. She let herself look at it, while her hand went on making the marks on the page, the thick strokes and the thin ones, each loop the right size. She let herself feel what the old man had felt. Being looked at. As if there was still someone there.
It was a strange feeling. She did not have a word for it. It was warm, and it hurt a little, the way your hands hurt when you hold them to a fire after coming in from the snow.
Kitchen gate and kitchen yard: two men.
The pen scratched.
And somewhere, very far off, at the edge of the room, the Long Office went quiet.
Wren did not notice at first. She was copying. The wet door, river side, and the boat stair. She was thinking about the girl's face. She was thinking about nothing. The pen went on. The quiet came closer. Desk by desk, from the far end of the room, the way it had come that morning when the Inquisitor's door opened. Pens stopping. Pages stopping.
Four men, in a boat. Mr. Vell to command.
She looked up.
They were looking at her.
Not all of them. Not openly. But the clerk at the next desk, a thin young man called Pocock with spots on his chin, had stopped writing and was sitting with his pen in the air, looking at the side of her face. And the watcher two desks along, the old one with the gray mustache, had turned his head. And at the end of the row, a woman clerk with spectacles had put down her book.
Wren looked back at them.
Pocock went red and bent his head over his ledger. The old watcher turned away. The woman clerk picked up her book again.
Wren looked down at her page.
It was the Pantry, she thought. She had gone into the Pantry, and sat at the long table, and eaten bread and cheese like a clerk. And now they were all looking at her. Wondering. Writing it down in their heads. Inquisitor Halloway, Wednesday, in the Pantry, with the Thorne boy. By tomorrow it would be in somebody's report.
She made her face still. She dipped her pen.
No barges to tie up below the Bourse after the eighth bell.
The quiet did not go away.
It stayed. It sat over the Long Office like snow sits over a field. She could feel it on the back of her neck. She went on copying, and the pen went on scratching, and the loops were all the right size, and the quiet stayed, and stayed.
At the end of the bench under the windows, Rowan Thorne was sitting very still.
She saw him out of the corner of her eye. She did not turn her head. He was sitting the way the cadets sat, very straight, with his back like a ramrod. But his face had gone white. Not pale. White, the way paper is white. And his right hand was lying flat against the front of his gray coat, over his chest, pressed so hard that she could see the knuckles. And he was looking at her.
Not the way the others had looked. Not curious. Not careful.
He was looking at her the way you look at someone who has just said your name in a crowd, in a voice you have not heard for years.
The roof, and the dome: two men.
Wren dipped her pen.
She did not know what was the matter with him. She thought perhaps it was the pie. She thought perhaps it was the sergeant, tomorrow, and the Inquisitor's lesson about how an honest man lies. She thought perhaps it was the old man, going down the east stair, making his noise. She did not think about it long. She had three copies to make by the fifth bell, and she was only on the first, and the light was going.
Inside. The great hall: none.
At the end of the Long Office, the Inquisitor's door was open.
She had not heard it open. It had been shut when she sat down. Now it stood open, and in the doorway, with the warm firelight of the little room behind him, Silas Kross was standing in his shirtsleeves, with his spectacles in his hand.
He was not looking at the room. He was not looking at the clerks, or the watchers, or the cadets on their bench.
He was looking at her.
He stood quite still. His mild kind face did not move. The spectacles hung from his fingers. And Wren, glancing up and down again, quickly, the way you glance at a clock, thought: He's waiting for his copy. He's wondering if I'll have it by the fifth bell. And she bent her head a little lower, and made her pen go a little faster, and did not look up again.
The gallery: the Inquisitor, masked. With Inquisitor Halloway, masked.
She did not see his face change.
She did not see him lift the spectacles, slowly, and put them on, and look at her through them, for a long moment, as if he were reading something written very small. She did not see his hand go to the pocket of his shirt, where the little black book lived, and stop there, and stay.
She did not see him close his eyes.
She did not hear, under the scratching of her pen, the thing that the whole of the Long Office had been hearing for nearly a quarter of an hour, desk by desk, from the far end to the near, until every pen in the room had stopped. A sound so small that it hardly moved the air. Three notes. Low, and then higher, and then higher still, up and up, and hanging there at the top, unfinished, the way a question hangs. And then back to the beginning. Low. Higher. Higher. Hanging. Over and over. Like somebody trying to remember the rest of a song.
She did not hear it because she was making it.
The upstairs doors, one to twelve.
The pen scratched.
In the doorway at the end of the Long Office, Silas Kross opened his eyes. He took his hand away from his pocket. He took his spectacles off, and folded them, and stood for a moment longer, looking down the long room at the white figure bent over the third desk in the second row, by the fourth window.
Then he went back into his room, and shut the door. Very quietly. So quietly that the latch made no sound at all.
At the end of the bench, Rowan Thorne took his hand away from his chest. He looked at it. It was shaking. He put it in his lap, under the edge of his coat, where nobody could see, and took a barley-sugar wrapper out of his pocket, and began, with his long thin fingers, to fold it.
In half. Corner to corner.
He got as far as seven, and could not do the eighth, and started again.
Excepting door three.
Wren wrote it with the arrow, exactly as it was, squeezed in between the lines in a smaller hand. She looked at it for a moment when she had finished. The little arrow. The two letters. D. S.
Then she went on to the next line.
And the three notes went on too, under her breath, so soft that she could not hear them. Low. Higher. Higher. Hanging there, at the top, unfinished. Waiting for the rest.
Mrs. Marta's Cloth
She finished the third copy as the fifth bell began to strike.
She sanded it, and shook the sand off, and laid it on top of the other two, and squared the three of them to the edge of the desk, and looked at them. They were perfect. She knew they were perfect. Every loop the right size. Every arrow where it should be. The two letters, D. S., squeezed in between the lines in a smaller hand, exactly as he had written them.
She took them up the Long Office between the desks.
The quiet had gone. She did not know when. Somewhere in the second copy, she thought, the pens had started again, one by one, and the pages had begun to turn, and Pocock at the next desk had sneezed and said beg pardon. By the third copy the Long Office had been the Long Office again, scratching and coughing and rustling, and she had forgotten that it had ever been anything else.
She knocked at the Inquisitor's door.
"Come."
He was at his desk. The lamp was lit now, with its green shade, and the fire had been made up, and the room was very warm. He held out his hand for the copies without looking up, and she put them in it, and he laid them on the green leather in front of him and turned the pages, slowly, one after another, and read every line. All three. It took him a long time.
She stood by the visitors' chair and waited.
"Beautiful," he said at last. "Quite beautiful. The Bank will find nothing to charge for." He laid them aside. "Thank you, Wren."
"Sir."
"One more thing. Before you go home." He opened the drawer of his desk, the top one on the right, and took something out, and laid it on the green leather. "An errand. On your way. It isn't far."
Wren looked at it.
It was a cloth. A square of cotton, folded small, into a hard neat square. Blue and white checks. A small darn near one edge. And in the corner, in red thread, in tiny even stitches, the letter M.
She did not know it. She had never seen it before.
But she knew what it was. Everybody in the Office knew. Watcher Fisk had talked about nothing else for two days, in the Pantry, by the stove. A pie cloth. Off a wreck on the Saltmarsh. Mr. Vell found it on a nail. The landlady's. From Tallow Street. Number fourteen. And then the Inquisitor had kept it, in the drawer of his desk, and not done anything with it, and Fisk had stopped talking about it, because nobody could think what it meant.
"Mrs. Marta's," said Kross. "Number fourteen Tallow Street. She lent it, I think, to a friend. A week ago. With a pie in it. Mutton and onion, with thyme." He touched the darn with one fingertip, very lightly. "I've kept it long enough. It isn't mine. I'd like you to take it back to her."
Wren did not move.
"Take it back, sir?"
"With my compliments." He smiled. "And my thanks for the pie, which I'm afraid I never got to taste. And tell her the Office is sorry to have kept her linen so long." He pushed the folded cloth across the desk toward her, an inch, with one finger. "That's all. Give it to her, and say that, and come away. Don't ask her anything. Don't look at anything. Don't go upstairs."
"Yes, sir."
"Fenwick is on the corner. He'll see you in." Kross picked up his pen. "He's been on the corner for a week. I expect he'd like somebody to talk to."
Wren picked up the cloth.
It was soft and worn. It smelled faintly, very faintly, under the smell of the Inquisitor's drawer, which was ink and barley sugar, of something else. Smoke. Salt. And mutton fat, and onion, and thyme.
She put it in the right-hand pocket of her white coat, with the beach stone.
"Good night, sir."
"Good night, Wren."
She went out, and down the Long Office, and took her gray cloak from the peg by the door, and put it on over the white coat, and pulled up the hood. And went down the east stair, ninety-six steps, and across the great cold hall with its black and white floor, and out through the bronze doors under the words WHAT IS WRITTEN IS KEPT, into the snow.
It was nearly dark.
The clerks were coming down the Ministry Steps in their hundreds, the long dark river of backs going the other way now, down the hill, toward their suppers. She went down among them. They made a space for her, the way they always did. One hundred and twelve steps.
At the bottom she turned left, along Cooper's Rise, and down the hill, toward the river. Past the chandler's on the corner, with its window full of candles and rope and tin lanterns, and its lamp lit. Into Tallow Street.
She had been here before. Of course she had. She had stood in this street every day for a week, in the autumn, watching a window at the top of a tall thin house. She had written down the times. Subject returns to lodging. Goes to top floor. Candle lit. Candle out. That had been Fisk's, the first week. Then it had been hers.
Tallow Street had not changed.
It went down the hill in a long steep crooked line, narrow, cobbled, slippery with old snow trodden to ice. Tall thin houses on either side, four floors and five, leaning toward each other overhead so that the strip of sky between their roofs was no wider than a path. Black brick, gone green at the bottom where the damp came up. Doors painted every color, long ago, and faded to no color. Washing lines strung from window to window across the street, high up, with nothing on them now but icicles. And at the bottom, where the street ran out, the river, black, with the lights of the far bank on it.
Number fourteen was halfway down, on the left. A tall thin house of black brick, five floors and an attic, with a street door that had been painted black once and had peeled to gray, and a crack in the fanlight mended with brown paper. Inside, Wren knew, at the end of the ground-floor passage, there was another door. A purple one. With a smell of onions behind it, and the ticking of a great many clocks.
Fenwick was on the corner opposite, in a doorway, stamping his feet.
He was a short man, round, in a brown coat with the collar up and a woolen muffler wound round and round his neck up to the nose, so that only his eyes showed, red-rimmed, watering with cold. He had a little iron brazier at his feet, the size of a bucket, with a few coals in it, and he was holding his hands over it. When he saw the white coat under the gray cloak coming down the street, he straightened up so fast that he kicked the brazier and nearly tipped it over.
"Inquisitor."
"Mr. Fenwick."
"The Inquisitor sent word, ma'am. By the boy. That you'd be coming." He pulled the muffler down off his mouth. His face underneath was red and raw. "To see the landlady."
"To return something."
"Yes, ma'am." Fenwick looked at the purple door across the street. "She's in. She's always in. Doesn't go out but to the pump and the baker's, and the step women come to her." He sniffed. "Lots of step women. All day. In and out. With their buckets." He looked at Wren. "I write them all down, ma'am. Every one. But they all look the same."
"I'm sure they do."
"I'll see you in, ma'am. Orders."
They crossed the street together. Fenwick went first, up the two worn steps to the gray street door, and pushed it, and it opened. It always opened. It had no lock that worked. He went down the dark narrow passage, with its smell of damp and cabbage and coal, to the purple door at the end, and knocked on it. Three times. Hard. With his fist.
The purple door opened at once.
Mrs. Marta stood in it.
She was small and stout and square, in her black dress with the white apron over it, and her sleeves rolled up to the elbows, and her gray hair screwed up in its tight knot on top of her head with a pencil stuck through it. She had flour on her forearms, and flour on her apron, and a smudge of it on her cheek. Behind her, in the warm yellow room, Wren could hear the clocks. Dozens of them. All ticking, and none of them together.
And her face did a thing that Wren had seen many faces do, in two years and five months, when they opened their door and saw an Inquisitor on the step.
It shut.
"Well," said Mrs. Marta. "I suppose you'd better come in. Before you let the cold in. Not you," she said to Fenwick. "You've had my step all week. You can keep it."
And she stood back from the door, and Wren went past her into the warm room, and Mrs. Marta shut the purple door in Fenwick's red face.
