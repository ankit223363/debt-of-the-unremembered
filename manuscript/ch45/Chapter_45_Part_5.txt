Chapter 45: The Usual Desk, Part 5 of 5: Under the Stairs
Under the Stairs
The room behind the purple door was the warmest room Wren had been in all week, and the smallest, and the fullest.
It was a kitchen and a parlor and a bedroom all at once, the way rooms are in the Lower Docks. A black iron range along one wall, glowing, with pots on it and a kettle and a big round loaf on the hob, rising under a cloth. A table in the middle, scrubbed white, covered in flour, with a ball of dough on it half kneaded. A bed in the corner behind a faded curtain. A dresser with plates. Two chairs by the fire, one with a cushion and one without.
And the clocks.
They were everywhere. On the dresser between the plates. On the mantelpiece, six of them, side by side, like people waiting for a coach. On the walls, hanging from nails, round ones and square ones and one shaped like a little house with a door for a bird to come out of. On the floor by the bed, a tall one in a case of dark wood, taller than Wren, with a brass face and a long slow pendulum going to and fro behind a glass door. Thirty of them. Forty. All ticking. None of them together. So that the whole room was full of a soft dry endless sound, like rain on leaves, or mice in a wall, or a great many small feet walking very fast and never getting anywhere.
None of them told the same time.
Wren stood just inside the door with the snow melting on her gray hood and looked at them.
"My husband's," said Mrs. Marta, behind her. "Terrence. He mended them. Forty years. Night porter at the Ministry, and clocks by day." She went past Wren to the table and stood by the dough with her floury arms folded. "He can't mend them now. He can't tell the time now. But he likes to hear them. So I keep them going. I wind them every Sunday. All of them." She looked at Wren. "I don't set them. He never set them either. He said a clock that's right is only right for a minute."
Wren did not say anything.
She put her hand into the right-hand pocket of her white coat, past the beach stone, and took out the cloth, and held it out.
Mrs. Marta looked at it.
She did not take it at once. She looked at it for a long time, lying folded small on Wren's gloved palm. The blue and white checks. The darn near the edge. The red M in the corner. And her face did not change. It stayed shut. But something happened in the stout square body under the black dress. Something went out of it, and came back, the way breath goes out of you when you miss a step.
Then she unfolded her floury arms and took it.
"The Inquisitor sends it back," said Wren. Her report voice. "With his compliments. And his thanks for the pie, which he says he never got to taste. And the Office is sorry to have kept your linen so long."
Mrs. Marta held the cloth.
She did not unfold it. She held it in both hands, against her apron, and then she lifted it, slowly, to her face, and smelled it. The way Wren had smelled it in the Inquisitor's room. Smoke. Salt. Mutton fat, and onion, and thyme.
She lowered it.
"Mutton and onion," she said. "With thyme. I made it on the Tuesday. For a man who wasn't going to get any supper." She looked at Wren. "Did he eat it? The man I made it for?"
"I don't know."
"No. You wouldn't." Mrs. Marta put the cloth down on the corner of the table, away from the flour, folded, square. "Your master knows. He knows where it was found and who was sitting by it and how warm the fire was. He knows everything. And he sends it back to me with his compliments." Her mouth went thin. "That's a man who wants me to know he knows. That's all that is. A man showing you the knife, and smiling, and putting it away."
The clocks ticked.
"He said I wasn't to ask you anything," said Wren. "Or look at anything. Or go upstairs."
"Then you'd better not."
"No."
They looked at each other across the floury table, the small stout woman in black and the young one in white.
Wren thought of the old man in room nine. Of the empty house, and the cupboard under the stair, and the dark place on the floor the size of a face. Of two men in brown coats carrying him down the east stair, and the sound stopping, all at once, like a door shutting.
She thought of the guard list. Three perfect copies, every loop the right size, lying on the green leather of the Inquisitor's desk under the lamp.
And she thought of the barley sugar.
It was in the left-hand pocket of her white coat. Not the sweet. She had eaten the sweet, at her desk, at the fourth bell, between the second copy and the third, because she had not eaten anything since the Pantry and her hand had begun to shake. The wrapper. Kross's wrapper. The one he had given her in the morning, already folded, warm from his fingers, the way you put a sweet into a child's hand. She had unfolded it at her desk, under the edge of the folder, with the tip of her pen. Eleven folds. She had counted them.
And on the inside of it, the smooth waxed inside, she had written. With the finest pen she had. So small that the words were hardly bigger than the grain of the paper. So small that you would need a glass to read them, the way the Archon's people needed a glass for Kross's hand.
Then she had folded it again. His way. Eleven. Tight and hard and sharp at every corner, the size of a fingernail.
It had taken her four tries. Her hands had not wanted to do it. On the fourth try they had done it, and it had looked exactly like his.
It was in her pocket now. With the gray book, and the white card that had come through the letterbox with a folded square drawn on it.
"Mrs. Marta," she said.
"What?"
"Your stair wants sweeping." Wren did not lower her voice. She did not need to. The clocks were louder than she was. "The bottom step. In the passage. There's paper under it. Sweet papers. Somebody's been dropping them." She paused. "The Inquisitor is a terrible one for dropping his sweet papers. He leaves them everywhere he goes."
Mrs. Marta looked at her.
She looked at her for a long time. Long enough for the bird to come out of the little wooden house on the wall, and say cuckoo in a cracked wooden voice, eleven times, at a quarter past five, and go back in again.
"Does he," said Mrs. Marta.
"Yes."
"Under the bottom step."
"In the crack. Where the tread's come away from the riser. At the wall end." Wren had seen it, a week ago, when she came down the stair behind Kross in her white boots, with Rowan clumping behind her. She had seen everything in that house. She always did. "You'll want to sweep it out tonight. Before the step women come in the morning. They'll only tread it in."
Mrs. Marta's face did not change.
"I'll do it now," she said. "When you've gone."
"Good."
Wren turned to the door.
"Inquisitor."
She stopped.
Mrs. Marta had picked up the pie cloth again from the corner of the table. She was holding it against her apron, in both floury hands.
"The man I made that pie for," she said. "He wrote a list once. In his book. Of people he owed. Did you know that?" She did not wait for an answer. "He let me read it, once. When he was ill. My name was on it. And Terrence's. And the girl's, my Juno's." She looked at Wren. "And there was a line at the bottom. In a different ink. That said Wren Halloway. Bring her stone back."
The clocks ticked.
Wren stood with her hand on the purple door, and did not turn round.
"He's alive," said Mrs. Marta. Very quietly. Under the clocks. "That's all I'll say. He's alive, and he's not far, and he's hurt, and he's still got your stone. I'm told he keeps it in his pocket and turns it over when he's thinking."
Wren's hand on the door did not move.
Then she opened it, and went out into the dark passage, and shut it behind her.
The passage was black. The only light came from the street door at the far end, where it stood a little open, and the snow-light came in round it, gray, and lay on the floor. Fenwick was outside on the step. She could see the shape of his back through the gap, and hear him stamping his feet.
The stair went up on her right. Steep and narrow and dark, with bare wooden treads worn into hollows. Ninety-one steps to the top. She had counted them, a week ago.
She walked past the bottom of it, toward the street door, and her white boot on the stone floor of the passage turned a little, on something, a wet place, and she put out her gloved left hand to the wall to steady herself. Low down. By the bottom step. At the wall end, where the tread had come away from the riser, and there was a dark crack the width of a finger.
It took no time at all.
She straightened up, and brushed off her glove, and went on down the passage, and out into Tallow Street, where Fenwick was waiting with his muffler pulled up to his nose.
"All well, ma'am?"
"All well, Mr. Fenwick. I slipped on her step. She doesn't sweep it."
"No, ma'am. She don't."
She went away up Tallow Street in the snow, without looking back, toward the chandler's lamp at the top of the hill.
Behind her, in the dark passage of number fourteen, before she had even reached the corner, the purple door opened again, very quietly. And a small stout woman in black came out with a broom twice as tall as she was, and began, in the dark, without a lamp, to sweep the bottom step.
Untidy
She walked home the long way.
Not because anyone was following her. She did not think anyone was. She had looked, the way the Office taught you to look, in shop windows and the brass plates on doors and the black puddles in the gutters where the snow had melted and frozen again. Nobody. Only the clerks going home, and a lamplighter with his ladder, and a dog. But she walked the long way anyway. Down to the river, and along the quay, and over the Lantern Bridge with its seven arches and its blue lamps like little houses, and back up through the old streets on the north bank, between the tall dark houses, along lanes so narrow that the snow on the cobbles had not been touched all day.
She needed to walk. She needed to count.
One thousand and six. One thousand and seven.
She went over it in her head while she counted. All of it. Every word she had written on the inside of Kross's wrapper, in letters hardly bigger than the grain of the paper. She had chosen every one of them at her desk, between the copies, the way you choose stones to cross a river: only the ones that would bear your weight.
Midwinter. Ours at the Bourse: front 6. Street 4 mounted (Vance). Cinder Lane 2. Kitchen yard 2. Wet door 4 in a boat (Vell). Roof 2. Doors 1 to 12 one man each, in her livery. NOT door 3: no post, the Treasurer's (D.S.) own men. K. and I in the gallery, white masks. K. at the box at midnight, clause 11. K. knows about a quarter to midnight. I don't know how.
Wick sent to the Health today. I couldn't stop it. I'm sorry.
Moll here tomorrow, 10th bell. K. wants me to take the notes.
W.
That was all. She had wanted to write more. She had wanted to write about the girl's face, at the back of the cupboard, and the field in the snow, and the white card through the letterbox. There had not been room. There was never room for the things that mattered. Only for numbers, and doors, and names.
One thousand two hundred and forty.
She turned into Mercer Street.
It was dark now. Properly dark. The lamps were lit along the street, the old oil kind on iron brackets, not gas, not Ash, and their light was yellow and small and did not reach far. The pawnbroker's with the clean windows was shut. The shop that mended clocks was shut. The glove-cleaner's was shut. Mr. Ambrose the bookbinder's was shut, with its shutters down, and its gold sign over the window, J. AMBROSE. BOOKBINDER, catching the lamplight faintly.
The narrow green door beside it was shut too.
She took out her key. She opened the green door, and went into the narrow hall with the brass letterbox, and shut the door behind her, and stood in the dark.
There was a smell.
Not leather. Not fish glue. Those were always there, on the first floor, coming down the stairs. Something else. Something that did not belong in Mr. Ambrose's house at all, and that Wren knew so well, from so many mornings and so many afternoons, that for a moment she thought she must be imagining it.
Burnt sugar. And lemon.
She did not move.
"Don't light the lamp," said Silas Kross pleasantly, from the stairs. "I've been sitting in the dark for nearly an hour. My eyes have got used to it. It would be a shame to spoil them."
He was sitting on the stair.
She could see him now. Just. A shape, darker than the dark, on the fifth step from the bottom, with his knees drawn up and his arms resting on them and his hands hanging down between. Like a boy waiting outside a schoolroom. His gray coat was buttoned to the throat. His hat was on the step beside him. And something else was on the step beside the hat. Something pale. Flat. A box.
"Mr. Ambrose let me in," said Kross. "A nice man. He was very worried. He thought I'd come about his taxes." He shifted on the step, and it creaked. "I told him I'd come to give something to his lodger, and that I would wait, and that he should go back to his supper. He went. His wife was not pleased. Their cat came and sat with me for a while, but it got bored."
Wren stood in the dark hall with her key in her hand.
"Sir," she said.
"Did Mrs. Marta like her cloth?"
"She—" Wren stopped. "She smelled it, sir."
"Did she." Kross sounded pleased. "Yes. I thought she might. I smelled it too, when Vell brought it. It's a good pie, I should think. Mutton and onion. I'm sorry I never had any." He was quiet for a moment. "Fenwick sent a boy. You slipped on her step."
"Yes, sir. It hadn't been swept."
"No. She doesn't sweep it. Fenwick says. All week, not once." Kross shifted again on the stair. "And then tonight, after you'd gone, he says, she came out with her broom and swept it in the dark. The bottom step. For a long time. With great care." He paused. "Fenwick thought that was strange. He wrote it down."
Wren did not say anything.
The smell of burnt sugar and lemon lay in the dark hall between them like a hand.
"I think," said Kross, "that she's a woman who likes a clean house, and was ashamed in front of an Inquisitor. That's what I think." He picked up his hat from the step, and turned it round in his hands. "Fenwick thinks too much. It comes of standing on corners. I'll move him."
He stood up.
He did it slowly, with one hand on the rail, the way a man stands who has been sitting on a cold stair for an hour. He came down the five steps to the hall. He was carrying the box. He stopped in front of her, close, in the dark, and she could see his face now, a little. Pale. Mild. Tired.
"For Monday," he said, and held it out.
She took it. It was light. A flat box covered in paper. She could not see the color.
"A mask," said Kross. "White. Plain. Nothing on it at all. I chose it myself." He put on his hat. "I didn't want you to have to buy one. They're very dear, the good ones. And you'll want a good one. People will be looking at the gallery all night. They'll want to see who's sitting with the Inquisitor."
"Thank you, sir."
"Not at all."
He went past her, toward the green door. Then he stopped, with his hand on the latch, the way she had stopped at his door that morning.
"Oh, Wren. The card."
She did not move.
"On your mat, this morning. You asked me about it, and I said what card." He did not turn round. "That was unkind of me. I'm sorry. It was mine. I came by on my way in, very early, and put it through the door. I'd been meaning to for a week."
"Sir," said Wren. "What does it mean?"
Kross was quiet for a moment, with his hand on the latch.
"It means small," he said. "That's all. It's a thing I draw when I want to remember to keep something small. A thought. A face. A plan." He lifted the latch. "I thought you might find it useful. This week. You have a great many things to keep small, I think, just now. More than you've ever had." He opened the door, and the cold came in, and the snow-light. "I find it helps to have a picture of it. Something to look at. When the face wants to move."
He stepped out onto the step.
And then, on the step, with his back to her, with the door open and the snow coming down past the yellow lamp on its bracket, he said one more thing. Lightly. As if it had only just occurred to him.
"You hum, you know. When you copy."
Wren stood in the dark hall with the box in her hands.
"Three notes," said Kross. "You always have. Since the first day. Since the white room." He pulled on his gray gloves, one finger at a time. "I used to think it was a nervous habit. Like my wrappers. Today I listened properly, for the first time, for a quarter of an hour. And I don't think it is." He did up the button at his wrist. "I think it's a song. And I think you only know the beginning of it."
He went down the two stone steps into Mercer Street.
And as he went, walking away from her under the yellow lamps in the snow, toward the river, with his hands in his pockets and his hat on, not fast, not slow, she heard him. Very softly. Under his breath. The way a man hums to himself on his way home after a long day.
Three notes.
Low. Then higher. Then higher still, up and up, and hanging there at the top, unfinished.
And then, the fourth.
It came down. Not all the way. A little. A small step down from the top, soft and sure, like a foot finding the stair in the dark. And then a fifth, lower again. And a sixth. The tune going on, past the place where it always stopped in her, down and round and home, the way a song goes when you know all of it.
Then he turned the corner, and it was gone.
Wren Halloway stood in the open doorway of the narrow house on Mercer Street, with the snow blowing in past her onto the mat, and a white mask in a box in her hands, and did not move for a long time.
She had never heard the rest of it before.
She did not know how she knew that.
She knew it the way you know your own hand in the dark. The three notes were hers. They had always been hers. They had been in her since the white room, and before the white room, in the place under all the empty rooms where nothing was. And the rest of the song, the part that came down and went home, had been missing all her life, like a stair that is not there when you put your foot down for it.
And Silas Kross had it.
He had walked away down Mercer Street humming it, as easily as a man whistles for his dog.
She shut the door.
She went up the forty-four steps in the dark, counting, past the bookbinder's skins and glue pots, past the bookbinder's bedroom where the cat was crying to be let out, up to the low gray door under the roof. She lifted the latch. She went in. She lit one candle, a stub of tallow in a tin lid, with hands that would not stop shaking, and it took her three matches.
Then she sat down at the small square table under the skylight, with the box unopened in front of her, and took a new black book from the shelf, and wrote on its first page, in her small square upright hand, the number. 482. And the date. And the old man's name. And everything she had written in the gray book at her desk, the field and the drum and the face, word for word.
And under it, in the same hand, she wrote one line more. A line that had nothing to do with Wick at all. She did not know why she wrote it there. It was the only place she could think of where it would be safe.
He knows the rest of my song.
Then she closed the black book, and put it on the shelf, at the end of the row, after four hundred and eighty-one others. And sat in the candlelight, under the slope of the roof, with her hands flat on the table, and did not open the box.
And very softly, without knowing she was doing it, she began to hum.
