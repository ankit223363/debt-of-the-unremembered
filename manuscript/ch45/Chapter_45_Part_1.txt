Chapter 45: The Usual Desk, Part 1 of 5: Mercer Street
Mercer Street
Wren Halloway woke at the fifth bell, the way she always woke, without being told.
She did not open her eyes at once. She lay on her back on the narrow iron bed under the slope of the roof, with the blankets pulled up to her chin and the cold lying on her face like a damp cloth, and counted.
One. The bell of Saint Clement's, down by the river, deep and slow, a little before the others, the way it always was.
Two. The bell of the Ministry, high on the Hill, sharp and thin.
Three, four, five. The others, all together, from all over the city, falling over each other in the dark like boys tumbling down a stair.
Then she opened her eyes.
The attic was black. The candles had burned out in the night, all but one, a stub of tallow in a tin lid on the floor by the bed, which had sunk down into a little pool of grease with a blue bead of flame floating on it, about to drown. By its light she could just see the nearest shelf. The plain pine plank on its iron bracket, close above her head where the roof came down. And on it, spine to spine, packed tight, the small black books.
She could not see the labels. She did not need to. She knew them. She knew every one, in order, from the first shelf by the door to the last shelf under the skylight. The number, and the date. Four hundred and eighty-one of them now. She had counted them on Sunday night, when she could not sleep. Four hundred and eighty-one small black books, a copper each from any stationer's, each one with a few pages filled in her small square upright hand, and the rest of the pages empty.
Four hundred and eighty-one people.
Every memory she had ever taken out of anyone, on the Inquisitor's orders, in two years and five months, was written down in one of those books. Not the memory itself. She could not keep that. The memory went into a jar, and the jar went to the Office, and the Office sent it to the Bank, or the Ministry, or wherever such things went. She did not know. She had never been told. But before it went, while it was still warm in her, while she could still feel the shape of it, she wrote it down. Every one. The name of the person it belonged to. The day. And what it was. As much as she could remember, as fast as she could write, before it faded.
Ellis Marchbanks. Carter. Daughter Bessie, blue ribbon, sat on the water trough, said Daa.
So that somebody would know it had been there. That it had been real. That it had been theirs.
She had told that to one person in her life. Corin Vane. In this room, in the autumn, with the candles lit, at the table under the skylight. She had told him, and then she had shown him the oak leaf, and then he had written a promise in his Ledger in front of her and underlined it twice.
She did not know where he was now.
She knew he was alive. She knew that because Silas Kross was still looking for him. If Corin Vane had been dead, the Inquisitor would have stopped coming to work early.
The blue bead of flame on the floor gave a small hiss and went out.
Wren lay in the dark for a moment longer. Then she got up.
She did everything in the dark. She had done it for two years. She did not need to see. She knew where everything was, the way you know where your own hands are. The washstand, three steps from the bed, with the jug on it and the water in the jug frozen over with a skin of ice that she broke with the edge of her hand. The cold water on her face, so cold it made her teeth ache. The towel on its nail. The hairbrush. Fifty strokes, counted. The pins, from the saucer on the shelf, one, two, three, four, until the fair hair was pinned up flat and tight at the back of her head where the cap would sit on it.
Then the clothes. From the nail on the back of the door, where she had hung them last night, buttoned and brushed and ready.
The white coat first.
It was long, the Inquisitor's coat, down to the middle of her calves, and it was not really white. It was the color of the inside of a shell, a white with a little gray in it, made of heavy wool that kept out the rain and kept in the cold. It had a high stiff collar that buttoned to the throat with a hook, and two rows of small flat silver buttons down the front, and on the left breast, stitched in gray silk, the badge. An open eye. She buttoned it in the dark. Twelve buttons. Then the hook at the throat.
Then the boots. Long white ones, up to the knee, with a small silver buckle at the side. She had polished them last night, by the last candle, until she could see the flame in them. She pulled them on and buckled them.
Then the gloves. White kid. Fine. Tight at the wrist.
Then the cap.
Then she stood in the middle of the dark attic in her white coat and her white boots and her white gloves, with her hands at her sides, and made her face still.
She could not see it. There was no light. But she knew when it was right. It was a feeling, like closing a door. Everything behind her face went quiet, and stayed quiet, and her face became a wall.
There.
She put her hand into the right-hand pocket of the white coat. There was a pocket inside the pocket, a small one, that she had sewn in herself, with thread she had bought for a farthing from a woman on a corner. And in it, a stone. Gray and smooth, about the size of a pigeon's egg, with a white line round it, like a ring.
She held it for a moment, in her gloved hand, in the dark.
There had been two of them. Once. She did not remember where they had come from. She only knew that she had always had them, as long as she could remember, which was six years, which was since the white room with the forty beds and the gray lady ringing a bell. Two stones from a beach, the same, with the same white ring round them. And eight days ago, in Mrs. Marta's kitchen, she had given one of them to Corin Vane and said, Bring it back.
She did not know why she had done that.
She thought perhaps it was because she had wanted something of hers to be in the world, somewhere, that Silas Kross did not know about.
She let go of the stone.
Then she took out her other book. Not one of the black ones. The gray one. Plain, cheap, gray-covered, with a stub of pencil on a string, that lived in the left-hand pocket of the white coat and never came out of it except at night, in the attic, with the door latched. The book she kept her own secrets in.
She did not open it. She did not need to. She knew what was on the last page with writing on it. She had written it a week ago, at the bottom of the Ministry, crouched over a lamp, in front of a door that was not a door. And then she had torn the page out and folded it small and sent it out into the rain with a girl who ran over roofs.
Do not go to the barge.
That was all that page had said, in the end. She had written three lines and torn up two.
She did not know if he had got it. She did not know if he had gone. She did not know if the barge was still on the river, or sunk, or burned, or if the old lord was still in the hay, or if the girl with the short dark hair was still sitting on the deck with the long knife across her knees.
She only knew that Silas Kross had written one line about her, in his little black book, and blown on it to dry it.
And then told her to come to work at the usual time, and sit at her usual desk, and do her usual work.
As if nothing happened at all.
She had done it. Every day for a week. She had come to work at the usual time. She had sat at her usual desk. She had done her usual work. And he had been kind to her. Every day. Kinder than he had ever been. He had brought her tea himself, twice. He had asked after her cough. He had given her a barley sugar every morning, out of the paper bag on the corner of his desk, and smiled, and said Good morning, Halloway, in his soft pleasant voice.
And every day, she had waited for him to open the little black book and read out the line.
She put the gray book back in her pocket.
Then she went to the low gray door, and lifted the latch, and went down the narrow stair in the dark, counting.
Forty-four steps to the street door. Past the second floor, where Mr. Ambrose the bookbinder slept with his wife and his cat. Past the first floor, where the bookbinder kept his skins and his glue pots and his gold leaf, and the whole landing smelled of leather and fish glue and hot iron, even in the cold. Down to the narrow hall with the brass letterbox, and the green door, and the snow on the step outside, gray in the dark.
She stopped with her hand on the latch.
There was something on the mat.
She bent and picked it up. It was a square of white card, the size of a calling card, folded once. It had come through the letterbox in the night. She unfolded it, and held it up to the gray square of the fanlight over the door, where the first of the morning was beginning, very faintly, to come through.
There was nothing written on it.
Nothing at all. Only, in the middle of the white card, very small, a drawing. In pencil. Neat. Fine. A square, folded corner to corner.
Wren stood in the dark hall with the card in her gloved hand for a long time.
Then she folded it again, along the same crease, and put it in the left-hand pocket of the white coat, with the gray book, and opened the green door, and went out into the snow.
She did not hurry. She did not look round. She walked down Mercer Street in the gray dawn with her face still and her hands at her sides, the way she always did, at the usual time, toward the usual desk.
One. Two. Three. Four.
The Office
The Ministry of Records stood on the shoulder of the Hill, below the Crescent and above the river, like a great gray filing cabinet that somebody had set down on the slope and forgotten to put away.
It was the biggest building Wren had ever been inside. It was so big that you could not see the whole of it from anywhere. From the bottom of the Ministry Steps, coming up from Cooper's Rise, you saw only the front: six floors of gray stone, flat and plain, with rows of tall windows all exactly the same size, like the squares on a page of figures. From the side, from Chancery Lane, you saw that it went back, and back, and back, wing after wing, court after court, all the same gray, all the same windows, until it disappeared into the snow. And under it, Wren knew, under the courts and the wings and the cellars, it went down. Basement after basement. Third Basement. Fourth. And below that, the places with no numbers, where the draught came through the bricks and smelled of the river.
Two thousand people worked there, it was said. Clerks, and copyists, and archivists, and porters, and messengers. And on the fourth floor, at the east end, behind a door of dark wood with a brass plate on it, the Office of the Inquisition.
Wren climbed the Ministry Steps in the gray light, among the clerks.
They were all going the same way. All at the same time. Hundreds of them, in black coats and gray coats and brown, with their collars up and their hats pulled down and their breath going up in clouds, climbing the wide shallow stone steps in a long dark river of backs. Nobody spoke. Nobody looked at anybody. They climbed with their heads down and their hands in their pockets, the way the poor walked into the Bank on Lantern Quay with their memories to sell.
They made a space for Wren.
They always did. Nobody told them to. They simply saw the white coat coming up the steps among them, and moved aside, a little, without looking round, the way water moves aside round a stone. A space opened in front of her, and closed behind her, and she walked up the middle of it alone.
One hundred and twelve steps. She had counted them every morning for two years and five months, and they had never once been a hundred and eleven, or a hundred and thirteen.
At the top, the great doors. Bronze, green with age, standing open. And over them, cut into the gray stone, the words of the Ministry: WHAT IS WRITTEN IS KEPT.
Inside, the great hall, cold and echoing, with its floor of black and white stone and its clerks hurrying across it in all directions like beetles. Then the stairs. Not the grand stair in the middle, with its red carpet and its brass rods, that went up to the Ministers' rooms on the second floor. The back stair, at the east end, stone, worn into hollows, that went up and up in a square spiral round an open well, with an iron rail. Four floors. Ninety-six steps.
She met nobody on it. Nobody used the east stair unless they had to. Everybody in the Ministry knew where it went.
On the fourth floor, the door of dark wood. The brass plate. THE OFFICE OF THE INQUISITION. BY APPOINTMENT.
She went in.
The Long Office ran the whole length of the east wing, on the river side, under a ceiling so high that the lamps hanging from it on long chains seemed to float in the air like jellyfish. Tall windows down one wall, twelve of them, looking out over the roofs of the city to the river, all gray this morning, all white with frost at the edges. Down the other wall, doors. Many doors. Plain, of dark wood, each with a small brass number. Behind them, the rooms where the Inquisitors did their work. The reading rooms. The rooms with no windows.
And in the middle, the desks.
Forty of them, in rows of four, facing the same way, like the desks in a schoolroom. Plain, of dark wood, scratched and inked and worn. Each with an inkstand and a blotter and a lamp with a green shade. And at the far end, facing the rest, raised up one step on a little wooden platform, a bigger desk, with a red leather top, that belonged to the Chief Clerk, Mr. Abernethy, who was eighty and had been Chief Clerk for fifty years and was said to have once filed a report on the Archon himself.
Most of the desks were full already. Clerks in black, bent over ledgers. Watchers in gray, writing their reports from the night, yawning. Two or three Inquisitors in white, here and there, among the black and gray, like gulls among crows.
And on a long bench at the side, under the windows, the cadets. Six of them this morning. Boys, really. Fifteen, sixteen, seventeen. In their cadets' gray coats with the high collars, sitting very straight with their hands on their knees, waiting to be given something to do.
Rowan Thorne was at the end of the bench.
He was sitting a little apart from the others. He always did. Sixteen, tall for his age and thin, with dark hair cut very short and a pale stiff face that tried, very hard, to look as if it did not care about anything. He had a barley sugar in his cheek. Wren could see the bump of it. And his hands were busy in his lap, under the edge of his coat, where he thought nobody could see. Folding something. Small. Very small.
He looked up as she passed. Their eyes met.
Then he looked down again, quickly, at whatever was in his hands.
Wren went to her desk.
It was the third desk in the second row, by the fourth window. The usual desk. She had sat there every working day for two years and five months, and it looked exactly as it had looked when she left it at the eighth bell last night. The inkstand, full. The blotter, clean. Three pens, laid side by side, with their points all facing the same way. The lamp, unlit. And in the top right-hand corner, squared exactly to the edge of the desk, the pile of folders that she had not finished yesterday, waiting.
She sat down.
She took off her gloves and laid them on the desk, one on top of the other. She lit the lamp. She opened the top folder.
And did her usual work.
It was not hard. It never was. That was the thing that nobody outside the Office understood. They thought the Inquisition was all dark rooms and screaming and men being dragged up the east stair in chains. Some of it was. But most of it was this. Folders. Reports. Lists. A clerk's work. Somebody had seen something, somewhere, and written it down, and the paper had come up the east stair, and now somebody had to read it and decide whether it mattered.
Report of Watcher Fisk, Tuesday night, Lower Docks, the Bell tavern. Subject (a docker, name unknown) heard to say in drink that the Bourse was "a slaughterhouse with a dance floor." Subject not followed.
She read it. She wrote on the bottom of it, in her small square upright hand: No action. File. She put it on the left.
Report of Watcher Crane, Tuesday, Velvet Street. A footman in Bank livery delivered a parcel to the dressmaker Hesper (Needle and Thimble) by the front door at ten and a quarter. Unusual. Footman returned to the Bank. Parcel not seen again.
She read it. She stopped.
She read it again.
Then she wrote on the bottom of it, in exactly the same hand: Bank business. Not ours. File. And put it on the left.
Her hand did not shake. She watched it not shake.
Report of Mr. Gant, for the Lady Vivienne, under the Office's warrant of Monday. Arches, Lower Docks, the river wall below the Steps, at first light Wednesday. Arches found empty but for one old man, simple, known as Wick. Old man taken. The Lady asks that the Office question him, as he will not be questioned by her people.
She read that one twice too.
Then she turned it over, face down, and put it on the right. The right was for the ones that would go to the Inquisitor's desk.
She looked up.
The door at the end of the Long Office, the one behind the Chief Clerk's platform, was shut. The Inquisitor's door. Plain dark wood like all the others, with no number on it at all. There was no light under it.
He was not in yet.
She looked down again at the folders. At the report about the parcel on Velvet Street, lying on the left with File written on the bottom of it in her own hand.
She thought of the card in her pocket. The square, folded corner to corner.
She opened the next folder.
At her side, along the bench under the windows, she heard paper rustle. Rowan Thorne had finished whatever he was folding. She did not look. She heard him put it down on the bench beside him, very quietly. Then she heard him take another barley sugar out of his pocket, and unwrap it, and put it in his mouth, and begin, slowly, to fold the new wrapper.
To the eighth bell, Wren read thirty-one reports, and wrote No action on twenty-six of them, and did not look up once.
At the eighth bell, the door at the end of the Long Office opened.
A Barley Sugar
He did not come in from the stair. He came out of his own room.
That was the first thing. The door at the end of the Long Office, the one with no number, that had had no light under it all morning, opened from the inside. And Silas Kross stood in it, in his shirtsleeves, with his spectacles on and a pen in his hand, as if he had been sitting at his desk in the dark for an hour, reading by no light at all.
Wren did not look up. She did not need to. She felt the Long Office feel him. It went quiet, not all at once, but from the far end down, desk by desk, the way a field goes quiet when a hawk's shadow goes over it. Pens stopped. Papers stopped. On the bench under the windows, the cadets sat up straighter. Somebody coughed and stopped coughing.
"Good morning," said the Inquisitor.
He said it to the whole room. Pleasantly. The way a man says good morning to a street.
The room said Good morning, sir back, in forty voices, not quite together.
Kross smiled. He took his spectacles off and folded them and put them in the pocket of his shirt. Then he came down the one step from the Chief Clerk's platform, past old Mr. Abernethy, who did not look up, and walked down the Long Office between the desks.
He was not a big man. That was what people who had never seen him did not understand. In the stories they told about him in the Lower Docks, he was tall, and dark, and terrible, with eyes like coals. He was none of those things. He was of middle height and slight build, with thin fair hair going gray at the sides, and a mild, kind, rather tired face, like a curate's, or a schoolmaster's, and pale eyes, washed-out and pleasant, the color of weak tea. He had a soft voice. He had long careful fingers, very clean, with the nails cut short and square. He walked lightly, and quickly, and did not make much noise. If you had passed him in the street, you would not have looked twice.
Unless he looked at you.
He stopped at Wren's desk.
"Good morning, Halloway."
"Good morning, sir."
"Your cough?"
"Better, sir. Thank you."
"Good. Good." He did not move on. He stood beside her desk, with one hand resting on the corner of it, very lightly, and looked down at the two piles of folders. The left pile, and the right. Then he reached out, and with one long finger, without asking, turned over the top folder on the left pile, and read the bottom of it.
Bank business. Not ours. File.
He read it for a while. Longer than it took to read four words.
"Quite right," he said. "The Bank does like its parcels." He turned it back again, face down, exactly as it had been. "Come in, would you, when you're ready. Bring the Lady's report. The one about the old man." He smiled. "I'll ring for tea."
He went back up the Long Office, and up the step past Mr. Abernethy, and into his room, and left the door open behind him.
Wren sat very still.
She did not look at the report on the left pile, with File on it. She looked at the inkstand. She counted the pens. Three. Their points all facing the same way. Then she picked up the folder from the right pile, the one from Mr. Gant, and stood up, and smoothed the front of her white coat with one hand, and walked up the Long Office between the desks, and felt the room not look at her.
The Inquisitor's room was warm.
It always was. That was the second thing people did not understand. They thought it would be cold, and dark, and full of iron things. It was a small square room at the very end of the east wing, with one tall window looking out over the river, and a fire burning low and neat in a small iron grate, with the coals heaped in a little pyramid. A desk with a green leather top. A chair behind it. A second chair in front of it, for visitors, that nobody ever sat in. Shelves on two walls, from floor to ceiling, of black books, all the same size, all the same black, with nothing written on their spines.
On the corner of the desk, a paper bag, folded over at the top.
Kross was standing at the window with his back to the room, looking out at the river.
"Shut the door, Wren."
She shut it.
"Sit down."
She looked at the visitors' chair. Nobody ever sat in it. She had been in this room perhaps a hundred times in two years and five months, and she had never once seen anyone sit in it.
She sat down in it.
Kross turned round from the window. He looked at her sitting there, in the chair, in her white coat, with the folder on her knees and her hands folded on top of it. And he smiled, as if she had done something clever.
"Barley sugar?"
He held the paper bag out to her across the desk, open.
Wren took one. They were wrapped in squares of thin waxed paper, twisted at both ends. She held it in her bare fingers. She did not unwrap it.
Kross took one for himself. He unwrapped it, slowly, and put it in his mouth, and sat down behind the desk, and began, with his long careful fingers, without looking at what he was doing, to fold the wrapper.
In half. Corner to corner. In again.
"The Lady's man," he said, round the sweet. "Gant. Have you met him?"
"No, sir."
"A pleasant fellow. Round. Pink. Smiles a great deal." Kross turned the little square of paper in his fingers. Under. Over. "I don't like him at all. He came here at the sixth bell this morning with his old man in a handcart, under a sack, and his warrant in his pocket, and stood in my door, and told me that the Lady would be very grateful if the Office would be so kind." He smiled. "The Lady is always very grateful. She is also very rich. And she has the Archon's ear, on certain subjects. So the Office is very kind."
He put the tiny square of paper down on the green leather, exactly in the middle, between the inkstand and the lamp.
"You've read his report."
"Yes, sir."
"Tell me what it says."
Wren did not open the folder. She did not need to.
"Arches below the Steps, sir. Lower Docks. Under our warrant of Monday, at the Lady's request. Found empty at first light, except for one old man. Simple. Called Wick. The Lady's people tried to question him and couldn't. She asks that we do it."
"And what does it not say?"
Wren was quiet for a moment.
"Who the arches were empty of, sir."
Kross smiled. A real smile. It went all the way up to his eyes, and the lines came out round them, and for a moment he looked like what he looked like: a tired, kind, middle-aged man who had been up very early.
"Yes," he said. "Exactly that. Who were they empty of, Wren? Forty people, Gant says. Forty people living under the river wall, and a fire still warm, and soup in the pot. And at first light, nobody. Not a child. Not a dog." He spread his long hands. "Somebody told them. Somebody told forty people to get up in the dark and walk away, and they did. Every one. Except an old man who could not be told anything, because he can't hear it."
He leaned back in his chair.
"And I think," he said, "that there was a man living in those arches this week. With a bad leg. Who writes everything down in a book."
Wren's hands did not move on the folder.
"And I think," said Kross, "that the old man saw him. Every day. Lying there. And the people who came to see him. And the people who carried him. And I think that somewhere in that old man, among all the empty rooms, there is a picture of a face." He looked at her. "Old men like that, Wren, with nothing left, they keep the last few things they see the way a drowning man keeps hold of a plank. They can't use them. They can't say them. But they keep them."
"Yes, sir."
"So I'd like you to go and find the face." He picked up the little folded square of paper from the desk and held it out to her, across the green leather, between finger and thumb. "Room nine. He's there now. He's had some soup. Gant's men knocked him about a little, I'm afraid, getting him into the cart. He doesn't like to be moved."
Wren looked at the folded wrapper in his fingers.
She did not know what he wanted her to do with it.
Then she understood that he wanted her to take it. She held out her hand, and he put it in her palm, very gently, the way you put a sweet into a child's hand. It weighed nothing. It was still warm from his fingers.
"Why me, sir?" she said.
Kross looked at her for a long moment over the desk. He was still smiling.
"Because you have the gentlest hands in the Office, Wren," he said. "Everybody says so. You go in like a nurse. You take only what you came for, and you leave the rest exactly where it was, and you shut the door behind you." He picked up his pen. "And because the old man doesn't like to be moved. And you, of all of us, understand what it is to have only a few things left."
He bent his head over his papers.
"Oh, and Wren."
"Sir?"
"Sergeant Moll is coming back tomorrow. The Commonwealth Foot. At the tenth bell, the same as before." He did not look up. He was writing something, small and neat. "I'd like you to sit in. And take the notes. Your hand is so much clearer than mine."
"Yes, sir."
"That's all."
Wren stood up from the chair that nobody ever sat in. She put the folded wrapper into the left-hand pocket of her white coat, with the gray book, and the white card with the folded square drawn on it. They lay there together against her side. The card, and the wrapper. The drawing, and the thing it was a drawing of.
She went to the door.
With her hand on the handle, she stopped, because she could not help it.
"Sir. The card."
Kross did not look up from his writing.
"What card, Wren?"
She stood with her hand on the door handle.
"Nothing, sir."
"Then go and find me a face," said the Inquisitor pleasantly. "Before your tea gets cold."
