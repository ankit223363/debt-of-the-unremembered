Chapter 35: The Service Card, Part 3 of 5: Saturday Morning
Saturday Morning
Gallows Lane was narrow and steep and paved with old round cobbles, slick with frost, and it went down. That was the only good thing about it. It went down, and the Dye-Works were down, at the bottom of the city, by the river, two miles away across the roofs.
Two miles.
Corin did not let himself think about it. He thought about the next cobble instead. And the one after that.
They went down the lane with his arm across Lyra's shoulders and her arm round his waist, and the horse blanket over both of them like a cloak, and Ellis's flat cap pulled down over Lyra's hair. His left leg would not bend. He swung it from the hip, stiff as a post, and put it down, and the pain came up through him, and he leaned on Lyra, and swung the right leg after it. Then the left again. Swing. Pain. Lean. Step.
"You look drunk," said Lyra, after the first hundred yards.
"Good."
"I mean it. You look like a man who's been drinking since Thursday." She shifted his weight on her shoulder. "And I look like the sister who's been sent to fetch him home."
"Good," said Corin again. "Nobody looks at that either."
They came out of the bottom of Gallows Lane into a wider street, and the city was awake.
It was Saturday. Market day. The street was full. Carts of cabbages and turnips and frozen fish, coming down from the north gate, their wheels clattering on the cobbles. Milk carts with big tin churns, and the milkmen ringing their bells. A man with a tray of hot pies on his head, shouting pies, hot pies, in a voice like a foghorn. Women with baskets. Children with nothing, running. A dog with a bone. And everywhere, steam: out of the pie man's tray, and the horses' noses, and the gratings in the road, and the open doors of the bakeries, where the smell of new bread came out into the cold so thick and warm that Corin's empty stomach turned over.
Nobody looked at them.
A drunk and his sister. On a Saturday morning. In the middle of a market street. There were three other drunks within twenty yards, and one of them was singing.
They went on.
At the corner of Chandler's Row there was a broadsheet seller. A boy with a cap too big for him and a voice too big for the cap, standing on an upturned crate with a sheaf of papers under his arm, shouting the news.
"Lock-keeper taken! Read all about it! Coldwater lock-keeper taken up the Hill! Inquisition hunts canal smugglers! Barges searched from Saltgate to Brindle! Read all about it! One penny! Lock-keeper taken!"
Lyra's arm tightened on Corin's waist.
They went past. Slowly. Swing. Pain. Lean. Step. Corin did not look at the boy. He looked at the cobbles.
"Barges searched from Saltgate to Brindle," said Lyra, very low, when they were past. Her voice shook. "Brindle. He said Brindle."
"He said Saltgate too. And everything in between." Corin made himself keep walking. "It's a broadsheet. They shout the biggest names they know. It doesn't mean they're there."
"And if they are?"
Corin did not answer.
He thought of Oren, standing by the hay with his hand on his sword. He thought of seven crews on a frozen wharf, watching. He'll get a cold welcome on this wharf, Hessa had said. Colder than the weather.
He did not know if it would be enough.
It will not, said Malakor quietly. Not if they come in numbers. You know that.
I know.
Then why are you walking away from it?
Because we can't stop it by going back, thought Corin. We can only stop it by finishing this.
The voice did not answer. He did not know if that meant it agreed.
They went down through the city.
It took a long time. It took longer than anything Corin could remember, and he could not remember much. They went by the back ways when they could. Alleys, and courts, and the narrow stepped lanes between the houses that went down the hill like gutters. They went past back doors and dust heaps and washing lines strung across from window to window, with frozen shirts on them stiff as boards. They went past a yard where a man was killing a pig, and the pig was screaming. They went past a little chapel with its door open and an old woman inside on her knees in front of a painted saint, with a candle.
Twice they had to stop so that Corin could sit down. Once on a doorstep. Once on the rim of a horse trough. The second time, he sat for a long time with his eyes shut and his head on his arms, while Lyra stood over him in her flat cap with her hand on his shoulder, and a woman came out of a house across the court and looked at them and said, Is he all right, love? And Lyra said, He will be, thank you. He always is, by dinner time, in a voice so tired and so ordinary that the woman laughed, and went back inside.
And once, on the corner of Coopers' Lane, they saw the poster.
It was pasted on a wall, beside a bootmaker's window, among a lot of other posters. Notices of sales, and a missing dog, and a cure for chilblains. It was newer than the others. The paste was still wet at the corners. And at the top of it, in big black letters, was a word Corin knew.
WANTED.
And under that, smaller:
For questioning by the Office of the Inquisition, in the matter of the Thorne Abduction. CORIN VANE, formerly Harvester of the Third Basement, Ministry of Records. Aged about thirty. Middle height. Thin. Dark hair, gray at the temples. Gray marks on the hands and wrists. Walks quietly. May be in company of a young woman of good family. A REWARD.
And under that, a drawing.
It was not a good drawing. It was the face of a man. Any man. A long thin face, with dark hair and tired eyes and a straight mouth. It could have been Corin. It could have been any of a thousand men in the city.
But Lyra stopped.
She stood on the corner of Coopers' Lane with his arm across her shoulders, in the cold, and looked at the drawing for a long time.
"It's not much like you," she said at last.
"No."
"The eyes are wrong." She tilted her head. "And the mouth. Your mouth's not that straight. It goes down at one corner. On the left. When you're thinking."
Corin looked at her.
She did not look at him. She was still looking at the poster. Her face, under the flat cap, was very still.
Walks quietly, said the poster. Gray marks on the hands and wrists.
May be in company of a young woman of good family.
"Come on," said Corin.
They went on. Down. Always down.
And at last, at the bottom of a long flight of slimy steps between two warehouses, they came out onto the river.
Corin smelled the Dye-Works before he saw them. A sharp, strange, chemical smell, like vinegar and metal and something burnt, that got into the back of his nose and stayed there. And then he saw the colors.
The Dye-Works stood on the riverbank, a great sprawl of black brick buildings with tall chimneys and long sheds and wooden walkways going from one to the other. And out of the bottom of the buildings, through gratings and pipes and channels cut in the stone, the dye ran down into the river. Not one color. Every color. A stream of blue, thick and dark as ink. A stream of red, like blood. A stream of yellow, bright as egg yolk. Green. Purple. A thin trickle of something orange. They came out of the black walls side by side and ran down over the stone and into the brown river, and where they met it they spread out and swirled and twisted round each other in long ribbons, so that the water all along the bank for a hundred yards was striped like a ribbon-seller's tray.
The steam rose off the colored water in the cold. It rose blue and red and yellow.
And under the Dye-Works, along the riverbank, the arches began.
They were old arches. Very old. Older than the Dye-Works. Great round arches of dark brick, set into the bank one after another like the doors of a row of houses, each one big enough to drive a cart into. They held up the weight of the buildings above them, and the walkways, and the chimneys. Some of them had been bricked up. Some of them were closed with rotten planks. Some of them were open, and dark, and dripping, with colored water running down their walls in thin bright stripes.
And in the mouth of the third one, sitting on an upturned bucket with his chin in his hands, there was a boy.
He was thin and pale, in a coat too big for him and boots too small. He had a mop of dirty fair hair and a face that was quite blank, and quite calm, and quite patient. As if he had been sitting there for a long time and would be happy to go on sitting there for a long time more.
He watched them come along the bank. The drunk and his sister. He did not move.
When they were close enough, he stood up.
"Hello, harvester," said Gideon. "You're bleeding."
He said it with mild interest, the way another boy might have said you've got mud on your boots.
"Yes," said Corin.
"Vespera said you'd come back," said Gideon. "She said you'd come back and you'd be bleeding. She said you always are." He considered Corin's leg. "That's a lot."
"Is she here?"
"She's always here. She doesn't go out." Gideon turned toward the dark mouth of the arch behind him. Then he turned back. His pale eyes rested on Lyra, under the flat cap. On her face. On the ragged hem of her dress.
"You're the Thorne girl," he said.
"Yes," said Lyra.
Gideon nodded slowly, as if that were a thing he had been asked to remember and had now remembered.
"Mother Grue says you can come in," he said. "Both of you. She said if you came. She said to tell you to mind the step."
He went into the dark.
They followed him.
Mother Grue's Arches
The step was a foot high and slimy with green weed, and Corin missed it.
Lyra caught him. Gideon, ahead of them in the dark, turned round and watched her catch him, and waited, and did not offer to help. Not because he did not want to. Corin knew that. It simply did not occur to him. Gideon had sold the part of himself that would have thought of it a long time ago, for a copper or two, to a woman in violet.
They went in under the arch.
It was darker inside than out, but not so dark as Corin had expected. There was light ahead. Firelight. Red and moving. And the arch, which from the outside had looked like a single brick cave, went back and back into the bank much further than he had thought, and opened at the back into the next arch along, through a doorway knocked in the old brick wall. And that one opened into the next. And the next. So that the whole row of arches under the Dye-Works was joined up inside into one long, low, curving tunnel, like the inside of a great brick snake, lit every twenty yards or so by a brazier glowing red.
The walls ran with color.
Corin had seen it from outside, on the brick of the arch mouths. In here it was everywhere. The dye from the works above came down through the old brick in a hundred places, slowly, drop by drop, and ran down the walls in thin bright stripes. Blue. Red. Yellow. Green. Where two stripes met, they mixed, and made new colors, purple and orange and a strange muddy brown. The whole curving wall of the tunnel was striped and streaked and dripping, like a painter's apron, like the inside of a rainbow that had gone rotten. And where the drips fell on the floor, they had made small bright pools, and stained the bricks, so that you walked on color.
It smelled of the dye. And of smoke. And of people. A great many people, living close together, with not much water.
There were people everywhere.
They were sitting on sacks round the braziers. Lying on piles of rags against the walls. Squatting in the doorways between the arches. Corin could not count them. More than nine. A great many more than nine. Men and women and children, old and young, in rags and in good coats gone ragged, all with the same look on their faces when they turned to see who was coming in. A quick, flat, frightened look, gone almost as soon as it came. The look of people who have learned to expect the worst from every footstep, and to hide that they expect it.
And then, when they saw Gideon, the look went away. And when they saw Corin, limping, with his leg black with blood and his arm across a girl's shoulders, some of them stood up.
A big man in a sack apron, by the first brazier. Corin knew him. Or knew that he should. He had been in the arches the night of Wick. The man in the sack apron. It was in the Ledger.
Two thin boys with no shoes. A girl with a broom.
And a man Corin did not know at all. Tall, with a round soft face and very red cheeks and a little curling beard, sitting by the second brazier with a bowl in his hands. He looked up at Corin. He looked at him for a long time. Then, very slowly, he lifted one hand and touched his own face. The round cheek. The beard. As if to check that they were still there.
He was on a broadsheet last week, Juno had said. And now his own mother wouldn't know him.
Corin looked at the man's face and could not see anything wrong with it. That was the frightening thing. It was a face. An ordinary, rather foolish, kindly face. It fitted him as if he had been born in it.
"Harvester."
The voice came from the third arch. Old, and cracked, and thick with cold.
Mother Grue was sitting on a pile of sacks by the third brazier, wrapped in so many shawls that she looked like a heap of washing that somebody had left by the fire. Gray shawls and brown shawls and a red one, and on top of them all a man's old coat with the buttons gone. Out of the top of the heap came a small, sharp, brown face, very old, with a nose like a beak and eyes like two black currants. Her hands, folded on top of the shawls, were twisted and knobbed like the roots of a tree.
She looked at Corin's leg.
"Sit him down," she said. "Before he falls down. Here. By the fire. Tobin, get off that sack, you've sat on it all morning, let the man sit."
One of the thin boys got off a sack. Lyra brought Corin to it. He sat. He did not so much sit as fall, very carefully, in a way that made the arch go white round the edges again.
Mother Grue leaned forward out of her shawls and looked at the leg. She did not touch it. She looked at Lyra's bandage, and the blood on it, and the way the leg lay straight out, stiff, not bending.
"Rod?" she said.
"Yes," said Corin.
"Gate?"
"Stables."
Mother Grue nodded slowly. "They've been doing that at the gates since Wednesday. The carts from the canal side. Poking." She turned her head. "Nell! Nell, the honey. And the bottle. The green bottle, not the brown, the brown's for drinking." She turned back. "Didn't hit the bone?"
"I don't think so."
"You'd know." She sniffed. "You'd be screaming, not sitting."
The girl with the broom came running with a jar and a bottle. Mother Grue took them in her root hands. She uncorked the green bottle with her teeth, the way Hessa had, and the smell that came out of it was sharp and green and burning, like crushed nettles.
"From the works," she said. "Up above. They clean the vats with it. It'll take the skin off a pig." She held it out to Lyra. "Undo that. I'll not bend, at my age, I'd never get up again. You do it. Pour it in. All of it, right in the hole. Then the honey on top, thick, and a clean rag. Nell'll find you a clean one."
Lyra knelt down.
Corin did not watch her undo the bandage. He looked at the fire instead. At the red coals in the brazier, and the colored drips running down the wall behind it, blue and red and gold.
She poured.
He did not scream. He was proud of that, afterward. But he made a sound. A long, low sound, through his teeth, that went on and on, and his hands made fists on his knees, and the arch went white, and then black, and then white again. And somewhere a long way off, he heard Mother Grue say, There. There, now. That's the worst of it. That's the worst. In the same voice Mrs. Hobb used for Robin, and Ellis used for his horses, and Hessa used for Wat. The same voice. As if there were only one voice in the world for that, and every kind person learned it.
When the white went away, Lyra was spreading honey on the wound with her fingers, very gently. Thick and gold. It shone in the firelight.
"Honey keeps it clean," said Mother Grue. "My mother swore by it. Her mother too." She watched Lyra work. "You've got good hands, girl. Somebody taught you."
"A bargewoman," said Lyra. "Yesterday."
"Ah," said Mother Grue. "Hessa Doon." Her black currant eyes went to Corin. "I heard about Jory. Gideon told me. Poor old devil." She shook her head slowly in her shawls. "He used to bring me honey. From his bees. Every autumn. Two jars. He'd row it all the way down from Coldwater and hand it in at the arch and go away again without a word." She looked at the jar in Lyra's lap. "That's his. That's the last of it."
Nobody said anything.
Lyra's fingers had stopped moving on Corin's leg. Then they started again. Slowly. Very gently. Spreading Old Jory's honey over the hole the iron rod had made.
Corin looked down the long curving tunnel of the arches, at the braziers, and the colored walls, and the people on their sacks.
And at the far end of the third arch, against the wall, in a small space of his own, he saw a little old man sitting on a sack.
He was very small and very thin, with a long gray beard that someone had combed. He was sitting cross-legged, with a heap of paper in front of him. Old paper. Scraps. Torn bits of broadsheet and wrapping paper and the backs of bills. And he was sorting it. Picking up one piece at a time, and looking at it, and laying it on one of several small neat piles beside him. And then picking up the next. Slowly. Carefully. With his whole attention, as if it were the most important work in the world.
He did not look up. He did not look at anything but the paper.
And as he sorted, his free hand tapped on his knee. Softly. Tap. Tap. Not four, two, nine, three. Just tap, tap. Over and over. Like a clock that had forgotten what time it was.
Corin knew him.
He did not remember him. But he knew him. The way you know a word in a language you have lost.
Wick, said the Ledger, in his head. Rag-and-bone man. He found the dead scribe in the lane. He told everyone the harvester would find her. He was proud of it. They emptied him for it.
He used to give Robin bits of colored paper.
"He's well," said Mother Grue, following his eyes. "As well as he'll ever be. He eats. He sleeps. He sorts his paper." She was quiet for a moment. "He doesn't know me. He doesn't know anybody. But he's never once been unkind."
Corin looked at Wick for a long time.
Then Lyra tied off the clean rag on his leg, and sat back on her heels. And from the next arch along, from beyond the doorway knocked through the old brick, a voice said:
"Well. You took your time."
A woman's voice. Low and dry and not at all pleased.
Corin turned his head.
Vespera was standing in the doorway with her arms folded.
