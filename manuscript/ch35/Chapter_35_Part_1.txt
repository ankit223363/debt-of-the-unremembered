Chapter 35: The Service Card, Part 1 of 5: Rule One
Rule One
The hole in the hay was about as big as a coffin for two.
Corin had been in worse places. He had been in a drain with the water up to his chin. He had been in a coal cellar with a sword at his throat. He had been, very recently, in a brick tunnel under a garden with a boat jammed sideways across it and the river pouring in. This was not worse than any of those. It was warm. It was dry. It smelled of summer. And nobody was trying to kill him.
But it was very small.
He lay on his left side with his back against the hay at the back of the hole and his knees drawn up, and Lyra lay in front of him, on her left side too, with her back against his chest. There was no other way to fit. Her hair, which was still tied up in its knot with string, was an inch from his face. It smelled of hay and woodsmoke and, very faintly, under all of that, of something he could not name. Something clean. Like the air after rain.
He tried not to breathe on her neck. It was difficult. There was nowhere else to breathe.
The wain swayed.
It swayed all the time, slowly, from side to side, like a ship at anchor. Every rut in the road came up through the great wooden wheels and the axles and the boards of the wain bed and the hay, and arrived in the hole as a long, soft lurch. First one way. Then the other. The hay creaked around them. Somewhere above, a rope sang as it tightened and loosened. And under all of it, steady and slow, the hooves of the six horses on the frozen road. Clop. Clop. Clop. Twenty-four hooves. Corin could not count them. He listened to them instead, the way you listen to rain.
It was completely dark.
Not the dark of the sewer, or the cellar. A soft dark. A dark you could have slept in. Corin could feel the warmth of Lyra's back through the gray coat, and her ribs going up and down as she breathed, and the small, sharp jolt that went through her every time the wain hit a bad rut. He could hear her breathing. Slow, and careful, the way a person breathes when they are trying not to cry, or not to speak, or not to think.
They had been on the road for perhaps an hour.
Neither of them had said a word.
She lied to you, said Malakor.
The voice was very soft. It had come back properly some time after the hay room, in the cold on the deck, and it had been quiet ever since. Now it was right behind his left ear, close and dry, like a page being turned in an empty library.
I know, thought Corin.
About the letters. C. T. She knows who it is. Or she thinks she does.
I know.
And you said nothing.
Corin said nothing now, either.
He was thinking about a line in the Ledger. Not a new one. An old one. One of the very first, from the first page of the second book, in his own hand, written in Vespera's back room on the night he met Lyra, with the needles turning over his head on their threads. He had read it every morning since. He had read it this morning, on the bucket, before he went to sleep. He knew it the way you know your own front door.
Rule one. No lies between you.
Vespera's words. Lyra had agreed to them. He had agreed to them. They had shaken hands on it, in the back room, over the long table with the ink pots. He had it written down. Shook hands. Her hand was cold and had ink on it. She did not let go first.
She has broken it, said Malakor.
Maybe.
There is no maybe. You saw her face.
He had. He had seen her read the two gold letters at the bottom of Lot 41, and he had seen something go across her face like a shadow across a field. Fast. Gone. And then she had said I don't know in a voice like somebody else's, and shut the catalogue very gently, as if it were asleep.
He knew what a lie looked like. He had spent six years kneeling in front of people and asking them what color, and listening to them lie, and lie, and lie, before they told him the truth. He knew all the ways a face could do it.
Hers had done it.
So ask her, said Malakor.
The wain lurched. Lyra's shoulder pressed back against his chest, and then away.
No, thought Corin.
Why not?
He did not know how to answer that. Not in words. He thought of the moment on the barge, in the fog, when she had come out from under the canvas and he had looked at her face, and for one breath there had been nothing there. No name. Nothing. And she had seen it. He knew she had seen it. She had said nothing about it. All day. Not one word.
She had let him keep that.
So you will let her keep this, said Malakor. That is not what the rule says.
I know what the rule says.
You wrote it so that you would not do exactly this.
Corin shut his eyes in the dark. It made no difference.
"Corin," said Lyra.
He opened his eyes again.
Her voice was very low. Almost lost in the creak of the hay and the clop of the hooves. She had not moved. She was still lying with her back to him, with her face to the wall of hay.
"Yes."
"You're awake."
"Yes."
She was quiet for a long time. The wain swayed. He felt her breathe in, once, as if she were going to say something, and then let it out again.
"You saw," she said at last. "Didn't you. When I read the letters. On the catalogue."
Corin did not answer at once.
Rule one, said Malakor.
"Yes," said Corin.
He felt her go still. Not the stillness of somebody caught. The stillness of somebody who has been carrying a heavy thing up a long stair and has just been told that there are more stairs.
"I can't," she said. "Not yet. I can't say it out loud. If I say it out loud it'll be true." Her rough voice was shaking. "And I don't know, Corin. I don't. It's only two letters. It could be anybody. And if I say it, and I'm wrong, then I've said a terrible thing about somebody, and I can never unsay it. And if I'm right—"
She stopped.
Corin waited.
"If I'm right," said Lyra, very quietly, "then I don't know who I am."
The hooves went on. Clop. Clop. Clop.
Corin lay in the dark with his face an inch from her hair and thought about that for a long time.
He thought about a white-gloved figure under the first lamp of the Lantern Bridge. He thought about a gold tooth, and a green coat, and an audit card whose seal had gone from green to gray. He thought about a girl of six on a wagon, and a line in his own collar, in his own hand, sewn shut so that he could not take it out and lose it. Elara. (which one?) (both?)
He thought about not saying things out loud because then they would be true.
"All right," he said.
She did not move.
"All right?"
"Not yet," said Corin. "When you can. Not before." He paused. "That's not a lie. That's a thing you haven't said yet. They're not the same."
They are exactly the same, said Malakor.
No, thought Corin. They're not. And you know they're not. Or you wouldn't be so angry.
The voice behind his left ear did not answer that.
In front of him, very slowly, Lyra let out a long breath. He felt it go out of her. All the way out, through her ribs, through her shoulders, through the back that was pressed against his chest. As if something that had been wound up tight inside her had been let go, one turn.
"Thank you," she said.
She did not say anything else.
After a while, he felt her hand move in the dark. It came back, behind her, slowly, feeling its way. It found his hand, where it lay on the hay between them. It took hold of his fingers. Just his fingers. Not tight.
And she did not let go first.
How the Bourse Works
They talked about the Bourse because it was easier than talking about anything else.
Lyra started. She did it the way she did most hard things, Corin had noticed. Suddenly, and all at once, as if she were jumping into cold water. One moment there was only the creak of the hay and the clop of the hooves. The next, her low rough voice was coming out of the dark in front of him, quick and steady, telling it.
"The carriages come up Exchange Street," she said. "From the ninth bell. One after another, all the way down the hill, nose to tail. You can't get near on foot. The Watch close the street. There are men with torches all along it, in plum coats. Vivienne's color. Everything at the Bourse is plum."
"I know," said Corin. "Tam wore plum."
She was quiet for a moment. He felt her hand tighten, very slightly, on his fingers.
"Yes," she said. Then she went on. "The carriage stops at the pillars. A footman opens the door. You don't get out. Not yet. You wait. And a man comes down the steps to the carriage. Not a footman. An old man, in black, with a silver chain round his neck and a little book in his hand. They call him the Weigher."
"The Weigher."
"He looks at you. Through the carriage door. In your mask. He looks at your mask, and your gloves, and your shoes. And then he looks at whoever's with you. Your sponsor. And he says your sponsor's name out loud. Not yours. Never yours. Just Lord So-and-so, and guest. And writes it in his little book." She paused. "And if he doesn't like the look of you, he doesn't say anything at all. He just closes the carriage door. And the coachman drives on. And you've been turned away from the Bourse, and by morning everyone on the Hill knows."
"Did that happen to you?"
"No. Father was with me." Her voice changed a little. "He said Lord Thorne, and guest, and wrote it down, and then he looked up at me and said, Welcome back, Miss Thorne. As if he'd known me all my life. I'd never seen him before." She was quiet. "He knows everybody. Under their masks. That's what he's for. The masks are only for show. Everybody knows that. The Weigher knows exactly who you are, and who you came with, and how much you're worth, before you've put your foot on the step."
Corin thought about that.
An old man in black who knows every face in the city under its mask, said Malakor. How useful. For somebody.
"Go on," said Corin.
"Up the steps. Between the pillars. Through the doors. They're bronze. Taller than this wain. And inside there's a round hall with a black and white floor, like a chessboard, and the dome over it, the glass one, all lit up. And the tables." She took a breath. "Long glass tables, all round the edge of the hall, in a ring. With the vials laid out on them. On velvet. Plum velvet. With the little cards. You walk round the ring, slowly, and look. That's what the first two hours are for. Looking. Everybody walks round and round, very slowly, in their masks, and looks, and says nothing, and pretends they can't see anybody else."
"And the fourth tier?"
"That's not on the floor." Lyra hesitated. "There's a gallery. All the way round, above the hall, under the edge of the dome. You can see it from below. A railing, and curtains. That's where the Lady sits. Vivienne. In violet, not plum. She's the only one in violet. She sits up there all night and watches. And there are rooms behind the gallery. Small ones. Father said that's where the private sales are. You go up a little stair at the back of the hall, if you're asked. Only if you're asked. And you go into a room, and there's a table with a cloth on it, and the thing is under the cloth."
"Did you go up?"
"No. We weren't asked." She paused. "Father didn't want to be."
The wain lurched over something in the road. A stone, or a frozen rut. They both slid a little in the hay, and came back.
"And the bids?" said Corin.
"There's a box. Brass. On a stand in the middle of the floor, under the very top of the dome. Where everybody can see it. At the eleventh bell, a bell rings, and people start going up to the box. You write your number on a card. They give you the card at the door, with your name on the back, in the Weigher's writing, so nobody can bid for somebody else. You seal it with wax. You put it in the box." She swallowed. "And at midnight, the Lady comes down from the gallery and opens the box. In front of everyone. And reads the cards. One lot at a time."
"Out loud?"
"No. She never says the numbers. She just says the lot, and then she says the name. Whoever won. Lot nine. Lady Harriet Langley. And everybody claps." Her voice went flat. "They clap for people who've just bought somebody else's violin."
Corin said nothing for a while.
He was thinking about the Ledger. About the pages from the autumn, from the night he had gone to the Bourse kitchen door and asked for Tam. He did not remember that night. Not really. Only bits, like Ash. The heat of the kitchen. The copper pans, hundreds of them, shining red. A girl on her knees picking up broken tarts. A young man in plum with quick brown eyes, eating bread and cheese at a long table in the servants' hall, and lying for him. He's from the Langleys' country place, isn't that right, Vane? Corin had all that written down. He had read it this morning. He knew it the way you know a story somebody told you about yourself.
And he knew how the story ended.
Tam Hollin. Who asked.
He had written that on the list in his collar. Tam had asked a question. About Corin. About the Thorne footman who was not a footman. And somebody had taken him down the back stair, and through the coal door, and along Cinder Lane to the ice door behind the Bell, and shut it. And a man had come and stood on it. And Gideon had waited until it got light, and nobody came out.
"The servants," Corin said. "Downstairs. Tell me about them."
"I don't know about them," said Lyra. "That's the point. You never see them. Not properly. They're just there. Taking your cloak. Holding a tray. Opening a door. Standing against the wall in their plum coats with their faces turned away." She paused. "You'd know more than me. You've been down there."
"I don't remember being down there."
"But you wrote it."
"Yes."
He had. He closed his eyes in the dark and saw the page. A long table in the servants' hall. Twenty or thirty of them. Every house has its own color. Thorne green. Langley sky blue. Marrow gold. The Bourse's own people are plum. The head of them is called Gorse. Small pale eyes. Looks at everyone's hands.
Looks at everyone's hands.
Corin looked down at his own, in the dark, though he could not see them. One of them was held in Lyra's. The other lay on the hay. He could feel the gloves on them. Ordinary gloves now, gray wool, Hessa's. And under the gloves, on the backs of his hands and up his wrists, he could feel the place where the gray began. The Ash. The faint dry roughness of skin that had held too many dead men's memories and had started, very slowly, to keep some.
Gorse had looked at his hands in the autumn. Tam had seen him look. And Tam had laughed, and lied, and saved him.
"The service card gets me through the kitchen door," Corin said slowly. "But it doesn't get me past Gorse."
"Then you'll need to look like somebody Gorse has never seen."
"Gorse has seen everybody."
"Then somebody he's seen a hundred times," said Lyra. "Somebody so ordinary he doesn't see them any more." She was quiet for a moment. "Like the man in the gray cape. Juno said. The sort of man you could stand next to in a queue for an hour and not remember."
Corin opened his eyes.
She is right, said Malakor, after a moment. Grudgingly. Not a disguise. A blank. Nobody looks at a blank.
"Vespera," said Corin.
"Vespera," said Lyra.
The wain swayed. The hooves went on.
"And you," said Corin. "At the front. On the floor. In a mask." He paused. "The Weigher will know you."
"Yes."
"He'll say welcome back, Miss Thorne."
"Yes."
"And everyone on the Hill will know by morning that the Thorne girl came back to the Bourse."
"Everyone on the Hill will know by midnight," said Lyra. "Before the box is opened." He felt her breathe in. "That's why I need the Duke. If I come on his arm, they'll all be looking at him. Wondering what he's paid for me. What he wants. Whether he's going to buy Lot 41 and give it to me, or buy it and keep it, or buy it and burn it in front of me for a joke." Her voice was hard and small. "They'll be so busy looking at him and me that nobody will look at a waiter."
Corin did not say anything.
He was thinking that she had worked all of that out in the dark, in the hay, with her back against him, in the last half hour, while he had been lying here thinking about rule one.
He was thinking that she was going to walk into that round hall under the glowing dome, on the arm of the man who had led her gray mare out of her stable yard on a rope, and stand there in a mask while every face in the room turned toward her. Knowing exactly what they were thinking. Letting them think it. On purpose. So that he could be invisible.
"Lyra," he said.
"Mm."
He did not know what he had been going to say.
"Nothing," he said.
She did not answer. But after a moment, he felt her fingers close a little tighter on his. And stay.
The North Road
Some time after midnight, Lyra fell asleep.
Corin felt it happen. Her breathing changed. It went slow and deep and even, and her fingers loosened on his, and her head tipped a little to one side against the hay. The jolt that had gone through her at every rut stopped going through her. She just rocked, with the wain, the way a child rocks in a cart.
He did not sleep.
He had slept all day. He was not tired, or not that kind of tired. He lay in the warm dark with her back against his chest and listened to the road, and after a while he found that if he turned his head a little to the right, and pushed the hay aside with his free hand, there was a gap. Not much of one. A crack between the edge of the canvas and the side of the wain, where the rope had pulled the cloth a little loose. About as wide as two fingers.
He put his eye to it.
The night outside was enormous.
He had not seen the sky properly in weeks. Not in the city, where the smoke from ten thousand chimneys hung over the roofs like a lid, and the lamps put out the stars. Not on the Saltmarsh, under the fog. Not on the canal. But here, on the north road, in the middle of the frozen fields, with no town for miles in any direction and the cold so hard and clear that the air itself seemed to ring, the sky had come out.
All of it.
There were more stars than he had known there were. They were not scattered, the way they were in the city, one here and one there. They were crowded. Thick. Heaped up across the black like frost on a window, so many and so close that in some places they ran together into a pale glowing river that went right across the sky from one edge to the other. He had a word for that river. He knew he did. He reached for it and found, as he had found so often lately, only the smooth place where it had been.
It did not matter. He looked at it anyway.
Below the stars were the fields. Flat and white with frost, going away on both sides of the road for as far as he could see, with black hedges dividing them into great pale squares, like a chessboard. Here and there a tree stood up on its own, bare and black, with every twig sharp against the stars. Once, far away, there was a light. A single yellow window in a farmhouse, alone in all that white. Somebody awake. Somebody sitting up with a sick child, perhaps, or a cow in calf, or a letter they could not finish. The light went slowly past, and was hidden by a hedge, and did not come back.
The road itself was a pale band, rutted and hard, going straight ahead between the hedges into the dark. The wain's two lanterns swung over it, front and back, and made two moving pools of yellow on the frost. In the front one, Corin could see the backs of the two lead horses. Huge and dark and steaming, their great feathered hooves going up and down, up and down, slow and patient. And beside them, walking, the small bent shape of Ellis in his sheepskin, with his whip over his shoulder and his pipe glowing red.
Ellis was singing.
Very softly. Under his breath. Corin could only just hear it over the hooves. A slow, wandering tune with no words, going up and down like the road. It was not a song for anybody else. It was the song a man sings to his horses on a long road at night, so that they know he is there.
Corin listened to it for a long time.
He thought about Hessa on the barge, singing to Robin in the fog. Oh, the river runs down and the river runs wide. He thought about the Lantern Bridge, and a busker playing a tune that made his chest hurt. He thought about Jory, in a closed cart, going over the bridge and up the Hill, sitting with his hands on his knees. Singing.
Everybody sang, he thought. Out here. On the roads and the rivers. To their babies and their horses and the dark. In the city, nobody sang. Not like this. Not to nobody.
Some of them do, said Malakor.
Corin did not answer. He was not sure what the voice meant, and he did not think it would tell him.
The wain slowed.
He felt it before he saw why. The swaying changed. The hooves slowed. Ellis stopped singing. Through the crack, Corin saw the front lantern swing, and stop, and the lead horses come to a halt, blowing.
There was a light ahead. Not a window. A lantern, on a post, beside the road. And a bar across the road, a long white-painted pole on a pivot, like the arm of a giant. And beside the bar, a little stone hut with a smoking chimney and a door with a slot in it.
A toll gate.
The door of the hut opened. A man came out. Old, and stooped, in a nightcap and a greatcoat thrown on over his nightshirt, with a candle in a tin holder. He held the candle up and peered at Ellis.
"Ellis," he said. "Saints. Is it Friday already?"
"Friday night," said Ellis. "Saturday morning."
"Is it." The old man yawned enormously. "I lose track. Out here. One day like the next." He looked along the great dark hill of the load. "Full one."
"Always a full one. Bridewell stables want it by dawn."
"They always want it by dawn." The old man held out his hand. Ellis put something in it. Coins. Corin heard them clink. The old man counted them by feel, his lips moving, and put them in his greatcoat pocket. Then he did not move to lift the bar.
"Had visitors," he said. "This evening."
Ellis took his pipe out of his mouth.
"Oh, aye?"
"Gray coats." The old man jerked his head back toward the city. "Three of 'em. On horses. Come out from the city about dusk and stopped here and asked me questions. Wanted to know what came through. Carts, carriages, men on foot. From the canal side. Since yesterday." He sniffed. "Wrote it all in a little book."
Inside the hay, Corin did not breathe. Against his chest, Lyra slept on. He put his free hand very lightly on her arm, ready.
"And what did you tell 'em?" said Ellis.
"Told 'em the truth." The old man yawned again. "Two carriers' carts, the post, a fat curate on a fat pony, and the Mallow boy with his pigs." He scratched under his nightcap. "They didn't like it. They wanted more. I said, I can't give you what didn't come, gentlemen. Can't make a cart out of nothing." He looked up at the wain. "Said they'd be back. Said they were stopping everything from the canal side, from tomorrow. Every cart. Every load." He shrugged. "From tomorrow."
Ellis put his pipe back in his mouth. He drew on it. The bowl glowed red in the dark.
"Then it's a good thing it's today," he said.
The old man looked at him for a long moment, holding up his candle. His old eyes were watery and pale, and they went from Ellis's face to the great dark hill of hay behind him, and stayed there. On the back of the load. On the left side. Low down.
Corin saw them stay there. Through the crack, by the light of the candle. He saw the old eyes rest on the exact place where the canvas had been pulled a little loose.
Then they moved away.
"Mind the frost on Gallows Hill," said the old man. "It's bad tonight. A carter went over last week. Broke both his wheels."
He set down his candle on the post, and put his shoulder to the white bar, and pushed. It swung up, slowly, creaking on its pivot, until it stood straight up against the stars like a finger pointing.
Ellis clicked his tongue to the horses.
The wain lurched, and swayed, and rolled on. Under the raised bar. Past the little stone hut. Past the old man in his nightcap, standing beside his post with his candle, watching it go.
Corin put his eye to the crack and watched him until the dark took him.
The old man did not move. He did not go back inside. He did not shout. He just stood there by his little light, in the middle of the frozen fields, and watched the wain go up the road toward the city, until it was out of sight.
Then Ellis began to sing again. Softly. Under his breath. To his horses.
And Corin lay back in the warm dark, with his heart going slowly back to its proper place, and Lyra's breathing steady against his chest, and thought: everybody owes Hessa. Most of the river.
And then: and the old man at the toll gate is not the river.
And then, after a long time, as the wain began to climb, slowly, up what must be Gallows Hill, with the horses leaning into their collars and Ellis talking to them low and steady and the frost crackling under the great wheels: some people are just kind.
He did not know why that should make his eyes sting. But it did.
He wrote it in the Ledger the next day. In the margin. Small. The toll man on the north road. Nightcap. Candle. He saw the hole in the hay and said nothing. I don't know his name.
And under it, because he could not think what else to write: Some people are just kind.
