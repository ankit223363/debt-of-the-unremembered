Chapter 35: The Service Card, Part 4 of 5: Vespera
Vespera
She was just as Corin's Ledger said.
Not tall. Round and solid and square, like a well-made chest of drawers, with broad shoulders and strong forearms and a plain, wide, weather-brown face. Her sleeves were rolled up to the elbow. And her arms, from the backs of her hands to the elbows and beyond, were covered in writing. Fine black writing, and blue, and here and there a line of red or green, so small and so close that from where he sat they looked gray, like a stone wall covered in lichen.
She did not come forward. She stood in the doorway between the arches with her writing-covered arms folded across her chest, and looked at him.
She looked at his leg first. Then at his face. Then at his hands. Then, for a long time, at his eyes.
Corin had the feeling, under that look, that he had always had in front of the great slate board in the Archive entrance hall. The board where the clerks chalked up every jar that came in, every day, with its number and its weight. He felt counted. Weighed. Written down.
"How much?" said Vespera.
"How much what?"
"Don't." Her voice was low and dry. "Don't do that, Corin. Not with me. How much have you burned, since the last time you sat in my chair? In my shop. By my stove." She did not unfold her arms. "How much?"
Corin did not answer at once.
He was aware of Lyra, kneeling beside him, gone very still. Of Mother Grue in her shawls, watching. Of Gideon, standing a little way off by the wall with his hands at his sides, watching too, with mild interest, as if this were a play.
He took out the Ledger.
He did not need to. He knew. But he took it out anyway, and opened it at the back, at the page he kept for this. The page headed SPENT. And he read it to her. Out loud. In order. The way you read out a bill.
"Wednesday," he said. "In the cellar under No. 14, to break the lantern. One word. I don't know which one. Something to do with my mother." He turned the page. "Wednesday. In the drain. To keep warm. Three winters, from when I was a boy. All the snow in them." He turned another. "Thursday. On the Saltmarsh. To speak to Juno. What was left of my first language." He paused. "That's gone now. All of it."
Vespera did not move.
"Thursday," said Corin. "Some time. I don't know when. Numbers. Everything above nine." He looked at the page. "I didn't write it down when it happened. I only noticed afterward."
"You didn't write it down," said Vespera.
"No."
"You always write it down."
"I know."
She was quiet. He went on.
"Thursday night. Friday morning. In the sluice under Old Jory's garden. To get the boat free." He swallowed. "My first night at Mrs. Marta's. The bread. The first plate."
He closed the book.
The arch was very quiet. The brazier hissed. Somewhere down the tunnel a baby was crying, and somebody was hushing it. On his sack against the far wall, Wick picked up a scrap of paper, and looked at it, and laid it on a pile.
Tap. Tap.
Vespera unfolded her arms.
She came across the arch. Slowly. Her boots made no sound on the stained brick. She stopped in front of Corin's sack, and bent, and took his chin in one square, writing-covered hand, and turned his face up toward the light of the brazier.
She looked into his eyes again. Very close.
He looked back. Her eyes were dark brown, almost black, with fine lines round them, like cracks in old glaze. Her breath smelled of clove oil. Her fingers on his chin were warm and dry and quite steady.
"You're running out," she said.
She said it quietly. Not to frighten him. Just telling him. The way Ellis had said rod's red at the tip.
"I know," said Corin.
"No. You don't." She did not let go of his chin. "You think you know. You've got a page in your book with a list on it, and you add to the list, and you think that's knowing. It isn't." Her thumb moved, very slightly, under his jaw. "I can see it. In your eyes. Like a house where they've started taking down the pictures. There are pale squares on all the walls now, Corin. Lots of them. And you're not even looking at them any more. You walk past them."
He did not say anything.
"A week," said Vespera. "Not much more than a week since you sat in my chair. And you've burned a word, three winters, a language, half the numbers in the world, and the first kindness anybody in this city ever showed you." Her voice did not rise. "At that rate, how long have you got? Before there's nobody left in there to walk past the pale squares at all?"
"I don't know," said Corin.
"No," said Vespera. "Neither do I."
She let go of his chin.
She straightened up, and stood there over him, and looked down at him for a moment longer. Then she turned her head and looked at Lyra. Kneeling beside him on the brick, in Ellis's flat cap, with her hands still sticky with honey and her dress torn off to the shin.
"Miss Thorne," said Vespera.
"Vespera."
"You tore your dress."
"For his leg."
Vespera looked at the ragged hem. Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It was not a smile. But it was somewhere near one.
"Good," she said.
Then she turned back to Corin.
"Rule one," she said. "Do you still keep it?"
Corin felt Lyra go still beside him. He did not look at her.
"Yes," he said.
Vespera looked at him. She looked at Lyra. She looked at the two of them, side by side on the brick, for a long, long moment, the way she had looked at his eyes. Counting. Weighing. Writing down.
"Mm," she said.
That was all.
She turned away. She went back to the doorway between the arches. And on the threshold she stopped, with her back to them, and said over her shoulder:
"Well, come on, then. If you're going to ask me for something, you'd better ask me in there. Where the light's good." A pause. "And bring that book of yours. I want to see what you've written about the Bourse."
Corin stared at her back.
"How do you know it's the Bourse?"
Vespera did not turn round.
"Because Juno came through here yesterday at noon on her way north," she said, "with a parcel in her coat that she held on to like a baby, and a look on her face like she was carrying a lit match. And because Moll's been tapping on his desk for three days like a drummer at a funeral. And because there's only one thing in this city that the Thorne girl would walk fourteen miles in the wrong direction for." She paused. "And because I've been waiting for you to come and ask me for masks since the night you were born."
She went through the doorway into the next arch.
Corin sat on his sack and looked at the empty doorway.
Since the night you were born, said Malakor softly. That is a strange thing to say.
It was. He did not know what she meant. He did not think she meant it the way it sounded. He thought it was just a thing people said. Like fold it small.
He wrote it in the Ledger anyway, before he got up. In the margin. Small.

Masks That Hide More Than a Face
Vespera had made the fourth arch into her shop.
Not the whole of it. Just the back end, where the brick curved down to meet the floor and a crack in the roof let in a long thin blade of gray daylight. She had strung a rope across the arch, from one wall to the other, at the height of a man's head, and from the rope, on fine threads, hung her needles. Hundreds of them. Every size, from needles as fine as a hair to needles as long as a finger. When Corin came through the doorway, the draft moved through them and set them all turning, and the whole back of the arch chimed, very softly, like rain on glass.
Under the needles there was a table. Not her long table from Hook Lane. A door, taken off its hinges somewhere, laid across two barrels. And on the door, in rows, in the gray light from the crack, her inks.
Tiny pots. Dozens. Clay and glass and horn and tin. Some no bigger than a thimble. Each one stoppered with a twist of rag or a plug of wax. Black and blue and red and green, and others Corin had no names for. A pot of something that looked like milk. A pot of something that looked like nothing at all, clear as water, that caught the light and held it. And at the end of the row, set a little apart from the others, a pot that was not like the rest.
It was old. Very old. Glazed a deep dark blue, with a crack mended in silver across its belly. And round its mouth, where the stopper went, there was a thin bright rim of gold. Not paint. Real gold. Worn smooth with years of fingers.
Corin looked at it. He did not know why. It was only a pot.
Beside him, Lyra drew in her breath.
"That's the color," she said. Very softly. Almost to herself.
Vespera, at the table, did not look round. "What color?"
"The edge." Lyra was staring at the gold rim. "At Pruett's. In the yard. When Oren put up his hand and made the air go hard. It was like glass. Clear. You could see right through it. But round the edge of it, where it stopped, there was a line. A thin line of gold. Just like that." She swallowed. "I'd forgotten. I'd forgotten until I saw it."
Vespera did not say anything.
She picked up the gold-rimmed pot, and looked at it, and set it down again a little further along the table. Further from the light. Then she turned round.
"Sit," she said.
There was a chair. Not her leather one. A wooden kitchen chair with a broken back, the only chair in the arches as far as Corin had seen. He sat in it, with his leg out straight in front of him on a crate. Lyra sat on the end of the door-table. Vespera stayed standing, with her writing-covered arms folded.
"Tell me," she said.
So Corin told her.
He told it the way he had told Hessa, slowly and in order, and he read some of it from the Ledger, and Lyra told the parts that were hers. The tapping. The word. Bourse. The black catalogue, and the bulk lots by the crate, and the fourth tier on its black page. Lot 41. A Lord's Last Evening. Taken whole before the fire.
He did not tell her about C. T. He felt Lyra not tell her, beside him.
Vespera listened to all of it without moving. When he got to Lot 41, something happened to her face. Not much. Her jaw tightened, and her eyes went past him to the far wall, to the colored stripes running down the brick. And stayed there.
"Aurelian," she said quietly.
Corin had forgotten that she knew him. It was in the Ledger. He had read it this morning. Vespera once wrote one word on Lord Thorne's wrist. Under the heel of the hand, where nobody would see. Four letters. In ink so pale you'd think it was a vein.
"He came to my shop," said Vespera. "Twelve years ago. Alone. On foot. In the rain. A lord of the first rank, with his collar turned up, standing in my doorway like a clerk who'd lost his way." She was still looking at the wall. "He asked me to write a word on him. Small. Where it wouldn't show. And he asked me not to ask him why." A pause. "So I didn't."
"What was the word?" said Lyra.
Vespera looked at her.
"You know what it was," she said. "You've seen it. You've held his hand."
Lyra did not answer.
"And now somebody's selling the last evening of his mind," said Vespera. "To the highest bid. Under Vivienne's dome." She unfolded her arms. "And you want masks."
"Yes."
"For who?"
"For her," said Corin. "On the floor. As a guest. And for me. In the kitchen."
"In the kitchen." Vespera looked at him. "With what face?"
"Nobody's," said Corin. "A blank. A man that Gorse has seen a hundred times and never once looked at."
Vespera was quiet for a long time.
Then she went to the table and picked up a pot. A small one, clay, with a plug of black wax. She held it up to the gray light from the crack.
"You know there's a lamp at the kitchen door," she said.
Corin did not know. He looked at her.
"Vivienne's," said Vespera. "An old one. Brass. Hanging on a chain inside the door, over the step, where everybody has to pass under it. It's got a gem in it instead of a wick. A burned gem. Ash, set in glass." She turned the pot in her fingers. "It doesn't give much light. That isn't what it's for. What it does is find other Ash. Anybody who walks under it with Ash in them, Ash they're carrying, Ash they've touched too often, the lamp finds it. It makes it glow. On the skin. Gray, like smoke."
Corin looked down at his hands. At the gray wool gloves. At the place, under the gloves, at the wrists, where the gray began.
"Tam saved you from Gorse in the autumn," said Vespera. "With a lie about the Langleys. But it was dark that night, and the lamp was out. Half the kitchen girls in that house have sat in my chair, and they talk while I work. They light it for the big nights. For Midwinter, it'll be lit." She looked at his hands too. "You walk under it in a black coat with a silver tray, Corin, and your hands will light up like the windows of a house on fire. Gloves or no gloves."
Nobody said anything.
"So you need more than a face," said Vespera. "You need a skin." She set the pot down. "I can do it. I can bind it. Ink over the Ash, in your hands and your wrists and up your arms, so the lamp looks at you and sees a waiter. Clean. Nobody. It'll take two days. It'll hurt like the Saints' own fire. And it'll last about a week before the Ash comes up through it again." She looked at him. "So I'll do it on Thursday. Not before."
"And the face?"
"The face is easy," said Vespera. "Faces are always easy. Ask the man in the second arch." Something moved at the corner of her mouth again. "It's the rest that's hard."
She turned to Lyra.
"And you," she said. "You'll need a mask that hides more than your face."
Lyra looked at her.
"The Weigher," said Vespera. "He'll know you. He'll know you the moment the carriage door opens. That doesn't matter. You want him to. You want them all to know." She came closer. "But you'll be standing on that floor for three hours, Miss Thorne. Walking round the tables. Looking at your father's last evening on a black page. Listening to people bid on it. Smiling at the man who took your house." She stopped in front of Lyra. "And every one of them will be watching your face. Not your mask. Your face. The bits round the edges. Your mouth. Your eyes through the holes. The way you breathe. Waiting for it to crack."
Lyra did not move.
"So I'll make you a mask," said Vespera, "with something in the lining. A thread of quiet. Ink, bound into silk, against your skin, all round the edge where the mask touches you. It won't stop you feeling anything. Nothing can do that. But it'll stop it showing. For a few hours. Your face will stay still while everything behind it burns." She paused. "It's the same thing they teach girls like you at school. To sit up straight and keep your face still. I just do it with a needle."
Lyra looked at her for a long moment.
"And the price?" she said.
Vespera did not answer at once.
She turned away from both of them. She went to the end of the arch, where the brick curved down to the floor and the light from the crack fell on it. There was a ledge there, a narrow shelf of old brick sticking out from the wall, at about the height of her shoulder. And on the ledge, by itself, there was a jar.
A harvester's jar. Corin knew it at once. Thick gray glass, with a ground-glass stopper sealed in black wax, and a paper label pasted on its side. He had made a thousand jars like it. He had filled a thousand.
The wax on the stopper had never been broken.
And on the label, in faded brown ink, in a hand he did not know, there was a single letter.
V.
Vespera stood looking at it with her back to them.
"When this is done," she said. "After Midwinter. If you're alive." She did not turn round. "You open this for me, Corin. You're the only one I'd let. You open it, and you look at what's inside, and you tell me what it is." A pause. "And then you tell me whether I should take it back."
Corin looked at the jar. At the letter on the label. At the unbroken wax.
"What is it?" he said.
"If I knew that," said Vespera, "I wouldn't need you to open it."
She turned round. Her plain, wide, weather-brown face was perfectly calm.
"That's my price," she said. "Both masks. The skin and the silk. For one jar." She looked at him. "Yes or no?"
"Yes," said Corin.
Vespera nodded once, the way Hessa did.
"Good," she said. "Then take your coat off. I want to see your hands."
He took it off. It was not easy, sitting down, with his leg out on the crate. Lyra helped him with the sleeves. Then he pulled off Hessa's gray wool gloves, one finger at a time, and rolled his shirt cuffs back, and held out his arms in the thin gray light from the crack in the roof.
Nobody said anything.
He had not looked at them properly in days. There had been no time. And no light. And, if he was honest, he had not wanted to.
The gray began at his fingertips now.
It had not, once. Once it had been only a little smudge at the wrists, like the mark a dirty cuff leaves. He remembered that. He had it written down. Tam saw the gray at my wrists. Now it began at the very ends of his fingers, under the nails, faint, like the gray of old ash in a grate. And it went up. Over the backs of his hands, thicker, darker, in fine dry lines that followed the creases of the skin, the way frost follows the cracks in a windowpane. Over his wrists. Up his forearms.
Past his elbows.
He had not known it was past his elbows.
Vespera took his left hand in both of hers. She turned it over. She ran her thumb along the inside of his forearm, slowly, from the wrist to the crook of the elbow, and he felt the skin there, dry and rough and strange, like the skin of somebody much older. Like paper that has been wet and dried.
"When did this happen?" she said quietly.
"I don't know."
"It was at your wrists. At Hook Lane. A week ago."
"I know."
She did not say anything else. She did not need to. She turned his hand back over and held it a moment longer, looking at it. Then she let go.
Lyra was staring at his arms. Her face had gone very white.
"What does it mean?" she said. "Vespera. What does it mean when it goes that far?"
Vespera did not answer at once.
"It means he's been carrying too much," she said at last. "Other people's Ash. Other people's endings. In his hands, in his jars, in his head. And it's started to stay." She looked at Corin. "Harvesters go gray. All of them, in the end. It's the job. Most of them retire when it reaches the elbow. The Ministry gives them a pension and a little house by the sea and nobody ever hears from them again."
"And if they don't retire?" said Lyra.
"Then it keeps going," said Vespera. "Up. To the shoulder. To the neck." She paused. "I've only ever seen one past the neck."
Nobody asked her what had happened to him.
Corin rolled his cuffs back down. Slowly. Over the gray. He put the wool gloves back on, one finger at a time. He did not look at Lyra.
"Thursday," said Vespera. "I'll bind it on Thursday. All of it, to the shoulder if I have to." She turned back to her table. "It'll hurt twice as much as I said. I'm telling you now so you can't say I didn't."
"All right," said Corin.
