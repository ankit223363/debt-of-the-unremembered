Chapter 35: The Service Card, Part 5 of 5: A Stranger's Face
A Stranger's Face
She did not start with his hands. She started with his face.
"For practice," she said. "Mine, not yours. I want to see what I've got to work with before I put a needle near you." She pulled a stool up close to his chair and sat on it, so that their knees almost touched. "And I want to know if it'll hold. A face that comes off in the rain is worse than no face at all."
She sent Lyra away first.
"Go and wash," she said. "There's a trough at the end of the sixth arch, with water that's only a little bit blue. Get the honey off your hands and the blood off your face. Nell will find you a dress that isn't torn. Then eat something. You look like a ghost that's been dragged through a hedge." She did not look round. "And don't come back for an hour."
Lyra hesitated. She looked at Corin.
"Go on," said Corin.
She went.
Vespera waited until she was gone. Then she took off Corin's flat cap, which was Ellis's, and the horse blanket, and dropped them on the floor. She took his face in both her hands, and turned it to the gray light from the crack in the roof, and looked at it.
She looked at it for a long time. The way he looked at Ash. Every line. Every bone.
"You've got a face like a good kitchen knife," she said at last. "Long and thin and plain, and it's been sharpened too often." She turned it a little to the left. "That's good. Plain is good. Plain takes ink."
She let go.
And she began.
Corin had watched her work before. It was in the Ledger. She works without speaking. She works the way Hessa ties a knot. You cannot see her hands move, and then it is done. But he had never watched her work on himself. He could not watch it now. He could only feel it.
First, a cloth. Warm and wet, smelling of clove oil, wiped over his whole face, brow and cheeks and chin and the sides of his nose. It stung the bramble scratches from Jory's garden. Then a dry cloth. Then something cold and greasy, rubbed in with her thumbs. It smelled of tallow and lavender. It went into his skin and stayed there, and his face felt heavy, and stiff, like a mask of soft wax.
Then the brushes.
They were tiny. He felt them on his skin. Fine as eyelashes. Going over his face in short quick strokes, here, and here, and here. Under his eyes. Along the sides of his nose. At the corners of his mouth. Down the line of his jaw. Each stroke so light that he barely felt it. Hundreds of them. And each one, he knew, was putting a little color on him. Darker here. Lighter there. A shadow where there had been none. A line taken away where there had been one.
"Shut your eyes," said Vespera.
He shut them.
He felt the brush on his eyelids. On his brows. Something being pressed onto his brows, a little at a time, thick and soft. Hair, he thought. Somebody else's hair, cut very fine, and stuck on with something that smelled of fish glue. He felt his brows get heavier. Lower. He felt them meet in the middle, over his nose.
Then her fingers in his mouth.
"Don't bite," she said. "Open."
He opened. She pushed something in between his back teeth and his cheek. On the left side, then the right. Soft and dry. Cotton, rolled tight. It pushed his cheeks out. It made his face rounder at the bottom, and his voice, when he tried to say something, came out thick and slow, like a man with a cold.
"Don't talk," said Vespera.
He did not talk.
She worked on his nose for a long time. He felt her take a little lump of something and press it onto the bridge of it, and smooth it with her thumb, and brush it, and smooth it again. Wax. Warm from her hands. It made his nose feel wider. Flatter. Like a nose that had been broken once and set badly.
She did his hair. She rubbed something into it from a pot, something that smelled of lamp oil and smoke, and combed it flat with a wet comb, and parted it on the other side, low, the way clerks wore it. It felt heavy and dull and wrong on his head.
Then, last, she took a needle.
"Hold still," she said. "Very still. This is the only bit that's real."
He held still.
She put the needle to his face. Just below his left eye, on the top of the cheekbone. A tiny prick. And another. And another. Quick, light, so fast he could hardly feel them one by one. A little nest of pricks, no bigger than his smallest fingernail. Then she wiped it with the clove oil, and he felt it sting.
"A mole," she said. "Brown. Small. Real ink, under the skin. It won't wash off. It won't ever come off." She sat back. "People remember a mole. They remember it instead of a face. Ask anybody who's met a man with a mole what he looked like, and they'll tell you he had a mole. That's all. That's all they'll ever remember."
She put the needle down.
She was quiet for a moment, looking at him.
Then she got up, and went to the table, and came back with something in her hand. A piece of mirror. A long jagged shard of it, with the silver going brown at the edges, like the little mirror on the wall of Hessa's cabin.
She held it up.
Corin looked.
There was a man in the mirror.
He was about forty. He had a heavy, rather sullen face, round at the bottom and broad across the nose, with a low thick line of eyebrow running straight across over small tired eyes. His skin was the gray-yellow of a man who works indoors and does not sleep enough. His hair was dull and flat and parted low on the right, the way a clerk would wear it. And just under his left eye, on the cheekbone, there was a small brown mole.
He looked like a man who carried trays for a living, and had carried them for twenty years, and would carry them for twenty more, and did not much care.
He looked like nobody at all.
Corin stared at him.
The man in the mirror stared back. With small tired eyes, under one thick brow. And Corin lifted his hand and touched his own face, his cheek, the place under his left eye, and saw the man in the mirror lift his hand and touch the mole.
Well, said Malakor, very softly. There goes another one.
Another what?
Another face that is not yours. You have worn so many. In jars. In other men's heads. Dying soldiers. Drowned scribes. Fighters in back rooms. A pause. Now one on the outside, too.
Corin did not answer. He was still looking at the mirror.
"Will it hold?" he said. His voice came out round and thick through the cotton. Not his voice.
"The face? A night. A long night. If you don't sweat too much and don't cry and don't get hit." Vespera lowered the mirror. "The mole forever. On Midwinter I'll do it again, fresh, better than this. This is only to see if I could."
She put the mirror face down on the table.
And at that moment, from the doorway between the arches, Lyra said: "Vespera, Nell says there's—"
She stopped.
She was standing in the doorway in a dress that was not torn. A brown one, plain, too big at the shoulders, with the sleeves rolled up. Her face was clean and pink from cold water. Her hair was down, wet at the ends, combed out over her shoulders. She had a hunk of bread in one hand.
She was looking at the chair.
At the man in the chair.
Corin saw it happen. He saw her eyes go to his face, and stop there, and see a stranger. A heavy, sullen, forty-year-old waiter with a mole. Sitting in Corin's chair, with his leg out on a crate where Corin's leg had been, in Corin's coat. He saw her not know him. He saw the nothing in her eyes. The same nothing he knew had been in his own, on the deck of the Patient Grace, in the fog.
She took a step back.
"Where's—" she began. And then she stopped again.
Because he had thought, she doesn't know me, and something in his face had moved. He could not see it. He did not know he had done it. But he felt it. The left side of his mouth, under the wax and the paint and the cotton, going down. Just a little. At the corner.
The way it did when he was thinking.
And Lyra's face changed.
All at once. Like a lamp being lit. The nothing went out of her eyes and something came into them instead, so fast and so hard that she put her hand out to the door frame to hold herself up.
"Corin," she said.
He did not say anything. He could not, with the cotton.
She came across the arch. She stood in front of the chair and looked down at him. At the heavy brow, and the broad nose, and the flat dull hair, and the mole. She put out her hand, slowly, and touched his face. His cheek. The wax under her fingers. The way he had touched the mirror.
"I didn't know you," she said. Her rough voice was shaking. "For a moment. I looked right at you and I didn't know you."
He looked up at her.
He thought: now you know what it's like.
He did not say it. He would not have said it, even without the cotton. But he saw her think it too. He saw it go across her face, the same thought, at the same moment, and he saw her understand that he had seen.
Neither of them said anything.
Behind them, Vespera was cleaning her brushes in a little pot of clove oil, with her back turned, very slowly and very carefully, as if it were the most important thing in the world.
"The mouth," she said, without turning round. "That's the one thing I can't do anything about. I can give him a new nose and new brows and a mole and twenty years. But he thinks with his mouth." She set down a brush. "Down at the left corner. Every time." A pause. "You'll have to stop thinking, Corin, on Midwinter Night. In that kitchen. At least where anyone can see you."
Lyra laughed.
It came out of her suddenly, like a cough. A short, wet, broken laugh. And then she put her hand over her mouth and sat down on the end of the door-table with the bread still in her other hand, and did not laugh again. And did not cry. Just sat there, with her hand over her mouth, looking at a stranger who was Corin.
And after a moment, she reached out with the bread and held it out to him.
"You should eat," she said. "Whoever you are."
Gideon Asks
Gideon had been listening.
Corin did not know how long he had been there. He had not heard him come. But when Vespera took the cotton out of his cheeks and began, very carefully, to peel the wax off his nose, he turned his head and saw the boy sitting on the floor by the doorway, with his back against the wall and his knees drawn up and his chin on his knees. Quite still. Quite calm. Watching.
"You're going to the Bourse," said Gideon.
It was not a question.
Vespera's fingers stopped on Corin's face for a moment. Then they went on.
"Who told you that?" said Corin. His voice was his own again, more or less. His cheeks ached where the cotton had been.
"Nobody. I heard you." Gideon did not move. "The walls in here are thin, in the doorways. Mother Grue says sound goes round the arches like water in a pipe." He considered this. "I listen a lot. People don't mind. They don't notice me. Like the waiter."
Corin looked at him.
The boy's face was perfectly calm. Pale and thin and dirty, under the mop of fair hair, with the large pale eyes resting on Corin's face without any expression at all. Not curious. Not afraid. Not anything. The eyes of somebody looking at weather through a window.
"Yes," said Corin. "We're going to the Bourse."
"On Midwinter."
"Yes."
"You'll want to go downstairs." Gideon said it slowly, the way he had said the route of Tam's last walk, as if it were a thing he had to get exactly right. "The bulk lots. In the lower rooms. You said." He paused. "I know the lower rooms."
Nobody said anything.
Vespera put down the little knife she had been using to lift the wax. She did not look round at the boy. But Corin saw her shoulders go stiff.
"I used to go in," said Gideon. "When I was ten. And eleven. When I was selling. Not by the kitchen door. By the coal chute, round the side, in Cinder Lane. Where the coal carts tip. There's a little door at the bottom of the chute, and a man sits behind it on a stool, and you knock, and say selling, and he lets you in." He was quiet for a moment. "And then you go along a passage, and down some steps, and there's a room with a table and a lady in a white apron, and you sit in the chair and she takes it." A pause. "My cold. My fright. My sad. One at a time."
He said it without any feeling at all. Corin did not think he would ever get used to that.
"And after," said Gideon, "they give you a copper and send you out the other way. Not back up the chute. Through the store rooms. So you can see." He tilted his head. "So you can see what happens to it. That's what the lady in the apron said. So you can see where it goes, and what it's worth, and come back when you've got more."
"What did you see?" said Corin.
Gideon thought about it.
"Crates," he said. "Lots of crates. Wooden ones. Stacked up to the ceiling. With straw in. And jars in the straw." He paused. "And men carrying them. Big men. Very big. Bigger than Wat." He paused again. "They didn't talk. They never talked. They just carried the crates, up and down, all day, and when you walked past them they didn't look at you." He looked at Corin with mild interest. "Like me."
Corin did not say anything.
He had written something in the Ledger, once. A long time ago. He did not remember what. He only knew, sitting there in the broken chair with Vespera's fingers on his face and the needles chiming over his head, that something in him had gone cold. The way it had gone cold on the deck, when Lyra said isn't that strange. The way it had gone cold in the hay, when her father's eyes found him.
A draft from a shut door.
"I could draw it," said Gideon. "The lower rooms. Where the passages go. Where the stairs are. Where the man on the stool sits." He looked at the floor in front of his boots, where the dye had made a small bright pool, blue and green. "I remember places. I remember them very well. Mother Grue says it's because I haven't got anything else in my head to get in the way."
"Gideon," said Vespera.
She said it quietly. Only his name. But there was something in it that made Corin look at her.
Gideon looked at her too. Calmly. Waiting.
Vespera did not say anything else. She looked at the boy for a long time. Then she picked up her little knife again and went back to Corin's face.
"Why?" said Corin.
Gideon turned his pale eyes back to him.
"Why would you do it?" said Corin. "Draw the rooms. Help us. You don't owe me anything. You said so, at the coal wharf. You said you were only coming because Moll paid you a shilling." He paused. "And you don't—" He stopped.
"Feel anything," said Gideon. "No." He did not seem to mind. "That's right. I don't."
He was quiet for a moment, with his chin on his knees.
"But I can want things," he said. "Mother Grue says. She says wanting's not the same as feeling. She says you can want a thing with your head, the way you want to go left instead of right. Even if you can't feel it." He paused. "So I'm going to want something. For the map."
"What?"
"I don't know yet." Gideon got up. He did it slowly, all in one movement, the way a much older person gets up from a chair. "I'll tell you tomorrow. It takes me a long time to want things. I have to think about it." He stood by the doorway, with his too-big coat hanging off his thin shoulders, and looked at Corin with his calm empty eyes. "I'll think about it all night. And I'll tell you in the morning. And then you can say yes or no."
"All right," said Corin.
Gideon nodded.
He turned to go. Then he stopped in the doorway, and turned back.
"The big men," he said. "In the lower rooms. With the crates." He frowned slightly. It was the first time Corin had ever seen his face do anything at all. A small crease, between the pale brows, there and gone. "There were two. Mostly. Two of them, together. The same size. They always carried together."
"Yes?"
"One of them," said Gideon, "used to hum."
He stood there a moment longer, as if he were waiting to see whether that meant anything. Whether it was something he ought to have felt something about.
Then he went out through the doorway into the next arch, and was gone.
Vespera's knife had stopped moving on Corin's face.
She did not say anything. She did not look at the doorway. She stood very still, with the little blade in her writing-covered fingers, a quarter of an inch from his skin, for so long that Corin began to be afraid she would cut him.
Then she put the knife down on the table.
"The wax will come off with clove oil," she said. Her voice was perfectly ordinary. "I'll do the rest later. Your face can wait." She turned away from him. "Miss Thorne. You'd better come and sit down. We need to talk about the Duke."
The Duke Receives on Saturdays
"He receives on Saturdays," said Vespera. "From the second bell after noon until the fourth. At Marrow House, on the Crescent. Anyone of good family may call. Most of them do. It's the fashion this winter."
She was sitting on her stool, with her writing-covered arms folded, and Lyra was sitting on the end of the door-table in the brown dress that was too big for her, with her wet hair drying over her shoulders. Corin was in the broken chair with his leg on the crate and the last of the wax still on his nose. Nobody had remembered to take it off.
"How do you know?" said Lyra.
"Because half the ladies' maids on the Hill have sat in my chair too." Vespera did not smile. "They talk. About their ladies. About where their ladies go. About the Duke's Saturdays, and the Duke's gallery, and what the Duke said to Lady Harriet Langley about her nose." She paused. "He's very rude, the Duke. Very charming and very rude. People like it. They call it wit."
Lyra said nothing.
"He'll know you're coming before you're up the steps," said Vespera. "He'll know you're coming before you've left this arch, probably. He knows everything that happens on the Hill. He pays for it." She looked at Lyra. "And he'll let you in. Of course he will. The Thorne girl, who sold the family silver and lives over a stable and broke into her own house. Calling on the man who bought her father's seat. On a Saturday. With half the Hill in his drawing room." Her voice was dry as dust. "He'll be delighted. He'll dine on it for a month."
"I know," said Lyra.
"Do you?"
"Yes." Lyra lifted her chin. "That's why I'm going on a Saturday. With half the Hill in his drawing room. So that he can't say no without everyone seeing him say it."
Vespera looked at her for a long moment.
Then the thing happened at the corner of her mouth again. Closer to a smile, this time.
"Your mother would have done that," she said.
Lyra went very still.
"You knew my mother?"
"No." Vespera's face closed. Like a shutter coming down over a window. "I knew of her. Everybody on the Hill knew of her. The lady from over the water. The one who stood at the window." She unfolded her arms and stood up. "That's all. That's all I know. You'd better ask the Duke about your mother, if you want to know about your mother. He knew her. He knew her better than anybody, they say. Better than your father."
"What do you mean?"
But Vespera had turned away. She was at the table, among her pots, with her back to them, picking things up and putting them down again.
"He'll want something," she said, without turning round. "For sponsoring you. You know that. He'll want something that you can't buy back. He always does. That's how he collects." She set down a pot. "Don't give him the first thing he asks for. The first thing is never the real thing. Make him ask twice." A pause. "And whatever he asks for the second time, Miss Thorne, think very hard before you give it. Because it'll be the thing he's wanted since before you walked in."
Lyra looked at her back.
"How do you know all this?" she said.
Vespera did not answer.
She picked up the old blue pot with the gold rim, the one that was not like the others, and looked at it. Then she put it in the pocket of her apron.
"You'll need a dress," she said. "Not that one. Something with a name."
"Madame Hesper has my mother's gray," said Lyra slowly. "The one she bought back from the man on Silk Street."
"Then go to Hesper's first. The back door. She'll dress you." Vespera turned round. "Nell will go with you. As your maid. She's fourteen and she's never been up the Hill in her life, and she'll stand behind your chair in the Duke's drawing room with her mouth open, and that's exactly right. A lady with a maid who gawps is a lady who's come down in the world. Everybody will feel sorry for you." She paused. "Let them."
Lyra stood up.
She looked down at herself. At the too-big brown dress, and her bare red hands, and her wet hair.
Then she looked at Corin.
He had not said anything for a long time. He did not know what to say. He sat in the broken chair with the wax on his nose and his leg on the crate, and he looked at her, and he thought about the Crescent. He had been there once. It was in the Ledger. A long curve of tall white houses on the very top of the Hill, with iron railings and steps scrubbed white every morning by women like Big Maud Croft, and lamps that were lit all night by the city. He thought about Lyra walking up those white steps alone in her dead mother's gray dress, with a girl from the arches behind her. Into a drawing room full of the people who had shut their doors on her. To ask a favor of the man who had led her horse away on a rope.
He could not go with her. Not with this leg. Not with that poster on the corner of Coopers' Lane. Not with Gorse and the Weigher and every footman on the Hill knowing his face.
He had never felt so useless in his life.
You have, said Malakor. Many times. You simply do not remember.
"I'll be back by dark," said Lyra.
"I know."
"Before dark."
"I know."
She hesitated.
Then she came across the arch, and bent, and very quickly, very lightly, she put her mouth against his forehead. Just for a moment. The way she had kissed her father's forehead in the hay. Cool and dry. And straightened up.
"Take that wax off your nose," she said. "You look ridiculous."
And she went.
He heard her go down the tunnel of the arches. Her quick steps on the stained brick. Mother Grue's voice, saying something. Nell's voice, high and excited. And then the steps going away, out under the last arch, onto the riverbank, into the striped and steaming light. Fainter. And gone.
Corin sat in the broken chair.
After a while, he took out the Ledger. He opened it on his knee to a clean page. He took the stub of pencil from the spine. And he wrote.
Saturday. Noon. Mother Grue's arches.
Rod through the back of the left leg at the Bridewell stables. Bess bit a constable. Ellis. The toll man. Honey from Jory's bees. Lyra did the bandage.
Vespera will make both masks. Skin on Thursday. My price: open the V. jar after Midwinter, and tell her what is in it, and whether she should take it back.
Her ink pot has a gold rim. Lyra says it is the color of the edge of Oren's barrier.
Gideon knows the lower rooms. Two big men who never talk carry the crates. One of them hums. Vespera went very still when he said it. I don't know why.
Vespera says the Duke knew Lyra's mother better than her father did.
Lyra has gone to see him. Alone, with Nell. The second bell. On the Crescent.
He stopped. He looked at that.
Then he wrote one more line under it.
If she is not back by dark—
And he could not think how to finish it.
He sat looking at the dash for a long time. At the end of the line, where the words stopped, and there was only white paper.
At the far end of the third arch, on his sack, Wick picked up a scrap of paper, and looked at it, and laid it on a pile.
Tap. Tap.
And Corin closed the Ledger with the line unfinished, and put it back inside his coat, against his heart, and leaned his head back against the broken chair, and listened to the needles chiming over his head like rain on glass, and waited for the dark.
