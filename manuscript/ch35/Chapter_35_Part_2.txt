Chapter 35: The Service Card, Part 2 of 5: Iron Rods
Iron Rods
They came into the city at first light, by the north gate, and nobody stopped them.
Corin watched it through the crack. The fields gave way to market gardens, long brown strips of frozen earth with cabbages in rows and glass frames glinting in the gray. The market gardens gave way to cottages, and the cottages to houses, and the houses to streets. The sky went from black to gray to the color of dishwater. Chimneys began to smoke. A dog barked. A woman came out of a doorway with a bucket and threw it into the gutter, and the water steamed. And then the great dark shape of the old north gate went over them, an arch of black stone with iron spikes along the top of it and no gate in it any more, only the hinges, rusted and huge. Two Watchmen were standing under it, stamping their feet and blowing on their hands. They did not look up.
Lyra woke as they went under the arch. He felt her come awake all at once, the way a cat does, her whole body going tight.
"Where are we?" she whispered.
"North gate. In."
She let out a breath.
"Nearly there," said Corin.
He did not tell her about the toll man. There would be time later.
The Bridewell stables were at the top of the city, on the north side, where the old prison had been. Corin had never been inside them. He had passed the gates a hundred times. Two great stone pillars with stone horses' heads on top, green with moss, and between them a wide yard of cobbles going back and back into steam and noise and the smell of horses.
The wain turned in between the pillars.
The yard was enormous. Bigger than the whole of the hay wharf at Brindle. It was a great square of wet black cobbles, with stables all the way round it on three sides, long low buildings of gray brick with green doors in rows, the top halves open, and a horse's head looking out over almost every one. There were hundreds of them. Bays and grays and chestnuts and blacks. Cart horses with feathered feet, and carriage horses with arched necks, and little rough ponies with their manes in their eyes. They were all awake. They were all shouting. They knew it was morning, and they knew the hay had come.
The noise was tremendous. Hooves banging on doors. Whinnying. Ostlers shouting at each other across the yard. Buckets clanking. Somewhere, a forge, a hammer going clang, clang, clang on an anvil. And over it all, rising off the cobbles and the horses and the dung heaps in the corners, a great warm cloud of steam, so thick in the cold air that the far side of the yard was lost in it.
The wain rolled into the middle of the yard and stopped.
And Corin saw the gray coats.
There were three of them. Standing by a little brazier in the middle of the yard, near the water trough, with their hands held out to the coals. Not Inquisitors. Constables. Corin could tell by the short capes and the round hats. One was big and red-faced and had a cold, and kept wiping his nose on his sleeve. One was thin and young and bored. And one, the oldest, had a sergeant's stripe on his arm and a long iron rod in his hand.
It was the rod Corin looked at.
It was as long as a man was tall. Thin. Straight. Pointed at one end like a spit, and with a ring at the other end for a handle. It was the kind of rod the Watch used at the city gates in summer, to probe the loads of turnips and cabbages for smuggled brandy.
Or the kind of rod you used to probe a load of hay for a man.
The sergeant looked up from the brazier as the wain stopped. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he said something to the other two, and the three of them came walking across the wet cobbles toward it.
Corin took his eye away from the crack.
He put his mouth right against Lyra's ear.
"Gray coats," he breathed. "Three. With a rod. Don't move. Whatever happens. Don't make a sound."
He felt her nod. Once. Very small.
Outside, he heard Ellis.
"Morning, Sergeant."
"Morning." A flat, tired voice. "Where from?"
"Brindle. Like every Friday in loading week."
"From the canal side."
"Brindle's on the canal. Always has been."
"Then you'll not mind if we have a look." A pause. "Orders. Every load from the canal side, from today."
"From today," said Ellis. "Toll man said from today. Said it was from tomorrow last night, and here it is today already." He sounded tired and a little cross, the way any carter would sound who had driven all night and wanted his breakfast. "Go on, then. Have your look. Mind the horses. Bess on the near lead kicks."
"Does she."
"She does. Kicked a farrier in the head last spring. He's not been right since."
Nobody laughed.
Corin heard footsteps on the cobbles. Going round the wain. Slowly. The squelch of boots in the wet. A cough. The big one with the cold, sniffing.
Then a sound he had been waiting for.
A long, soft, hissing slide. Shhhhhk. Like a knife going into a loaf of bread.
The rod, going into the hay.
It was on the right side of the wain. The far side. Up near the front. He heard it go in, and stop, and come out again. Shhhk. And go in again, a little further back. And come out.
Shhhhhk.
Closer.
Corin lay very still in the dark with his arm round Lyra and his face against her hair, and listened to the rod coming down the side of the wain toward them. In, out. In, out. Every few feet. Steady. Unhurried. A man doing a dull job carefully because he has been told to, and because he is the sort of man who does dull jobs carefully.
Shhhhhk.
It went round the back.
Corin heard the sergeant's boots come round the tail of the wain, past the rear lantern. Heard them stop. Right behind the load. Right behind the hole.
Lyra had stopped breathing. He could feel that she had.
Shhhhhk.
The rod went in. Low down. On the left. A foot from Corin's head.
He heard it go past. Felt the hay shift beside his ear. It stopped. It came out.
Shhhhhk.
It went in again. Lower. Further along. Toward his legs.
And it came through.
It came through the back wall of the hole in the hay in the dark, with no warning at all, as if the hay were not there. Corin did not see it. He felt it. A sudden, cold, enormous blow, in the back of his left thigh, above the knee. Not pain. Not at first. Just a punch, very hard, very deep, and a coldness, as if somebody had pushed a long icicle into him.
Then the pain came.
It came like fire. All at once. Up through the leg and into his belly and his chest and the back of his throat, white and huge and roaring, and every part of him wanted to scream.
He did not scream.
He did not know how he did not. He found that he had bitten into the sleeve of his own coat, the arm that was round Lyra, and was biting it so hard that his jaw shook. He found that his whole body had gone rigid, from his heels to his skull, the way Wat's had gone rigid on the sacks when Hessa poured the brandy. He found that he was not breathing, and that he must not breathe, because if he breathed he would make a sound.
The rod stayed in him.
One breath. Two.
Outside, the sergeant grunted. Corin felt the rod twist, very slightly, in the hay. In his leg. As if the man had felt something. A resistance. Something softer than hay.
Do not move, said Malakor. The voice was very close and very calm. Do not move. Do not breathe. It is nothing. It is hay. He feels hay. Let him feel hay.
The rod twisted again.
And then, from the front of the wain, there was a scream.
Not a man's scream. A horse's. High and furious and enormous. And a crash, and a clatter of iron shoes on cobbles, and a man's voice, the big one with the cold, yelling: "Saints! Saints, she's—get her off me—"
And Ellis, shouting: "Bess! Bess, you devil, stand! I told you, I told you, mind the near lead—"
The rod came out.
It came out of Corin's leg all at once, with a long, wet, dragging slide that he felt all the way up his spine, and was gone.
He heard the sergeant's boots go running round the wain toward the front. He heard more shouting. The horse screaming again. The thin young constable laughing, high and frightened. Ellis swearing at the horse in a long, steady, inventive stream. And then, gradually, the noise dropping. The horse quieting. Ellis's voice, low now, talking to her. There. There, my girl. There, my lady. There.
And the big constable, from somewhere on the ground, saying thickly, "She bit me. She bit me on the arm."
"I said she kicks," said Ellis. "I never said she didn't bite."
The sergeant's voice. Flat and tired and disgusted. "Get up, Pratt. Get up and go and get that seen to." A pause. "And you. Get that animal off my yard. Go on. Unload and get out."
"You don't want to finish your look?"
"I've had my look. It's hay. It's all hay. Get it off my yard."
The boots went away across the cobbles. Three sets. One limping.
And the wain lurched, and rolled forward, slowly, toward the far side of the yard and the steam.
Corin let go of his sleeve.
He breathed.
It came out of him in a long, shuddering rush, and there was a sound in it after all, very small, a sound like a dog makes in its sleep. He could not stop it. He put his face against the hay and shook.
Lyra had turned round. He did not know how she had done it, in that space. But she had. She was facing him now in the dark, and her hands were on his face, both of them, and then on his chest, and his arms, feeling, frantic.
"Where?" she breathed. "Where?"
"Leg," said Corin. "Back of the leg. Left."
Her hand went down. Found it. He felt her find it. He felt the wet warmth of it soak through his trousers into her fingers, and he heard her draw in her breath, and then her other hand was in her coat, and there was the sound of cloth tearing, and she was pressing something against the back of his thigh, hard. Very hard. So hard that the pain came roaring back up through him and he bit his sleeve again.
"Hold still," said Lyra Thorne. Her rough voice was perfectly steady. "Hold still. Hessa showed me. Press. Press and don't stop." She pressed. "I've got you. I've got it. Hold still."
He held still.
And the wain rolled on through the steam, toward the hay barn at the back of the yard. And Corin lay in the warm dark with his face against the hay and Lyra's hands pressing the back of his leg, and listened to Ellis up at the front, talking to his horse, low and steady and kind.
There. There, my girl. Good girl, Bess. Good girl.
And he thought that Bess had kicked a farrier and bitten a constable, and was the best horse in the world.
Three Bangs
The hay barn was at the very back of the yard, and it was as big as a church.
Corin could not see it. He could only hear it. The wain rolled in under a roof, and the sound of the yard went suddenly hollow and far away, and the light through his crack went from gray to brown. The wheels rumbled on boards instead of cobbles. The wain stopped. And then there was the sound of men with forks, high up, somewhere above, and the soft heavy whumph of hay being thrown, and a voice calling down, Is that Ellis? You're late, Ellis. You're always late.
"I'm never late," said Ellis. "The rest of you are early."
The unloading took a long time.
It was the worst wait of all. Worse than the rod. Corin lay in the dark with Lyra's hands pressed against the back of his leg and listened to the forks working down the load toward them from the top. Every forkful that came off was a forkful less between them and the light. He could hear it getting closer. He could feel the hay above them getting thinner, lighter, letting through more of the brown barn light. He began to be able to see Lyra's face, an inch from his own. Pale. Dirty. With her lips pressed together and her eyes fixed on his.
She did not say anything. She did not take her hands away.
The cloth she was pressing on his leg was soaked through. He could feel it. Warm and wet and heavy. She had torn it from the hem of Hessa's mother's dress, he thought. He thought about Hessa's mother, whom he had never met, and whose dress was now soaking up his blood in the back of a hay wain in the Bridewell stables, and it seemed to him the strangest thing that had ever happened. He wanted to laugh. He knew that if he laughed, he would not be able to stop.
You are losing blood, said Malakor. That is why you want to laugh. Do not.
He did not.
The forks stopped.
There was a pause. Voices, high up, a long way off. Somebody saying breakfast. Somebody else saying about time. Boots on a ladder, going down, and away. A door. Quiet.
Then, right beside his head, on the boards of the wain bed, so close that he felt it in his teeth:
Bang.
Bang.
Bang.
Lyra let out a long breath.
"Can you move?" she whispered.
"Yes."
He could not. Not well. When he tried to straighten his left leg, the pain went through him like a bell, and the barn went white, and he heard himself make the sound again, the small sleeping-dog sound. Lyra's hand went over his mouth. He bit his lip until the white went away.
"Slowly," she said. "Slowly. Come on."
She went first. She pushed back the flap of canvas and slid out, feet first, and he heard her land on the boards of the barn floor. Then her hands came back in and took hold of his coat, and pulled, and he came out of the hole in the hay like a calf being born, all at once, in a tangle, and fell onto the floor beside her.
The barn was enormous and brown and dim and full of floating dust. There were great piles of hay everywhere, rising up into the dark under the roof, and ladders going up to lofts, and long shafts of gray light coming in through gaps in the planks of the walls, with the dust turning in them like gold. Pigeons were walking about on the beams overhead, cooing. Somewhere a cat was watching them.
Ellis was standing beside the wain with a fork in his hand.
He looked down at them. At Lyra, kneeling, with her hands red to the wrist. At Corin, lying on his side on the boards, with his left trouser leg black and wet from the hip to the boot.
He took his pipe out of his mouth.
"Ah," he said.
That was all. He looked at the leg a moment longer. Then he put his pipe back, and went to a peg on the wall, and took down a horse blanket, an old brown one with a red stripe, and a flat cloth cap. He dropped the blanket over Corin and the cap on Lyra's head. Then he went to a bucket by the wall, and dipped a rag in it, and came back, and held the rag out to Lyra.
"Hands," he said.
Lyra looked at her hands. She took the rag. She began to wipe them. Her fingers were shaking now. They had not shaken in the wain. They were shaking now.
"Rod's red at the tip," said Ellis.
He said it the way he said everything. Slow and flat, round the stem of the pipe. As if he were telling them the price of oats.
"He's leaned it by the brazier," he said. "Sergeant. In the yard. With the tip in the straw." He drew on his pipe. "It's dark yet, in the yard. With the steam. He'll not see it." He looked at the shafts of gray light coming in through the wall. "He'll see it when the sun's up. Or the next man that picks it up will."
"How long?" said Corin.
Ellis looked at the light again.
"Half an hour," he said. "Less, if the boy comes to stoke the brazier." He jerked his head toward the back of the barn. "There's a door. Behind the oat bins. Gives on Gallows Lane. Nobody uses it but the rats and the lads going courting." He looked at Corin's leg. "Can you walk?"
"Yes," said Corin.
Ellis looked at him for a long moment.
"No, you can't," he said. "But you will."
He turned away. He picked up his fork. He went back to the wain and began to fork the last of the hay off it, slowly, the way a man does who has been driving all night and has a long day ahead and does not want to finish too soon.
Lyra had cleaned her hands as well as she could. They were pink now instead of red. She knelt by Corin's leg and pulled the blanket back. She looked at the soaked cloth.
"I have to look," she said.
"Look, then."
She peeled the cloth away. He felt it come away from the wound, slowly, sticking, and he put his face against the floorboards and breathed through his teeth.
"It's small," said Lyra. Her voice was very quiet. "The hole. It's small. Like a finger. It's not—it's not torn. It just went in." She was quiet for a moment. "It went in deep."
"I know."
"It's still bleeding. Not as much. But it's still—"
"Tie it," said Corin. "Tight. Hard as you can. Above it, then on it."
She did. She tore another strip from the hem of the dress, a long one, and wound it round his thigh above the wound, and pulled it so tight that he saw white again. Then she folded the rest of the strip into a pad and pressed it on the hole and tied it there with the last of the cloth. Her hands were quick and sure, now. They had stopped shaking. She knotted it, and pulled the knot, and knotted it again.
"Hessa showed me," she said. Not to him. To herself. "Hessa showed me. On Wat. Press, and tie, and don't stop."
She sat back on her heels.
The hem of Hessa's mother's dress came down to the middle of her shins now, ragged, with her stockings showing underneath. She looked at it. She did not seem to mind.
"Up," she said.
She got her shoulder under his arm. He got his good leg under him. Between them, with a sound from him that he did not try to stop this time, they got him up.
He stood. On one leg. Leaning on her. The barn swung slowly round him, the hay and the ladders and the shafts of gold, and came back.
"Can you?" said Lyra.
He put his left foot down.
The pain was enormous. It came up through him and filled him and he thought, for a moment, that he was going to fall. But he did not fall. The leg held. It shook, and it burned, and it did not want to bend at the knee. But it held.
"Yes," he said.
She looked up at him. Her face, under Ellis's flat cap, was very white and very dirty and very determined.
"Liar," she said.
And she almost smiled.
They went across the barn. Slowly. Past the great hills of hay. Past the ladders. Past a row of oat bins, big wooden chests with sloping lids and the smell of oats coming out of them, dry and sweet. Behind the last one, in the dark corner, there was a low door in the plank wall. It had a wooden latch and no lock.
Lyra lifted the latch.
Corin looked back, once, across the barn.
Ellis was standing on the empty wain with his fork, in a shaft of gray light, with the dust turning round him like gold. He was not looking at them. He was looking at the barn's great front doors, and the yard beyond them, and the steam.
He did not wave. He did not say anything.
But as they went out through the low door into the cold, Corin heard him begin, very softly, to sing to his horses again.
