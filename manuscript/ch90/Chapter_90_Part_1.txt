Chapter 90: The Way Back, Part 1 of 5: The Cairn
The Cairn
Nobody can dig a grave in glass, so they built a cairn of gray stones on the shore of the Eye, and each of them laid one.
Arran was not under it. Arran was a long way back, under his own heap of black glass at the start of the short way, where they had left him on the night he died. But Kestrel said that did not matter. Kestrel said the Ashborn built a cairn wherever they stopped to remember somebody, and it did not need a body under it, no more than a fire needs a pot over it to be a fire. A cairn was for the ones who were left. So that they had a place to stand.
So they built it on the shore, in the morning, before the heat.
Corin had not slept. None of them had, much. They had come up out of the bunker at dawn, he and Lyra and Vespera and Kestrel, with the gray light coming down the roof shaft behind them and the old man's book under his arm, its pages lying flat now. And Oren had been standing at the foot of the iron stair, where he had said he would be, with his sword over his shoulder. Standing. Not sitting. Corin did not think he had sat down all night.
Oren had looked at Corin's face, and not asked.
And they had gone up, all of them, into the morning, and crossed the black glass to the shore.
The shore of the Eye was a beach. Corin had not thought of it that way, coming in, in the dark, with the glass rain still in his eyes. But it was. A long low beach, curving away on both sides round the black water of the glass, as far as he could see. And all of it stones. Small gray stones, round and smooth, the size of a hen's egg, or a child's fist, or a thumb. More of them than anybody could count, even Lyra. Lying in drifts and ridges along the edge of the black glass, the way pebbles lie along the edge of the sea.
And every one of them had a ring.
A thin white line, going all the way round. Some of them had it round the middle, like a belt. Some of them had it slanting, like a sash. A few had two. It was like nothing Corin had ever seen, a whole beach of them, gray and white, gray and white, as far as the eye could go, under the brown morning sky.
Lyra had one in her pocket. A boy called Davin had given it to her, a long way south, before they ever came onto the Glass. And she had shown it to Corin on the first night here, holding it up beside one from the beach. The same. Exactly the same.
They chose a place a little way up from the edge of the glass. Where the stones were thickest. Where you could look out across the whole black flat of the Eye and see the dome, small and half-sunk, in the middle of it.
And they each went and found a stone.
That was the Ashborn way. Kestrel told them. You did not just pick up the nearest one. You walked along the beach, by yourself, and looked, and you found the one that was right. You would know it when you saw it. And then you brought it back and laid it on the cairn, and said who it was for. Out loud. So the others heard.
Corin walked a long way.
He went slowly, on his crutch, along the curve of the beach, with the stones shifting and clicking under his boots and the black glass on his right hand going on and on. The heat was beginning. He could feel it coming up off the glass already, though the sun was hardly up. The sky was brown and low. There was no wind.
He did not know what he was looking for.
And then he did.
It was a small stone. Smaller than most. Gray, and smooth, and a little flatter than the others, like a stone that has been carried a long time in a pocket and rubbed by a thumb. And the white ring on it was not one ring. It went round, and round again, in a spiral. Twisting. Like the twist on a silver rod.
It went to the right.
Corin bent, slowly, with his crutch, and his lame leg complaining, and picked it up.
He held it in his living hand for a long time.
Then he went back.
The others were coming back too, one by one, along the beach, each with a stone in their hand. Lyra. Oren, walking slowly, with his sword on his back. Kestrel, and Sef, and Oda, and Brin limping with his ribs. Marrit, with the child on her hip and two stones, one in each hand. Vespera, last, very slow, holding hers against her chest.
And Tansy.
Tansy came from the far end of the beach, the south end, with the silent man walking beside her. She had her eyes shut. She was holding a stone in both hands, out in front of her, the way she had held the tin cup of water for Oren. Very carefully. As if it might spill.
The man did not have a stone. He walked beside her, slow, with his wrapped hands hanging. And when they got to the place, he stopped a little way off, and stood, and looked south. The way he always did.
Kestrel laid the first stone.
She knelt on the beach and set it down on the bare place they had cleared, a big flat stone, with a ring round the middle like a belt. And said: "Hask. Who taught us the notes."
And Sef laid one on it. And Oda. And Brin, with a grunt. And Marrit, two. Each one saying a name. Some of them Corin knew. Some of them he did not. People from the camp, he thought. People who had died on the way, or before. People he had never met. The Ashborn said them all.
Then Tansy.
She knelt down. Her eyes were still shut. She felt in front of her with one hand until she found the heap, and then she laid her stone on it, carefully, on the top, the way you put a cup down on a table in the dark.
"Arran," said Tansy. "Who got in the way."
Nobody said anything.
She stayed kneeling a moment, with her hand on her stone. Then she got up, and went back to the silent man, and took hold of his sleeve.
Then Oren.
He did not kneel. He could not, easily, with the sword on his back and his back the way it was. He bent from the waist, slowly, and laid his stone on the heap, and straightened again, and stood looking at it.
"Sola," said Oren. "My father's sister." He was quiet a moment. "I didn't know her long. She threw me at a wall." A pause. "She was a Vance."
Then Vespera, who laid hers without a word, and when Kestrel looked at her, said only, "An old friend. You didn't know him. Nobody did."
Then Lyra.
She had a stone in her left hand, her good one. A plain gray one, with a plain white ring. She knelt and laid it on the heap and looked at it for a long time.
"The man I cut," she said. "On the stair. Yesterday morning." Her voice was very steady. "I don't know his name. I meant to ask it. Harrow said it, when he carried him back. I didn't hear." She stopped. "I'll find it out. When we're home. And I'll come back and say it properly."
Kestrel looked at her a long moment. Then she nodded.
And then Corin.
He got down. It took a long time, with the crutch and the leg. He knelt on the gray stones, and the heat of them came up through his trousers, and he held out his living hand with the small flat stone in it, with the white ring going round and round, twisting to the right.
He laid it on the top.
"Malakor," said Corin. "Who got me out."
That was all he could say.
And Kestrel took the bone flute out from the front of her coat, where she had carried it over her heart since the night of the Ridges. Arran's flute. Small and yellow-white and smooth, with its holes. And she put it to her mouth.
And played.
Three notes. Low. The same three. Hask's three. Hm. Hm. Hm. The ones the old man had hummed by the fire, mending his nets. The ones that had hummed in Corin, ever since, low, in his corner. They came out of the flute thin and sweet and a little cracked, and went out over the black glass of the Eye, and did not come back, because there was nothing out there to send them back.
And in the room at the back of Corin's head, where it was so quiet now, Hask hummed with them. Very low. All three.
And then Kestrel played a fourth.
Higher. Not one of Hask's. Her own. Corin had never heard it before. A single high clear note, held, long, until her breath ran out and it wavered and stopped.
She took the flute from her mouth.
"That one belongs to this one," said Kestrel. "For when it's this one's turn." She touched her own chest. "So you'll know it."
And she put the flute back over her heart, and stood up, and walked away down the beach by herself.
The Stair
He went down into the bunker once more, in the heat of the day, while the others slept in the shade of the dome.
He did not tell anyone. He did not know himself, quite, why he was going. Only that there was a thing down there he had not looked at, and he could not leave without looking at it.
The iron steps rang under him. Step, and the crutch, and step. Down out of the hot white light at the way in and into the dim, and the cool, and the smell of iron and old stone. Past the seventh step, where the light gave out. Into the dark.
He had a lamp. A small one, from the store room, with a tin hood, the kind that throws its light down in a circle at your feet. He held it in his living hand, with the crutch under the same arm, awkwardly, and the circle of light went down the iron steps in front of him, step by step, showing him the little raised squares on each plate, and the rust, and the big round bolt-heads in the wall.
Near the bottom, the stain.
He had known it would be there. He had walked past it last night, going down to the command room with the journal, and again this morning, coming up, and both times he had not looked. He had walked round it, the way you walk round a hole in a road.
He looked now.
It was on the third step from the bottom, and the second, and the floor at the foot of the stair. Dark. Dry now. Gone almost black on the black iron, so that if you did not know it was there you might take it for rust, or for oil, or for a shadow. But it was not a shadow. It had a shape. It had run. Down from the third step to the second, in a long thin tongue, and over the edge, and down onto the stone floor, where it had spread out and stopped, in a wide flat pool, the way water stops when it finds a level place.
He stood on the fourth step and held the lamp out over it and looked.
Kross had said the pale one fell.
On the iron steps. In the dark. He fell a long way.
Corin looked up the stair, into the dark above him, where the steps went up and round the curve of the wall to the way in, far up, a small gray square of light. He looked at the iron rail. At the plates of the steps, each one as wide as a man's two arms. At the bolts.
A man could fall on a stair like this. In the dark. In a hurry. A man who did not know it.
But he would not fall here. Not at the bottom. A man who fell on a stair fell from the top, or the middle, and rolled, and fetched up at the bottom with his neck wrong. He did not stand at the foot of the stair, on the flat floor, and bleed there, in one place, the way a man bleeds who has been standing still.
Corin did not let himself think about it past that.
He did not need to. Lyra had seen the short blade, and the dark on the gray glove. Oren had guessed. Everybody knew. Nobody would say. And he found that he did not want to say it either. Not to anybody. Not even inside himself, in words.
And then he saw the rod.
It was lying on the floor, at the edge of the dark pool, half in it. Where it had rolled, perhaps, from a hand that opened. Silver. As long as his hand. With a twist in it, like the shell of a sea snail.
Twisting to the left.
Nobody had picked it up. Kross had not taken it. He had taken the notebook, the black one with the silver pencil, but he had left this. Lying in the dark at the foot of the stair, in the blood of the man who had carried it.
Corin stood on the fourth step and looked at it.
And his hands began to shake.
He did not know why. That was the thing. He looked at the small silver thing on the stone floor, with its wrong twist, and his living hand began to shake, so that the lamp swung, and the circle of light jumped and slid on the iron steps and the dark floor. And his gray hand, at his side, the one he could not feel, the one that did not move unless it moved by itself, began to shake too. He saw it. Hanging there at the end of his gray arm. Trembling. Like a leaf on a tree when there is no wind that you can feel.
He did not know why.
He had seen it before. Out on the glass, yesterday morning, in the pale one's hand. And his hands had shaken then, too. And the old man had gone small and quiet in the back of his head and would not say what it was.
The old man was not there to ask now.
Corin stood on the iron stair with the lamp swinging and both his hands shaking and looked at the left-turning rod in the dark, and tried to think what it was.
The old man had made rods. Rack after rack of them, in the round room. All twisting to the right. Mine go right, he had said, once, sour, in the back of Corin's head. They always went right. That's how you tell mine.
So who made the ones that went left?
And why did his hands know them?
He did not have an answer. He stood there a long time, and none came. Only the shaking. Going on and on, in both his hands, the living one and the dead one, as if they knew something about that small twisted thing on the floor that he did not, and were trying to tell him the only way they could.
At last he turned round.
He did not go down the last three steps. He did not touch it. He did not want to touch it. He went back up the stair, step and crutch and step, with the lamp swinging and his hands shaking, and the circle of light going up the iron in front of him, and the rod left behind him in the dark.
When he came out into the white heat at the way in, the shaking stopped.
All at once. As if somebody had closed a hand round both of his.
He stood in the way in and breathed, and looked out at the black glass and the gray shore and the cairn, small and far off, at the edge of the Eye.
And he took out his Ledger, the brown one, from his coat, and the stub of pencil from its spine. And on a clean page, at the back, away from everything else, in his big crooked capitals, he wrote:
FERRAND'S ROD. IT TURNS LEFT. IT IS AT THE FOOT OF THE STAIR. I LEFT IT THERE.
And under it:
MY HANDS SHAKE WHEN I SEE IT. BOTH OF THEM. I DO NOT KNOW WHY.
And under that, after a long time:
FIND OUT.
Far Side or Home
Kestrel asked him that evening, in the command room, with the rod on the map between them.
They had all come down. Out of the heat, which was worst at the end of the day, when the glass had been soaking up the sun since dawn and gave it all back at once. Down the iron steps, past the dark place at the foot of the stair that nobody looked at, along the corridor, through the gray blanket. Into the long low room with the shaft in the roof and the brazier on its three feet, not lit, and the table.
And the rod.
It was still lying where Corin had put it at dawn. On the thick glass over the painted map. By the small black dot of the dome, in the middle of the Eye. Pointing north.
Nobody had moved it. Corin did not think anybody would. It lay there in the last of the gray light from the shaft, plain silver, catching nothing, pointing at the empty brown edge of the vellum past the painted mountains, and everyone who came into the room looked at it once, and then looked away.
Kestrel stood at the table and looked at it longer.
She stood by the oak seat at the head, the one with the striped blanket, that nobody sat in. She did not sit in it either. She stood beside it with her spear upright in her hand and her lined gray face turned down to the map, and the light from the shaft fell on her and made her look very old, and very tired, and very sure.
"There," she said.
She put out one finger and touched the glass. Not the rod. Past it. Where it pointed. North, over the painted Glass, to the mountains like teeth, and over them, to the brown.
"The far side," said Kestrel. "Kesh-Amaran, the old ones called it. Over the rest of this Glass. Over the mountains. Down the other side. The land where they burn their dead." She looked up at Corin. "Where the old man in your head was born."
Corin did not answer.
"Nobody there knows your face," said Kestrel. "Nobody there has your name on a list. There are no gray coats. No Ministry. No Spire. No men with lamps." She paused. "We know the way. Part of it. The Ashborn have been over, a few, long ago. We know where the water is. We know how to cross the mountains in the spring, before the snow goes soft." She looked at the rod. "You could go. All of you. We would take you. And you would never be hunted again."
The room was very quiet.
Corin looked at the rod.
He had laid it there. Pointing north. Without thinking. His hand had done it. And all day, through the cairn and the stair and the heat, he had not let himself ask why his hand had done that. What it meant. Whether it was the old man, one last time, from wherever the old man was now, telling him which way to go.
He asked himself now.
And he looked at the map.
At the far side, up at the top, where the paint ran out. Empty. Brown. Nobody's face. Nobody's name.
And then down. Past the Eye. Past the Ridges, and the short way, and the long gray flat of the Glass. To the bottom edge. To the small painted tree with the crown in its branches, and the old letters beside it. And past that, off the edge, where there was no paint at all, only the brown vellum going on to the edge of the table.
The way home.
The city.
The Bank, and the Ministry, and the Spire. The men with lamps.
And a girl who could draw the crown with more points.
"No," said Corin.
Kestrel did not move.
"Thank you," said Corin. "Kestrel. Truly. I know what it costs you to offer it." He looked at the rod a moment longer. "But no. My sister's in Veridia."
He heard Lyra, beside him, let out her breath.
And then she said, very quietly, so quietly he almost did not hear: "And my brother."
Corin turned his head and looked at her.
She was not looking at him. She was looking at the map. At the bottom edge of it. At the place past the paint where the city would be. Her face was pale and set, and her bound hand was held against her chest, and her eyes were wet.
"Ro," said Lyra. "He's sixteen. He's alone in Aunt Cressida's house. With nobody." She stopped. "I left him a letter. That's all. A letter somebody else wrote for me, because I couldn't. I told him to keep counting." Her mouth shook. "I'm not going over the mountains while he's counting by himself."
Nobody said anything.
And from the gray blanket at the way in, where he had been standing all this time, half in and half out of the room the way he always was, Oren cleared his throat.
They all looked at him.
He was standing with his sword over his shoulder and his big hand on the edge of the blanket, holding it aside. He did not come in. He never did, much. He looked at the map on the table, and the rod, and at Kestrel, and at the two of them. And his face worked, the way it worked when he was coming near one of the holes.
"And the—" said Oren.
He stopped.
Corin watched him look for it. Watched him reach for the word, and find the place where it had been, and stop at the edge, the way a man stops at the edge of a ditch in the dark.
"The—the thing you sit on," said Oren slowly. "By the fire. In the evening. When you come in. With your boots off." He frowned at the map. "I've got one. At home. An old one, with a cushion that's gone flat. By the fire. In my room." He was quiet a moment. "I don't know what it's called anymore. But I know it's there. And I know it's mine. And I'd like to sit in it again."
He looked at Kestrel.
"Home," said Oren. "That's what I mean. I've a word for that, still. Home."
Kestrel looked at him for a long time.
Then she looked at Lyra. And at Corin. And at the rod on the map, pointing north.
And she put out her hand toward the rod on the map, as if she meant to turn it round. Corin saw her fingers come close to it. Close enough to touch.
She did not touch it.
She drew her hand back, slowly, and left the rod where it lay, on the glass by the dome, pointing north. At the mountains. At the empty brown edge of the vellum.
"Then the way is south," said Kestrel. "And we will walk it with you. Some of us." She looked down at the rod. "But this stays as it is. Pointing where your hand put it. The Ashborn do not move a thing the dead have touched."
She looked at Corin.
"The far side will still be there," she said. "It has been there a long time. It can wait for you to need it."
