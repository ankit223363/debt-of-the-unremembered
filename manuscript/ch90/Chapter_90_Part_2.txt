Chapter 90: The Way Back, Part 2 of 5: Who Goes
Who Goes
They settled it the Ashborn way, which was quickly, and without much talk, standing round the table in the last gray light from the shaft.
Kestrel said who would go. Nobody argued. Corin had learned that about the Ashborn, on the Glass. When Kestrel said a thing, in that voice, it was not a thing to be argued with. It was a thing that had already been thought about, all of it, from every side, and decided, and all that was left was the saying.
"This one goes south with you," said Kestrel. She touched her own chest. "As far as the city. Further, if there is need."
Corin opened his mouth.
"Do not," said Kestrel. "It is decided."
He shut it.
"Oda goes," said Kestrel. "And Sef."
Oda nodded, once. She was sitting on one of the iron seats with her spear across her knees, rubbing the shoulder Oren had put back for her, and she did not look surprised. Sef, standing by the wall, smiled a little, the slow smile he had, and said nothing.
"Marrit goes east," said Kestrel. "To the Salt Wells. With the child."
Marrit's face did not change. She stood holding the child on her hip, asleep, with its fat arm round her neck. But Corin saw her arm tighten round it.
"The others went east," said Kestrel. "From the camp. After the fire. The families. The old ones. The ones with children. They are at the Salt Wells now, if they got there. They do not know what happened here. They do not know about Arran. They do not know about the iron men, or the Eye, or the old man's book." She looked at Marrit. "Somebody has to tell them. And somebody has to take them the child. It is not a place for a child, where we are going."
"No," said Marrit. "It is not."
"You know the way."
"This one knows the way."
And that was all. Marrit did not argue. She did not cry. She stood with the child on her hip and her face like stone, and Corin knew that she would walk east across the Glass alone with a sleeping child on her back, by night, for as many days as it took, and get there, and tell them.
"Brin goes ahead," said Kestrel. "With the old one."
She looked at Vespera.
Vespera was sitting on the floor by the cold brazier with her back against the wall and her old inked arms folded and her eyes half shut. She opened them.
"Ahead," she said.
"By the short way," said Kestrel. "Back through the Ridges. Faster than the long way, if you know it. Brin knows it." She looked at Corin. "They will be in the city before you. Days before. They can see what is waiting. Who is watching the gates. Who is looking for whom. And send word back to meet you, at the Edge, or after." She paused. "It is better than walking into the city blind."
Corin looked at Vespera.
She was looking back at him. Her old pale eyes, the color of weak tea. And he saw in them that she had known. That she had known this was coming before Kestrel said it, and had already decided, and was only waiting to be told.
"Brin's ribs," said Corin.
"Are cracked," said Brin, from where he lay on the floor by the wall with his hands on his chest. "Not broken. Cracked. I can walk on cracked. I walked here on cracked." He turned his head and grinned at Corin, a big slow grin in his big slow face. "And she can't carry a pack. Somebody has to."
"I can carry a pack," said Vespera.
"You can't," said Brin. "I've watched you try."
Vespera looked at him. Then she shut her eyes again.
"Very well," she said. "The big one carries the pack. And I tell him where to put his feet."
"That's fair," said Brin.
And then there was only one more.
Corin had been waiting for it. He did not know what Kestrel would say. He did not think Kestrel knew either. Nobody could say where the silent man would go. Nobody could tell him. Nobody could ask him and get an answer.
Kestrel did not say anything.
She looked toward the way in, at the gray blanket. And Corin looked too, and so did all of them.
And Tansy was standing there.
She had come down. Nobody had heard her. She was standing just inside the blanket, in her three coats, the long dark man's coat with the sleeves turned back and back, and the brown one, and her own small faded rose-red one underneath, too small. With her eyes open, for once. Bright. Sharp. Looking at all of them.
"He'll walk you," said Tansy.
Nobody said anything.
"To the Edge," said Tansy. "He'll walk with you as far as the Edge. Where the snow starts. I asked him."
"You asked him," said Lyra.
"I asked him," said Tansy. "On the beach. After the stones. I said, Are you going to walk them to the Edge? And he looked at me." She paused, as if that settled it. "That's yes. When he looks at you like that. I know."
Corin looked at her.
"And then?" he said.
"And then we're coming back," said Tansy.
She said it simply. Not sad. Not anything. The way you say what you are having for supper.
"Back here?"
"Back here. To the door. To the knocking." She looked round the room, at the long table and the map and the shaft and the iron seats and the old oak one with the striped blanket on it. "He knocks. Every month. He has to. And somebody has to be here when he does. In case." She stopped. "In case somebody answers, one day. From inside."
Nobody said anything.
"And anyway," said Tansy, "this is home."
She looked at Corin.
"It's his home," she said. "I don't know why. He doesn't know why. But it is. And it's mine now, because I'm his." She lifted her chin. "So we're going home. After the Edge. Because this is home."
And she turned round, and went back through the blanket, and they heard her feet going away up the corridor toward the stair, quick and light, and her voice, very faint, beginning to sing. To nobody. The way she always did.
The room was quiet for a long time after.
"Well," said Oren at last, from the way in. "That's that, then."
"Yes," said Kestrel. "That is that."
And they began, in the last of the light, to get ready.
Vespera
Vespera and Brin went first, at dusk, before the rest of them.
The short way was faster, but it was harder, and it was worse in the dark. Brin said they would want to be through the first broken part of the Ridges before the moon went down. So they would go now, while there was still a little light in the sky. And the others would go later, in the full dark, by the long way, south across the open Glass, the way the night-walking rule said.
Corin found Vespera in the sleeping room.
It was a small square room off the corridor, with iron cots along the walls, the old kind, with chains instead of springs and thin straw mattresses gone gray with age. Most of them had nobody's things on them. One had Vespera's. Her pack, already tied. Her needle case, rolled. Her little bag of bitter green herbs. And the old woman herself, sitting on the edge of the cot, with her hands on her knees, the way a person sits who has done everything and is waiting for the moment to go.
She looked up when he came in.
"Harvester," she said.
"Vespera."
"Shut the curtain."
He did. There was a blanket over the way in, like the one at the command room, only smaller. He let it fall. The room was dim. There was one small lamp on the floor, with its tin hood, throwing its light down in a circle on the stone.
"Sit," said Vespera.
He sat. On the cot across from hers. It creaked, and the chains swung, and the straw crackled. He laid his crutch along the floor.
Vespera looked at him a long time.
She did not say anything. She only looked. The way she had looked at him every morning and every night since the Eye. Reading him. And he sat still and let her, the way he always did, and waited.
"Open your shirt," said Vespera.
He did not ask why. He unbuttoned his coat, one-handed, slowly, and then his shirt under it, and pulled it open. The air of the room was cold on his chest. He looked down.
There they were.
The lines. Her lines. The grounding ones, that she had put on him out on the Glass, the day he lost his name. Dark blue, almost black, on the pale skin of his chest and ribs. Small and close, in her old letters, in rows. He could not read them. They were upside down to him, and in the old letters, and he had never been able to read either. Lyra read them to him. Every morning. Your name is Corin Vane. You are a harvester. You have a lame leg. You are on the Glass. Lyra is with you. Oren is with you. And on, and on, down his ribs. A whole page of him, written on him, so that he could not lose it even if he lost everything else.
And on the lowest rib, on the left side, one line by itself. In the old letters. That Lyra had read to him once, frowning, and said she did not understand.
For later.
Vespera leaned across the space between the two cots.
She reached out her old hand. Thin. Blue to the wrist with indigo, and marked above it with old ink, her own ink, from a lifetime of needles. And she laid two fingers on his chest. On the top line. The first one. The one over his heart.
Her fingers were cold. Very cold. And dry, like paper.
"Read yourself," said Vespera.
Corin looked at her.
"Every morning," said Vespera. "Out loud. Before anything else. Before you eat. Before you drink. Before you speak to anybody, even her. You read yourself." Her two fingers pressed, a little, on the top line. "You've the book, I know. You read the book. That's good. Keep reading the book. But the book is paper. Paper burns. Paper gets wet. Paper gets taken out of a man's coat in the night by somebody in gray." She did not take her fingers away. "This doesn't. This is on you. Under the skin. Nobody takes this but the worms."
"I can't read it," said Corin. "It's upside down. And it's in the old letters."
"Then she reads it to you," said Vespera. "And you say it after her. Out loud. Every word. Every morning. Until you know it the way you know your own breathing. Until you could say it with your eyes shut in the dark with nobody there."
She looked at him.
"And then, harvester, one morning, you'll find you don't need her to read it. Because it's in you. Not just on you. In."
Corin did not say anything.
He looked down at her two old fingers on his chest, on the dark line of ink over his heart. And then up at her face. Very old in the lamplight. Very tired. The deep lines in it like cracks in a dry riverbed.
"Will I see you again?" he said.
Vespera did not answer that.
She took her fingers away. Slowly. And sat back on her cot, and put her hands on her knees again.
"Do your shirt up," she said. "You'll catch cold."
He did his shirt up.
And she stood, with a grunt, and picked up her needle case, and her little bag of herbs, and tucked them into her belt. And bent, and lifted the corner of her pack, and let it go again, because it was too heavy, and Brin would carry it.
"Where's the girl?" said Vespera.
"Outside. In the corridor."
"Fetch her."
He did not need to. He lifted the blanket over the way in and Lyra was there, in the corridor, a step off, with her back to the wall and her bound hand held against her chest, waiting. She had been waiting all this time. He thought she had heard all of it.
She came in.
Vespera looked at her.
She looked at her for a long time, too. The same way. Reading. And Lyra stood in the dim little room among the old iron cots with her chin up and let her.
"Your hand," said Vespera.
"Three days," said Lyra. "I know. Not a cup. Not a sword."
"Especially not a sword."
"I know."
Vespera nodded. And then she jerked her head, sharply, at Corin, sitting on the cot with his crutch on the floor.
"Every morning," she said. "He reads himself. Out loud. You read it to him and he says it after you. Every morning. Before anything." She paused. "He'll forget. He'll be tired, or hurt, or in a hurry, or the world will be on fire. He'll say Later. He'll say Tomorrow." Her old eyes went hard. "Don't let him."
Lyra looked at her.
"Make him," said Vespera.
"I will," said Lyra.
"Good."
And that was all.
She did not say goodbye. She did not touch either of them again. She went past them, out through the blanket, into the corridor, with her ragged gray hair and her needle case at her belt. And they heard her call, "Brin! Where's that pack?" in her thin sharp voice, and Brin's slow rumble answering from the stair.
And then her feet on the iron steps. Slow. One at a time. With Brin's heavy tread behind her, and the creak of the pack.
Going up.
Corin and Lyra stood in the little room and listened to them go. All the way up. Ring. And ring. And ring. Until the sound was very far off, at the top, at the way in. And stopped.
And then there was nothing.
Corin found that his living hand had gone to his chest. To the top line. Over his heart. Where her two cold fingers had been.
He did not take it away.
"Your name is Corin Vane," said Lyra quietly, beside him. Reading it. From memory. She did not need to see it. "You are a harvester."
"My name is Corin Vane," said Corin. "I am a harvester."
"You have a lame leg."
"I have a lame leg."
And they went on, the two of them, in the dim little room, in the light of the one lamp, line by line, all the way down. Out loud. Although it was evening, and not morning at all.
The Ledger
He wrote it at the long table, by the light of the brazier, the last thing before they left.
The others were up at the top. He could hear them, faintly, through the stone. Packs being lifted. Water skins being filled at the cistern, the bucket going down on its rope and coming up again, the splash. Kestrel's voice, low. Oren's, saying something about a strap. Tansy singing, a long way off. They had lit the brazier once more, to see by, and to warm their hands before the cold of the night walk, and when they had all gone up he had stayed behind, by himself, at the table.
The rod lay where it lay. On the glass. Pointing north.
He did not look at it. He opened his Ledger instead.
It was the eighth one. He knew that because the first page said so, in his own crooked capitals: LEDGER VIII. He did not know where the first six were. The seventh was in the city, with a girl called Juno, who could read now, a little, because Lyra had pointed at the letters. This one he had begun in the cold, in the north, the day they left the city. It was half full. The pages at the front were soft and gray with handling, from being read every morning. The pages at the back were clean.
He turned to the first clean one.
He took the stub of pencil out of the spine. It was very short now. He would need another. He held it in his living hand, the right one, the only one that would write. And he bent over the page in the red light from the brazier and began.
Slowly. Pressing hard. Each letter big and separate and leaning, the way they always leaned, like a row of fence posts in soft ground.
I AM A BRIGHT.
He stopped. He looked at it.
It did not look like anything. Four words. Big crooked capitals on a gray page. He did not know, even now, what a Bright was. Not properly. Somebody with a gift in them. Somebody the men with lamps wanted, enough to come up a road in the rain and pack children into a crate. He did not know what the gift was. He did not know if he still had it, or if it had gone out of him at the Edge with everything else. Perhaps it was the harvesting. Perhaps it was something else. Perhaps it was nothing at all now.
But it was true. The old man had written it. In the crease. The boy is a Bright.
So he wrote it.
I AM THE ONE WHO GOT OUT.
One. Out of seven. In a crate, in the rain. The one who was awake.
He pressed the pencil down hard on the last letter, so hard the point went blunt.
THE GIRL WAS KEPT.
He stopped again.
His hand was shaking. Not the way it had shaken on the stair, looking at the left-turning rod. A different way. A smaller way. The way a hand shakes when it has been holding something heavy for a long time, and is about to put it down.
HER NAME IS ELARA.
He looked at the name.
He had never written it before. Not in this book. Not in any book, that he knew of. And yet the letters came out of the pencil as if his hand had made them before, a long time ago, slowly, with a stick, in the dust of a yard, under somebody's careful eye.
Now it was in this one.
In his own hand. Big and crooked. On the page.
He sat and looked at it for a long time, in the red light. And he found he was saying it. Under his breath. The way Lyra counted under her breath, without knowing she was doing it.
Elara.
Elara.
Then he went on.
A line. A gap. And under it:
READ YOURSELF EVERY MORNING. OUT LOUD. VESPERA SAYS.
And under that, another gap.
And then he stopped for a long time, and did not write anything, and the brazier ticked, and somewhere far up at the top of the stair he heard Oren laugh at something, short, and Kestrel say his name.
And then he wrote:
MALAKOR.
HE GOT ME OUT.
HE WAS GLAD.
And put the pencil down.
He did not hear Lyra come down. He never did. She was there, all of a sudden, on the other side of the table, in the red light, the way she was always all of a sudden there. With her coat buttoned and her pack on her back and her bound hand held against her chest. Looking at him.
"They're ready," she said. "Kestrel says the moon's up. We should go."
"Yes."
She did not move.
"You've written something," she said.
"Yes."
"Do you want me to read it back?"
That was the rule. Rule one. One of them. Whatever he wrote in the Ledger that mattered, she read it back to him, out loud, so that he would know it was right. So that he would hear it in her voice, and know that it was true, and not something he had dreamed and written down in his sleep.
"Yes," said Corin. "Please."
He turned the book round on the glass and pushed it across the table to her.
She bent over it. In the red light. He watched her face. He watched her eyes go along the first line, and the second, and he saw her mouth begin to move before her voice came.
"I AM A BRIGHT," read Lyra.
Her voice was steady. Low and clear. The voice she read with every morning, from his chest, from his ribs, from the pages of the book.
"I AM THE ONE WHO GOT OUT."
A pause.
"THE GIRL WAS KEPT. HER NAME IS ELARA."
Her voice did not change on the name. He listened for it, and it did not. She said it the way she said his name. Carefully. As if it were a thing that could break.
"READ YOURSELF EVERY MORNING. OUT LOUD. VESPERA SAYS."
She looked up at him, for a moment, over the book, and something went across her face that might almost have been a smile. And then she looked down again.
"MALAKOR," read Lyra.
"HE GOT ME OUT."
And then she stopped.
Corin waited.
She was looking at the last line. She did not say it. He watched her look at it. He watched her mouth open, and stay open, and nothing come. He watched her bound hand, against her chest, close a little, as far as it would go in the linen.
"HE WAS—" said Lyra.
And her voice broke.
It went in the middle of the word. Just went. Like a stick snapping. And she stood there on the other side of Sola's table with the Ledger open in front of her, and her mouth open on the broken word, and her eyes full, and she could not finish it.
Corin did not say anything.
He reached across the table. Across the glass. Across the painted map, the Eye and the Ridges and the Glass and the small tree with the crown at the bottom edge. He did not touch her. He could not touch her, here. He laid his living hand flat on the glass, beside hers, beside the book. A hand's width away. The way they always did.
And waited.
And after a while, very quietly, Lyra said it.
"HE WAS GLAD."
She shut the book.
She pushed it back across the glass to him, and he took it, and put it in his coat, in the inside pocket, over his heart, over the top line of Vespera's ink.
And they stood there a moment longer, the two of them, on either side of the long table, in the last red light of the brazier. With the rod lying between them on the map, pointing north.
Then Lyra wiped her eyes on her sleeve, hard, the way a child does.
"Come on," she said. "Kestrel's waiting. And the moon won't."
