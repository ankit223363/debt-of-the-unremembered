Chapter 90: The Way Back, Part 5 of 5: The Cleansing
The Cleansing
The Archon was quiet for a long time.
Nobody else spoke. Nobody moved. Elara stood at the reading stand at the foot of the long table with her pen lifted off the page and waited, and the whole Council waited with her, twenty old men and women in fine gray in their red seats, with their gloved hands folded, looking at the head of the table. Or not looking at it. Looking at their own hands. At the polished wood. At the painted clouds upside down in it.
The snow went past the windows.
And the Archon sat with his own gloved hands folded on the table in front of him, and looked at nothing. At the far wall, perhaps. Or at the snow. His long pale face did not change. He did not frown. He did not sigh. He only sat, very upright, very still, in his red seat, the way a man sits who has been told the price of something and is working out in his head whether he will pay it.
Lucian stood behind him with the little strip of paper and did not move either.
Elara counted.
She did not mean to. She had caught it, somewhere, the counting. She did not know from where. She found herself counting the silence the way she had counted the pause after the girl's four notes in pen two. One. Two. Three. And on. She got to a number she had never counted to in silence before, and still nobody spoke.
And then the Archon unfolded his hands.
He laid them flat on the table. One on each side. The gloves were very fine, and close, and pale.
Elara did not look at them.
"Well," said the Archon.
His voice was the same. Dry. Pleasant. Ordinary. As if he were about to remark on the weather.
"That is a great pity about Ferrand," he said. "He was a careful man. A very careful man. I shall miss his notes." He paused. "And the harvester gone on. Over the far side. Well. That is a long way. Few come back from it." He paused again. "And the journal burned."
He looked down the table. Not at any of them. At the table itself. At the polished wood, and the painted ceiling upside down in it.
"I had wondered what was in that journal," said the Archon. "I confess it. I had wondered for a long time." He was quiet a moment. "Now I shall not know. Fire is very final."
Nobody said anything.
"But I do not think," said the Archon, "that we should take this as the end of a thing. Do you?"
He looked up.
And now he did look at them. All of them. Down the long table, one by one, slowly. Pellow. Fennimore. Langley. The Duke of Ashcombe, red in the face, scowling back. Dorian Sterling, with his white rose, smiling a little. The empty seat with the black ribbon. He looked at all of them, and his pale eyes went over each face the way a man's hand goes over a row of books on a shelf, looking for the one that is out of place.
"A harvester," said the Archon, "unregistered, held the roof of the Bourse on Midwinter night. In front of half the Hill. And the whole city has been talking of it ever since. In the chophouses. In the markets. On the bridges. I hear them. Lucian hears them for me." He did not look round at Lucian. "They say a man held the roof up. With his hands. They say it as if it were a good thing."
He let that sit.
"And the harvester is gone," he said. "Over the far side. But the city is still talking. And the city does not know he is gone. And there are others."
He laid one gloved finger on the table. Lightly.
"Scribes," he said. "Who are not on any list. Who write things down for people who cannot write. Letters. Pages. Things that should not be written."
A second finger.
"Ink-Binders," he said. "Old women with needles, who put words under people's skin. So that those words cannot be taken."
A third.
"Harvesters," said the Archon. "Who are not of the Ministry. Who work for themselves. Or for nobody. Or for people we do not know. Who take, and burn, and give back, without being told."
He lifted his hand from the table.
"I think," said the Archon, "that the city has become untidy."
The word lay on the long table like a stone.
"It happens," said the Archon. "In any house. Things pile up in corners. Dust gathers. People forget, after a while, where things are meant to go. And then one day somebody comes into the house from outside, and looks round, and says: What a mess." He smiled. It was a small smile. Kind, almost. "I should not like anybody to come into our house and say that."
Nobody said anything.
"So," said the Archon, "I declare, from today, a state of need across Veridia. Under the old law. The one my predecessor's predecessor wrote, for times of plague and fire, that nobody has used in a hundred years." He did not look at the Council when he said it. He looked at Elara. At the foot of the table. At her pen. "Write it, Auditor."
Elara wrote it.
Her hand was steady. She made it be.
"Every scribe not on the Ministry's list," said the Archon. "Every Ink-Binder. Every harvester not of the Ministry. To be found. And brought in. Gently. With their books, and their needles, and their jars. To the Ministry, to be weighed. Every one. By Midsummer."
Elara wrote it.
"Their names to come up to the Fourth Desk on the Thursday lists. Every week. The Bank to hold what they own until they are weighed."
Elara wrote it.
At the far end of the table, the Duke of Ashcombe made a sound. A loud one. Through his nose. And half rose from his seat.
"Archon," he said. "This is—"
The Archon turned his head and looked at him.
Just looked.
And the Duke of Ashcombe, who was a big man, red and white-haired and loud, who had shouted at the Council for forty years about walls and drains and the price of corn, sat down again. Slowly. And did not finish what he had been going to say. And put his big hands flat on the table in front of him, and looked at them.
The Archon turned back.
"Thank you, Ashcombe," he said. "I knew you would see it."
He looked down the table once more. At all of them.
"We will not call it what it is," said the Archon. "There is no need. People do not like hard words. They frighten easily, and it is bad for trade." He folded his gloved hands again, one over the other, on the polished wood. "We will call it a Cleansing."
He smiled again.
"People like a clean house," said the Archon.
Elara wrote it.
Word for word. In her small neat hand. In the great black ledger of clause eleven, under the report from the north, the way the Fourth Desk had written every word said in that room for longer than anybody could remember.
We will call it a Cleansing. People like a clean house.
She blotted it.
And she looked at it on the page. Black. Clear. True. Every word of it exactly what had been said.
And she thought of a round boy's voice, out of the dark, before the ninth of the Thaw month. Only one, Ellie. They have to last.
And of a scribe she did not know, somewhere in the city, writing a letter in big leaning capitals for a girl who could not write anymore.
And of an old woman with blue hands and a needle.
And of a man on his knees under a dome, holding it up, with the light round him like a pillar.
Gone on over the far side.
She did not believe it.
She did not know why. There was no reason. It was in the report, and the report was the record, and the record was never wrong.
She did not believe it.
"That will be all," said the Archon. "Thank you, Auditor. The Fourth Desk may go."
Elara shut the great black ledger, and lifted it off the reading stand in both arms, and held it against her chest. It was very heavy. Heavier than it had been when she came in.
She turned.
And as she turned, she saw, without meaning to, without wanting to, the Archon's hands. Folded on the table. One over the other. In their fine close gloves.
Pale gray.
She did not stop. She did not let her face change. She walked down the long room toward the doors, with the ledger in her arms and Gage a step behind her and a little to the left, and the snow going past the tall white windows, and the painted figures looking down through their broken ice. And she did not look back.
But her hands, inside her white gloves, round the black ledger, had gone so cold that she could not feel them at all.
The Great Stair
The Treasurer caught her on the great stair.
She had known he would. She had heard his feet behind her as soon as the Council chamber doors closed, light and quick on the marble of the gallery, the feet of a man who has never had to hurry in his life and does it only for the pleasure of arriving first. She did not stop. She went on down the great stair, slowly, because of the weight of the ledger in her arms, with Gage a step behind her and a little to the left.
And Dorian Sterling came down beside her, on her right, and matched her step, and smiled.
"Auditor," he said.
"Treasurer."
"A long afternoon."
"Yes."
The great stair of the Bank went down in a long white curve from the first floor to the front hall, wide enough for six people side by side, with a rail of polished brass and a carpet of dark blue held to each step with a brass rod. Halfway down there was a landing, with a tall window over the square. The snow was going past it, thick and white. And the light that came in through it lay on the white marble and the blue carpet and made everything look clean.
Sterling stopped on the landing.
Elara stopped too. She did not want to. But he had stopped, and he was the Treasurer, and the Fourth Desk did not walk on when the Treasurer stopped.
He looked out of the window at the snow.
He was a handsome man. Elara had heard people say so, and supposed it was true. Not young, not old. Silver hair, brushed back smooth from a high forehead, gone silver before he was forty, they said, and he had liked it, and kept it long. A long fine face. Gray eyes, very clear. A gray coat cut so well that it did not look like a coat at all, but like the shape of the man inside it. And in his buttonhole, the white rose. Always a white rose. Even in this, the dead middle of winter, with snow on the river. Elara did not know where he got them. She had wondered, once, and then decided she did not want to know.
"The Thorne estate," said Sterling, looking at the snow. "You went up yesterday."
"Yes."
"And?"
"The audit is begun," said Elara. "The papers of the estate are held by the heir. They were not available to the Bank."
She said it exactly as she had written it. Word for word.
Sterling turned his head and looked at her.
"Held by the heir," he said.
"Yes."
"The boy."
"He is the heir."
"He is sixteen, Auditor." Sterling smiled. Pleasantly. "He is a cadet at the Long Office. He copies out lists for a living."
"He is the heir under the law," said Elara. "And under the law an heir may hold the papers of an estate until they are asked for in writing, under the Treasurer's seal." She paused. "They were not asked for in writing."
Sterling looked at her for a long moment.
The smile did not go. It stayed exactly where it was. But something behind it moved. Elara saw it. The way you see a curtain move at a window when somebody is standing behind it. Something looked out at her, and let the curtain fall again.
"No," said Sterling slowly. "No, they were not. I asked you to go up kindly. And you went up kindly."
"Yes."
"And you wrote it down."
"I write everything down."
"Yes," said Dorian Sterling. "Yes, you do." He turned back to the window. "The record is never wrong. Isn't that so, Auditor? I told you that. A long time ago."
"You did."
"Do you still believe it?"
Elara did not answer at once.
She stood on the landing of the great stair with the black ledger of clause eleven in her arms, heavy against her chest, with the Cleansing written in it in her own hand, and a report from the north with a fire in it that could not have been. And she thought about the question. She thought about it plainly, the way she thought about everything.
"I believe the record says what was written in it," she said.
Sterling laughed.
It was a real laugh. Short and light and surprised, the laugh of a man who has been told a good joke at a dinner. It went up the great stair and came back down off the painted ceiling, and a porter crossing the front hall below looked up.
"Oh, very good," said Sterling. "Very good, Auditor. I shall remember that." He touched the white rose in his buttonhole, lightly, with one gloved finger, the way he did when he was pleased. "You have been learning."
Elara did not say anything.
"From whom, I wonder," said Sterling. He looked at her sideways. "Not from me. I never taught you that."
She did not say anything.
He looked at her a moment longer. And then past her. At Gage. Standing on the step above the landing, a step back and a little to the left, with his slate on its cord and his wooden face.
Sterling looked at Gage for a long time.
Gage looked back.
"The Thursday lists will be long, now," said Sterling at last. Not to Gage. To her. Lightly. "Very long. Every week. Scribes and needlewomen and back-street harvesters by the cartload. The Fourth Desk will be busy." He smiled again. "I do hope you will tick them all, Auditor. Every name. Carefully. The way you always do."
"I tick every name," said Elara.
"I know you do."
He went on down the stair.
Light and quick, on the blue carpet, past the brass rods. Without looking back. At the bottom he stopped to say something to the porter, who bowed. And then he crossed the black and white floor of the front hall and went out through the great doors into the snow, and the doors swung shut behind him, and he was gone.
Elara stood on the landing.
She stood there a long time, with the ledger in her arms and the snow going past the tall window, and Gage behind her.
And then she went on down, slowly, step by step. Back to the Fourth Desk. To the river windows and the black cupboards and Mister Harrow's small gray whiskers, and her own desk with the black book on its corner, and the long dark evening, and the snow.
He Knows Now
It was night when she sat down at her desk again.
She had not meant to stay so late. There had been nothing to stay for. The great black ledger of clause eleven was back in its place, in cupboard four, locked. The Thursday lists were in nine. The green audit book for the Thorne estate was in her drawer, with its one true line in it. Mister Harrow had gone home at the sixth bell, with his umbrella under his arm, and said good night, and stopped at the way in by Gage's bench, and looked back at her, and not said anything else. Penn had gone before him. Remy was in eleven, behind the Charter box, with three new candles she had brought him, and bread, and the news. She had told him all of it. He had listened without a word, and when she finished he had opened his black book, and turned to a clean page, and written at the top of it, in his small tidy hand: THE CLEANSING. And under that, nothing. Yet.
And she had come back to her desk, and sat down, and not got up again.
The room was dark except for her lamp.
Outside, the snow was still coming down. It had come down all day, since before dawn, steadily, without any wind, and it was still coming. She could see it in the light from her lamp, going past the black glass of the tall windows, big and slow and soft, one flake and then another and then another. And past the snow, the river. Black. Quiet. With the snow falling on it and vanishing, falling and vanishing, the way it does on water. And along the quay, the Ash lamps, blue, each one with its own small cold ring of light, and the snow going down through the rings and coming out the other side and going on down into the dark.
It was very quiet. Snow made everything quiet. She could hear her own breath. She could hear the lamp, a small faint hiss. She could hear nothing else at all.
She sat with her hands flat on the green leather of the desk, one on each side of the black book, and looked out at the snow on the river.
And thought.
She thought about the report. The harvester spent, and gone on over the far side. She thought about a man on his knees under a dome. She thought about a lamp boy with a limp on the great stair of the Bourse, and Gage's big hand on his head, lifting him over a step. Mind your leg, grandfather. She thought about a line in her own book of days that she had read so many times she could have said it in her sleep. I passed my brother on a stair tonight and did not stop him.
Gone on over the far side.
She did not believe it.
She thought about the fire that could not have been, on a plain of glass where there was nothing to burn. And a tired man in a gray coat who wrote reports in which every word was true but one.
She thought about a cream card with a plain circle pressed into red wax. And a blank card, open, on the floor by a woman's feet. And a line in purple ink. The girl. Done.
She thought about the Archon's hands.
She made herself stop thinking about that.
And she thought about the Cleansing. Every scribe not on the list. Every Ink-Binder. Every harvester not of the Ministry. To be found, and brought in. Gently. And their names coming up to the Fourth Desk on the Thursday lists. Every week. In the Ministry's gray leather case. For her to read standing, and then sitting, with a pencil, and tick, one by one, and file in nine.
She would be the one who ticked them.
She sat very still.
And behind her, by the way in, she heard the chalk.
Small. Dry. A little squeak on the slate.
She did not turn round at once. She sat and listened to it. Slow. Careful. Pressing hard. Big square letters. Not many of them. Then it stopped.
And she heard Gage get up off his bench.
His feet on the boards. Heavy. Slow. Coming down the long dark room toward her, past Penn's desk and Mister Harrow's, out of the dark and into the edge of the lamplight. She heard him stop beside her. She felt him there, big and quiet, the way she always felt him. Like a wall at her back.
She turned her head.
He was holding up the slate.
Not to the lamp. To the window. The way he had held it up last night. Turned to the black glass, so that the cold light from the blue Ash lamps on the quay came in through the falling snow and lay on it, pale, and she could read it.
There were three words on it.
He knows now.
Elara read it.
She read it again.
Then she looked up at Gage. At his wooden face, in the blue light from the window and the yellow light from the lamp, half one and half the other. At his small pale eyes.
"Who knows what?" she said.
Gage did not answer.
He did not write anything else. He did not rub out what he had written. He held the slate up a moment longer, to the window, with the three words on it in white chalk and the snow going past behind it. And then he let it down, and it hung on its cord against his chest, with the words still on it, facing out.
And he looked at her.
Only looked. For a long time. Standing beside her desk in the dark Fourth Desk, with the snow on the river behind him and the blue lamps along the quay.
Elara looked back at him.
She did not ask again. She did not need to. She found that she did not want to. She found that she was afraid of the answer, whichever answer it was. That he might be the man at the head of the long table, with his pale gloves folded, who had looked at her for a long time when she wrote the line about the fire. Or a tired man in a gray coat, somewhere on the Glass, walking south. Or a harvester with a limp, on his knees under a dome, who had gone on over the far side, and had not gone at all.
Or all three.
She did not know which she was most afraid of.
Gage did not wipe the slate.
He went back to his bench. Slowly. Through the dark. And sat down on it, the way he always sat, with his big hands on his knees and his back against the wall. And the slate hung on its cord against his chest, with the three words on it, facing the room.
He knows now.
Elara turned back to the window.
She sat with her hands flat on her desk, one on each side of the black book with its number in it, 2,411, and looked out at the snow coming down on the river, and the blue lamps, and the dark, and did not move for a long time.
And the snow went on falling. On the river. On the quays. On the roofs of the old town, and the bridges, and the white barriers on Lantern Bridge, and the black needle of the Spire. On the Hill, and the cream house with every curtain drawn, and a calm woman by a window among dead lilies. On Saint Bride's, and the crypt under it. On the road going north out of the city, past the last houses, past the fens and the farms and the last thin trees, toward a clean white line a long way off where the snow stopped and the Glass began.
And on a small group of people, very far away, walking south through it.
To the city.
Home.
