Chapter 90: The Way Back, Part 4 of 5: The Cut, Every Morning
The Cut, Every Morning
Lyra did the cut every morning.
She had promised Oren, on the Eye, and she kept it. Every dawn, when Kestrel called the halt and the Ashborn began to stretch the canvas over the spears, before anybody lay down, Lyra walked a little way off on the gray glass, by herself, into the first light. And set her feet. And did it.
With her hands empty. Because of the bound one. Three days, Vespera had said. Not a cup, not a sword. So she held nothing. She brought her two empty hands down low behind her left heel, the left under the right, around a grip that was not there, and stood in the lie. Tired. Giving up. And then went in. Slowly. The way Oren had shown her, under the iron horse, in a time he could not remember. The back foot driving. The hip turning. The body going round like a gate. The empty hands coming up and round in the long flat curve, and stopping, high, where the stroke ends.
And holding there.
And Oren watched.
He sat on his pack at the edge of the canvas with his sword across his knees, every morning, and watched her do it. He did not say anything. He did not move. He sat with his big hands on the flat of the blade and his face very still, and watched the thin girl in the too-big coat turn round on the gray glass in the dawn like a scythe going through long grass.
Corin watched him watch.
He could not help it. He lay under the canvas with his head on his pack and his eyes half shut, and he watched Oren's face. Every morning. And every morning it was the same. Interested. Kind. A little puzzled. The face of a man watching something beautiful that he does not understand.
On the first morning, nothing.
On the second morning, nothing.
And on the third morning, the last morning before the Edge, with the sky going white in the east and the snow line not far off, Lyra set her feet on the gray glass and brought her empty hands down behind her heel and went in. And she was tired. They were all tired. Three nights of walking, and no sleep to speak of, and the heat, and the hum in their teeth. And her back foot, when she drove off it, slid.
Just a little. A finger's width. On the smooth glass.
And she went round anyway, and her hands came up, and the stroke was there, mostly. But a little short. A little wrong at the top.
And Oren said: "Back foot."
Lyra stopped.
She stopped dead, at the top of the stroke, with her empty hands up by her shoulder. And did not turn round.
Corin had opened his eyes all the way.
Oren was sitting on his pack with his sword across his knees, and his face had changed. Not much. But it had. The puzzled look had gone out of it. And something else had come in. Something Corin had not seen in that face since the noon on the Eye. A sharpness. A frown, not of not knowing, but of knowing exactly, and not liking what he saw.
"Your back foot," said Oren. "It went."
He sounded surprised. As if somebody else had said it, using his mouth.
Lyra turned round. Slowly. And looked at him across the gray glass.
"It slid," she said. "On the glass."
"Aye." Oren was frowning at her feet. "Aye, it slid. You didn't set it. You've to set it. Before you drive. Into the ground, like a post." He stopped. He looked down at his own hands on the blade. Then up at her again. "Like a post, lass. Or it all goes."
Nobody said anything.
The Ashborn had stopped stretching the canvas. Kestrel was standing with a spear in each hand, not moving. Oda had her mouth a little open.
"How do you know that?" said Lyra.
Very quietly.
Oren looked at her a long time.
"I don't know," he said at last. "I don't know, lass. I can't do it. I couldn't show you. If I stood up and tried I'd look like a scarecrow, same as on the stair." He was quiet. "But I saw it go. Your foot. I saw it go, and something in me said No. Like a dog barking at a step it knows."
Lyra stood on the glass.
And then, without a word, she turned back round. And set her feet. Slowly. Carefully. The back one first, pressing it down into the smooth gray glass as if she could drive it in, like a post. And brought her empty hands down behind her heel. And stood in the lie.
And went in.
And this time the back foot held. Corin saw it hold. And the hip turned, and the body came round like a gate from the ground up, and the empty hands went up and round in the long flat curve and stopped at the top, high, exactly where the stroke ends.
And held.
Nobody breathed.
Then Oren nodded.
Once. Slowly. The way a man nods when a thing has been done right and there is nothing more to say about it.
"Aye," said Oren. "That's it."
And he looked down at the sword on his knees, at the bright edge he had sharpened on the stair, and turned it over, and his face went back to puzzled again, and kind, and a little lost.
But Corin had seen it.
And so had Lyra. She came back across the glass to the canvas, with her bound hand held against her chest, and her face very white, and she did not say anything at all. She lay down under the canvas beside Corin, a hand's width away, and turned her face to the gray cloth over them, and Corin heard her breathing go short and quick, and then slow, and then very slow.
And under her breath, so low he almost did not hear it, she said: "Every morning."
"Every morning," said Corin.
The Edge
They came to the Edge on the morning after that, three nights out from the Eye, in a white dawn.
Corin saw it from a long way off. A line. Thin and white and straight, lying across the whole of the world in front of them from one side to the other, the way a line of chalk lies across a slate. At first he thought it was the sky. The sky low down, at the bottom, where it met the gray of the Glass. Then the light came up, and he saw that it was not the sky.
It was snow.
The Glass ended. Just like that. Clean. As if somebody had laid a rule across the land and cut along it with a knife. On this side, gray glass, flat and hard and humming, warm under the boots even at dawn. On that side, white. Snow. Lying deep and soft and blue in the hollows, on the dead bracken and the stones and the low hummocks of the good land, going away south under a sky the color of milk.
And the line between them so sharp that you could have stood with one boot on each side of it.
They stopped a little way back from it. All of them. Without anybody saying to.
And stood, and looked.
Corin could smell it. That was the first thing. After all the days on the Glass, where there was no smell at all, nothing, not even dust. The smell came across the line to him on a thin cold wind. Wet earth. Rot. Snow. Old leaves under the snow. Something green, very faint, a long way down. The smell of a world where things grew, and died, and grew again.
He had forgotten it.
He stood on the warm glass with his crutch and breathed it in, and his eyes stung, and he did not know if it was the cold.
And beside him, the footprints stopped.
He looked down. The small crooked ones, and the long ones with the holes beside them. They went on to a place a few paces from the line. And there they stopped. Both lines. Side by side. At a place on the glass where the prints went deeper than anywhere else, and the edges of them had run a little, as if two people had stood there, once, for a long time, without moving, looking at something on the other side.
Corin did not go and stand there.
He looked at it from where he was. And then he looked past it. Over the line. Into the snow. To where, a few paces on, on the good land, there was a flat stone.
Just a stone. Gray. Flat on the top. Half buried in the snow.
There was no candle on it.
He looked at it a long time.
And then Oren's big hand came down on his shoulder. Through his coat. Heavy and warm.
"Come on, lad," said Oren. "It's only a stone."
And Corin let himself be turned away.
They crossed the line one at a time.
Kestrel first. She did it the Ashborn way. She stopped on the very edge of the glass, with the snow a hand's width in front of her boots, and she bent, and laid her palm flat on the gray glass, once. The way you lay a hand on the neck of a horse that has carried you a long way. And then she straightened, and stepped over, onto the snow. It squeaked under her boot. She stood on the white with her spear, and turned, and looked back at them.
Then Oda. Then Sef. Each one bending, and touching the glass, and stepping over.
Then Oren. He did not bend. His back would not let him. He looked down at the line a moment. Then he stepped over it, big and slow, onto the snow, and his boot went in to the ankle, and he stood there, and said, "Saints, that's cold," and laughed.
Then Lyra.
She stopped at the edge and looked back at Corin. A hand's width away. And then she stepped over too, onto the white, and turned round, and waited.
And then Corin.
He stood on the last of the gray glass and looked down at the line.
He did not touch the glass. He did not bend. He did not know if he could get up again. He only stood there, on the warm humming glass, with the snow a hand's width from the end of his crutch, and looked down.
And then he stepped over.
The hum stopped.
Not slowly. All at once. The way it had stopped at the Eye. As if somebody had laid a hand flat on a drum. One moment it was in his teeth, and his bones, and the back of his head, the low steady sound he had lived inside for days and days. And then it was not. And the quiet came in where it had been, a different quiet from the Eye. Not the big empty quiet of black glass. A small quiet. A soft one. Full of little sounds. The wind in the dead bracken. A bird, somewhere, very far off. The snow settling under his boot.
He stood in the snow.
It was cold. It came up through his boots at once, and through the bottom of his crutch, and into his lame leg, and made it ache. He did not care.
He turned round.
And the silent man was standing on the other side of the line.
On the glass. On the gray. A few paces back from the edge, where the footprints stopped. Tall. Wide in the shoulders. With his long arms hanging and his hands wrapped in the brown-stained strips of cloth. And Tansy beside him, holding his sleeve. Her eyes open. Bright. Looking at all of them across the line.
He did not come over.
Corin had known he would not. Tansy had said. He'll walk you to the Edge. Where the snow starts. And here was the Edge. Here was where the snow started. And here was where he stopped.
The man stood on the glass and looked at them.
Not south. That was the strange thing. Every night on the Eye, every morning on the shore, he had sat with his back to everything and looked south. Toward this. Toward the Edge and the snow and the good land. As if he were waiting for something to come up over it.
Now he was here. And he was not looking south.
He was looking at Corin.
Corin looked back at him.
At the long hard face, older than it should be. The gray stubble. The pale gray eyes, the color of the sky before rain. Looking at him across the line, and the snow, and the few paces of the world between them.
And something moved in the man's face.
Corin saw it. Far back. Very far. Behind the eyes. The way a fish moves, deep down, in dark water, so that you only see the water stir. Something came up, a long way, toward the surface. And did not reach it. And went down again.
And the man lifted his hand.
His right hand. The wrapped one. Slowly. Up from his side. Up. Past his chest. Past his shoulder. Until it was up by his face, open, with the brown-stained cloth hanging from it.
And he waved.
Once.
Not the way a grown man waves. Not a lifted hand, held still. He moved it. From side to side. Quickly. Twice. The way a boy waves. The way a boy waves from the back of a cart, going away down a road, to somebody standing at a gate. Without thinking. With his whole arm.
And then the hand came down again, and hung at his side, and the face was the same face it had always been. Nobody's face. Still.
Corin stood in the snow.
He did not know why his throat had shut. He did not know why his eyes had gone hot, and then wet, and then run. He did not know anything. He only knew that he had seen a wave like that before. Somewhere. A long time ago. From the back of something going away.
He lifted his own hand.
His living one. The right one. Up. Past his chest. Past his shoulder. Until it was up by his face.
And he waved back.
The same way. Side to side. Twice. Like a boy.
The man watched him do it.
And then he turned round. Slowly. On the gray glass. And Tansy turned with him, still holding his sleeve. And the two of them began to walk away. North. Back up the long gray line of the footprints, the small ones and the long ones, toward the Ridges and the Eye and the dome, where somebody had to be, every month, to knock.
Tansy looked back once.
She let go of his sleeve, just for a moment, and turned round, and lifted both her arms over her head, and waved them, wildly, the way a child waves at a ship. And shouted something. The wind took it. Corin did not hear what it was.
Then she took hold of the sleeve again, and they went on.
They got smaller and smaller on the gray. Two shapes. One tall, one small. Going north.
Until they were only marks.
Until they were gone.
And Corin stood in the snow on the good side of the Edge, with Lyra a hand's width from him and Oren's hand on his shoulder and the Ashborn waiting, and looked at the place where they had been, for a long time after there was nothing there to see.
At last Kestrel touched his arm. Through his sleeve. Lightly.
"South," she said.
"Yes," said Corin.
He turned his back on the Glass.
It was harder than he had thought. He had walked across it, all those nights, wanting nothing more than to be off it. And now he was off it, and the snow was under his boots, and the good land was in front of him, white and soft and silent, going away to where the city was. And he found that his body did not want to turn. That some part of him wanted to stay at the line, and look north, the way the silent man had looked south, every night, waiting for something to come over the edge of the world.
He turned anyway.
And Lyra turned with him, a hand's width away. And Oren, with his sword on his back, and his flat-cushioned seat by a fire waiting for him somewhere at the end of it. And Kestrel and Oda and Sef, with their spears.
And they walked into the snow.
The Council Chamber
On Friday, at the second bell after noon, the Fourth Desk went down to the Council.
Elara went in her green coat, with clean white gloves, and the black record book under her arm. The Bank's record. The great ledger of clause eleven, bound in black leather with brass corners, so heavy that she had to hold it in both arms against her chest. Gage walked a step behind her and a little to the left. Mister Harrow did not come. Mister Harrow had never been called to the Council in twenty-two years, he said, and did not mean to start now. He had stood at the way in of the Fourth Desk as they left, with his umbrella under his arm, and watched them go down the corridor, and said nothing at all, which was not like him.
The Council chamber was on the first floor of the Bank, at the front, over the great doors.
Elara had been in it four times before. She remembered three of them. It was a long room, and high, higher than it was wide, with a ceiling painted a long time ago with clouds and gold sunlight and figures in robes, faded now and cracked, so that the figures looked as if they were looking down through broken ice. There were tall windows down one side, looking out over the square in front of the Bank and the river beyond it. Today they were white. The snow had come in the night, and was still coming, thick and slow, and the windows were full of it, falling past the glass, so that the light in the room was white and soft and cold and came from everywhere at once.
Down the middle of the room ran the table.
It was long. Longer than Sola's, though Elara did not know that. Dark wood, polished so deep that you could see the painted ceiling in it, upside down. And round it, in high-backed seats of dark red leather, the Council.
They were all in gray. That was the rule, in the chamber. Not the Ministry's gray. A softer gray, finer, the gray of good wool and silk, the gray of old families. Gray coats. Gray gowns. Gray gloves on the hands folded on the table. Twenty of them, perhaps. Elara did not count. Men, mostly, and a few women. Old, mostly. With white hair, and gray hair, and no hair. Faces she knew from the Bank's books. Pellow. Fennimore. Langley. The Duke of Ashcombe, red-faced, at the far end, scowling at nothing.
And one seat empty.
Halfway down the table, on the left side. A high-backed red seat, like the others. Pushed in. With nobody in it. And tied round the top of its back, in a bow, a broad black ribbon, with its long ends hanging down.
Cressida Thorne's.
Elara looked at it as she came in. She could not help it. She looked at the black ribbon and thought of a calm gray face turned to a brown window, among dead lilies. And of a line in purple ink. The girl. Done.
She looked away.
The Treasurer was there. Dorian Sterling. On the right side, near the head, with his silver hair brushed back and a white rose in his buttonhole, the way he always wore one, even in winter. He looked up as she came in, and smiled at her, a little, the smile he used for her in the corridors. Kindly. She did not smile back. She had never learned how to smile back at him.
And at the head of the table, the Archon.
Elara had seen him three times. That she remembered. He did not come to the Bank often. He did not need to. The Bank came to him. He sat at the head of the long table in a seat no different from the others, dark red leather, high-backed, and he did not look different from the others, either, at first. An old man. Not as old as some. Thin. Upright. With white hair cut close, and a long pale face, and pale eyes. Dressed in gray like all of them, plain, without any mark of rank at all. His hands were folded on the table in front of him. Gloved.
Elara did not look at the gloves.
She did not know why she did not. She only knew, coming down the long room toward the place where the Fourth Desk stood to witness, at the foot of the table, that she did not want to look at his hands. So she looked at his face instead. And his face was looking at her. Calm. Interested. Kind, almost. The way a man looks at a clock he wound himself, to see that it is keeping good time.
She stopped at the foot of the table.
She laid the great black ledger on the reading stand that stood there for it, and opened it, and found the page, and took out her pen. Gage stood behind her, a step back and a little to the left, with his slate on its cord.
"The Auditor," said the Archon.
His voice was ordinary. That was the first thing she noticed. She had expected, she did not know what. Something. A voice that filled the room. It did not. It was a dry, pleasant, ordinary old man's voice. The voice of somebody's grandfather asking for the salt.
"The Fourth Desk witnesses," said Elara. "Under clause eleven."
"Thank you, Auditor." The Archon inclined his head to her, very slightly. "We are grateful for the Bank's attendance. In the snow."
He turned his head, a little, to his left.
There was a man standing there. Behind the Archon's seat. A little to one side. Elara had not seen him at first, because he stood so still. Tall, and thin, and young, or not old, in plain gray, with smooth dark hair and a smooth pale face that showed nothing at all. Lucian. The Archon's secretary. The man who had come up the stairs to the Fourth Desk yesterday with the summons and stood by Gage's bench and looked at Gage for a long time.
He had a paper in his hand.
Small. Very small. A strip, no wider than a finger, no longer than a hand, rolled tight, and now unrolled, and held flat between his two gloved thumbs. The kind of paper that is tied to the leg of a bird.
"Read it, Lucian," said the Archon.
And Lucian read it.
He read it in a clear, flat voice, without any feeling in it at all. The voice of a clerk reading out a list.
"From the Glass. By the Spire's birds. To the Archon's hand."
A pause.
"Ferrand fallen on the bunker stair."
Elara's pen moved on the page. She wrote it. Word for word. That was what the Fourth Desk did. It wrote what was said.
"The harvester spent, and gone on over the far side."
Her pen stopped.
Just for a breath. She felt it stop. She felt her fingers, inside the white glove, go still on the pen, the way they had gone still on the open page of Cressida Thorne's day book. Cold.
The harvester spent.
Gone on over the far side.
She made her fingers move again. She wrote it. Word for word. Her hand was steady. She made it be steady. The ink went down on the page in her small neat hand, black, clear.
Behind her, she heard Gage breathe. Once. Long and slow.
"The journal destroyed in the fire," read Lucian.
A pause.
"S. K."
And he lowered the strip of paper, and held it flat between his gloved thumbs, and looked at nothing.
The room was very quiet.
The snow went past the tall windows, thick and slow and white. The painted figures looked down through their broken ice. Somebody at the table coughed. Nobody spoke.
Elara wrote the last line.
The journal destroyed in the fire. S. K.
And looked at it.
And something in her, something small and careful and cold, that had been an auditor for six years and had read a great many reports, put its finger on the line, the way she put her finger on a figure in a ledger that did not add up.
The fire.
What fire?
She did not know anything about the Glass. Nobody in this room did. Nobody had ever been there and come back to tell it, except a few. But she knew what everybody knew. That it was glass. Flat, gray, hard. For days and days. That nothing grew on it. That there was nothing on it to burn.
What fire?
She did not ask. It was not the Fourth Desk's place to ask. The Fourth Desk witnessed. It wrote down what was said, word for word, and the record stood.
She wrote it down. Word for word.
And she thought of a tired man in a gray coat, with one glove off, standing at the bottom of the back stair, reading her one true line in Tom Briggs's book, and saying Good.
And she thought: Every word of it will be true. That is what he does. Every word of it will be true.
Except the one that isn't.
And she knew, as surely as she knew her own name, which was more surely than she knew most things, that the one that was not true had been put there on purpose. Like a thread left hanging from a hem. For somebody to find.
She did not look up.
She kept her eyes on the page, and her pen on the paper, and her face still. And she felt the Archon look at her, from the far end of the long table, for a long time.
