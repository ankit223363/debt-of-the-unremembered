Chapter 90: The Way Back, Part 3 of 5: The Knock
The Knock
They were all at the top when he came up. Standing in a loose ring on the black glass outside the way in, under the stars, with their packs on and their water skins full and the cold of the night already coming down. Kestrel. Oda and Sef. Oren, with his sword across his back. Marrit, with the child bound to her back in a sling of hide, asleep. And a little way off, on the glass, the silent man, and Tansy.
The moon was up. Small. White. Low over the far shore. It made the black glass of the Eye shine like water under ice, and laid a long pale road across it from the shore to the dome.
Marrit went first.
She did not say much. The Ashborn did not. She went round the ring, one by one, and gripped each of them by the forearm, the Ashborn way, hard, the hand closing round the arm below the elbow. Kestrel. Oda. Sef. Oren, who did not know the grip and took her arm the soldier's way instead, and she let him. Lyra, who gave her left arm, because of the bound hand.
And then Corin.
Marrit gripped his living arm. Hard. Her face in the moonlight was like stone, the way it always was. And then, very quickly, so quickly that he hardly saw it, she let go of his arm and put her hand flat on his chest. Over the place where the Ledger was, inside his coat.
"Read it," she said. "Every morning. The old one said."
"Yes."
"And come back. To the Salt Wells. When it is done. The child will want to see the one who held the roof."
Corin looked at the child, asleep on her back, its round face turned sideways on her shoulder, its mouth open.
"I will," he said.
She took her hand away. And turned, and walked off east, across the black glass of the Eye, along the edge of the moon's pale road, toward the shore. Alone. With the child on her back. Not hurrying. Not looking back. Corin watched her go until she was a small dark shape at the edge of the glass, and then a smaller one on the gray stones of the beach, and then nothing.
Nobody said anything for a while.
And then the silent man moved.
Corin did not see him start. Nobody ever did. One moment he was standing a little way off on the glass with Tansy, and the next he was walking. Past them all. Past the ring. Toward the dome. Toward the way in, the black square of it, at the front of the great curved wall, where the rust ran down in long red tears.
Tansy let go of his sleeve. She stood where she was and watched him go. Her eyes were open.
The man went up to the way in.
The iron thing stood open. It had stood open since the siege. A great slab of black iron, as thick as a man's hand, hanging on hinges as big as a man's head, carved all over with the four hand-calls round a sun. It had opened for Oren's blood, and Oren had held it shut with his back and his last word for it, and then it had stood open, and nobody had shut it since.
The man put out his wrapped hand and took hold of the edge of it.
And pulled.
Corin heard it move. A long low groan of old iron on old iron, going on and on, slow, the way the dome itself had groaned at the Bourse. The great slab swung, inch by inch, on its huge hinges. Out of the open. Across the black square of the way in. Until it met the stone of the frame, and stopped, with a deep soft boom that went down into the bunker below and came back up, a long time later, from the bottom of the iron stair.
Shut.
The man stood in front of it.
And lifted his right hand. The wrapped one. And made a fist.
And knocked.
Four times. Hard. The sound rang out across the black glass in the moonlight, iron under bone, and went away over the Eye.
Then two.
Then nine. Slower. Each one on its own.
Then three.
And stopped.
Witness. Hold. Fold. Home.
Corin heard Lyra take a breath beside him. He knew what she was thinking. Her father's taps. The ones he used to tap on the table, on the arm of his seat, on the back of her hand. Four, two, nine, three. She had known them all her life without knowing what they were.
The man stood in front of the shut iron with his fist still raised, and waited.
Nothing answered.
Nothing ever did. Tansy had said so. Every month he knocked, and every month nobody answered, and he went back to the shore and sat down facing south. Corin did not know who he was knocking for. He did not think the man knew either.
And then, behind Corin, somebody knocked back.
Not on iron. On something softer. Corin turned round.
It was Oren.
He was standing in the ring with his sword on his back and his big hand raised, and he was knocking with his knuckles on the flat of the hide strap across his own chest, over his heart. Soft. Muffled. But clear, in the quiet of the Eye.
A short pattern. Quick, and then slow, and then quick again. The kind of pattern a man taps without thinking, the way he whistles a tune he learned as a boy.
Oren looked surprised. He looked down at his own hand as if it belonged to somebody else. As if it had done the thing by itself, the way his hands had found the whetstone on the stair.
"What's that?" said Lyra. Very quietly.
"I don't know," said Oren slowly. "I don't know, lass. My hand knows it." He frowned at it. "It's an answer. I think. A soldier's answer. To the calls." He was quiet a moment. "I think it means heard."
At the way in, the silent man had turned round.
He was looking at Oren. Across the black glass. In the moonlight. With his fist still half raised.
And slowly, he let it down.
He stood there a long moment, looking. Then he came back across the glass, the way he had gone, without a sound, and stopped by Tansy, and she took hold of his sleeve again.
And he did not turn his face to the south.
Corin saw that. For the first time since they had found him, the man did not turn to the south when he stopped. He stood beside Tansy on the black glass of the Eye, in the moonlight, and he kept his face turned toward Oren. Toward all of them.
"Well," said Kestrel, after a long time. "That is something."
Nobody asked her what.
And they turned, all of them, and walked down off the black glass of the Eye along the moon's pale road, toward the shore and the gray ringed stones and the cairn, and the Glass beyond it, and the long way south.
The Footprints
They walked south for three nights.
The long way. Not the short way through the Ridges that Vespera and Brin had taken. The open way, across the flat gray Glass, under the stars, with the hum coming back into their teeth as soon as they stepped off the shore of the Eye and felt it there again, low, under everything, the way it had been for all the days before.
Corin had forgotten the hum. A day and two nights in the Eye, where there was none, and he had forgotten it. And when it came back, on the first step off the gray stones of the beach onto the gray glass, it came into him so suddenly that he stopped and nearly fell, and Oren caught his arm.
"Lad?"
"The hum," said Corin.
"Aye," said Oren. "It's back. I'd missed it, nearly." He let go of Corin's arm. "Strange thing, to miss."
They walked.
Kestrel in front, with her spear. Then Oda and Sef. Then Corin and Lyra, side by side, a hand's width apart. Then Oren, with his sword on his back. And a little way behind, always a little way behind, the silent man, with Tansy holding his sleeve.
By night they walked. By day they lay under the canvas, stretched over two spears, in the heat, and tried to sleep, and mostly did not. The night-walking rule. It did not matter now that there were no iron men behind them. It mattered because of the sun. The Glass by day would kill you. The Glass by night would only make you very tired.
The stars went round overhead. The great pale smear of them, the spilt flour, swung across the sky. There was no moon after the first night. Only the stars, and the glass, and the hum.
And on the second night, near the end of it, with the sky beginning to go gray in the east, they came near the Edge.
Corin knew it before he saw it. He felt it under his boots. The glass was changing. It had been hard all the way from the Eye, hard and smooth and flat, like the top of a table. Now it was beginning to give, a little. Not much. You would not have noticed it unless you were looking for it. But it was warmer, here, under his feet, even at night. And a little softer. The way a road is softer when you get near the sea, and the sand begins to come up through the stones.
And Kestrel stopped.
She stopped dead in front of them, in the gray light, with her spear held out sideways to stop the others, and stood looking down at the glass at her feet.
Corin came up beside her.
And looked down.
Footprints.
In the glass. Sunk into it. A finger deep, no more. Hardened. Shiny at the edges, where the glass had gone smooth and set round them, long ago. Two lines of them, going south. Toward the Edge.
One line was long. A man's boots. Long flat prints, deep at the heel, and beside every second one a small round hole, where something had gone down into the soft glass and come up again.
A stick.
And beside them, the other line.
Small.
Very small. Close together. Short steps. A child's. In boots that were too big, so that the prints were long for the steps, and a little crooked, as if the boots had slid about on the feet inside them. Going south. Beside the man's. Not behind. Beside. As if the two of them had been walking together, side by side, all the way.
Kestrel looked at them a long time.
"The Ashborn have seen these," she said at last. "Long ago. Some of the old ones. They thought it was a story. A man and a child, on the Glass, going north, and then coming back." She looked at Corin. "There are others. Going the other way. Further east. Nobody has found them in a long time."
Corin did not say anything.
He was looking at the small prints. At the first one, by Kestrel's foot. Small and crooked and long, with the edges gone smooth and shiny and set.
He knew whose they were.
He knew it the way he had known the crooked C on the drawing in the iron box. Before he could think it. In his hands and his feet and the back of his neck.
He had watched them being made.
In the crease. In the old man's memory. Walking back toward the Edge, because the boy would not stay. The boy ahead, and the old man behind with his stick. And he had watched them sink into the warm soft glass, and the glass keep them.
The glass kept everything.
"Corin," said Lyra.
He did not answer.
He stepped forward. Past Kestrel. Past her spear. And stood on the glass beside the small prints. Not on them. Beside them. Where the old man's had been. And looked down at them, in the gray light, the small crooked prints going away south in a long line toward the Edge.
And he began to walk.
Beside them. Slowly. On his crutch. Step, and the crutch, and step. He put his own boots down on the glass next to the small prints, one at a time, the way the old man had. Not behind. Beside.
He did not look round. He did not need to. He could hear the others coming after him. Lyra first. Then Oren. Then the Ashborn. Then, a long way back, the silent man, and Tansy, holding his sleeve.
And Corin walked beside the footprints of a small boy who had wanted his sister, and would not stay where he was safe, and had walked all the way back across the Glass on his own small feet to get her. A boy who did not get her. A boy who got to the Edge, and turned his face toward a gentle voice, and lost everything.
The prints went on.
So did he.
He walked beside them until the sky was white, and the heat began, and Kestrel called the halt. And when they stretched the canvas over the spears and lay down under it, in the heat, to wait for night, Corin lay with his face turned toward the small prints, a little way off on the glass, in the white light.
And looked at them until he slept.
And for the first time in a long time, he did not dream about anything at all.
Rule One
He asked her the next night, walking.
They were a little way ahead of the others. Not far. Kestrel was in front, as always, a dark shape with a spear against the stars. Oda and Sef were behind her. And then the two of them. And then a gap, a long one, before Oren, because Oren walked slowly now, with his sword on his back, looking about him at everything the way he had looked about him since the Eye, like a man in a town he had never seen. And behind Oren, further still, the silent man, and Tansy.
The footprints went on beside them. Corin could not see them now, in the dark. But he knew they were there. He could feel them, almost, the way you can feel somebody walking beside you in the dark without seeing them.
"Lyra," he said.
"Yes."
"There's a page in the book. About you."
She did not answer at once.
He went on. He had been thinking about how to say it for two nights. He still did not know.
"In the Ledger. The eighth one. Near the front. Vespera wrote it. You told her what to put, and she wrote it down for you, because you can't write anymore." He walked a few steps. "I've seen it. I've seen your name at the top of it. I've never read it."
"I know," said Lyra.
"Why haven't I?"
"Because I asked you not to."
He thought about that. Step, and the crutch, and step.
"I don't remember you asking," he said.
"No," said Lyra. "You wouldn't."
They walked.
The hum was in his teeth. The stars were over him. And under them, beside him, a hand's width away, the girl who had held his hand through a linen bandage for a whole night while the old man went out of him.
He did not know her.
That was the thing. He had never been able to say it to her plainly, and he did not know if he could say it now. He knew her voice. He knew it better than any voice in the world. He had followed it up out of the dark more times than he could count. He knew her face, now, from looking at it. He knew she counted. He knew she had a brother called Ro, and a dead father, and a mother who had gone over the water. He knew she had the cut. He knew she had stood on the black glass with a bent sword and a bleeding hand and waited for an Inquisitor to come and take it out of her.
But there were holes.
There were holes in him, everywhere she had been. From before. From the first time. The time he had taken her into himself, and carried her, and given her back. He could feel them, the way Oren felt his. He could walk round them. But he could not see what was in them.
And the page in the book would tell him.
"Do you want me to read it?" he said. "The page. You could read it to me. The way you read the rest. Or I could. It's in my letters. Vespera wrote it in my letters, I think, so I'd be able to." He stopped. "I'd know, then. What I've lost. Who you were. To me. Before."
Lyra did not answer for a long time.
He heard her breathing. Walking beside him in the dark. He heard her boots on the glass.
And then she said: "No."
He waited.
"No," said Lyra again. "Don't read it. Not yet. Not like that."
"Why?"
"Because it's a page." Her voice was low and steady. "It's a page I said out loud to an old woman with a needle, in a hurry, in a tent, the night before we came onto the Glass. Because I was afraid you'd die out here and never know. It's true. Everything on it's true. But it's all at once. It's everything, all at once, in a list, like the things in a dead man's pockets." She stopped. "I don't want you to have me like that. In a list."
Corin did not say anything.
"I'll tell you myself," said Lyra.
He turned his head and looked at her. He could not see her face. Only the shape of her, against the stars, walking.
"A bit at a time," said Lyra. "When it comes up. When there's a thing that matters. When you ask. I'll tell you. Myself. Out loud." She was quiet a moment. "And no lies. Not one. Not even kind ones. If you ask me a thing and the true answer's a hard one, I'll give you the hard one."
"Rule one," said Corin.
"Rule one."
He knew rule one. It was in the Ledger. Near the very front, in his own crooked capitals, underlined twice. RULE ONE: TELL HER. He did not remember making it. But he had read it every morning for months, and he knew what it meant. Whatever he knew, he told her. Whatever he did, he told her. Whatever he was afraid of. No hiding.
It had not occurred to him that it went both ways.
He walked a long time, thinking about it.
"That's fair," he said at last.
And Lyra laughed.
Not much. A short sound. Not happy, exactly. But not unhappy either. The sound of somebody who has been carrying something heavy a long way and has just found somewhere to put it down for a moment.
"It isn't," she said. "It isn't fair at all. I know everything about you. Everything. I've read your book every morning since the city. I've read your chest. I know what you had for breakfast the day we left the city, because you wrote it down. And you don't even know what color my mother's hair was, or what I'm frightened of, or the name of the horse I had when I was little." She took a breath. "It's not fair. Not at all."
She walked a few steps.
"But it's what we've got," said Lyra.
Corin did not say anything for a while.
Then he said: "What was the horse's name?"
Lyra stopped walking.
She stood on the glass in the dark, and he stopped too, and they stood there, a hand's width apart, with the stars over them and the footprints beside them that they could not see. And behind them, a long way back, he could hear Oren's slow steps coming, and further back still, the faint thin sound of Tansy singing.
"Biscuit," said Lyra.
Corin waited.
"She was brown," said Lyra. "With a white foot. The front left. She was fat and lazy and she bit, and she ate my hat once, and I loved her more than anything in the world." Her voice had gone strange. "I was little. My mother bought her for me. Not long before she went."
"Biscuit," said Corin.
"Yes."
"Brown. With a white foot. Front left."
"Yes."
"She bit."
"She bit everybody," said Lyra. "Except me."
And they stood there a moment longer. And then she began to walk again, and he walked beside her. And after a while he heard her counting, under her breath. Not the stars. Not the steps. Something else. He did not ask what.
And in the morning, under the canvas, before he slept, he took out the Ledger and wrote, in his crooked capitals, on the page after the one about Malakor:
LYRA HAD A HORSE CALLED BISCUIT. BROWN. WHITE FOOT, FRONT LEFT. SHE BIT EVERYBODY EXCEPT LYRA.
SHE TOLD ME HERSELF.
