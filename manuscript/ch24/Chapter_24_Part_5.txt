Chapter 24: Oren's Barrier, Part 5 of 5: Nine O'Clock
Nine O'Clock
He should have gone home. He knew that. Vespera had told him to. Moll had told him to. Every bone in his body was telling him to, and his eyes were so heavy that the river kept going blurred in front of them.
But he did not go home.
He sat on the step by the Patient Grace with the empty tin mug in his hands, and he thought about what Moll had said. A black carriage with no crest. An order signed in gray ink. A little drawing of a barley sugar.
I want to see who he runs to.
Silas Kross had said that. Corin had not heard him say it. Wren had told him. In her attic over the bookbinder's shop on Mercer Street, with the rain on the window, she had told him: He let you go on purpose. He's watching you. He wants to see who you run to.
And Corin had run. All night. To the Bell. To the rope-walk. To Pruett's. To the coal wharf.
He looked down at the barge. At the little cabin with the smoke going up from its tin chimney. At the old man asleep inside it.
"Juno," he said slowly. "When you came down the wharf just now. Did anybody follow you?"
Juno looked offended.
"Nobody follows me," she said. "I went round by the tanneries and over the rope bridge at the dye works and through the back of the chandler's. Nobody could follow me through the chandler's. Old Mr. Toller keeps geese."
"And me?" Corin said. "Did anybody follow me?"
Juno opened her mouth. Then she shut it.
"I don't know," she said. "I wasn't with you."
Corin stood up. His legs nearly gave way, and he held on to the wall.
"Then I can't stay here," he said. "If I'm being watched, then everywhere I go, I'm showing them. I've already shown them the coal wharf. I can't show them this boat." He looked at Hessa. "Can you move it? Now? Somewhere else? Somewhere I don't know?"
Hessa looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once.
"Wat'll be back in a quarter of an hour," she said. "We'll go up on the tide. Past the north locks. There's a hundred coal barges up there, all black, all alike. And I won't tell you where." She paused. "I won't tell her either." She nodded at Lyra.
Lyra had gone pale. But she did not argue.
"I'll stay with him," she said. "On the boat. Wherever it goes."
"I know you will," said Corin.
"How will I find you?"
"Juno will find you. Juno finds everybody." He looked at Juno. "Won't you?"
"Course I will," said Juno.
So Corin went back up the steps, alone, and walked away along the coal wharf, slowly, in plain sight, the way a man walks who does not care who sees him. He did not look back at the barge. He walked all the way along Wharf Street and back through the fish market and up the hill past Saint Bride's church, and he did not hurry, and he did not hide. Anybody who was following him would see him going back the way he came. Away from the river. Away from the red sail.
And then, at the top of Saint Bride's, at the corner of Linden Walk, he went into the chandler's yard by the back gate and climbed up the coal chute.
It was much harder going up than coming down. He did it on his hands and knees, with his boots slipping on the tin and the coal dust going up his nose. At the top he lay flat on the roof for a while, breathing, with his face against the cold wet slates. Then he crawled along the ridge, keeping low, behind the chimney stacks, until he came to the last roof in the row. The one that looked down on Linden Walk. On the two old trees, and the black board over the arch that said PRUETT & SON, and the empty cobbled street in front of it.
He lay down behind a chimney and looked over the edge.
The street had been swept. Somebody, young Pruett probably, had cleared away the broken handcart and the clubs and the caps. The great iron-bound log had gone. There was only a scorch mark on the cobbles in front of the arch, black and long, where the red fire had broken against Oren's barrier. And a scattering of fine white dust, along the bottom of the archway, like flour, that the wind had not quite blown away.
The doors along the street were open again. The woman was back on her step with her bucket and brush, scrubbing at it as if nothing had happened. The boy with the hot rolls had come back too, and was standing on the corner calling Rolls! in his cracked voice. It was as if the street had decided, all together, that nothing had happened there this morning. That it had all been a dream.
Corin lay behind the chimney and waited.
He heard the bells of Saint Bride's ring the quarter. Then the half. Then the three-quarters. The sun had got a little higher. It was still thin and pale, but it was warmer now, and it was drying the slates, and he could feel it on the back of his neck. His eyes kept closing. Twice he woke with a jerk and did not know where he was, and then he did, and his heart was pounding.
Then the bells rang nine.
And on the ninth stroke, exactly on the ninth stroke, a carriage came round the corner from Saint Bride's into Linden Walk.
It was black. All black. The wheels were black, and the doors were black, and the two horses pulling it were black, with black plumes nodding on their heads, like the horses that pull a hearse. There was no crest on the door. There was no name. The driver sat high up on the box at the front, wrapped in a black cloak, with a black hat pulled down over his face. The carriage came down Linden Walk at a slow, steady walk, the horses' hooves clopping on the cobbles, and it did not hurry, and it did not stop, until it came to the arch of Pruett's yard.
Then it stopped.
The woman on the step picked up her bucket and went inside and shut the door. The boy with the rolls was gone.
The carriage door opened.
A man stepped down.
He was slim. Not tall, not short. He wore a long gray coat, very well cut, buttoned to the throat, and gray gloves, and no hat. His hair was very pale, almost white, and cut short and neat. His face was pale too, and smooth, and quite pleasant, the kind of face that nobody would ever look at twice in a crowd. He stood on the cobbles beside the carriage for a moment and looked around him. At the street. At the houses. At the two old trees. At the scorch mark on the stones.
Then he took something out of his coat pocket. A small thing, wrapped in a twist of paper. He unwrapped it slowly, with his gray-gloved fingers. It was a barley sugar. Gold and clear, like amber. He put it in his mouth.
And he folded the paper.
Corin watched him do it. He could not look away. The man folded the twist of paper in half. And in half again. And again. And again. Small and neat and careful, smaller and smaller, until it was no bigger than a fingernail. A tiny hard square of paper. Then he put it back in his pocket.
Silas Kross.
He walked to the arch. He did not go in. He stood just outside it, on the scorch mark, and looked into the yard.
Oren was there.
Corin saw him come out of the kitchen door at the far end of the yard. He had left the blanket behind. He walked across the yard slowly, with his sword in his hand, and stopped a few paces inside the arch, and stood there. He did not lift the sword. He just held it, low, the way he had held it at first light, before the men came.
The two of them looked at each other. The Inquisitor in his gray coat. The old soldier with his scarred face.
Corin was too far away to hear what they said. He could only see. He saw Kross speak, a few words, pleasant and easy, with his head a little on one side. He saw Oren answer. One word, or two. He saw Kross smile.
Then Kross looked up.
Not at Oren. Past him. Over the roof of the arch. Over the roofs of the houses opposite. Along the row of chimney pots. Slowly. Like a man looking for a bird he has heard singing.
His pale eyes found Corin's chimney.
And stopped.
Corin did not move. He did not breathe. He lay flat on the cold slates behind the chimney with only the top of his head and his eyes showing over the edge, and he looked down into the pale, pleasant face of Silas Kross, and Silas Kross looked back up at him.
Kross smiled.
It was a small, kind, friendly smile. The smile of a man who has just seen an old friend across a crowded street. He lifted one gray-gloved hand, with the barley sugar still in his mouth, making a little lump in his cheek, and he touched two fingers to his pale forehead, the way a gentleman touches the brim of his hat to a lady.
Then he turned, and walked back to the carriage, and got in, and shut the door.
The driver flicked the reins. The black horses leaned into their collars. And the black carriage with no crest rolled away down Linden Walk, slow and steady, and turned the corner into Saint Bride's, and was gone.
Corin lay on the roof for a long, long time.
He did not go down until the bells rang the quarter past. Then he crawled back along the ridge, and down the coal chute, and across the chandler's yard, and in at the back of Pruett's, where Oren was sitting on an upturned bucket in the shed with his sword across his knees, waiting for him.
"He knew you were up there," Oren said.
"I know."
"He said to tell you good morning." Oren's voice was flat. "And he said, Tell Mr. Vane he did very well last night. Very well indeed. He ran to all the right places. I have them all written down."
Corin sat down on the floor of the shed, in the straw, because his legs would not hold him up anymore.
"What else?"
"He asked for the guest." Oren looked at him. "I said there was no guest. He said he knew. He said he'd never expected one. He said the carriage wasn't for Lord Thorne at all."
"Then who was it for?"
Oren was quiet for a moment.
"He said it was for whoever was standing in the gate," he said. "He said he only wanted to see who it would be." He looked down at his sword. "And then he looked at me for a long time. And he said, Captain Vance. Your brother sends his regards. And he got back in his carriage."
Corin stared at him.
Oren's face did not change. But his big scarred hands had closed very tight round the grip of his sword. So tight that the knuckles had gone white.
The Summons
Young Pruett heated water for him in the big copper in the wash-house, and Corin washed.
It took a long time. The wash-house was a small whitewashed shed at the side of the yard, with a stone floor and a drain in the middle and a big round copper pot built into a brick stove in the corner. Young Pruett lit the fire under it and filled it with buckets from the pump, and when the water was steaming he ladled it out into a tin bath on the floor and went away and shut the door. Corin took off his clothes. They were stiff with soot and coal dust and smoke and dried blood, and they smelled so strongly of the fire that when he dropped them on the floor the whole shed filled with the smell of burning tar.
He sat in the tin bath with his knees up to his chin and the hot water up to his waist, and he scrubbed. He scrubbed with a lump of hard yellow soap and a rough cloth until his skin was red. The water went gray, and then black. Young Pruett knocked and passed in another bucket, and he tipped it over his head, and scrubbed again. The cut on the back of his hand stung in the soap. He looked at it. It was not deep. Just a long thin line from the broken window glass, already closing. It would leave a scar. A thin white line, like Oren's, only smaller.
He thought, I'll remember how I got that. And then he thought, For now.
He put his clothes back on, because there was nothing else. Young Pruett had brushed them as best he could, and banged the coal dust out of the coat against the wall, but they were still dark and stiff and they still smelled of smoke. The Ledger was in the inside pocket, where it always was. He took it out and looked at it. The edges of the pages were a little brown, as if they had been held too near a candle. But it was whole. Everything in it was whole.
He put it back, next to the folded cream paper that smelled of lilies.
Then he lay down in the straw in an empty stall at the end of the stable, next to a big quiet brown horse called Duchess who looked down at him over the partition and breathed warm hay-smelling breath on his face, and he slept.
He did not dream. Or if he did, he did not remember it.
When he woke up, the light in the stable had changed. It was coming in at a different angle through the open top half of the door, yellow and slanting, with dust turning in it. The sun was much higher. He had no idea how long he had slept. He lay in the straw for a moment, not moving, with Duchess's big soft nose nudging at his hair, and tried to remember where he was.
The stable. Pruett's. Linden Walk. Monday.
Then it all came back at once, the way it always did. The rope-walk. The jars. The barrier. The black carriage. And he sat up in the straw so fast that Duchess threw up her head and snorted.
Somebody was standing in the doorway of the stall.
It was a boy. A thin boy of about eleven or twelve, in a neat dark green jacket with silver buttons that was a little too big for him, and a flat green cap with a small silver badge on the front. He had a leather satchel over one shoulder. His face was clean and pale and very serious. Corin knew the uniform at once. Everybody in the Lower Docks knew it. It was the uniform of a Bank runner. The boys who carried messages for the Oblivion Bank, between its great white-columned hall on Lantern Quay and its branches and its debtors all over the city, all day, every day, in every weather, at a steady jogging trot.
Behind the boy, in the yard, Corin could see Oren, standing with his arms folded and his face like stone.
"Corin Vane?" said the boy.
His voice was high and clear and quite flat. He said it the way Gideon said things. As if he had learned it by heart.
Corin did not answer.
"Corin Vane," the boy said again. "Senior Investigator. Harvesting Section. Ministry of Records. Assigned to the Intake Hall, Lantern Quay, to clear the glasses." He did not wait for an answer this time. He opened his satchel and took out an envelope and held it out. "For you. By hand. I'm to wait and see you read it."
Corin got up out of the straw. He brushed it off his coat. He took the envelope.
It was not cream-colored. It did not smell of lilies. It was plain pale green paper, thick and stiff and very smooth, the kind the Bank used for everything, with the corners cut square. On the front, in neat black ink, it said:
C. Vane. By hand.
On the back there was a seal. Not wax. A small round stamp of silver ink, pressed into the paper so hard that it had left a dent. Inside the circle there was the number 4, very small and plain, and round the edge, in tiny letters, the words FOURTH DESK.
He had seen that seal before. Back at the very start of all this, on a little card that had come down to the Harvesting Section with his morning post. Auditor. Fourth Desk. Signed: Elara.
He broke the seal with his thumb. Inside, there was a single sheet of the same thick white paper, folded once. He unfolded it.
The writing on it was small and neat and slanted a little to the right. It was quite plain, with no flourishes. But it was not like the fence-post writing on Cressida's paper. It was a real hand. A person's hand. Careful and quick, as if the person who wrote it had a great deal to do and not much time to do it in.
Mr. Vane,
You will present yourself at the Fourth Desk, the Oblivion Bank, Lantern Quay, at two o'clock this afternoon. Use the side door by the slag-room stairs. The Warden will let you up.
You will come alone.
You will bring your Ledger.
You are not under arrest. This is not a request.
E.
That was all.
Corin read it three times. Then he folded it again, slowly, along its crease.
The Ministry runner was watching him with his pale serious face.
"You've read it," said the boy. "I'm to say so. And I'm to ask if there's an answer."
Corin looked at him.
"If I said no," he said. "If I said I wasn't coming. What would happen?"
The boy thought about it. He seemed to take the question very seriously.
"I'd go back and tell her you said no," he said. "And then I expect somebody else would come. Not me. Somebody bigger." He paused. "They always come, mister. When it's that seal. I've been carrying for two years, and I've never once known anybody not go, in the end."
Corin nodded slowly.
"Tell her I'll come," he said.
The boy nodded once. He shut his satchel. He touched the brim of his blue cap, quick and neat. And then he turned and went away across the yard at his steady jogging trot, past Oren, under the arch, into Linden Walk, and was gone.
Oren came over to the stall. He looked at the paper in Corin's hand.
"The Auditor," he said.
"Yes."
"You know what she can do."
"Yes."
Corin did know. He had sat across a desk from her once already, in the Harvesting Section, with a sheet of dead glass between them and a silent giant called Mister Gage standing by the door. The Auditor did not burn memories. She did not take them. She did not buy them or sell them or bottle them. She read them. She put her bare hand on a thing, a card or a cup or a jar or a man's sleeve, and she shut her eyes, and she saw. Every hand that had held it. Every account it was written in. Every debt. And every time she did it, it cost her a day. A whole day of her own life, gone. Burned. Never to come back. She had told him so herself, quite calmly, the way a clerk tells you the price of a stamp.
She had read his intake card this morning. At six o'clock. Standing at Warden Briggs's ledger stand by the slag-room stairs. She had taken off her white glove and put her bare hand on it and shut her eyes.
And when she opened them, she had looked very tired. As if she had been up all night.
Thank you, Warden. That's all I need.
What had she seen? He had carried that card every morning for a week now, ever since the Archon's paper came down and said he was to be kept busy and out of the way. He had held it in his hand when he came in at the side door, wet with rain, and when he signed his name in Briggs's book, and when he went down the stairs to the slag room to clear the glasses. He had held it with the same hand that held the rod. The same hand that wrote in the Ledger. What had it picked up, in a week, from him? What had it heard?
What did it know about him that he did not know himself?
"She'll want your Ledger," said Oren quietly.
"I know."
"Everything in it. Everyone in it. Lord Thorne. Lyra. Me. Vespera. Juno. Hessa Doon and her boat." Oren's gray eyes were very cold. "If she puts her hand on that book, she'll know everything you know."
Corin took the Ledger out of his coat.
He looked at it. The old brown leather cover, worn soft at the corners. The pages, a little scorched at the edges. Six years of his life, in his own small careful hand. Everything he had burned. Everything he had lost. Everything he had found. The names. The places. The list of things he owed.
He opened it at the very first page. The page he read every morning when he woke, before he did anything else, because some mornings it was the only way to know who he was. The letters on it were big and black and pressed so deep the pen had nearly cut the paper.
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin.
You were born in Oakhaven.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
You used to fear the dark.
He read the third line twice. Then a third time.
He put his hand flat on his chest, over his shirt, over the place where the one faded word lay. He thought of a woman with short dark hair cut straight, and dark careful eyes, and a gold front tooth over a gap. He thought of the glass going white under her bare hand, and of what he had seen in it. A pool. A small girl with a gap in her teeth, laughing, who called him Cor.
He looked at it for a long time.
Then he closed the Ledger and put it back in his coat.
"I'll take it," he said.
"Vane—"
"If I don't take it, she'll know I've got something to hide. And then she'll come looking for it. And she won't stop. You said so yourself. They always come." He looked up at Oren. "Better she reads it on her own glass desk, with me sitting across from her, than she reads it off the back of Mrs. Marta's door at midnight."
Oren said nothing for a long moment.
"And the cream paper?" he said at last. "Cressida's paper? With the lilies?"
Corin put his hand on his coat, over the place where it lay.
"That," he said, "I'm giving to you."
He took it out and held it out to Oren. Oren looked at it. Then he took it, carefully, and put it inside his own coat.
"Keep it safe," said Corin. "Somewhere she'll never think to look. And if I don't come back from the East Tower—"
"You'll come back."
"If I don't. Give it to Lyra. It's her father's name on it. It's hers."
Oren nodded slowly.
Out in the yard, a clock was striking somewhere. Not Saint Bride's. A smaller clock, inside old Pruett's house, through the open kitchen door. Corin counted the strokes. Eleven.
Three hours.
He looked out through the open top of the stable door, at the sky over the stable roofs. It was blue now, a pale washed-out winter blue, with small white clouds going across it very fast. To the north, high on its hill above the roofs, he could see the gray towers of the Ministry, where his own desk sat empty in the Harvesting Section. And to the south, low down, over the river, there was still a faint brown smudge where the smoke from Fuller's Yard had been.
And under that smudge, on the edge of the water, he knew without seeing it, there stood the Bank.
The old warehouse with its great blank walls of dark brick and its new white front. The four tall smooth columns. The long gray line of people waiting outside it, collars up, to sell an afternoon or a song or the smell of their mother's kitchen for a handful of coppers. And over the doors, in gilded letters that caught the light, the three words that every child in the Lower Docks could read before they could read their own names.
FORGET YOUR TROUBLES.
Somewhere inside it, up a stair he had never climbed, behind a desk of glass, a woman with a gold tooth was waiting for him. And the one faded word over his heart ached like a bruise.
