Chapter 24: Oren's Barrier, Part 3 of 5: The Barrier
The Barrier
The woman in the gray apron pulled the cork out of the red vial with her teeth.
She did it neatly, the way a woman pulls the cork out of a bottle of vinegar in her own kitchen. She spat the cork onto the cobbles. Then she tipped back her long pale head and drank. Corin saw her throat move as she swallowed. He saw the red light go down into her, like a coal dropped down a well.
For a moment nothing happened.
Then her eyes changed. They had been pale and empty, like her face. Now they were full. Full of something that was not hers. Corin had seen that look before, on mages at the end of a long night of burning, in the moment before he took up his rod and went to them. It was the look of someone with a stranger's memory sitting behind their eyes. For one breath, the tall woman's face was not empty at all. It was angry. Terribly angry, the way a man is angry when he comes home and finds his house broken into and his children gone. It was somebody's rage. Somebody's worst day. Bought and bottled and sold.
And then she burned it.
Corin saw the burn go through her. He saw the rage go out of her eyes, all at once, like a lamp blown out, and her face went empty again. And in the same moment her gray-gloved hand came up and opened, palm out, toward the arch. And out of it came fire.
Not fire like a candle. Not fire like a stove. It was a long rope of red flame, twisting, thick as a man's arm, and it came down the street toward the arch faster than a thrown stone. Corin felt the heat of it on his face from thirty paces away. He heard it roar. He saw the paint on the railings it passed curl up and blister and turn black.
He had no time to move. He had no time to think. He only had time to see Oren, in the middle of the gateway, close his eyes.
Oren did not lift his sword. He did not step back. He stood with his feet planted on the cobbles and his sword held low, and his scarred face went very quiet and very still, the way a man's face goes when he is listening for something a long way off. And his lips moved. Just a little. As if he were saying a word to himself. One small ordinary word.
And the air in the gateway changed.
Corin felt it before he saw it. He felt it in his teeth, and in the roots of his hair, and in the Ledger pressed against his chest. A kind of pressure. A kind of hum, too low to hear, like the hum of a great bell long after it has stopped ringing. The cobbles under his boots seemed to shiver.
Then he saw it.
It came up out of the stones of the gateway like water coming up out of a spring. It was clear. Almost perfectly clear, like the cleanest glass. But it was not glass. It moved. It rippled. It shone faintly, the way the surface of a still pond shines on a gray morning. It rose up out of the cobbles in front of Oren in a smooth curved sheet, from one side of the arch to the other, from the stones to the keystone at the top, and it filled the gateway completely, the way a pane fills a window.
The rope of red fire hit it.
There was a sound like a great sheet of iron being struck with a hammer. A deep, ringing, shuddering boom that Corin felt in his chest and in his knees and in the back of his skull. The barrier bowed inward, a little, where the fire struck it. It went white for a moment, white as snow. And the fire broke against it. It broke the way a wave breaks against a sea wall, and flew apart into a thousand pieces, and the pieces went spinning away to the left and the right, red sparks and red streamers, and hit the walls of the arch and the cobbles of the street and went out, hissing, in black smoke.
The barrier held.
And then Corin saw its edges.
They were gold.
Not the whole barrier. The barrier itself was clear, clear as water. But where it met the stones of the arch, all the way round, along the cobbles and up the sides and over the curve of the top, there was a thin line of light. Bright and warm and steady. The color of honey with the sun shining through it. The color of a candle flame seen through a closed eyelid. It ran round the edge of the clear barrier like the gold leaf round the edge of a holy picture, and it did not flicker, and it did not fade.
Corin knew that gold.
He had seen it once before. Not in the world. In a memory. In the slag he had drawn out of a little girl in a crypt, with his own bare hand, he had seen a field. A wide green field under a gray sky, and a man in a plain coat standing in the middle of it with his hands lifted up, and over the man, over the field, over everything, a dome. A great high dome of light, clear as glass. And round its edges, where it met the ground, a line of gold. Just like this. The same warm steady color. The same feeling, deep in his chest, of something very old and very strong and very kind.
He stared at the gold line round Oren's barrier. He could not look away from it. His heart was beating very fast.
Where did you learn that, he thought. Oren. Where did you learn to make it gold?
But there was no time to ask. Not now.
Because the men were coming.
They had not waited to see what the fire would do. They had come on behind it, running now, roaring, a wall of rough coats and red faces and lifted clubs. The two men with the handcart came at the front, pushing it as fast as they could, so that the great iron-bound log jumped and rattled on its bed. Corin saw their faces. Young faces, most of them. Dockers. Carters. The kind of men who would do a hard night's work for a silver and a drink, and not ask what the work was for.
They hit the barrier.
The handcart hit it first. The log slammed into the clear wall, iron-bound end first, with all the weight of the cart and the two men behind it. Boom. The barrier went white again. It bowed inward, further this time, so far that Corin saw the curve of it come within a hand's width of Oren's face. Oren did not move. He did not open his eyes. His sword arm was shaking.
The log bounced back. The handcart tipped over sideways. One of the men pushing it fell under it and screamed.
Then the others hit it. Not all at once. In ones and twos and fours, the way waves come in on a beach. They hit it with their shoulders. They hit it with their clubs and their iron bars and their nail-studded sticks. They beat on it with their fists. The man with the hooked pole tried to get the hook round the edge of it, where it met the stones of the arch, to pull it away. The hook touched the gold line and the man let go of the pole with a yell and fell back, shaking his hand as if he had burned it.
Boom. Boom. Boom.
Each blow went through the barrier and into Oren. Corin could see it. Each time a club came down on the clear wall, Oren's whole body jerked, as if he himself had been hit. His face had gone gray. There was sweat running down it, though the morning was bitterly cold. The scar on his cheek had gone white, very white, against the gray. His lips were still moving. Still saying the same small word, over and over, under his breath.
Corin stood behind him, under the arch, and did not know what to do.
He had the rod. He had Big Harl's long knife. Neither of them was any use against twenty men on the other side of a wall. He could burn something himself. He felt the edges of his own memories, the way a man in the dark feels along a shelf for a candle. He could burn something big. Something old. He could make a light that would blind them, the way he had done in the cellar at the Bell. But a light would not stop twenty men. It would only make them angrier.
And then he felt it.
In the air. On the other side of the barrier. Drifting.
Ash.
The woman in the gray apron had burned again. He had not even seen her do it. But he felt it now, the way he always felt it, in the back of his throat and the tips of his fingers. The thin, gray, bitter drift of a memory burned down to nothing. It was hanging in the air of Linden Walk like smoke after a bonfire. Not hers. She had none of her own to burn. It was somebody else's. Somebody's worst day, bought and bottled and drunk and burned, and left hanging in the morning air like dirty washing.
Corin put his hand on the rod.
It was cold. The deep cold, the cellar cold, the cold it always went when there was Ash near it. He drew it out of the long narrow pocket on the right side of his coat, slowly. It was a thin silver rod, about the length of his forearm, with a fine spiral cut into its tip, turning to the right, the way a clock turns. So that it's always going forward, Malakor had said once. He held it up, so that the tip of it touched the inside of the barrier. Not hard. Just touched.
Through a wall, boy? said the dry voice behind his left ear. You've never done that.
Then I'll learn, Corin thought.
And he pulled.
He had never done it through a wall before. He did not know if it would work. But the barrier was clear, and the Ash was just on the other side of it, and the rod did not care about walls. The rod only cared about what was burned. He felt the Ash come toward him. Slowly at first. Then faster. A thin gray thread of it, drifting in the air, through the clear wall as if the wall were not there, toward the end of the rod. Into it. Up it. Into his hand. Into his arm.
Into him.
The memory hit him like cold water.
A kitchen. A small dark kitchen in a house by the river, with a low ceiling and a stone floor. A man coming in at the door, wet with rain, laughing, shaking his cap. A table set for supper. Three bowls. Three spoons. And the man stopping, in the doorway, with the laugh going out of his face. Because the table was set. But the room was empty. And the back door was open, and banging in the wind, and there was a child's shoe lying on the step outside, in the rain.
And then a voice. The man's own voice, from inside his own head, so loud and so terrible that Corin nearly dropped the rod.
The Bank. The Bank took them. For the debt. For my debt. My fault. Mine.
Corin pulled his hand away from the barrier. He was gasping. His eyes were wet. He stood there under the arch with the rod in his shaking hand and the stranger's grief burning cold in his chest, and he looked out through the clear wall at the tall woman in the gray apron.
She had drunk that. That man's worst day. The day his family was taken for his debt. She had bought it from the Bank's own vaults, in a little glass vial, and drunk it, and burned it, and thrown it at Oren as fire. And she had not felt a thing.
And the Bank had taken that man's family in the first place.
Corin felt something go hard and cold and very clear inside him.
"Oren," he said. "How long can you hold it?"
Oren did not open his eyes. His lips stopped moving, for just a moment.
"Not long," he said. His voice was very rough. "It's eating the word. I can feel it. Like a candle burning down. When the word's gone, the wall goes."
"What word?"
"I don't know," said Oren. "I didn't choose it. I just reached. It was the first one that came." His sword arm jerked again as another club hit the barrier. Boom. "Something small. Something from this morning. I can't—" He stopped. His face twisted. "I can't see it anymore. It's going."
The gold line round the edge of the barrier flickered.
Just once. Just for a moment. Like a candle flame when a door opens somewhere in a house.
On the other side of the clear wall, the tall woman in the gray apron saw it too. Corin saw her empty pale face turn toward the gold line. He saw her gray-gloved hand go down to her belt again.
She unhooked a vial that glowed blue.
The Price of a Word
The tall woman drank the blue vial.
Corin watched her drink it. He watched the blue light go down her long pale throat. He watched her eyes fill up, for one breath, with somebody else's memory. This time it was not rage. It was fear. Pure cold fear, the kind a child feels alone in the dark when something moves under the bed. Somebody's worst night. Bought. Bottled. Drunk.
And burned.
This time it did not come out as fire. It came out as cold.
She put her gloved hand flat against the barrier, and the cold went out of her palm into the clear wall like ink going into water. Corin saw it spread. White. Feathery. Crawling across the clear surface the way frost crawls across a window on a winter night, in long fine branching lines like the veins of a leaf. Where it went, the barrier stopped rippling. It went stiff. It went hard. It went white and cloudy and brittle, like ice on a puddle.
And where the frost touched the gold line at the edge, the gold went dim.
Oren made a sound. A low sound, deep in his chest, like a man lifting something too heavy for him. His knees bent. He was holding the sword with both hands now, the point down on the cobbles, leaning on it like a crutch.
"Now," said the tall woman. It was the first word Corin had heard her say. Her voice was as empty as her face. "Hit it now."
The men drew back. Five of them. Six. They got their hands on the great iron-bound log, which had fallen off the tipped-over handcart, and they lifted it between them by the handles nailed onto its sides. They backed away down the street with it, ten paces, fifteen, to get a run at the gate.
The frost was still spreading. The whole barrier was white now, from the cobbles to the keystone. Corin could hardly see through it. He could see the shapes of the men, dark and blurred, like shapes seen through a steamed-up window. He could see the long dark shape of the log between them. He saw them begin to run.
And he knew that when that log hit the frozen wall, the wall would break.
He did not think. There was no time to think. He only knew one thing, very clearly. He had a stranger's memory sitting in his chest, cold and terrible. And it did not belong to the tall woman. She had bought it. And it did not belong to the Bank. The Bank had sold it.
It belonged to someone.
And the men on the other side of that wall were dockers and carters and laborers from the Lower Docks, and every one of them was in debt to somebody, and half of them, Corin would have bet his last copper, were in debt to the Black Bank.
He pressed his face close to the frozen barrier, and he shouted.
"Three bowls!"
His voice cracked. It was loud, much louder than he had meant, and it rang off the walls of the arch and went out down the empty street.
"Three bowls on the table! Three spoons! And you came in from the rain and you were laughing and you shook your cap, and the back door was open, and there was a shoe on the step! A child's shoe! In the rain!"
The men with the log were still running. Ten paces away. Eight.
"She drank it!" Corin shouted. "Your worst day! The day the Bank took them! She bought it out of the Bank's vault in a bottle and she drank it and she threw it at us! That was your day! That was yours!"
Six paces. Five.
And one of the men stopped.
He was at the back of the log, on the left side. A big man, with a red beard going gray, and a docker's flat cap pulled down over his eyes. He stopped so suddenly that the man behind him ran into him. His hands came off the handle of the log. And the log, with one man gone from it, swung sideways, and dipped, and the front end of it hit the cobbles with a great iron clang, three paces short of the barrier, and the men carrying it went down in a heap on top of each other, cursing.
The red-bearded man did not go down. He stood in the middle of Linden Walk, with his empty hands hanging at his sides, and he stared at the barrier. At Corin, behind it.
His face had gone white under the beard.
"What did you say?" he said.
It was not loud. But the street had gone very quiet, and Corin heard it clearly.
"A shoe," Corin said, through the frost. "On the step. In the rain. And three bowls. And the Bank took them, for your debt, and you thought it was your fault." He swallowed. "You sold it, didn't you. That day. You sold it to the Bank, so you wouldn't have to remember it anymore. And they bottled it. And she drank it."
The red-bearded man stared at him.
Then he turned his head, slowly, and looked at the tall woman in the gray apron.
She did not look back at him. She did not look at anything. Her face was empty. She was reaching down to her belt again, for another vial.
"I don't remember," said the red-bearded man. His voice had gone strange. "I don't remember any shoe. I don't remember any bowls. I don't—" He stopped. He put one big hand up to his chest, over his heart, the way Hollis had done in the loft. "There's a hole. There's a hole here, and I don't know what was in it. I've never known. I sold it four years ago for twelve silver off my debt, and I never knew what it was." He looked at the tall woman again. "And you drank it?"
The tall woman pulled the cork out of a green vial with her teeth.
The red-bearded man hit her.
It was not a clever blow. It was the blow of a big man who had been carrying bales on the docks for twenty years and had never been taught to fight. He just swung his whole arm round, like a door swinging on a hinge, and his great fist caught her on the side of the head. She went down like a dropped coat. The green vial flew out of her hand and smashed on the cobbles, and a sickly green mist came up out of it and hung there, and nobody drank it, and nobody burned it, and it drifted away slowly on the little wind, into the gutters.
For a moment nobody moved.
Then everything happened at once.
Somebody shouted. Somebody else shouted back. Two of the men on the log scrambled up and went for the red-bearded man, and another two went for them, and suddenly there was not one crowd in Linden Walk but two, and they were fighting each other. Corin saw fists and clubs and caps flying. He heard somebody yelling She drank mine too! She drank mine too, I know she did! He saw Gant, at the edge of it all, step back neatly out of the way, onto the step of a house, and fold his small pale hands in front of him, and watch, with his head on one side and a small polite frown on his face.
And then, from the far end of the street, from the corner of Saint Bride's, there came a sound.
A whistle.
A long, high, shrill whistle, the kind the Watch carries on a chain round their necks. And another. And another. And the sound of boots on cobbles, a lot of boots, running.
Round the corner came the Watch. A dozen of them, maybe more, in their dark blue coats with the brass buttons, with their tall hats and their heavy sticks. And at the front of them, red-faced and puffing and with his hat gone and a cold pipe clamped between his teeth, came Sergeant Moll.
The fight broke apart like a dropped plate.
The men ran. They did not stop to think about it. They ran the way men in the Lower Docks always run when the Watch comes round a corner, without looking back, in every direction at once. Into doorways. Over railings. Down the narrow alleys between the houses. Two of them picked up the tall woman in the gray apron between them, by the arms, and dragged her away, her long legs trailing on the cobbles and her belt of vials clinking. The red-bearded man did not run. He sat down on the edge of the pavement, under one of the two old linden trees, and put his face in his hands.
Gant stepped down off his doorstep.
He looked at the arch. At the white frozen barrier. At Corin behind it. He took off his gold spectacles, and polished them on the end of his cardigan, and put them back on.
"Another time, Mister Vane," he called. Quite pleasantly. "Properly, next time."
And he turned, and walked away up Linden Walk, with his hands folded behind his back, not hurrying at all. The Watch ran right past him. Not one of them stopped him. Not one of them so much as looked at him. Corin did not know if that was because they did not know who he was, or because they did.
The barrier went out.
It did not break. It did not crack. It simply stopped. One moment it was there, white and frozen and filling the arch from side to side. The next moment it was not. The frost on it fell to the cobbles as a fine white dust, like flour, and blew away. The gold line round the edge flickered once, twice, and went dark.
And Oren fell down.
Corin caught him. Or tried to. Oren was a big man, and heavy, and he came down all at once, the way a tree comes down, and Corin only managed to slow him so that he did not hit his head on the stones. They went down together in the gateway, Oren on his back, Corin on his knees beside him. The sword clattered on the cobbles.
Oren's eyes were open. He was breathing. Short, quick, shallow breaths, like a dog after a long run. His face was gray as ash and wet with sweat, and the white scar on his cheek stood out against it like a crack in a plate.
"They've gone," Corin said. "Oren. They've gone. You held it."
Oren looked up at him.
"The wagon?" he said.
"Gone too. Long gone. Nobody looked at it."
Oren closed his eyes. Something in his face let go.
"Good," he said.
He lay there for a while, on the cold stones of the gateway, breathing. Sergeant Moll came puffing up and stood over them both with his hands on his knees, wheezing like a broken bellows, and said a great many things about rope-walks and fire bells and people who did not tell the Watch when they were going to start a small war in Linden Walk at seven o'clock on a Monday morning. Corin did not hear most of it.
Later, much later, it seemed, though it was not really very long, they were in the kitchen again. Young Pruett had made tea in the big brown pot. The cat was back on its chair by the stove. Oren sat at the long table with a blanket round his shoulders, and his big scarred hands wrapped round a mug, and the color slowly coming back into his face.
Young Pruett put the sugar bowl down in front of him.
Oren looked at it. Then he looked at the table. At the sugar bowl, and the milk jug, and his own mug, and the little dish of butter. He frowned.
"Pass me the—" he said. And stopped.
Corin looked up.
Oren was staring at the table. His frown had gone deeper. He lifted one hand and made a small movement with it in the air, a turning, stirring movement, round and round.
"The—" he said again. "The thing. For the sugar. The small thing. You put it in the cup and you go round." He made the stirring movement again. His voice was quite calm. But his hand, Corin saw, was not quite steady. "I had it in my mouth this morning. In the porridge. I know I did. I can see the shape of it. Round at one end. Long. Silver. I just… can't…"
Young Pruett looked at him. Then he went to the dresser, and opened a drawer, and took one out, and put it down on the table by Oren's mug.
Oren looked at it for a long time.
"Spoon," Corin said quietly.
Oren said it back. "Spoon." He said it slowly, carefully, the way a man says a word in a language he is just beginning to learn. And Corin watched it go into him, and sit there for a moment, and then slide away again, like rain sliding down a window. He saw Oren's eyes go empty for one breath, reaching for it. Not finding it.
Oren picked up the spoon. He looked at it. He put it in the sugar bowl, and then in his tea, and stirred, round and round, the way his hands still knew how to do.
"That was the word," he said, after a while. "For the wall. The first one that came." He was quiet. "Yesterday it was the thing you sit on. Today it's this. Tomorrow…" He shrugged. A small, tired movement under the blanket. "Tomorrow it'll be something else. That's the price. You don't get to choose."
He drank his tea.
Corin took out his Ledger. He opened it at the back, at the page where he had started keeping a list only last night, on the way out of the cellar under the Bell. Words Oren has paid. There was one line on it already.
Chair. Sunday night. Pruett's yard.
He wrote, under it, in his small careful hand:
Spoon. Monday morning. Pruett's gate. He held it gold.
