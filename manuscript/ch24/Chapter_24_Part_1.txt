Chapter 24: Oren's Barrier, Part 1 of 5: Over the Roofs
Over the Roofs
Juno had not lied. Over the roofs, it was faster than a horse.
It was not easier. Corin would remember that run for the rest of his life, or for as long as he was allowed to keep it, and he would never remember it as easy. The roofs of the Lower Docks were old and steep and slippery. The slates were wet with the night's frost, and where the frost had started to melt in the first gray light they were wetter still, and slick as soap. Some of them were loose. Twice a slate slid out from under Corin's boot and went skittering down the slope of the roof and over the edge, and he heard it smash in the street below, a long way down, a small sharp sound like a plate dropped in a kitchen. Each time, he froze, and pressed himself flat against the roof, and waited for a shout or a window to open. Each time, nothing happened. The city was still asleep. Or the part of it that was not standing in Fuller's Yard, watching the rope-walk burn.
Juno knew every inch of the way.
She ran along the ridges of the roofs with her arms held out a little at her sides, like a girl walking along the top of a wall. She knew which chimney stacks were solid and which ones were crumbling. She knew where the gutters were strong enough to stand on and where they would bend and tear away. She knew where the planks were. That was the strangest thing. All across the roofs of the Lower Docks, in the gaps between one building and the next, somebody had laid planks. Old gray boards, worn smooth and thin, some of them no wider than a man's hand. They lay across the gaps like little bridges. Some had ropes tied along them to hold on to. Some had nothing at all.
"Who put these here?" Corin panted, as they crossed the third one. It bent under his weight and creaked.
"Everyone," said Juno over her shoulder. "Nobody. Sweeps. Thieves. Kids. The roof is the only road in the Docks the Watch doesn't walk on." She jumped down onto a flat stretch of lead roof and turned to give Vespera her hand. "Mother Grue says there's a whole city up here, if you know how to look. She says it's older than the one down there."
Vespera came down off the plank slowly. She was out of breath. Her face was gray under the soot. She had the bundle of black wool held tight against her chest in both arms, and she had not let go of it once, not on the planks, not on the slates, not when she slipped on a gutter and Corin had to catch her by the coat.
"Mother Grue," Vespera said, "talks a great deal of nonsense."
"She says that about you too," said Juno, and grinned, and ran on.
They went north, and a little west, away from the river. The sky got lighter as they went. It was still gray, but it was a softer gray now, and low in the east, over the roofs, there was a thin band of the palest yellow, like the inside of a shell. Below them, the city was starting to wake up. Corin could hear it. A cart on cobbles, somewhere, its iron wheels grinding. A dog barking. A man coughing in a back yard, a long wet morning cough. A baby crying, high and thin, behind a window with a red curtain. Smoke was starting to go up from the chimneys around them, here and there, as people lit their fires for the day. It smelled of coal and wet wood and frying fat.
Behind them, the black column from Fuller's Yard was still going up. It had leaned over now, as a little wind had started to blow off the river. It made a long dark smear across the pale sky, like a thumb-mark on a window.
They crossed a street on a rope.
It was the only way. The street was too wide for a plank. Somebody had stretched a thick rope between two chimney stacks, one on each side, high above the cobbles, and tied a second rope above it to hold on to. Juno went across first, with her feet on the bottom rope and her hands on the top one, sideways, step by step. She made it look like nothing. Vespera went next. She gave the bundle to Corin to hold, and she went across with her teeth shut tight and her eyes fixed on the far chimney, and she did not look down once. Corin went last, with the bundle of black wool tucked inside the front of his coat, buttoned in, where it lay against his chest like a small cold stone.
He did look down. Halfway across. He could not help it.
The street was a long way below him. He could see the cobbles, wet and gray. He could see a milk cart standing outside a door, and a horse with a nosebag on, and a man in a white apron carrying two big cans. The man stopped in the middle of the street and looked up. For a long moment he and Corin stared at each other. Then the man shook his head, slowly, the way a man shakes his head at something he has seen too many times to be surprised by anymore, and went on to his cart.
Corin got to the far side and climbed down off the rope onto the roof and sat there for a moment with his back against the chimney, breathing.
"Nearly there," said Juno. "Two more roofs. Then down the coal chute behind the chandler's, and we're in Linden Walk."
Two more roofs. Then the coal chute, which was a long sloping tin slide down the back wall of a chandler's shop into a yard full of coal heaps, and which Corin went down on his back, feet first, in a cloud of black dust, landing in a heap of coal with a crunch. Then a gate. Then an alley, narrow and dark, that smelled of horses. Then Linden Walk.
Linden Walk was a quiet street. It was nicer than the streets around it, though not very much nicer. There had been trees along it once, lindens, which was how it got its name. Most of them were gone now. Only two were left, near the far end, old and black and bare in the winter cold, with their roots heaving up the paving stones around them. The houses were narrow and tall, made of yellow brick gone dark with soot, with steps going up to their front doors and railings in front. Some of the doors were painted. Some of the windows had curtains. At the far end, past the two old trees, there was an archway between two houses, wide enough for a carriage. Over the arch, painted in faded white letters on a black board, it said:
PRUETT & SON. LIVERY. HORSES FOR HIRE. CARRIAGES KEPT.
There was a man standing under the arch.
He was standing quite still, with his back against the wall of the arch, in the shadow. He was tall and broad, and he wore a long dark coat, open at the front. His head was bare. His gray hair was cut very short, and he had a short gray beard, and there was a long pale scar that ran from his left temple, down across his cheek, to the corner of his mouth. In his right hand he held a sword. Not a fancy one. A plain, heavy, straight soldier's sword, with a worn leather grip and a scratched steel guard. He held it low, with the point down, resting on the stones. The way a man holds a sword when he has been holding it for a very long time and his arm is tired, but he is not going to put it down.
He heard them coming. Corin saw his head turn, and his body change, quick as a dog's, from tired to ready. The sword came up.
Then he saw who it was. And the sword went down again. Slowly.
"Vane," said Captain Oren.
His voice was rough and low and very tired. He looked at Corin. He looked at the black coal dust all over him, from his hair to his boots. He looked at the soot on his face and the blood on his cut hand and the torn sleeve of his coat. He looked at Juno, black as a chimney sweep. He looked at Vespera, small and gray and dusty, with her hair coming down.
"You smell," said Oren, "like a bonfire."
"There was a fire," said Corin.
"I saw." Oren tipped his head toward the south, toward the black smear across the sky. "From the yard. An hour ago. I thought, that'll be Vane." He did not smile. Corin did not think he had ever seen Oren smile. But something in his face eased, just a little. "Are you hurt?"
"Not much."
"Hollis?"
"Gone. Alive. He won't do it, Oren. Whatever they asked him. He won't." Corin put his hand on the front of his coat, where the folded cream paper lay against his Ledger. "But I know what it was now. I've got it here. In writing."
Oren looked at him for a long moment.
"Then come in," he said. "Quickly. All of you. And tell me. Because they'll be back soon, and we haven't got long."
He stood aside. Corin went past him, under the arch, into the yard.
It was a big cobbled yard, long and narrow, with stables along both sides. The stable doors were old and wide and painted dark green, with the top halves open. Horses looked out of some of them, big brown heads with white stripes down their faces, breathing steam into the cold air. One of them stamped. One of them blew out through its nose, a long soft flapping sound. The yard smelled of horses, and hay, and the sharp sweet smell of horse dung, and it was the best smell Corin had smelled all night.
But the yard was not tidy.
There was a cart turned over on its side near the gate, with one wheel broken. There were dark stains on the cobbles, near the water trough, that Corin did not want to look at too closely. There was a long scar in the paint of one stable door, as if somebody had swung an axe at it. And there were things lying about. A cap. A club with nails in it, broken in half. A boot with no man in it. A lantern, smashed, with its oil soaked black into the cracks between the stones.
At the far end of the yard there was a house. It was a plain square brick house, two floors high, with a slate roof and a crooked chimney. Its back door stood open. A light was burning in one of the upstairs windows, a soft yellow light behind a white curtain.
And on the step of the open door, with a lamp in one hand and a long kitchen knife in the other, wearing a gray dress with a man's coat thrown over it and a bandage tied round her left arm, there was a young woman with short dark hair.
She saw him. She put down the lamp on the step. She did not put down the knife.
"Corin," said Lyra Thorne.
The White Room
The stairs in Pruett's house were narrow and steep and they creaked on every step. Lyra went first, with the lamp. Corin went behind her. Vespera came last, slowly, holding the rail with one hand and the black wool bundle with the other. Oren stayed below, in the yard, under the arch, with his sword.
"How is he?" Corin asked.
"The same," said Lyra. She did not turn round. "He slept, after they left. After the fight. Mrs. Hobb gave him something in warm milk and he slept. He woke up about an hour ago. He's been tapping ever since."
"Tapping."
"The same as always. Four. Two. Nine. Three." She stopped on the top step and looked back at him. In the lamplight her face was pale and tired, and there were dark smudges under her eyes. There was a scratch on her cheek that had not been there yesterday. "Over and over. On the wall. On the blanket. On my hand, when I hold it. As if he's knocking on a door, and nobody's coming."
The white room was at the end of the upstairs passage. It was called the white room because everything in it was white, or had been once. The walls were whitewashed, and the whitewash was flaking a little, near the ceiling, where the damp had got in. The curtains at the window were white, thin cotton, and the first gray light of the morning was coming through them, soft and pale. The bed was an old iron bed painted white, with brass knobs at the corners that had gone dull. The sheets were white. The blankets were white wool, worn thin and soft with washing. There was a white jug and a white basin on a little table by the window, and a white chair beside the bed, and a white rag rug on the bare boards of the floor.
The only things in the room that were not white were the people.
Mrs. Hobb sat in the chair by the bed. She was a big, soft, round woman, with a round red face and gray hair pinned up under a cap, and she wore a brown dress with an apron over it. She had a bowl of something in her lap, porridge, Corin thought, and a spoon in her hand. She was humming. It was a low, slow, wandering tune, with no words, the kind of tune a woman hums to a baby when she is not really thinking about it. When she saw them come in, she stopped humming and nodded to Corin, as if he had only been gone an hour or two to fetch something from the shops.
"Mr. Vane," she said. "You look like something the cat brought in."
"Something the cat brought in, and then dropped in the coal," said Corin.
"Well, don't sit on my clean sheets."
And in the bed, propped up on three white pillows, there was Lord Thorne.
He was not old. Corin had to keep reminding himself of that. Lord Aurelian Thorne was not even fifty. But he looked old. He looked like a man of eighty. His hair, which had been dark once, was white and thin and lay flat on the pillow. His face was thin, with deep lines in it, and the skin was gray and soft. His eyes were open. They were a pale gray-blue, like a winter sky, and they were looking at the ceiling, at nothing. They did not move when Corin came in. They did not move when Lyra set the lamp down on the table. They did not move at all.
Only his hand moved.
It lay on the white blanket, thin and white as paper, with the blue veins standing up on the back of it like the lines on a map. And the first finger tapped. Softly. On the wool.
Tap tap tap tap. Pause.
Tap tap. Pause.
Tap tap tap tap tap tap tap tap tap. Pause.
Tap tap tap. Pause.
And then again. Four, two, nine, three. Four, two, nine, three. Never stopping. Never changing. Like a clock.
On the little table by the bed there was a box. It was a small box, about the size of a hand, made of white shells pressed into some kind of plaster, the sort of thing people buy at the seaside to remember a holiday. Its lid was open. Inside, on a bed of faded blue velvet, there lay a stone. It was blue too, a deep clear blue, smooth and round, about the size of a pigeon's egg. There was a crack running across it from one side to the other, thin as a hair.
Corin had seen that stone before, in a memory that was not his own, on the night it cracked. He looked away from it.
"Sit down, Vespera," said Mrs. Hobb, getting up out of her chair. "You look done in."
"I am done in," said Vespera. But she did not sit down. She went to the bed. She stood there for a moment, looking down at the man lying in it. Then she put the black wool bundle down very carefully on the end of the bed, by his feet. She took off her coat. She pushed up the sleeves of her dress, past her elbows, so that her own arms showed, covered with their thousand fine lines of old ink. And she bent over and took Lord Thorne's left hand.
Not the tapping one. The other one. It lay still on the blanket, on the side nearest the wall.
She turned it over, gently, so the palm faced up. She pushed back the sleeve of his white nightshirt. And there, on the inside of his wrist, just below the heel of his hand, where the skin was thin and the veins showed blue, there was a word.
It was very small. Smaller than Corin remembered. Four letters, in black ink, faded a little to gray at the edges with the years. Written in a hand Corin knew now as well as he knew his own. Firm and plain and upright, with no flourishes. Vespera's hand.
FOLD.
Vespera looked at it for a long time. She did not touch it. She held his wrist in her two stained hands and turned it a little, toward the light from the window, and bent close, and looked at the letters the way a jeweler looks at a stone.
"It's whole," she said at last. "It's not been touched. Nobody's written over it. Nobody's tried." She let out a long breath. "Good."
"Written over it?" said Lyra sharply. "What do you mean, written over it? Who would—"
Corin reached into his coat.
He took out the folded cream-colored paper. It was creased and smudged with soot now, and one corner was scorched brown where a spark had caught it. But the smell of lilies was still coming off it, faint and sweet and heavy, and he saw Lyra smell it, and saw her face go very still.
He unfolded it and held it out to her.
She took it. She held it under the lamp. She read it.
Corin watched her read. He watched her eyes go along the first line, I, Aurelian Thorne, being of sound mind, and saw her mouth go tight. He watched them go down the page. All my papers, all my lands, all my rights in the Thorne name, and the keeping of my children. Her hand began to shake, a little, so that the paper shook too. I remember that I have two children only. A son and a daughter. I remember there were never any others.
She stopped reading. She looked up. Her face was white.
"Two," she said.
"Yes."
"Never any others." Her voice was very quiet. "Why would she write that? Why would anybody need to make him believe that, unless—" She stopped.
Corin knew what she was thinking. He was thinking it too. He was thinking of the height chart on the nursery wall in the shuttered house on Crown Walk. Three sets of marks, in pencil, going up the white paint. Lyra's. Rowan's. And a third set, older, from a child about two years older than Lyra, measured every birthday from two to six. And then the name beside them gouged out with a knife. And under the gouge, when he had rubbed it with a pencil, a letter. W. And a little drawn oak leaf.
He thought of the empty chair at the Midwinter dinner. For someone who couldn't come.
"Unless there was another one," Lyra said. "Unless there was another child. And she's afraid he'll remember."
Nobody answered her.
On the bed, Lord Thorne's finger went on tapping. Four. Two. Nine. Three.
Lyra looked down at the paper again. She read the bottom line. Write it over the old word. She looked at Vespera. At her father's wrist, held in Vespera's hands. At the four small letters.
"She wanted to put it on top of that," Lyra said.
"Yes," said Vespera.
"What would it have done?"
Vespera laid Lord Thorne's hand down on the blanket, gently, palm up, with the word showing. She smoothed his sleeve. Then she straightened and looked at Lyra across the bed.
"Your father came to me eleven years ago," she said. "Late at night. On his own. And he asked me to write one word on him. Here. Small, where it wouldn't be seen. I asked him why. He wouldn't tell me. He only said, I have to put something away, Vespera. Somewhere safe. Somewhere no harvester would ever think to look. And I need a lock on the door. So I wrote him a lock." She touched the edge of the blanket near his wrist. "That's what this is. Not a word, really. A lock. On a door, deep down inside him. Whatever he folded away before they burned him, it's behind that door. Still there. The burning didn't touch it. The ash didn't touch it. Only his blood can open it. That's the way he asked for it."
"And if somebody wrote over it," said Corin.
"Then the lock would be gone. And the door. And whatever's behind the door." Vespera's voice was flat. "There'd be a lie lying on top of it all like a gravestone. Nothing folded. Nothing kept. There is nothing more to find. And he'd believe it. And so would anyone who ever went looking inside him, after. Harvester. Auditor. Daughter. They'd look and look and find nothing, because the lie would tell them there was nothing to find."
Lyra sat down slowly on the edge of the bed. She still had the paper in her hand. She looked at her father's face, at his pale empty eyes looking at the ceiling.
"She was going to take him away," she said. "Today. At noon. In her carriage. To Fallowfield. And I'd have let her. I'd have had to let her. She has the papers. She's his sister. She's on the Council. If she'd come up those stairs with a Ministry order, I couldn't have stopped her. And they'd have taken him into some room in that house and written this on him and brought him back, and he'd have looked exactly the same. And I'd never, ever have known."
"Well, you know now," said Mrs. Hobb from the door. Her voice was firm and kind. "And that's what matters. Here. Eat this. Both of you. You look like a pair of ghosts."
She was holding out the bowl of porridge. Lyra shook her head. Corin took it, without really thinking, because Mrs. Hobb was the kind of woman you did not say no to. The porridge was thick and gray and still a little warm, with a pool of yellow butter melting in the middle and a sprinkle of brown sugar on top. He ate a spoonful. It was the best thing he had ever tasted. He had not known how hungry he was.
On the bed, the finger tapped.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
Corin stopped eating. He looked at the finger. Then he set down the bowl on the white table and reached out and took Lord Thorne's tapping hand in his own.
It was cold and dry and very light. Like holding a bird.
He tapped back. Softly. On the back of the thin white hand, with his own first finger.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
The tapping stopped.
For a moment the room was completely still. Corin could hear Mrs. Hobb breathing behind him. He could hear a horse stamp in the yard below. He could hear his own heart.
Lord Thorne's pale eyes moved.
Not much. Just a little. They came down from the ceiling, slowly, slowly, as if they were very heavy, and they found Corin's face. And they looked at him. Not through him. At him. For the first time.
His dry lips moved. They pressed together, and pushed out, the way they had done before, in Lyra's rooms. Making a shape.
W.
And then, very faintly, so faintly that Corin was not sure afterwards whether he had heard it or only felt it, a breath. A sound. Barely a sound.
"—ait."
Then the eyes went back to the ceiling, and the finger began to tap again.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
