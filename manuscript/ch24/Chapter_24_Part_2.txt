Chapter 24: Oren's Barrier, Part 2 of 5: The Hay Wagon
The Hay Wagon
They talked in the kitchen, because it was the only room with a fire.
It was a low, dark, smoky kitchen at the back of the house, with a stone floor and a black iron stove and a long scrubbed table down the middle. Bunches of onions hung from the beams. A row of copper pans hung on the wall, green with age. There was a dresser with blue and white plates on it, some of them cracked, and a big brown teapot on the hob, and a cat asleep on a chair by the stove, curled up so tight that it looked like a loaf of gray bread.
Old Pruett sat at the end of the table.
He was a small, bent, bald old man, with a brown face as wrinkled as a walnut and big hands with swollen knuckles. He wore a leather waistcoat over a striped shirt and he smelled strongly of horses. He was deaf in his left ear, and so he sat with his head tipped to one side, so that his right ear pointed at whoever was talking. His son, young Pruett, who was not young at all but about fifty, with a red face and a big belly, stood by the back door with his arms folded and a pitchfork leaning against the wall beside him. He had a black eye. He had got it last night, in the fight, Corin learned later. He was very proud of it.
Oren came in from the yard and stood by the door too, where he could see out. Lyra sat at the table, with the cream paper in front of her. Vespera sat by the stove, close to the warmth, with her black bundle in her lap. Juno sat on the floor by the cat, eating bread and dripping out of her hand, very fast, as if somebody might take it away from her.
Corin stood. He was too tired to sit. If he sat, he thought, he would never get up again.
He told them all of it. As quickly as he could. The rope-walk, and the false ink on the floor, and the ladder he could not see. Hollis and his jars. The paper. The fire. Gorse at the bottom of the trap door, eating his meat pie. Lady says you talk too much.
When he finished, nobody said anything for a while.
"So the lady with the lilies," said Oren at last, "has lost her ink-man."
"Yes."
"And she knows she's lost him. Because she tried to burn him."
"Yes."
"Then the carriage from Fallowfield won't come at noon." Oren's voice was slow and thoughtful. "There's no point. She can't do anything with Lord Thorne at Fallowfield now. Not today. Not without Hollis."
"Or it will come anyway," said Lyra, "and take him there, and keep him there, until she finds somebody else who can do it." She looked down at the paper. "She'll find somebody. She always does. There's always somebody who'll do anything for enough gold."
"Either way," said Corin, "he can't be here at noon."
"He can't be here at nine," said Oren. "The Ministry carriage. One guest." He looked at Corin. "I've been thinking about that all night. Standing out there under the arch with my sword. One guest, collect, Monday, nine. And an address crossed out. I don't like it. I don't like a Ministry carriage that doesn't want anybody to know where it's going."
"And he can't be here at first light," said young Pruett from the door. "Because they said they'd be back at first light. And it's first light now."
They all looked at the window. It was a small square window over the stone sink, with green bottle-glass in it. Through the glass, the sky over the stable roofs was a pale, cold, watery gray. Getting lighter every minute.
"Then we move him now," said Corin. "Mrs. Hobb. Can he be moved? In the daylight? If he's wrapped up warm?"
Mrs. Hobb was standing at the stove, stirring something in a pot. She did not turn round. "He can be moved," she said. "If he's wrapped up warm and laid flat and nobody shakes him about. His heart's tired, Mr. Vane. It's not broken. It's just tired. Like the rest of him. Don't make it run, and it'll go on ticking."
"Where to?" said Lyra.
"The river," said Corin. "The coal wharf. A woman called Hessa Doon has a barge there. The Patient Grace. Black, with a red sail. Juno took her a message last night. She said she'd take him. She said nobody looks twice at a coal barge."
"Nobody looks once at a coal barge," said Juno, through a mouthful of bread. "That's why it's good."
"How far?" said Oren.
"A mile. Maybe a bit more. Down through Saint Bride's, past the fish market, along Wharf Street."
"In the daylight. Through the Docks. With an old man who can't walk."
"Yes."
Oren looked at him for a long moment. His gray eyes were cold and steady. Then he nodded once.
"How?"
Corin had not thought about how. He opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He was so tired that his head felt like it was full of wet wool, and every thought in it moved very slowly, like a fish under ice.
It was old Pruett who answered.
"Hay," said old Pruett.
Everyone turned and looked at him.
"Hay," he said again. He had a high, cracked old voice, like a gate that needs oiling. He tapped one swollen finger on the table. "Monday morning. Every Monday morning for forty-one years, I've sent a load of hay down to the coal wharf. For the barge horses. The ones that pull the barges up the canal to the north locks. Big old things, they are. Eat like nobody's business." He nodded slowly. "Everybody in the Docks knows Pruett's hay wagon. It goes down Linden Walk and through Saint Bride's and along Wharf Street every Monday at seven, regular as a church bell. Nobody looks at it. Nobody's looked at it for forty-one years."
"You want to put him in the hay," said Lyra slowly.
"Under it," said old Pruett. "There's a space. Under the boards at the front. Where I used to keep—" He stopped, and coughed, and looked at his son. "Where I used to keep things. When I was younger. Things that didn't need the Customs men looking at them."
Young Pruett grinned. It made his black eye wrinkle.
"Brandy," he said. "From the Ostmark boats. Dad was a terror in his day."
"Never you mind what I was," said old Pruett. "It's dry under there. And it's long enough for a man to lie flat, if he's not too tall. We'll put straw down, and blankets, and the hay on top, and the boards on top of that, and nobody'll ever know."
Corin looked at Mrs. Hobb. Mrs. Hobb looked at the pot she was stirring. Then she put down her spoon and turned around.
"I'll lie in with him," she said.
"Mrs. Hobb—"
"Don't you Mrs. Hobb me, Mr. Vane. I'll lie in with him, with a hot brick wrapped in flannel, and I'll keep my hand on his chest the whole way. If his heart starts to run, I'll know. And I'll knock on the boards." She folded her big arms. "I'm not having him shut in a box under a pile of hay on his own. Not after everything. He's been shut in enough boxes."
Nobody argued with her. Corin did not think anybody in the room would have dared.
"Seven o'clock," said Oren. "That's an hour."
"Less," said young Pruett, looking at the window.
"Then we start now." Oren pushed himself off the doorframe. "Pruett. Get the wagon out and get it loaded. Leave the space at the front open. Lyra, get your father ready. Blankets. Everything warm you've got. Mrs. Hobb, the brick." He looked at Vespera, by the stove. "You. Can you walk a mile?"
"I walked across the roofs of half the Lower Docks tonight," said Vespera, without opening her eyes. "I expect I can manage a pavement."
"Good. You'll walk beside the wagon. With Lyra. Two women going to the fish market. Nobody looks at that either." His eyes went to Corin. "And you'll stay here with me."
Corin stared at him.
"Here?"
"Somebody has to be here," said Oren. "When they come. If they come to this gate and find nobody, they'll know he's gone. And they'll go looking. They'll look at every cart on every road between here and the river, and sooner or later somebody's going to look at Pruett's hay." He shook his head. "But if they come to this gate and find me standing in it, the same as last night, they'll think he's still upstairs. They'll fight. And while they're fighting, the wagon goes down Linden Walk at seven o'clock, the same as every Monday for forty-one years, and nobody looks at it at all."
It was quiet in the kitchen. The fire crackled in the stove. The cat stretched, and yawned, and curled up again.
"That's not a plan," said Lyra. Her voice was tight. "That's you standing in a gate and getting killed."
"It's a plan," said Oren. "It's an old one. Soldiers have been doing it for a thousand years. You hold the gate, so the others can get away. It's a good plan. It works." He paused. "Mostly."
"Then I'll stay too."
"No. You'll go with your father. If anything happens on the road, he needs his blood beside him. You heard Vespera. Only his blood can open it." He looked at Corin again. "Vane stays. Because they want Vane. They've wanted Vane since he sent the lady his answer last night. If they see him here, in the yard, they won't be thinking about Lord Thorne at all. They'll be thinking about how much she'll pay for the man who eats Ash."
Corin found that he was nodding. He did not remember deciding to. But he was nodding, slowly, and his mouth was dry.
"Juno," he said.
She looked up from the floor, with bread in her hand.
"I made a promise," he said. "To Warden Briggs. At the Ministry. That I'd be in his hall at six o'clock this morning to clear the glasses, the same as every day. I can't keep it. Will you go and find him? Not at the front. At the side door, by the slag-room stairs, where the carts come. Tell him I'm sorry. Tell him I'll come when I can. And then go to the coal wharf, and wait for the wagon, and help Hessa get him on board."
Juno looked at him for a long moment. Then she got up off the floor, and wiped her hands on her trousers, and pushed the last of her bread into her mouth.
"You'll come?" she said, with her mouth full. "After? To the barge?"
"I'll come."
"Promise?"
He thought of all the promises he had made in the last two weeks. Some he had kept. Some he had not. Some he could not remember making at all.
"I promise," he said.
Juno nodded. And then she was gone, out of the back door, across the yard, under the arch, quick and light as a sparrow, with the brass key bouncing on its string against her chest.
First Light
They loaded the wagon in the long shed at the back of the yard, where nobody passing in the street could see.
It was a big old farm wagon, with four tall wheels painted red, and high wooden sides, and a long pole at the front for the horses. The paint was faded and chipped, and the iron bands on the wheels were worn thin and shining. On the side, in white letters, someone had painted PRUETT a long time ago, and someone else had painted it again more recently, a little crooked, over the top. Two great brown horses stood in front of it, already in their harness, with their heads down and their breath steaming. They had feathery white hair around their hooves, and backs so broad that a man could have lain down on them and gone to sleep.
Old Pruett pulled up the boards at the front of the wagon bed. Underneath, there was a long, low, dark space, like a coffin with no lid. It smelled of old wood and dust and, very faintly, of brandy.
They laid straw in it first, a thick soft layer. Then blankets. Then Mrs. Hobb climbed in, slowly, grumbling, with her skirts bunched up around her knees, and lay down on her side at the back of the space, with a hot brick wrapped in red flannel in her arms.
Then Oren and young Pruett carried Lord Thorne down.
They carried him on a door. It was Lyra's idea. They took the door of the white room off its hinges, and laid blankets on it, and laid him on the blankets, and wrapped him up so that only his face showed. Then they carried him down the narrow creaking stairs, very slowly, Oren at the head and young Pruett at the foot, with Lyra walking beside him holding his hand. His eyes were open. He did not seem to know that he was moving. He looked up at the ceiling of the stairs, and then at the gray sky over the yard, and then at the dark roof of the shed, the same way he had looked at the ceiling of the white room. As if they were all the same. As if nothing in the world was any different from anything else.
Only his finger moved. Tapping on Lyra's palm. Four. Two. Nine. Three.
They lifted him off the door and laid him down in the space beside Mrs. Hobb. She put one big arm over him at once, and pulled the blankets up to his chin, and tucked the hot brick in against his side. She put her other hand flat on his chest, over his heart.
"There," she said softly. "There now. That's not so bad. That's cozy."
Lyra knelt on the edge of the wagon bed and looked down at them both. She put the little shell box in beside her father's hand, with the cracked blue stone inside it. She did not say anything. Her face was very still.
"He'll be all right," said Mrs. Hobb, looking up at her. "I've got him."
"I know," said Lyra.
Then they put the boards back. Corin watched the dark space close over the two pale faces, one round and red and one thin and white, until there was only a crack of darkness left, and then nothing. Just boards. Old Pruett knocked the pegs back into the holes with a wooden mallet, tock, tock, tock, tock, and put his ear down to the wood.
"All right in there?" he called.
A knock from underneath. Two knocks. Mrs. Hobb's knock, firm and cross.
"She's all right," said old Pruett, with satisfaction.
Then the hay.
They forked it up from the big pile at the back of the shed. Young Pruett and Oren and Corin, all three of them, with long wooden pitchforks, as fast as they could. It was good hay, sweet-smelling and green-gold, full of dry flowers and seeds that got into Corin's hair and down the back of his neck and up his sleeves. They piled it on the wagon bed until the boards were buried and the hay was higher than the sides, a great soft golden hill of it, and then young Pruett threw a net over the top and tied it down at the corners.
It looked like a hay wagon. That was all. It looked exactly like a hay wagon going down to the coal wharf on a Monday morning, the same as it had looked every Monday morning for forty-one years.
"Twenty to seven," said old Pruett, looking at a big brass watch he pulled out of his waistcoat. "Early. But not too early."
He climbed up onto the high seat at the front, slowly, with his son pushing him from behind. He took the reins. He put on a battered old hat with a wide brim and pulled it down over his eyes. Then he looked down at Lyra and Vespera.
"Walk on the left side," he said. "Close to the wheel. Not too close. Talk about fish."
"I don't know anything about fish," said Lyra.
"Then talk about anything. Talk about the price of bread. Talk about your mother's bad leg. Just talk. Two women talking is the most ordinary thing in the world. Nobody ever listens."
Lyra almost smiled. She pulled a shawl up over her dark hair. She had changed out of the man's coat into a plain brown one of Mrs. Hobb's, much too big, with the sleeves rolled up. Vespera stood beside her, small and gray, with her own shawl gone and the black wool bundle hidden under her coat. Neither of them looked like a lord's daughter or an ink-binder. They looked like two tired women from the Lower Docks, on their way to market.
Lyra looked at Corin.
"Come to the barge," she said. "After. When it's done."
"I will."
"You said that to Juno too."
"I meant it to Juno too."
She looked at him a moment longer. Then she turned to Oren.
"And you," she said. "Don't be a fool."
Oren looked down at her. His scarred face did not change.
"I'm a soldier, my lady," he said. "It's the same thing."
Old Pruett clicked his tongue. The two great horses leaned into their collars. The wagon creaked, and groaned, and began to roll. Out of the shed, across the cobbled yard, toward the arch. The tall red wheels turned slowly, crunching on the stones. Lyra and Vespera walked beside the left front wheel, their heads close together, talking. Corin could not hear what they said.
The wagon went under the arch and out into Linden Walk.
Corin and Oren went after it, as far as the arch, and stood in the shadow and watched.
The street was waking up. There was a woman on her knees on the front step of the house opposite, scrubbing it with a brush and a bucket. There was a boy going along with a tray of hot rolls on his head, calling out Rolls! Hot rolls! in a high cracked voice. There was a man in a long coat walking a small white dog. The wagon went past all of them. Slowly. The horses' great hooves clopped on the stones, and the wheels rumbled, and the hay swayed a little, golden in the gray light. The woman on the step did not look up. The boy with the rolls stepped out of the way without thinking about it. The man with the dog did not even turn his head.
Nobody looked at Pruett's hay.
The wagon went on down Linden Walk, toward the two old trees at the far end, toward the corner where the street turned left into Saint Bride's.
And round that corner, coming the other way, came the men.
Corin saw them first. He felt his whole body go cold.
There were a lot of them. More than he could count at once. They came round the corner from Saint Bride's in a crowd, not marching, just walking, the way men walk when they are going to work. They wore the ordinary clothes of the Docks, rough coats and caps and heavy boots. Most of them carried something in their hands. Clubs. Iron bars. Short heavy sticks with nails driven through the ends. One of them had a long hooked pole, like a boat hook. Two of them, near the front, were pushing a handcart, and on the handcart there was a great thick log with iron bands round it and handles nailed onto the sides.
A ram. For a gate.
At the front of them all, walking a little ahead, there was a small neat man in a brown cardigan with a coat over it, and a pair of gold spectacles on his nose. Mister Gant. Somebody had found him, then, tied to his own chair in his own warm room at the Bell, and cut him free. There were red marks round both his small pale wrists, where the cardigan sleeves had held him. He was not hurrying. He walked with his hands folded behind his back, like a clerk taking the air on a Sunday, and his neat face was calm and polite and a little puzzled, as if the whole morning were a sum that had not quite come out right.
And beside him, a little behind, there was a woman.
She was tall. Very tall, and very thin, taller than any of the men around her. She wore a long gray apron, like a shopkeeper's apron, over a black dress, and she had gray gloves on her hands. Her hair was pulled back very tight from her face, and her face was long and pale and quite empty, like a plate. Round her waist there was a belt. And hanging from the belt, in little leather loops, all the way round, there were glass vials. Dozens of them. Each one no bigger than a finger. Each one full of something that glowed. Some of them glowed red. Some of them glowed blue. Some of them were a pale, sickly green.
"Teller," said Oren softly, beside him. His voice had gone flat and hard. "From the Bank. A burner. She doesn't burn her own. She buys them. Other people's memories, in bottles. She drinks them, and she burns them, and she never feels a thing."
"Is that the whole downstairs?"
"No," said Oren. "That's the front of it."
The crowd came on down Linden Walk. And the hay wagon went on up Linden Walk to meet them.
Corin could not breathe.
He watched the wagon roll toward the crowd. He watched old Pruett on the high seat, in his battered hat, with the reins loose in his hands, not looking left or right. He watched Lyra and Vespera walking beside the wheel, their heads still close together, still talking. He watched the gap between the wagon and the men get smaller, and smaller, and smaller.
The men did not stop. They did not spread out across the street. They did not even slow down. The crowd simply opened in the middle, the way water opens round a rock, and flowed round the wagon on both sides. One of the men put his hand up and slapped the flank of the nearest horse as he went past, the way a man does without thinking, and the horse flicked its ear. Another one shouted something up at old Pruett, something friendly and rude, and old Pruett lifted one hand from the reins and waved it at him without turning his head.
Gant walked right past Lyra. So close that his sleeve brushed her shawl.
He did not look at her.
The tall woman in the gray apron walked right past Vespera. Her empty pale face turned, for a moment, toward the wagon. Toward the great golden hill of hay. Corin saw her eyes move over it. Slowly. Her nose lifted a little, as if she had smelled something.
Then she looked away. And walked on.
The crowd closed up again behind the wagon. And the wagon went on. Past the two old trees. Round the corner. Into Saint Bride's.
And was gone.
Corin let out a breath that he had been holding for so long that his chest hurt.
"Forty-one years," said Oren quietly. "Nobody looks at Pruett's hay."
Then he stepped out of the shadow of the arch, into the middle of the gateway, and planted his feet on the cobbles, and lifted his sword.
Gant saw him. Corin saw the gold spectacles flash, and the neat face turn toward the arch. He saw Gant stop walking. He saw him unfold his hands from behind his back. He saw him smile, a small, mild, polite smile.
"Good morning, Captain," Gant called down the street. His voice was soft and mild, but it carried a long way in the still morning air. "How nice to see you up and about. And Mister Vane, too, I see." His eyes went to Corin behind the gold spectacles and stayed there. "The lady sends her regards. She was very sorry you couldn't come to the kitchen door last night with her books. She'd like to talk it over. Properly, this time. A thing has to be said properly, or it isn't sold."
The men behind him had stopped too. They were spreading out now, across the width of the street, from railing to railing. Corin heard the sounds of it. Boots scraping. Clubs knocking against iron. Somebody laughing. The two men with the handcart pushed it forward, and the great iron-bound log rolled on its bed, heavy and slow.
The woman scrubbing her step on the other side of the street picked up her bucket and went inside and shut the door. The boy with the rolls had vanished. The man with the white dog had vanished. All along Linden Walk, Corin heard doors closing. Windows closing. Shutters coming down. Bolts sliding home.
The street was empty now, except for the men.
And the tall woman in the gray apron reached down to her belt, slowly, with one gray-gloved hand, and unhooked a vial that glowed red.
