Chapter 24: Oren's Barrier, Part 4 of 5: Wharf Street
Wharf Street
Sergeant Moll walked with Corin as far as the fish market.
Oren stayed behind. He did not want to. He stood up from the kitchen table and said he was coming, and then he sat down again, quite suddenly, because his legs would not hold him. Young Pruett put a hand on his shoulder and said, "You'll sit, Captain, or I'll sit on you," and Oren looked up at him with his cold gray eyes and, after a long moment, sat. Corin had never seen anybody make Oren do anything before. He thought it was probably the black eye. A man with a black eye that he was proud of was a hard man to argue with.
"Somebody has to be here at nine," Oren said, from under his blanket. "When the carriage comes. In case."
"In case of what?"
"I don't know. That's why it's in case."
So Corin went with Moll.
The city was fully awake now. The sun had come up over the roofs of the Lower Docks while they were in the kitchen, and it was a thin, pale, watery sun, low in a sky the color of skimmed milk, with no warmth in it at all. But it was light. Real light. After the long night, after the dark rope-walk and the dark roofs and the gray dawn, it felt to Corin like coming up out of deep water.
Saint Bride's was full of people. It was a wide crooked street that ran down the hill from Linden Walk toward the river, with shops on both sides and the tall gray tower of Saint Bride's church standing at the bottom, where the street opened out into a little square. The shops were opening. Men in aprons were taking down shutters and pulling out awnings. A baker had put trays of loaves out on a table in front of his door, and the smell of them filled the whole street, warm and brown and sweet. A woman was selling hot chestnuts from a brazier on a corner, and the smoke from it went up blue into the cold air. There were carts and barrows and donkeys. There were children running. There was a man with a monkey on a chain, and a girl selling matches, and a very old woman sitting on a step with a basket of eggs in her lap, calling out, "Fresh eggs, new-laid, fresh eggs," in a thin cracked voice, over and over, like a bird.
And everybody was talking about the fire.
Corin heard it everywhere. In the baker's doorway. At the chestnut brazier. Between two women with baskets on their arms, standing in the middle of the street so that the carts had to go round them.
"—the old rope-walk, in Fuller's Yard—"
"—burned right down to the ground, they say, not a stick left—"
"—and the funny thing was the smoke. My Tom saw it. He said there were colors in it. Gold and blue and all sorts. Like a rainbow, he said, going up—"
"—my little one woke up this morning laughing. Laughing! Said she'd dreamed about the seaside. She's never been to the seaside in her life—"
Corin stopped walking.
Moll stopped too, and looked at him.
"What?" said Moll.
"Nothing," said Corin. He found that he was smiling. It felt strange on his face, stiff and tired. "Nothing. Just something somebody said."
They walked on.
Moll was quiet for a while. He walked with his hands deep in the pockets of his blue coat and his cold pipe in his teeth and his hat, which one of his men had found in the gutter of Linden Walk, pulled down crooked on his head. He was a big slow man, with a face like a pink potato and small clever eyes. He did not look at Corin when he talked. He looked straight ahead, down the street, at the church tower.
"I'll have to write it up," he said at last. "This morning. Linden Walk. A riot at a livery stable, twenty men, one injured. I'll have to put it in the book."
"What will you say?"
"That it was a quarrel about money. Between dockers. That's what it'll look like to anyone who reads it. Dockers fighting about money in the Lower Docks." Moll took the pipe out of his mouth and looked at it and put it back. "Nobody'll read it twice. Nobody ever does."
"And the woman? The one with the vials?"
"What woman?" said Moll. "I didn't see any woman." He walked on a few steps. "And neither did any of my lads. Because if any of my lads saw a Bank Teller burning stolen memories in a public street, then I'd have to write that up too. And then somebody up the Hill would read it. And then there'd be questions. And then there'd be a man in a gray coat at the Watch house asking who else saw it." He shook his head. "No. No woman. Just dockers. Fighting about money."
Corin looked at him.
"You're protecting them," he said slowly. "The Bank."
"I'm protecting you," said Moll. "And the captain. And the old man in the hay. And the girl with the knife. And the little one with the key on the string, who came banging on the back gate of my Watch house at twenty past six this morning, knocking fast, the way I told her, and said there'd be trouble in Linden Walk any minute, and to bring every man I'd got. And then ran off up the Hill before I could ask her a single thing." He snorted. "Thirty-one years in the Watch. Never been told what to do by a child before."
"Juno," said Corin.
"Is that her name? She wouldn't tell me. Said you'd told her not to give it to anybody in a blue coat." Moll almost smiled. "Clever girl. You want to keep hold of her."
They came down into the little square at the bottom of the hill, where Saint Bride's church stood. The church was very old, older than most of the Docks, built of gray stone gone black with soot, with a square tower and a great round window over the door. The bell in the tower was silent now. It had rung half the night, Moll said, for the fire. On the steps of the church a priest in a black robe was sweeping, slowly, with a broom made of twigs. He looked up as they passed. He had a young face and very tired eyes.
Past the church, the street went down again, steeper now, toward the river. And then it opened out, all at once, into the fish market.
Corin had been to the fish market before. Everyone in the Docks had. But he had never been at this hour of the morning, when it was busiest, and he stopped at the top of the steps for a moment and simply looked.
It was a great wide open place, as big as a field, paved with old gray stones that were wet and shining and slippery with fish scales. Over it there was a roof, held up on iron pillars, a long roof of glass and iron that let the pale light down onto everything below. And under the roof there were stalls. Hundreds of them. Long wooden tables in rows, and on every table there were fish. Silver fish and gray fish and spotted fish. Long thin fish like knives and fat round fish like loaves. Great flat fish as big as a cartwheel, with both their eyes on one side of their heads. Crabs, in heaps, red and brown, still moving, their legs waving slowly. Eels in barrels, black and twisting. Oysters in sacks. Mussels in baskets, blue-black and shining. There was ice everywhere, crushed ice in heaps and ice in blocks, and the water running off it ran down the stones in little streams into gutters at the sides.
And the noise. Everybody was shouting. The fishwives behind the stalls, big women with red arms and leather aprons, shouting their prices. The porters with baskets on their heads, shouting at people to get out of the way. The buyers, shouting back. Gulls, hundreds of gulls, wheeling and screaming under the glass roof and on the iron beams, waiting for scraps. And under it all, from the riverside of the market, the sound of boats. Oars knocking. Ropes creaking. Men calling to each other across the water.
And the smell. Corin had forgotten the smell. Fish, and salt, and wet stone, and tar, and the river itself, brown and cold and old.
Moll stopped at the top of the steps.
"This is as far as I go," he said. "I've got a riot to write up." He looked at Corin for the first time, straight on, with his small clever eyes. "Wharf Street's on the far side. Go out by the eel barrels and turn left. The coal wharf's at the end. You'll smell it before you see it."
"Thank you," said Corin. "For this morning."
"Don't thank me. Just don't make me write anything else up today." Moll put his pipe back in his mouth. Then he took it out again. "Vane."
"Yes?"
"That Ministry carriage. At nine. The one the captain keeps talking about." Moll's voice was quiet. "I asked about it. Last night. Quietly. A friend of mine drives for the Ministry stables up the Hill. He says there's a carriage booked out for Linden Walk at nine this morning, right enough. Black, with no crest. And he says the order for it wasn't signed by a clerk."
"Who signed it?"
Moll looked at him.
"It was signed in gray ink," he said. "That's all he'd tell me. Gray ink. And a little drawing next to the name. A sweet. A barley sugar, in a twist of paper."
Corin felt the cold go through him, all the way down to his boots.
I want to see who he runs to.
"Kross," he said.
"I didn't say that," said Moll. "And neither did you." He touched the brim of his crooked hat. "Go and see your old man on his barge. And then, if I were you, I'd be a long way from Linden Walk at nine o'clock."
He turned and went back up the hill toward the church, slow and heavy, with his hands in his pockets.
Corin stood at the top of the steps for a long moment, in the noise and the smell and the pale cold light.
Then he went down into the fish market, and out by the eel barrels, and turned left.
Patient Grace
Moll was right. He smelled the coal wharf before he saw it.
It was a thick, black, dusty smell, dry and bitter, that got into the back of his throat and made him want to cough. It was the smell of the inside of a chimney. Of a stove that has not been cleaned in a year. And it came with a sound, a rumbling, rattling, sliding roar, over and over, like a great beast clearing its throat.
Wharf Street ran along the edge of the river, behind a long low wall of black stone. On one side of it there were warehouses, tall and blind and sooty, with great doors and iron cranes sticking out over the street from their top floors. On the other side, over the wall, there was the river. Brown and wide and slow and cold, with a thin mist still lying on it in places, and the far bank only a gray line in the distance.
At the end of Wharf Street, the coal wharf stuck out into the water like a long black tongue.
It was a wide stone pier, with railway lines running down the middle of it, and on the railway lines there were little iron wagons, piled high with coal. Men in black from head to foot, black faces and black hands and black clothes, were tipping the wagons over the edge of the pier into the barges below. That was the roar. The coal went down out of the wagons in a great black slide, with a cloud of dust going up off it, and fell into the open holds of the barges, and the barges sank a little lower in the water with every load. There were a dozen barges along the side of the pier. Long, low, black boats, all alike, with their decks hardly higher than the water, and stumpy masts, and their sails rolled up and tied.
Corin walked out along the edge of the pier, keeping out of the way of the wagons. Nobody looked at him. He was black enough with soot and coal dust from the night that he looked as if he belonged there.
He was looking for a red sail.
He did not find one on the pier. He found it below it. At the very end of the wharf, where the stone pier stopped and the river began, there were steps. Old worn stone steps, green with weed at the bottom, going down into the water. And tied up at the bottom of the steps, away from the others, in the shadow of the pier, there was a barge.
It was black, like the others. Long and low. But it was cleaner than the others. The paint on its sides was fresh, and the brass on its little cabin door was polished, and there were pots of herbs, frosted and drooping in the cold, standing in a row along the roof of the cabin. A thin thread of smoke was going up from a tin chimney. And its sail, rolled up tight along its boom and tied with cord, was a deep rusty red, the color of old bricks.
On its stern, in neat white letters, it said PATIENT GRACE.
And on the deck, at the top of the little ladder that went down into the cabin, sitting on an upturned bucket with a baby on her knee, there was Hessa Doon.
She was a broad, short, solid woman, with a weathered brown face and gray hair cut short like a man's. She wore a man's canvas jacket and a long woolen skirt and boots. The baby on her knee was wrapped in so many shawls that only its face showed, round and pink and fat, with a little knitted cap on top. It was chewing very seriously on the end of a wooden spoon.
Hessa saw him coming down the steps. She did not get up.
"You took your time," she said.
"People keep saying that to me today."
"People are right." She jerked her head toward the cabin. "He's in there. With the big lady. And the girl, and the little ink woman. They came an hour ago. Old Pruett brought them right down to the top of the steps, cool as you like, and stopped his wagon, and got down to see to his horse's foot, and while he was doing it, Wat had the boards up and the old gentleman out and down the steps and into my cabin before you could say fish." She looked approving. "Pruett's got nerve, for an old man. Then he got back up on his seat and drove on down to the horse-pens and unloaded his hay, like any Monday."
"Where's Wat?"
"Gone up to the horse-pens with him. To help with the hay. So it looks right." Hessa shifted the baby on her knee. "Robin, stop that. That's my good spoon."
The baby took the spoon out of its mouth, looked at Corin very solemnly with big round dark eyes, and put the spoon back in again.
Corin found that he was sitting down. He did not remember deciding to. He was sitting on the bottom step, with his boots almost in the river, and his back against the cold stone of the pier, and he could not have got up again if the Bank had come down Wharf Street with every Teller it owned.
Hessa looked at him. Then she got up, with the baby on her hip, and went down the ladder into the cabin. He heard voices. Then she came back up with a tin mug in her free hand and held it out to him.
It was tea. Black and very strong, with a great deal of sugar in it, and something else, something hot and sharp that burned all the way down.
"Rum," said Hessa. "Not much. Drink it."
He drank it.
Lyra came up the ladder while he was drinking. She came and sat on the step beside him. She had taken off Mrs. Hobb's brown coat, and she was wearing the man's coat again, the one she had been wearing at Pruett's door, with the sleeves rolled up. There was coal dust on her face now too. She looked at him for a long moment without saying anything.
"Oren?" she said at last.
"Alive. Sitting in Pruett's kitchen with a blanket round him, drinking tea. Young Pruett won't let him get up."
She let out a long breath.
"What did it cost him?"
"A word," said Corin. "Spoon."
Lyra looked at the baby on Hessa's hip. At the wooden spoon in its fat pink fist.
"Spoon," she said quietly. She did not say anything else. She did not need to.
"And your father?" said Corin.
"Sleeping." She looked down at the cabin. "Mrs. Hobb says his heart didn't run. Not once. Not the whole way. She says the hay was like a featherbed and the wagon was like a cradle and she nearly went to sleep herself." She almost smiled. "She says he tapped the whole way, though. On her arm. Four, two, nine, three. All the way down Saint Bride's."
"Did he say anything else?"
Lyra looked at him sharply. "Else?"
Corin hesitated. Then he told her. About the moment in the white room, when he had tapped the numbers back on her father's hand, and the tapping had stopped, and the pale eyes had come down from the ceiling and looked at him. The W. And the breath that might have been a word. —ait.
Lyra sat very still.
"Wait," she said slowly. "Or—" She stopped.
"Or a name," said Corin. "Something that starts with W. And ends like that."
They sat side by side on the cold step, with the river lapping at their boots, and thought about it. Neither of them said what they were both thinking. The marks on the nursery wall. The third child. The gouged-out name. The little oak leaf.
"He's been trying to tell us," Lyra said at last. "All this time. Tapping and tapping on the wall. And none of us could hear him."
"We'll hear him," said Corin. "Sooner or later. Vespera says the lock's still whole. Whatever he folded away, it's still there. And it's still his. Nobody's written over it."
"Because of you."
"Because of Hollis," Corin said. "Mostly. And a jar he couldn't stop holding."
Vespera came up the ladder then.
She came up slowly, the way she did everything, and stood on the deck at the top, small and gray in her black coat. The black wool bundle was not in her arms anymore. Corin did not see it anywhere. He guessed it was inside her coat, against her chest, where she could feel it.
"I'm going home," she said. "To Hook Lane. I have a shop. I have customers. A woman is coming at ten for her husband's name on her wrist, and I have not slept, and I have not washed, and I smell like a bonfire." She looked at Corin. "You smell worse."
"I know."
"Go and wash. Go and sleep. Don't do anything else foolish today." She paused. "And Corin."
"Yes?"
"Thank you. For climbing the ladder."
Then she went up the steps, past him, slowly, holding the cold wall with one hand. At the top she stopped and looked back down at the barge for a moment. At the cabin, with the old man asleep inside it. Then she turned and went away along the coal wharf, small and straight and gray, through the black dust and the roar of the wagons, and was gone.
Hessa had been watching her go.
"That's a woman who's had a long night," she said.
"We all have."
"Not all of you." Hessa nodded up the steps. "Here comes one who looks like she's had a short one and enjoyed every minute."
It was Juno.
She came down the wharf at a run. Not dodging the coal wagons, but going between them, quick and light, so that the black-faced men tipping them shouted at her and she shouted something rude back. She came down the steps two at a time and stopped at the bottom, panting, with her face pink under the soot and her short hair sticking up and the brass key bouncing on its string.
"He's all right?" she said. "The old one?"
"He's all right."
"And Oren?"
"All right. Lost a word. But all right."
She let out a breath. Then she turned to Corin, and her face changed. It went serious. Older.
"I found Briggs," she said. "At the side door. Like you said. By the slag-room stairs." She swallowed. "He said to tell you he's not angry about the glasses. He said he'll clear them himself. He said—" She stopped, and frowned, the way she did when she was getting something word for word. "He said, Tell him not to come in today. Not for anything. Tell him somebody came to my stand at six o'clock this morning and asked for him. By name. Asked where he was. Asked what time he usually came. And then asked to see his intake card."
Corin set down the tin mug on the step very carefully.
"Who?" he said.
"He didn't say a name. He said I'd know her when I saw her. He said she wore white gloves." Juno looked at him. "And he said she has a gold tooth. At the front. And when she asked for your card, she took off one glove, and put her bare hand flat on it, and shut her eyes. And when she opened them again, Briggs said, she looked very tired. As if she'd been up all night. And she said—"
Juno stopped again. Her frown deepened.
"She said, Thank you, Warden. That's all I need. And she wrote something in a little book. And went away up the stairs."
Nobody said anything.
On the deck, the baby took the spoon out of its mouth and banged it on Hessa's shoulder, three times, tap, tap, tap, and laughed.
Corin looked at his own hands. Black with coal dust. Cut across the back from the broken window. Shaking, just a little.
He knew who wore white gloves and had a gold tooth at the front, and burned a whole day of her own life every time she put her bare hand on a thing and read it.
