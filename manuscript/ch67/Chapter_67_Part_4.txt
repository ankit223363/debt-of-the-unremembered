Chapter 67: Vespera Comes North, Part 4 of 5: The Man at the Door
The Man at the Door
There was a man at the door.
Corin saw him when young Vespera turned her head. Just for a moment, while the woman in the bed lay back gasping between one push and the next. Young Vespera's eyes went to the door, quick, the way you glance at a clock, and Corin saw what they saw.
The door of the cottage was across the room from the bed, by the window. A heavy wooden door, low, with a latch of black iron and a bar that could be dropped across it into two iron brackets. The bar was not down. The door was open. Just a little. A hand's width. And the night came in through the gap, cold and wet, and the sound of the rain came in louder, and the sound of the river.
And in the gap, a man stood with his back to the room.
He was tall. He had to stoop under the low lintel. He wore a long dark coat, wet through at the shoulders, and his hair was dark and wet and stuck to the back of his neck. Corin could not see his face. He never turned round. He stood in the doorway with one hand on the edge of the door, holding it, and the other hand at his side, and looked out. Out into the dark. Into the rain.
At the road.
Corin could see it, past the man's shoulder, through the gap in the door. Or he could see where it was. A paler line in the dark, going away from the cottage, up a slope, between black shapes that might have been hedges or trees. A road. Empty. Shining a little, wet, in the faint light from the window behind him.
The man was watching it. Not glancing. Watching. The way a man watches a road when he is waiting for something to come down it that he does not want to come. His whole body was still. Only his hand on the edge of the door moved. The fingers tightened, and loosened, and tightened.
He did not turn round when the woman cried out. Corin saw his back go stiff. Saw his head come up, and his shoulders, and his hand grip the door hard. But he did not turn round. He went on looking at the road.
"Anything?" said young Vespera. Low. Over her shoulder.
"No." The man's voice was low too. Rough. Tired. Like the woman's voice, it was blurred at the edges. Corin could hear the word but not what the voice was like. "Nothing. Not yet."
"They won't come in this."
"They came in worse at Harlow's Cross."
Young Vespera did not answer that.
"If they come," said the man, "you take him out the back. Through the yard and over the wall and down to the water. Don't wait for me. Don't wait for her. Take him and go."
"I know."
"Say it."
"I take him out the back," said young Vespera. "Over the wall. Down to the water. I don't wait."
The man nodded. Once. Without turning round.
Corin, inside the young red-haired body, felt something go through it that was not his own. A cold, tight feeling, low in the stomach. Fear. Vespera's fear. Young Vespera, kneeling by a bed in a cottage by a river, with her hands full of a woman's hands, afraid.
Of the road.
Of what might come down it.
Corin knew. He knew without being told. He knew the way you know the end of a story you have heard before, even if you do not remember hearing it. Men in long gray coats. Coming down a wet road in the dark. With lamps. With rods. Coming to a cottage by a river. Coming for what was in it.
Gray coats.
He had worked beside them. For years. He had walked into cellars behind them and taken what they held down for him. He had been one of them, in everything but the coat.
He felt the river pull at him then. Not the river outside. The other river. The one Vespera had told him to stand in. The memory, going past him, cold and fast. It was pulling. It wanted him to reach out. To grab hold of it. To drink. To take the man at the door and turn him round, by force, and see his face. To take the woman in the bed and wipe the blur off her like steam off a window, and see her. Who she was. Who she...
He wanted it so badly that his hands closed.
Not young Vespera's hands. His own. Somewhere far away, on a fur, in a tent, in the dark. He felt them close.
And from very far away, from somewhere up above the rain and the thatch and the dark, a voice said his name.
Not loud. Not yet. Low and steady. A warning.
"Corin."
He let go.
He made his hands open again. It was like the morning, under the black thorn tree. The hardest thing. Opening his hands when everything in him wanted to close them. Letting the leaves go by.
The man at the door did not turn round. The woman's face stayed blurred. And Corin let them. He stood in the cold river of Vespera's memory and let them go past him, unseen, the way they had been kept. Wrapped up. Soft. Put away by someone who had wanted them kept from him.
Or kept for him.
He did not know which.
The woman in the bed cried out again. The longest yet. And young Vespera turned back from the door, fast, and bent over the bed, and her quick hands went down under the blankets, and she said, in a voice suddenly sharp and bright and full of something that was not fear at all:
"There. There. That's it. Oh, that's it, my love. He's here. He's here."
And there was a new sound in the room.
Thin and high and furious. A small voice, new to the air, crying out against the cold and the light and the hugeness of everything.
And the man at the door, for the first time, bowed his head.
The Mark
Young Vespera held the baby.
She held him in her two quick freckled hands, wet and red and furious, the way you hold something that might break. And Corin, inside her, felt the weight of him. It was nothing. Lighter than a loaf. Lighter than a cat. A small hot slippery weight, kicking, with a voice much too big for it.
She cleaned him. Fast, with a cloth from the basin of hot water on the hearth, wiping his face and his eyes and his small creased fists. She did it without thinking, the way she did everything, and talked to him while she did it, low and quick, in her soft northern voice. There now. There now, little one. That's a fine noise. That's a fine big noise. Let them hear you in the city.
The woman in the bed was crying. Not in pain now. Corin could hear the difference. She had her blurred face turned toward the baby and her arms out from the blankets, reaching.
"Give him..." she said. "Let me..."
"In a moment, love. In a moment. One thing first."
And young Vespera turned, with the baby in the crook of one arm, to the table by the hearth.
There was a roll of leather on it. Corin knew it at once. He had seen it in Vespera's shop under the Dye-Works, and on her rope belt, and he had seen it this very day, laid on the furs in Kestrel's tent. Older now. Then it was new. A roll of soft brown leather, tied with a thong, and young Vespera pulled the thong loose with her free hand and the roll fell open on the table.
Needles.
More of them than he could count, in little leather loops, in a row. Long and short. Thick and fine. And beside them, in a row of small round holes cut in a block of wood, little stoppered pots of ink.
She took the finest needle. The very finest. It was so thin that in the firelight it almost was not there. A thread of light. And she took the smallest pot, and drew the stopper out with her teeth, and spat it into her palm. The ink inside was dark. Black, Corin thought, until the light caught it, and then it was not black. It was a very deep green. The green of a forest at night.
The baby had stopped crying. He was lying in the crook of her arm, blinking, his small face screwed up against the light, his mouth opening and shutting.
Young Vespera sat down on a stool by the fire, with the baby on her knees. And very gently, with two fingers, she turned his small head to one side. To the right. So that the left side of it was toward her and toward the light.
And she bent close, and looked behind his left ear.
Corin felt the shock of it go through him. Through both of them. The young red-haired body and his own, far away, on a fur, in the dark.
The same. The same as this afternoon. The same hand on the chin, the same turn of the head, the same bending close to look behind the left ear. The same.
But that afternoon there had been nothing there to see.
Young Vespera dipped the needle in the green-black ink.
She did not hesitate. Her hand was quite steady. She held the baby's small head still with the fingers of one hand, very lightly, and with the other she put the needle to the soft skin behind his ear and began to work.
The baby cried out. Once. Thin and high. Then he stopped, and only made a small cross snuffling sound, and screwed up his face.
It did not take long. Corin, inside her, felt the needle go in and out, in and out, quick and light and shallow, a hundred tiny touches, each one leaving a speck of green-black under the skin. He felt young Vespera's breath held. He felt her eyes narrowed. He felt her lips moving, without sound, the way Lyra's lips moved when she counted.
And then it was done, and she wiped it with the corner of the clean cloth, and held the baby's head turned to the firelight, and looked.
Corin looked with her.
It was very small. Smaller than a fingernail. A tiny dark green shape, sunk just under the new pink skin behind the left ear, at the edge of the dark damp hair.
A tree.
A tree with a straight trunk and round spreading branches. And in the branches, very small, so small that you would have needed a lens to be sure of it, a crown.
The tree with a crown in its branches.
Corin had seen it before. He knew he had. He did not know where. Somewhere. Cut in stone over a door, perhaps. Painted on a gate. Stitched on a corner. Pressed into wax. Somewhere, somewhere, he had seen that tree.
He did not hold on to the thought. He let it go by.
Young Vespera bent down and put her lips very close to the baby's ear. Close to the tiny green tree. And she said, in a voice so low that only he could have heard it, if he had been old enough to hear:
"There. So that your own blood can find you."
She straightened up.
On the bed there was a blanket, folded, ready. Young Vespera reached for it with her free hand and shook it out. A small blanket. A baby's blanket. Soft, made of some pale wool, cream or the color of milk, with a border round the edge in a darker thread. Blue, perhaps, or gray. And she laid the baby in the middle of it and began to wrap him. Corners in. The way you wrap a parcel. Snug, so that only his small red face showed.
And Corin, looking down through young Vespera's eyes at the blanket as she folded it, saw the stitches.
The border. The darker thread round the edge of the pale wool, sewn in a neat running line. Every stitch the same length. Every stitch the same.
And every one of them leaning backward.
All the way round. Every single stitch, slanting the wrong way. Not the way stitches go when a right-handed woman sews them. The other way. Leaning back, against the line, like a row of people walking into a strong wind.
He looked at them.
Then young Vespera finished the last fold, and lifted the small wrapped bundle up, and turned, and carried him to the bed.
And Corin let the stitches go by.
Out
The memory did not end. It thinned.
That was how it felt. Young Vespera carried the wrapped baby to the bed, and bent, and put him into the blurred woman's reaching arms. And the woman drew him in against her and bent her blurred face over him, and made a sound that was half a laugh and half a sob. And the man at the door turned his head, at last, a little, over his shoulder, toward the bed.
And Corin did not see his face. Because as the man turned, the whole room began to go thin.
Like mist burning off a field in the morning. The low dark beams, the white walls, the rag rug, the fire. The rain on the thatch, going faint. The river outside, going faint. The smell of soap and blood and wet earth. All of it going pale and thin and see-through, the way a reflection in a window goes when someone lights a lamp in the room behind it.
He tried to hold it.
He could not help it. He felt his hands close again, far away, on the fur. Wait, he thought. Wait. Let me see him. Let me see her. Just once. Just for a moment. Let me...
"Corin."
Louder now. Not a warning. A call. Sharp and clear and close, cutting through the thinning rain like a bell through fog.
"Corin. Come back. Now."
The man at the door was turning. The woman's face was bent over the baby. Another moment. Only another moment.
"Corin!"
And he knew that voice.
Not from the Ledger. Not from anything he had read. He knew it the way he knew the sound of his own heart. The one thing he had kept. The one thing out of all of it that he had not burned, on his knees under the dome with the sky coming down. Her voice saying his name.
He let go.
And the cottage went out like a candle, and there was dark, and then there was red.
Red light. A low red glow. Firelight, through his shut eyelids. And warmth. And furs under his back. And the smell of burnt honey and smoke and goat. And a cold hard ring pressed against his forehead, where the neck of the jar was. And a hand in his right hand, dry and rough and strong, holding on very tight.
He opened his eyes.
The smoke hole was over him. A round patch of sky, dark green now, nearly black, with one star in it.
And Lyra's face, upside down, very close. Bent over him from where she sat at his head. White in the red light, with the faded gray line down her nose, and her eyes wide and wet and furious.
"There you are," she said.
Her voice shook.
"I'm here," said Corin.
His own voice came out strange. Thick. As if he had not used it for a long time. He felt the jar lift off his forehead. Vespera, taking it away. He felt her hand let go of his.
"You started to go," said Lyra. "At the end. Your face went... it went loose. Like last night. Like in the ring. You were holding on to something." She swallowed. "I said it three times."
"I heard you," said Corin. "All three."
She looked down at him for a moment longer, upside down. Then she sat back, out of his sight, and he heard her let her breath out, long and shaky, in the dark.
He lay there.
The fire hissed. Kestrel was still sitting by the door, very still, her long legs drawn up. She had not moved, he thought, the whole time.
He put up his hand. Slowly. His left hand, the gray one, with the black fingertips. And he put it behind his left ear. To the place. The smooth tight shiny patch of skin, the size of a thumbprint, where the hair did not grow.
He pressed it with his fingers. Felt along it. Felt the edges of it.
Nothing. Smooth skin. A burn.
"There was something here," he said.
It was not a question.
Vespera was kneeling beside him with the empty jar in her lap. She had put the cork back in it, he saw, out of habit. She did not look at him. She was looking at the jar.
"There was," she said.
"A tree. With a crown in it." He pressed the place again. "You put it there. On the night I was born."
"Yes."
"Where did it go?"
Vespera turned the jar in her blue fingers.
"The lamp took it," she said. "The Ash lamp. The one that burned you. It's in your little book, I expect, if you look." Her mouth went thin. "It didn't know what it was burning. It just burned the place it was pointed at. And when it was done, there was nothing left there to read. Nothing at all." She put the jar down on the furs beside her. "Which may be no bad thing. Considering who might have been reading."
Corin lay still.
He thought about the cottage. The rain. The river. The man at the door with his back to the room, watching the road.
The woman in the bed with no face.
"Who was she?" he said.
Vespera did not answer.
"The woman," said Corin. "In the bed. You held her hands. You called her love. Who was she?"
The fire hissed. Kestrel did not move. Lyra, behind his head, out of sight, did not breathe.
Vespera looked at him then. At last. Her thin gray face in the red light was very tired, and very old, and there was something in her eyes that he had never seen there before, not in the shop, not in the Ledger, not anywhere.
"That's not mine to tell," she said quietly.
"Then whose is it?"
"Yours." She reached out and touched, very lightly, with one blue fingertip, the back of his gray hand where it lay on the fur. "That's yours to walk, harvester. One day. Not out of a jar of mine. From your own. When you find it."
"And if I never find it?"
Vespera took her finger away.
"Then she stays as she is," she said. "Wrapped up. Kept safe. The way I kept her." She looked at the jar. "I didn't let you see her face because I didn't keep it, harvester. Not properly. I wrapped it up the moment I put it away. Twenty-eight years ago. On purpose. So that nobody who ever took it from me could see who she was."
"Not even you?"
"Not even me," said Vespera. "Not anymore."
Paid
She got up.
It took her a long time. Longer than it had taken her to get out of the cart. She put one hand flat on the furs and pushed, and her knees cracked, and she got one foot under her, and stopped, and breathed. Then the other foot. Then she was up, bent over, with a hand in the small of her back. Kestrel watched her from the door and did not get up to help, and Corin understood that this, too, was a kind of respect.
Vespera had the jar in her hand.
The little blue egg of glass, empty now. The faint glow that had been inside it was gone. When she held it up to the firelight there was nothing in it at all. Only the blue, gone dull, and a thin gray film on the inside of the glass, like the frost left on a window when the breath that made it has gone.
She looked at it for a moment.
Then she went to the door of the tent. Kestrel moved aside for her and held the flap. And Vespera stooped and went out.
Corin got up too. It took him almost as long. Lyra had his crutch. She gave it to him without a word and he pulled himself up on it and followed Vespera out, and Lyra came after.
It was night outside.
The sun had gone while they were in the tent. The sky in the west was a long low band of dark red, going to purple, going to black. Overhead it was black already, and full of stars. And over the ridge to the north, the milky light of the Glass had come up, pale and cold, like moonlight with no moon. The camp was lit by small fires outside the tents, and by the bigger fire in the middle, and the smoke went up straight in the still cold air.
The cold hit him. It was the deep dry cold of the edge, coming down out of the clear sky. His breath smoked.
Vespera had gone a few steps from the tent, to where a flat gray stone lay half sunk in the gravel by the path. A big one, smooth on top, the size of a small table. Children sat on it in the day, Corin had seen, to eat their bread.
She stood beside it and looked down at it.
Then she set the empty blue jar on the middle of the stone. Very carefully. Upright. The way you set down a cup.
And she lifted her foot, in its heavy old boot, and brought the heel of it down on the jar.
The crack was very loud in the quiet camp. Sharp and short. Like a branch breaking in frost. A man by the nearest fire looked round.
The jar was gone. In its place on the gray stone there was a small heap of blue glass. Little curved pieces, and splinters, and fine glittering blue dust. In the firelight they shone like chips of sky.
Vespera looked down at them.
"Paid," she said.
She said it quite quietly. Not to Corin. Not to anyone. To the stone, perhaps. Or to the glass. Or to a cottage by a river twenty-eight years ago, with the rain on the thatch.
Then she sat down.
She did it all at once, without any warning. Her legs went out from under her and she sat down on the edge of the gray stone beside the heap of broken glass, hard, the way a puppet sits when its strings are cut. Her hands dropped into her lap. Her head went down.
And for a moment, in the firelight and the starlight and the cold milky light from over the ridge, she looked old.
Not the way she had looked old before. Not small and gray and sharp and furious. Old the way a house looks old when the last of the family has moved out of it. Empty. Quiet. Done. Her thin shoulders were bowed under the crushed dark coat. Her blue hands lay in her lap, palms up, open, the way his own had lain on his knees that morning under the black thorn tree. Letting go.
Corin did not know what to do.
He stood there on his crutch and looked at her, and did not know what to say, or whether to say anything. Lyra, beside him, had gone very still.
It was Kestrel who moved.
She came out of the tent behind them, and walked past them without a word, and went to the stone, and sat down on it beside Vespera. Close. So that their shoulders touched. She did not put her arm round her. She did not say anything. She only sat there, tall and straight, with her face to the Glass, and let her shoulder be there, against the small bowed shoulder beside it.
After a long time, Vespera lifted her head.
"Twenty-eight years," she said. To nobody. Her voice was hoarse. "I carried that twenty-eight years. In a jar on a shelf. And then in a tank. And then in my coat, for two days, in a box, under the turnips." She looked down at the blue glitter on the stone. "I'd forgotten what it weighed. Till it was gone."
Kestrel said nothing.
"That's the trouble with carrying," said Vespera. "You get used to it. You stop feeling it. And then you put it down, and you nearly fall over."
"Yes," said Kestrel. "We know."
Vespera made a small sound. It might have been a laugh.
Then she put her blue hands on her knees and straightened her back, a little at a time, until she was sitting up again. Small and gray and sharp. Herself. She brushed a fleck of blue glass off her skirt.
"Well," she said, in her own voice again. Dry and low. "That's done. The harvester's paid his price and I've had my say. And I'm hungry again, and my knees are killing me, and somebody's going to have to lift me off this stone." She looked up at Corin. "Not you. You'd drop me."
"Probably," said Corin.
And Vespera's mouth went up at the corner, and Kestrel stood and lifted her off the stone, and the three of them went back toward the fire. And Corin stayed a moment longer by the gray stone, looking down at the little heap of blue glass, shining in the starlight like a broken piece of the night sky.
Then he went too.
