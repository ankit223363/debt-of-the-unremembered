Chapter 67: Vespera Comes North, Part 5 of 5: Rope
Rope
Lyra caught him up before he reached the fire.
She did not say anything at first. She walked beside him in the dark between the tents, slowly, at the pace of the crutch, with her hands in the pockets of the black coat and her breath smoking. The small fires outside the tents lit her face from one side, orange, and then from the other, and then left it dark.
"You held on," she said at last. "In there. At the end."
"Yes."
"Three times."
"Yes."
She walked on a few steps. "What were you holding on to?"
He thought about it. He did not want to lie to her. He did not know why that mattered so much, or how he knew it mattered, but it did. It was the first rule. It was on the first page of the green book, in his own hand. NO LIES. NOT TO HER.
"Faces," he said. "There was a man at the door with his back to me, and a woman in a bed I couldn't see. I wanted to see them. I wanted it so much." He stopped walking. "I nearly took it. Out of Vespera. Out of the jar. I was going to make them turn round."
Lyra stopped too.
"And then I heard you," said Corin.
She did not say anything.
"I don't know you," he said. It came out very simply. He did not mean it to hurt, and he saw that it did, a little, in the way her face went still, and he said the rest quickly. "I know I don't. I've read about you. Pages and pages. I know you're Lyra Thorne and your father had a green lamp and you count stairs and you sing flat and you hit a constable with a milk jug. I know all of it the way you know a story. But I don't know you. I don't have you."
The fire at the next tent cracked. Somebody inside laughed.
"Except your voice," said Corin. "Saying my name. That's the one thing I've got. I don't know where it's from. I don't know when you said it, the first time, or why. But it's there. It's the only thing in me about you that isn't written down." He looked at her. "And in there, in the rain, when I was holding on, I heard it. And I knew it. Before I knew anything else. Before I knew where I was. I knew that was you."
She was looking at him very hard.
"So I let go," he said. "Because it was you."
For a long moment she did not answer. She stood in the dark between two tents, with the cold milky light of the Glass behind her over the ridge, and her hands in her pockets, and looked at him.
Then she said, quite steadily, "Good."
And turned, and walked on toward the fire.
He followed her. After a few steps she slowed down, so that he could catch up, and they walked the rest of the way side by side, not talking.
At the edge of the firelight she stopped again.
"Write it down," she said, without looking at him. "Before Garrett goes. Before you forget. Write down what you saw."
So he sat down on a mat at the edge of the light, and took out the green book, and the pencil, and wrote it. Slowly. Checking each letter before he made the next. While Lyra stood behind him and read it over his shoulder, her lips moving, and said nothing, and once, when he made an R, touched the paper beside it with her gloved finger, and he looked, and it was the right way round, and she took her finger away.
MONDAY. VESPERA CAME IN A BOX OF TURNIPS.
I WALKED THE V. JAR. A BABY. A NEEDLE. A TREE WITH A CROWN.
THERE WAS SOMETHING BEHIND MY LEFT EAR. THE LAMP TOOK IT.
He stopped. He looked at the three lines. Then, under them, he wrote one more.
SHE CALLED ME BACK. I KNEW HER VOICE. I LET GO BECAUSE IT WAS HER.
Lyra read it.
She stood very still behind him for a long time.
Then she reached down and turned the page over for him, so that the next one was clean, and went to sit by the fire, on the far side, where he could not see her face.
Garrett Goes South
Garrett would not stay the night.
"No," he said. "No, no, no. Thank you. Very kind. Lovely camp. Lovely bread. Lovely people, very lovely, nobody's eaten me, I'm grateful, I'll tell everybody. But no."
He was standing by the fire with his red cap pulled down over his ears and his red scarf wound up to his nose again, and the tired brown pony, watered and fed and brushed by three solemn Ashborn children until its matted coat almost shone, was standing between the shafts of the empty turnip cart behind him, looking at him with an expression of deep betrayal.
"It's dark," said Oren. "You'll go off the path."
"I've got a lantern."
"You'll go off the path with a lantern."
"Then I'll go off it slowly." Garrett looked over his shoulder, up toward the black line of the Lip against the stars. He did it every few breaths. Corin had stopped noticing. "There were gray coats at the toll on the north road. Two days ago. A lot of them. More than usual. And a cart going the same way as me, a closed one, black, that I let get ahead." He swallowed. "And people at Haverstock were saying there'd been more, the day before. Two closed carts. And riders. Going toward Thistle Ford."
Nobody said anything.
Corin felt the small warm thing in him go still. The hum, which had been coming and going all evening in the back of his throat while he ate, stopped.
"So I'm going now," said Garrett. "Tonight. Back over the top. There's a sheep path, the boy at Haverstock told me, that goes round the side of the valleys and doesn't go near Thistle Ford at all. I'm going to take it. And I'm going to keep going, and not stop, and not look at anybody, until I'm back in the city with the door locked. And then I'm going to get very drunk." He looked at Vespera. "You're not coming back with me, are you."
It was not a question. Vespera did not answer it.
Garrett nodded, as if she had.
"Right," he said. "Right. Then I'll need the thing. The thing I'm taking. You said there'd be a thing."
"There's a thing," said Corin.
He had it ready. He had cut it out of the back of the green book an hour ago, by the fire, with his knife, very carefully, along the stitching, while Vespera ate. The last leaf. The putting-back page. Covered top to bottom in his big leaning capitals, with the two small crossings-out where Lyra had found his wrong letters and the right letters written in above.
He had read it over one more time before he cut it. Lyra had read it with him, over his shoulder, her lips moving. It was right. Every word.
Now he took it out of his coat. It was folded once, and wrapped in a square of oilcloth that Kestrel had given him, the kind they used to keep the bread dry. He unwrapped it.
And took out his pencil.
At the very bottom of the page, under Malakor's last line, there was a little space left. Not much. A finger's width. He bent over it, close to the firelight, with the page flat on his knee, and wrote in it. Small. Smaller than he usually wrote. He made every letter slowly, and checked which way it faced before he made the next.
IT SAYS QUICK. GO QUICK. C.
He looked at it. Then, under it, squeezed in at the very edge of the page, he wrote one more line. Smaller still.
JUNO. READ IT SLOW.
He held it out to Lyra without a word. She took it, and read the two new lines, and looked at every letter. And nodded.
"They're right," she said.
He folded the page again, once, and wrapped it in the oilcloth, and tied the oilcloth with a thong. Then he held it out to Garrett.
"Saint Bride's," he said. "Down by the river. There's a crypt. Twenty-three steps down. There's a boy there called Gideon. He's small, and he doesn't smile, and he writes five words on everything in blue chalk. And there's a big man sitting against the wall who doesn't talk. That's his father." Corin's voice was very steady. "Give it to the boy. Not to anybody else. Not to the doctor. Not to the woman with the broom. The boy. Or to a girl called Juno, if he isn't there. She talks faster than you do. She lives with her mother, Mrs. Marta. The boy will know where."
Garrett looked at the oilcloth packet in Corin's gray hand. He did not take it at once.
"What is it?" he said.
"A way to put a thing back," said Corin. "Into somebody who's lost it."
Garrett looked at him for a long moment, over the red scarf.
Then he took it. And put it inside his coat, deep, against his chest, and buttoned the coat over it. And put his hand flat on it, once, the way Corin had seen people do with things that mattered.
"The boy," he said. "Gideon. Twenty-three steps. Not the doctor."
"Yes."
"Quick, it says."
"Quick and warm," said Corin. "Tell him that. If you forget everything else. Tell him quick and warm."
"Quick and warm," said Garrett. "I don't know what it means and I don't want to."
He turned and went to the pony. He took its bridle. He looked back, once, at the fire and the faces round it. At Vespera. Then over his shoulder, up at the Lip.
Then he lit his lantern from the fire with a burning twig, and hung it on the front of the cart, and clicked his tongue at the pony, and the pony, after a long and bitter pause, leaned into its collar.
The cart creaked away up the goat path through the thorns. Corin watched the small swinging yellow light go. Up the dark slope. Slowly. Round a bend. Behind a thorn bush. Out again, higher. Smaller. Garrett's voice came back down on the cold air, very faint, talking to the pony, about nothing, about everything, without stopping.
And then, a long way up, near the top of the slope, almost at the Lip, the voice changed.
It was singing.
The same song. Corin could not make out the words or the tune. But he could hear that it was the same few lines, round and round, over and over. The only song Garrett knew all the words to.
The little light went over the Lip and was gone.
"He'll make it," said Vespera, beside the fire. She did not sound sure. "He always makes it. He's too frightened not to."
Corin put his hand inside his coat. To the green book. To the place at the back where the last leaf had been, and now was not. A rough edge of stitching, and nothing.
He pressed his fingers on it, and let the hum come back, slowly, into his throat.
Counting the Days
Kestrel came and sat down by the fire after the light had gone.
She had been at the south edge of the camp, watching the slope, the whole time the little lantern climbed it. Now she came back between the tents with her long stride and sat down on the bare earth, facing north, and held her hands out to the fire. Corin had not seen her do that before. She did not seem to feel the cold. But tonight she held her long brown hands out flat to the flames, and turned them, as if they were stiff.
"Two closed carts," she said. "And riders. Going to Thistle Ford."
"Yes," said Oren.
He had put his whetstone away. He was sitting forward with his elbows on his knees and his sword lying across his boots, and his face in the firelight was the face Corin had seen at the bottom of the thorn slope that noon, with the blade out. A soldier's face. Counting.
"How far is it?" he said. "From Thistle Ford to here. For men who don't stop."
Kestrel thought about it. "For a man walking, two days. Haverstock, then the Lip, then the thorns. For a man on a horse, a day and a half, if the horse is good and the snow is kind." She turned her hands over to warm the backs. "For a man who does not sleep..."
"They don't sleep," said Corin.
They all looked at him.
He had not told them the rest of what Garrett had told him. Low, by the pony, while the others were not listening, with his eyes going over his shoulder all the time.
He told them now. That when the closed black cart on the north road had stopped at the toll, ahead of Garrett, there had been a moment of quiet. And in the quiet, Garrett had heard a sound from inside it. Not voices. Not breathing. Not horses or chains or men shifting on a bench. Ticking. Steady and patient and many, all together, like a roomful of clocks waiting for the same hour. And that the gray coats at the toll, who had poked a pike through Garrett's turnips, had not opened that cart. They had not gone near it. They had stood back from it and waved it through.
Nobody spoke when he finished.
Then Arran, across the fire, said very quietly, "Clocks." As if it were a word in a language he did not know.
"Something wound up," said Oren. He was looking at his sword. "Iron, then, or brass. Something with a spring in it." He turned the blade a little in the firelight. "Well. That's a thing a sword can argue with, anyway. More than a burn can." But his face did not say he believed it.
"Out on the Glass," said Kestrel slowly, "nothing burns. Nothing is taken." She looked at Corin. "But iron walks. Iron does not need to burn."
"No," said Corin.
"Then the Glass will not stop them."
"No."
Kestrel was quiet for a long time. The fire hissed. Somewhere in the dark a child was crying in a tent, and a woman's voice hushed it.
"How many days?" said Lyra. "Before they're here?"
Oren held up his hand, the fingers spread, and began to count on them, under his breath. Thistle Ford tonight, if Garrett's right. Then Haverstock. Then the Lip. Corin watched the big fingers fold down, one after another. One. Two. Three. He could follow it as far as that. Then the fingers went on folding, and the numbers Oren murmured went on, and Corin felt them go past him like birds in the dark. He could hear them. He could not feel them. They had no weight. He did not know if three was a lot, or not.
He did not say so. He waited for Oren to finish.
"Tomorrow night," said Oren at last. "At the soonest. If they come straight, and don't stop to burn anything on the way." He let his hand fall. "The day after, more likely. They'll want to be sure we're here before they come over the top."
"Then we are not here," said Kestrel.
She said it quite simply.
"We go before them. Out. Onto the Glass. By night." She took her hands back from the fire and put them on her knees. "Not tomorrow. Tomorrow we make ready. Felt for the boots. Hide for the eyes. Water. Bread. Stones for the heat. Tomorrow the children learn which of them are staying, and are angry about it. And tomorrow night we sleep." She looked round the fire at them, one after another. Oren. Lyra. Vespera. Arran. Corin. "And the night after, if they have not come, we walk."
"And if they come before that?" said Lyra.
Kestrel looked at her.
"Then we will walk sooner," she said. "And faster. And with less bread."
Nobody laughed.
Vespera had been quiet all this time, rolling and unrolling the end of her rope belt in her blue fingers. Now she looked up.
"Who's going?" she said. "Of yours?"
"Five," said Kestrel. "And us." She did not look at Arran. "We have not said who, yet. We will say tomorrow."
Arran, across the fire, lifted his head.
He did not say anything. He only looked at Kestrel, with his red swollen eyes, for a long moment. And Kestrel, without turning her head, without looking at him at all, gave the smallest nod.
Arran looked down at his flute again.
And Corin, sitting by the fire with the hum gone quiet in his throat, thought of Oren's fingers folding down in the firelight, one by one, past the place where he could follow them. And of whatever was lying in a black cart on a white road somewhere to the south, not sleeping, not tiring, ticking, coming on through the dark at the walking pace of a man.
He could not feel how many days.
He could only feel that it was not enough.
Lyra was watching him across the fire. He did not know how long she had been watching. She did not say anything. But after a moment she leaned over to Oren and said something to him, very low, and Oren looked at Corin, and then held up his big hand again in the firelight, all five fingers spread.
"Like this," said Oren. "Look. Not numbers. Nights." He folded down his thumb. "Tonight. They're at the ford. Warm, in the long hall, if the old woman lets them in, which she won't." He folded down the first finger. "Tomorrow night. On the moor, past Haverstock. Cold. Still coming." The second finger. "The night after. At the Lip. Looking down at us." He held up the hand with the two fingers left standing. "And by then we're gone. Out there." He tipped his head at the ridge. "Two fingers ahead of them. That's all. Two fingers."
Corin looked at the two fingers standing up in the firelight.
Two. He could feel two. Two was a thing he could hold.
"Two fingers," he said.
"Two fingers," said Oren. "And then we keep it that way." He let his hand fall and picked up his whetstone again, and the soft steady scrape began once more along the blade. "That's all a march is, lad. Keeping it two fingers."
And Corin, without knowing he was doing it, felt the hum come creeping back into his throat.
The Needle
Later, when the camp had gone quiet, and most of the small fires outside the tents had burned down to red, Vespera unrolled her needles.
She did it by the big fire in the middle of the camp, where they had eaten. Most of the Ashborn had gone to their tents. A few sat on at the edge of the light, wrapped in furs, faces to the ridge, talking low. Arran was there, at last. He had come and sat down by the fire a while ago without a word, a little apart, with his knees up and his flute in his hands, and he had not played it, but he had eaten a piece of bread that Lyra put beside him, and that was something. Oren sat on the far side, with his sword across his knees and his whetstone in his hand, drawing it along the blade slowly, with his eyes half shut, the soft steady scrape of it going on and on under everything like a cricket. Lyra sat beside Corin.
And Vespera knelt on a mat in the firelight and took the rope belt from round her waist and laid it out on the ground in front of her and began to undo it.
She did it slowly. Carefully. Each thing in its turn. She took the needles out of their small leather sleeves one by one and laid them on a strip of soft hide in a row, from the longest to the shortest, from the thickest to the finest. Steel and silver and bone. Some of them were as long as Corin's hand. Some were so fine that he could not see them at all except as a glint when the firelight caught them. She did not hurry. She touched each one before she put it down, with the tip of one blue finger, the way you might touch the head of a sleeping child.
Then the bottles. Little ones, stoppered with wax, in a row beside the needles. Black ink and blue and green, and a red so dark it was almost brown, and one that seemed to have no color at all until it moved, and then it shone like the inside of a shell. Then the gold-rimmed blue pot, the sealing pot. Then the bird-shaped scissors. Then a roll of gauze. Then a little jar of something that smelled, when she opened it to look, of burnt honey and pine.
She hummed while she did it. Very low. A tune with a shape to it, not like Hask's. Corin did not know it.
When it was all laid out, she sat back on her heels and looked at it. All of it, in its rows, glinting in the firelight. Then she licked her thumb and rubbed a smear of old blue ink off the side of the sealing pot.
"What are you doing?" said Corin.
He had been watching her for a long time. He could not have said why. There was something about watching her do it that made the hum in his throat come easily, round and round.
"Counting," said Vespera. "Seeing what I've got. Seeing what I'll need." She did not look up. "Seeing what got wet in that box."
"Need for what?"
Vespera picked up one of the fine needles and held it up to the firelight and looked along it, with one eye shut, the way a man looks along the barrel of a gun.
"I'm coming onto the Glass," she said.
The scrape of Oren's whetstone stopped.
Lyra turned her head.
Arran, across the fire, looked up from his flute for the first time.
Corin did not say anything for a moment. Then he said, "Why?"
Vespera put the needle down. In its place in the row. Exactly in its place. Then she looked up at him at last, over the rows of needles and bottles, across the low red fire.
"You know what the Glass does, harvester," she said. "Kestrel's told you. Nothing burns. Nothing's taken. You can't pull a thing out of anybody and you can't put a thing of your own into a fire. But you can give." She touched the row of ink bottles with one blue finger. "Ink is giving. Ink goes in. It doesn't come out. It's the only magic I know that works the wrong way round. And the Glass doesn't stop it, because it isn't taking anything. It's putting something there."
"I know," said Corin. "But why?"
Vespera was quiet for a moment.
Then she said, "Because I looked behind your ear today."
Nobody spoke.
"I looked behind your ear," said Vespera, "and there was nothing there. Where I put something, once, with my own hand, so that it would always be there. Nothing." Her voice was very level. "And I looked at your arms. A hand's width from the shoulder. Less a finger. And I looked at the way you look at her," she did not glance at Lyra, "as if she's a page you've read and liked and can't remember the end of." She paused. "And I thought, there's not much left of him that's written down anywhere except that little book of his."
The fire hissed.
"And you're going out there," said Vespera, "onto the Glass, where the old ones walked in the war and came back not knowing their own names. To the middle. To whatever's in the middle. With that girl, who can't write. And that soldier, who can't read. And a crowd of Ashborn who've never been further than the second cairn." She did not look at Arran either, though he flinched. "With the gray coming up your arms and an old man inside you who's gone quiet."
She picked up the finest needle again. Turned it in her fingers so the firelight ran along it like a drop of water.
"Something out there," she said, "is going to try to take your name, harvester."
Corin did not move.
"I don't know what," said Vespera. "I don't know how. But I've been inking people for forty years who've lost things, and I know the look of a man who's about to lose something big. And you've got it. You've had it since you came in under my arch with your hands in your pockets. You've got it worse now." She held the needle up between them. Very fine. Very bright. "Something's going to try to take your name. And when it does..."
She stopped. And the slit of her mouth went up at one corner.
"When it does," said Vespera, "I'd like to be standing next to you with a needle."
The fire crackled. Across it, Arran had put his flute to his lips, and then taken it away again without playing. The hum of the Glass came up through the ground. And beside Corin, very quietly, Lyra let out a breath that she had been holding for a long time.
Corin looked at the needle in Vespera's blue fingers.
He thought of the green book inside his coat. Of the first page in it, in her mother's back-leaning brown ink, and his own crooked capitals under it. READ THIS FIRST.
He thought of how little was written there. How much was not.
"All right," he said.
Vespera nodded once. As if she had expected nothing else. And put the needle back in its place in the row, and began, carefully, one by one, to roll them all up again.
And Oren, on the far side of the fire, went back to his whetstone. The soft scrape of it started up again, slow and steady, and did not stop.
