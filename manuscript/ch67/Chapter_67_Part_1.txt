Chapter 67: Vespera Comes North, Part 1 of 5: Carrying Hask
Carrying Hask
"Don't hold him," said Kestrel.
They were sitting on the ridge, under the big black thorn tree, where they had sat two nights before. It was the middle of the morning. The sun was up over the hills in the east, low and white, and the sky was a hard pale blue with no cloud in it anywhere. Below them on the north side, a mile off, the Glass blazed. Even with the hide strips tied over his eyes, Corin could not look at it for long. It was like looking into a mirror with the sun in it. The warm breath of it came up the slope and over the crest of the ridge and lay on his face.
Behind them, on the south side, Last Thorn was quiet. Quieter than it had been. Smoke still went up from the tents, and goats still bleated in the pen, and children still ran between the bread racks. But slower. Everyone was moving a little slower this morning, and speaking a little lower, the way people do in a house where someone has died. Every tent in the camp had a new handprint on it. A small one. Pressed in ash, very clear and sharp, among all the old faded ones. Corin could see them from here, dotted across the camp, one on every tent, like a word written over and over on a page.
"You're holding him," said Kestrel again. "This one can see it. In your shoulders."
Corin made his shoulders go down. It was hard. They did not want to.
"I don't know what else to do," he said. "I've never had one like this before. One that was given. They were always taken. Pulled out. And I put them away. On shelves. In the dark. And I didn't look at them." He stopped. "That's what I know how to do. Put them away."
"Yes," said Kestrel. "And that is holding. Putting away is holding. Shutting a door on them is holding. Keeping them in a room in the dark where they cannot get out, that is holding them very tight." She looked at him sideways. "That is how you drown."
Corin said nothing. He sat with his back against the black trunk and his bad leg out straight in front of him and the crutch across his knees, and tried to feel what was in him.
It was very small. That was the first thing. Smaller than anything he had ever taken. Smaller than a single face. When he went looking for it, inside himself, the way he would go looking down the long dark aisles of the shelves for something he had put away, he almost could not find it. It was not on a shelf. It was not anywhere he had put it. He had not put it anywhere. It had just come in, on a breath, in the gray of the dawn, and gone somewhere on its own.
It felt warm. That was all he could say about it. Like a small warm stone in a pocket.
"What do I do with it?" he said.
"Nothing," said Kestrel.
"Nothing?"
"Let him sit in you." She said it simply, the way she said everything. "The way an old man sits by a fire. You don't hold an old man by a fire. You don't lock him in. You let him sit. You give him a place, a warm place, and you let him be in it. Sometimes he will talk to you. Mostly he will not. Mostly he will just sit there, and hum, and look at the fire, and you will know he is there. And that is all." She was quiet for a moment. "That is all carrying is. Letting them sit."
Corin shut his eyes behind the hide strip.
He tried.
He did not go looking for it, this time. He did not go down the aisles of the shelves. He did what she said. He made a place. He did not know how he did it. He thought about a fire, a small one, in a little round tent with a smoke hole and three stars in it. And a heap of furs. And a clay pot and a bone comb. And he thought, there. You can sit there, if you like.
And he let go.
It was the hardest thing he had ever done. Harder than taking. Harder than anything. Everything in him wanted to close his hand. To grab. To put it on a shelf and lock the door, the way he always had, so that it could not get out, so that it could not do anything to him. He made himself not do it. He made his hand stay open. He sat there under the black thorn tree with his eyes shut and his hands lying open on his knees, palms up, the gray fingers loose, and let the small warm thing in him do whatever it wanted.
It did not do anything.
It sat.
And after a while, he felt it settle. The way a cat settles on a lap. Turning round once, twice, and then going down, heavy and warm, and staying.
He let out a long breath.
"There," said Kestrel softly. "Like that."
He opened his eyes. The Glass blazed through the slits in the hide. The warm wind came up over the ridge.
"It doesn't feel like anything," he said. "Not really. It just feels like... I'm not by myself."
"Yes," said Kestrel. "That is what it feels like."
They sat for a while longer. A hawk went over, very high, turning slow circles in the pale sky, and Corin watched it. It did not go out over the Glass. It turned at the line of the ridge, every time, and went back south.
"There are hundreds in you," said Kestrel after a while. "The ones you took. Not given. Taken." She was not looking at him. She was looking at the hawk. "You cannot let them sit. Not like this. They did not come to you on a breath. They came torn. They do not want to sit by your fire. They want to go home, and they cannot." She was quiet. "We do not know what you can do for those. We have never had one like you here. Not in our time."
"Malakor might know," said Corin.
He had not meant to say it out loud.
Kestrel turned her head and looked at him. "Who is Malakor?"
Corin did not answer at once. He thought of the dark place in himself where the old man had been. Where, last night, when he went looking, there had been nothing at all. Not a voice. Not a breath. Not even the feeling of someone sitting quiet in a dark room.
He went and looked again now.
Nothing.
"Someone I carry," he said at last. "Who wasn't given and wasn't taken. He just came in. To hold a door."
Kestrel looked at him for a long time.
Then she said, very quietly, "Ah," and looked back at the hawk, and did not ask anything more.
The Hum
He found out about the hum by accident.
It was later that morning. He had come down off the ridge, sideways, with his face to the Glass, the way his body seemed to do now without asking him. And he had gone to sit on the log outside their tent at the north edge of the camp, in the thin sun, with the green book on his knee, meaning to write.
He did not write. He sat there with the pencil in his hand and looked at the tent.
At the new handprint on it. The small one, clear and sharp in gray ash, beside the six or seven old faded ones. Lyra had pressed it there. He had not seen her do it. He had been inside, putting the tent to rights. But Oren had told him, afterward, in a low voice: she did it with his left hand. She held his wrist and put it flat on the hide and pressed. And then she stood there a long time looking at it.
He looked at it now. A small old hand, the fingers a little bent.
And he was humming.
He did not know he was. That was the thing. He only found out because Lyra came round the side of the tent with a water skin over her shoulder, and stopped dead, and looked at him.
"What?" he said.
"Nothing," said Lyra, too quickly.
She was a bad liar. He did not need the Ledger to tell him that. He could see it in the way her eyes went to his mouth and then away, and the way her hand tightened on the strap of the water skin.
"What is it?" he said again.
She hesitated. He could see her decide something, and then decide the other way, and then stand there not deciding at all. "I was going to tell you," she said at last. "Some other day. Not today." She put the water skin down on the gravel. "But you're doing it right in front of me. So."
"Doing what?"
"Humming," said Lyra. "You've been doing it all morning, on and off. When you were rolling up your coat. When you came down the ridge. It's not a tune. It just goes round. Up a bit and down a bit."
Corin opened his mouth to say she was wrong.
And heard it.
Not out loud. Inside. Very faint, somewhere in the back of his throat, where he had not been listening. A low tuneless sound, round and round, like water going over stones. It had been there all along. He had thought it was the hum of the Glass. It was not. It was in him.
Hask.
He shut his mouth. The humming stopped.
"Oh," he said.
Lyra sat down on the other end of the log. Not close. Not far. She did not say anything.
He sat and listened to the place where the humming had been. It was quiet now. The warm small thing was still there, the old man by the fire. But it was not humming.
He tried to make it hum again. He could not. He tried to hum himself, the way it had gone, round and round. It came out wrong. Thin and stiff, like a man trying to whistle with a cold. Nothing like.
"It's stopped," he said.
"Because you're thinking about it," said Lyra.
He looked at her.
"That's what it looked like," she said. "When you were doing it, you weren't thinking about anything. You looked..." She stopped, and frowned, and chose the word carefully. "Easy. Like a man sitting in the sun. And then I said it, and you went stiff, and it stopped."
He thought about that.
Then, slowly, he made himself stop thinking. It was not easy. He looked at the tent. At the handprint. At the line of thorn bushes at the foot of the ridge, all leaning south. He let his shoulders go down, the way Kestrel had said. He let his hands lie open on his knees. He thought about nothing. About the sun on the back of his hands. About the smell of bread from the middle of the camp.
And after a while, very faint, in the back of his throat, it came back.
Round and round. Up a little and down a little.
He did not try to hold it. He let it go on.
Lyra, on the other end of the log, let out a breath.
They sat there in the sun together, not talking, while he hummed an old blind man's hum without knowing the tune. And the strange thing was that it made him feel better. Lighter. As if someone very old and very calm had come and sat down beside him and put a dry hand on his knee and said, there now, little one, there.
Then a goat bleated, sudden and loud, from the pen at the east end of the camp. And someone shouted. And Corin's head came up quick, and his hand went to his coat, to the place where the wrapped rod was, the way it always did. And his heart jumped.
And the hum stopped.
It was only the goats. A kid had got out, and a boy was chasing it between the tents, and a woman was shouting at the boy. Nothing.
But the hum did not come back. Not for a long time. Not until his heart had slowed down again, and his hand had come away from his coat, and he had looked round at the camp and the tents and the people and the sun and seen that nothing was wrong.
Then, slowly, it crept back.
"It goes when I'm frightened," he said. He said it slowly, working it out. "When I'm calm, it comes. When I'm afraid, it stops."
Lyra looked at him.
"That's useful," she said.
"Is it?"
"Yes." She picked up a small stone from the gravel and turned it over in her gloved fingers. "You never know when you're frightened. You never have. You just go quiet and stiff and polite, and say I don't know, and everyone thinks you're fine." She threw the stone, not hard, at the foot of the tent. "Now you'll know. If it stops, you're frightened."
He did not answer that. He did not know how she knew it. He supposed it was in the Ledger, somewhere. Or he supposed it was not, and she just knew.
Across the camp, by the big fire, Arran was sitting alone.
Corin had seen him there all morning, on and off. He was sitting on the bare ground by the cold ashes of the great fire from the night of the rite, with his knees up and his arms round them and his forehead on his arms. He had not moved for a long time. The bone flute hung round his neck on its cord. He had not played it. Not once. People went past him and did not speak to him. One of the women from the bread stones went and put a cup of something by his foot, and touched the top of his head, and went away. He did not drink it.
"Hask was his grandfather," said Lyra quietly. "His real one. Kestrel told me. His mother's father. He raised him, after his mother went over the Glass."
Corin looked at Arran for a long time. At the long thin brown back, bent over. At the plait of dark hair with the red thread, hanging down.
The small warm thing in him stirred. Just a little. As if the old man by the fire had lifted his head at the sound of a name.
Corin did not hold it.
He let it look.
Arran
He went over in the end.
He did not decide to. He got up off the log, and took his crutch, and went across the camp between the tents and the bread racks, slowly, with the gravel shifting under the crutch, and Lyra did not ask where he was going. When he looked back once she was still on the log, watching him, with the water skin at her feet.
Arran did not look up when he came. Corin stood beside him for a moment. Then he let himself down onto the bare ground, beside the raked ashes of the rite fire, with his bad leg out stiff in front of him. Not close. An arm's length off. Facing north, toward the ridge, the way Arran was facing.
They sat there.
The cup of something the woman had left was still by Arran's foot. It had gone cold. A skin had formed on it. Up close, Corin could see that Arran's eyes were red and swollen, and that the fresh gray line he had painted down his face for the rite was smeared at the end, where he had wiped his nose on his wrist. The plait of dark hair with its red thread lay over his shoulder. His long brown fingers were wound tight round the little bone flute on its cord, so tight the knuckles showed pale.
Corin did not say anything. He did not know what to say. He was not good at this. He knew he was not. It was in the Ledger somewhere, in his own hand. I DO NOT KNOW WHAT TO SAY TO PEOPLE WHO ARE SAD. I SAY NOTHING. SOMETIMES THAT IS RIGHT.
He said nothing.
After a long time, Arran spoke. Without lifting his head. His voice was rough and thick.
"He was my grandfather."
"I know," said Corin. "Lyra told me."
"He taught me the flute." Arran's fingers moved on it. "He couldn't see it. He couldn't see the holes. He had me put my fingers on his, and played it with my hands on his, so I'd learn where they went. Over and over. All one winter. I was six." He was quiet. "Then my mother went."
"Over the Glass?"
Arran nodded, his forehead still on his arms. "She carried too much. She'd been at a bad death. A bad one, in a hard winter, when there weren't enough of us to share it. She took more than her part. And after that she couldn't sleep. She'd sit up at night and talk to people who weren't there. And one morning she walked over the ridge." A long pause. "And Grandfather took me in. And didn't let go."
The Glass hummed. The sun was warm on the side of Corin's face.
"He said goodbye to her," said Corin. "At the end. To the woman. The one he took from me."
Arran lifted his head.
"Lyra was with him," said Corin. "In his tent. Before dawn. She told me. He said nobody had said goodbye to the woman, and somebody should, even her. And he said it. Goodbye, Vivienne. Goodbye, little fish girl. You can go home now." Corin stopped. "And then he went. Smiling."
Arran looked at him for a long time. His red eyes were wet.
"That's him," he said at last. "That's him. He'd say goodbye to a rat."
And he laughed. His bark of a laugh. Only it broke in the middle and turned into something else, and he put his face back down on his arms and his shoulders shook.
Corin sat beside him and let him.
And as he sat there, the small warm thing in him stirred again. Not much. It lifted its head, the old man by the fire. And Corin felt, very faintly, something come up into him that was not his. A feeling. Not a picture. Not a word. Only a feeling, warm and heavy and fond, the way you feel about a small boy who is crying and whom you love very much and cannot reach anymore.
He did not hold it. He let it be there.
And without meaning to, without thinking, he put out his gray hand and laid it on the top of Arran's bent head. On the shaved skin, warm from the sun. Lightly. And left it there a moment. Then patted it, twice. The way you pat a horse to say you are done.
Arran went very still under his hand.
Corin took his hand away.
Arran lifted his head again and looked at him. And his face had changed. All the grief was still in it. But there was something else now. Wonder, and something like fear.
"He did that," Arran said. "Just like that. Twice. Every time."
Corin looked at his own gray hand.
"I know," he said. "I think he still does."
Arran stared at him. Then, slowly, he reached down and picked up the cup of cold broth by his foot, skin and all, and drank it down in one go, and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand.
"Well," he said, hoarse. "Then I'd better eat something. He'd have something to say about that."
And he got up, all elbows and knees, and went off toward the bread racks, and Corin sat alone by the raked ashes with his gray hand on his knee, and the hum came back into his throat, round and round, very quietly, as if the old man by the fire were pleased.
