Chapter 67: Vespera Comes North, Part 3 of 5: The Ear
The Ear
They fed her first. That was the rule. Bread in your hands before anything else.
She sat on a grass mat by the big fire in the middle of the camp, where the ashes of the rite fire had been raked flat and a small new fire built on top of them, and ate two of the flat dark loaves and a bowl of the brown broth and a handful of the sticky black berries, very fast, without speaking, the way a person eats who has been lying in a box for two days with nothing but a water bottle and Garrett's singing. Garrett sat a little way off and ate three loaves and two bowls, and talked between mouthfuls to anyone who would listen, which was nobody, about the toll gate on the north road and the men in gray who had poked the turnips with a pike.
"Right through," he said. "Right down through the turnips. I thought, that's it, they've got her. They've stuck her like a pig. And I'm singing, I'm still singing, I'm singing louder, because what else can you do. And the pike stopped a finger's width off the boards. A finger's width. And he pulled it out and looked at it and there was a turnip on the end of it. And he laughed. And he waved us through." He shook his head. "I'll never eat a turnip again. Not as long as I live."
"You've just eaten one," said Oren. "In the broth."
Garrett looked at his bowl in horror.
Vespera finished her broth. She put the bowl down on the mat beside her, very neatly, and wiped her mouth on the back of one blue hand, and looked up.
At Corin.
He was sitting on the other side of the small fire, facing north, the way the Ashborn sat. Lyra was beside him. Oren beyond her. Kestrel had gone back to the gray tent for something. The afternoon light was going long and gold across the camp, and the faces round the fire were lit warm on one side and cold blue on the other.
Vespera looked at him for a long time.
Not at his face. At his arms. His hands, lying on his knees, gray to the wrist and beyond, with the black fingertips. And then up. Up the arms of his coat, slowly, as if she could see through the cloth. To the shoulder. And stopped there. At the place. A hand's width below, less a finger.
Her mouth went thin.
She did not say anything about it. Corin waited for her to. She had always said something, he knew. You're running out. That was what she had said once, in her shop. It was in the Ledger. You're running out, harvester. He waited for her to say it again.
She did not.
She got up instead. Stiffly, with one hand on the ground, and her knees cracking. And came round the little fire to where he sat, and stood over him.
"Sit still," she said.
He sat still.
She put out one blue hand and took hold of his chin. Her fingers were dry and rough and strong and very cold. She did not hold it hard. But she held it firmly, the way a barber holds a chin, or a doctor. And she turned his head. Slowly. To the right. Away from her. So that the left side of his head was turned toward her and toward the low gold light.
And she bent down and looked behind his left ear.
Corin knew what she was looking at. He did not need to see it. He had felt it every day for weeks, with his fingertips, when he thought nobody was watching. A patch of skin behind the left ear, about the size of a thumbprint, where the hair did not grow. Smooth and tight and shiny. A burn. Old Hask had found it with his fingers, two nights ago. Corin did not know how he had got it. It was in the Ledger, he thought. A lamp. Something about a lamp. He had read it, and it had not stayed.
Vespera looked at it for a long time.
She did not touch it. She bent close, so close that he could feel her breath on his neck, and looked, and did not move. The fire crackled. Garrett had stopped talking. Lyra, beside Corin, had gone very still.
The silence went on.
Corin could not see Vespera's face. Only the edge of it, at the corner of his eye. The long sharp nose. A gray strand of hair. But he could feel her looking. It was like the feeling of the Glass on the back of the neck, that first night, when he had sat with his back to it. A steady dry prickle, on that one small patch of skin, as if she were reading something there that was no longer written.
Then she let out her breath.
"Ah," she said.
That was all.
She let go of his chin. She straightened up, with a hand in the small of her back. She stood there for a moment looking down at the top of his head. Her face had no expression on it at all.
Then she turned and went back round the fire to her mat, and sat down, and picked up her empty bowl and looked into it as if there might be more broth in it.
Corin put his hand up behind his ear. To the smooth place. It was warm, from her breath, and from the fire.
"What?" he said. "Ah, what?"
Vespera did not look up from the bowl.
"Ah, nothing," she said. "Ah, I'm old, and I've been in a box, and my eyes aren't what they were."
She was lying. He knew it. He did not know how. He supposed she had never been a good liar either, or that he had once known her well enough to tell.
Lyra, beside him, was looking at Vespera very hard.
Vespera turned the bowl round in her blue hands. Then she put it down again, very carefully, on the mat, and looked up at last. Not at Corin. At the sky over the ridge, where the milky light of the Glass was beginning to show as the sun went down in the west.
"Later," she said. "After. When you've paid me." She drew a breath. "Then we'll see what's behind your ear, harvester. And what isn't."
The Price
"Paid you?" said Lyra.
It was the first thing she had said since Vespera climbed out of the cart. Her voice was sharp.
Vespera looked at her. And Corin saw something pass between them that he could not read. A look. The look of two people who have stood in the same room together many times. He did not remember any of those times. He only saw the look, and knew that it was old.
"Paid me," said Vespera. "Yes, Miss Thorne. He owes me a price. He's always owed me a price. That's how it works, with me. I don't do anything for nothing." She sniffed. "Well. Not often."
"You said there was no price anymore," said Lyra. "You said so. The day you came in through Amos's door. You said you'd lost the jar and you were doing it for nothing." She stopped. Her face changed. "I remember. You were blue to the waist."
Corin did not remember. But he believed it.
Vespera's mouth did its small twist at the corner.
"I was," she said. "Blue to the waist and wet to the bone and very cross. And I had lost it. In the third tank. I'd had to choose, you see, in the dark, with Gant's men coming down the steps, between the jar and the bundle. I could only carry one. I chose the bundle." She was quiet for a moment. "And then I said there was no price. Because there was nothing left to pay it with."
She put her hand inside her crushed dark coat. Into some deep inside pocket, on the left, under the arm.
And brought out a jar.
It was small. No bigger than a hen's egg. Glass, thick and old and slightly greenish, with a lip round the top and a stopper of dark wax pressed down hard into the neck and sealed over. And the whole of it, glass and wax and all, was blue.
A deep, dark, perfect blue. The blue of the sky an hour after sunset. The blue of Vespera's hands. It had gone into the glass and stayed there, staining it right through, so that when she held it up between her finger and thumb in the gold light of the low sun, the light came through it blue and lay on her face in a small blue patch, like a bruise.
Inside the blue, very faint, something glowed.
The whole fire went quiet.
"I went back," said Vespera.
She turned the jar in her fingers. The blue light moved on her cheek.
"After the fire. After Midwinter. When the city had gone to bed and the gray coats had stopped poking about in the Dye-Works. I went back down. To the vats. With a bucket and a ladder and a lamp." Her voice was quite flat. "The third tank's twelve feet deep and it's been full of indigo for sixty years. It took me two days to bale it. Two days and two nights. On my own. And at the bottom, in the muck, in the corner, there it was." She held it up a little higher. "Seal not broken. Not a crack in it. Blue to the neck. But whole."
Nobody said anything.
"So you see," said Vespera, "there's a price again."
She held the jar out. Across the small fire. To Corin.
He looked at it. At the little blue egg of glass in her blue fingers, with the faint glow inside it.
He did not take it.
"The V. jar," he said slowly. "In the Ledger. It says I'm to open it. After Midwinter. That's the price." He looked up at her. "It doesn't say what's in it."
"No," said Vespera. "It doesn't. Because I didn't tell you."
"What's in it?"
"Something of mine." She did not lower her hand. "Something I've kept for a long time. Something I want you to see. That's the price, harvester. You don't keep it. You don't take it. You walk it. You go in, and you look, and you come out again, and then it's done, and you've paid."
"Why now?" said Lyra. Her voice was still sharp. Corin heard what was under it. Fear. "Why here? He's tired. He's only just... last night, they took a whole woman out of him. A whole person. He's barely..."
"Because where he's going," said Vespera, "I can't follow him to collect."
She said it very simply. She did not look at Lyra. She looked at Corin.
"You're going onto the Glass," she said. "Tomorrow, or the day after. Kestrel told me as soon as I got out of that box. Out there, nothing burns and nothing's taken. You couldn't open this out there if you wanted to. You couldn't walk it. And when you come back off the other side..." She stopped. "If you come back. You won't be the same man. Nobody is." Her blue fingers tightened very slightly on the jar. "I'd like to be paid, harvester. While you still know my name."
The fire crackled. The sun was going down behind the hills in the west, red, and the shadows of the tents stretched out long across the camp toward the ridge.
Corin looked at the jar.
He thought about the shelves. About all the things on them that he had not looked at. About Vivienne, who had sat in the front room of his mind for six days, shouting, until a ring of strangers and a blind old man had breathed her out of him. About how light he felt now. How quiet. How easy it had been, under the black thorn tree that morning, to let a small warm thing sit in him.
This was not taking. This was walking. Going in and coming out. Like walking through a house and closing the door behind you.
He had walked Lyra's father's study. He had felt the sea in a harbor.
He put out his gray hand.
"Corin," said Lyra.
"It's all right," he said. He did not look at her. "It's a price. I owe it. I'd rather pay it now, while I've got room."
Vespera put the jar into his palm.
It was cold. Colder than the air. Colder than the glass of a window in winter. It lay in the middle of his gray palm, small and heavy and blue, and the faint glow inside it seemed to shift, a little, when it touched his skin. As if something inside it had turned over in its sleep.
He closed his fingers round it.
"Where?" he said.
"Kestrel's tent," said Vespera. "Where it's warm, and dark, and nobody will come in." She got up off her mat, slowly, with a hand on the ground. "And bring the girl. You'll want somebody to call you back."
Walking a Stone
Kestrel's tent was bigger inside than it looked.
Corin had seen it from outside, the tent of hands, gray all over. He had not been inside. Nobody went inside, it seemed, unless Kestrel asked them. Now she held the flap back for them, and they went in one after another, stooping: Vespera, and Corin with his crutch, and Lyra, and then Kestrel herself, last, letting the flap fall shut behind her.
It was dark. Warm and close and dark, after the long gold light outside. A fire burned low in a ring of black stones in the middle of the floor, under the smoke hole, and gave a deep red glow and not much more. The smell of burnt honey was very strong. The floor was covered in furs, thick and soft, layer on layer of them, so that your feet sank in. And round the walls, on the inside of the hides, there were things hanging. Bone charms on strings. Small bundles tied with thread. A long spear with a bone point. A string of dried herbs. A cloak of white fur. And a great many small bags, of hide and of cloth, each one tied at the neck and hanging from a peg on the frame, and each one, Corin saw as his eyes got used to the dark, with a little mark painted on it in gray. A different mark on each. A bird. A fish. A hand. A wavy line.
"What are those?" he said.
"The ones we carry," said Kestrel. "A thing from each. A tooth. A lock of hair. A button." She did not look at them. "So that our hands remember, when we forget."
There were a great many bags.
"Lie down," said Vespera. "There. By the fire. On your back."
Corin lay down. It took a while, with the bad leg. The furs were soft under him and warm from the fire and smelled of smoke and goat. He lay on his back and looked up at the smoke hole, a small round patch of sky going from gold to green as the sun set outside.
Vespera knelt down on his right side. Slowly, with her hand on the ground and her knees cracking. She took off her rope belt with all its needles and bottles and laid it on the furs beside her, carefully, out of the way. Then she took his right hand in her left. Her blue fingers closed round his gray ones. They were dry and cold and strong.
"You know how this goes," she said. "It's in your little book. You read it to me once, the page from Linus. To walk a stone, and not keep it."
He did know. He had read it. He did not remember reading it to her, but he knew the page. It was copied in Ledger VIII, near the front, from the old book Gideon had.
"On the brow," he said. "Not the mouth. Not the chest. The brow. And let it come up into you like water, and don't drink."
"That's it." She nodded. "Don't drink. Don't hold. Look at it and let it go past. Like standing in a river and watching the leaves go by."
"And if I start to drink?"
"Then she calls you." Vespera jerked her chin across him.
Lyra was sitting on his other side. Not beside him. At his head. Cross-legged on the furs, close, so close that if he tipped his head back he could see her upside down, her face red in the firelight and very serious, with the gray line the child had painted on it last night still there, faded, down her nose. She had her gloved hands in her lap. She was not touching him.
"You'll know," said Vespera to her. "You know his face. If it starts to go slack. If he stops looking and starts taking. You'll see it."
"I'll see it," said Lyra.
"Then you say his name."
"I know."
"Say it like you mean it."
"I said it last night," said Lyra. "In the ring. Loud enough for the whole camp." She looked down at Corin, upside down. "I can do it again."
Kestrel had sat down by the door flap, with her back against the frame and her long legs drawn up. She did not speak. She was there, Corin understood, the way the stone outside the ring had been there. To watch.
Vespera held out her free hand.
Corin put the jar in it.
She held it up, for a moment, between her finger and thumb, in the red light of the fire. The blue glass glowed dark. Then she took the small sharp knife from her belt and, very carefully, cut round the wax seal on the neck. Once round. Twice. The wax came away in a thin blue ring, and she laid it on the furs. The stopper under it was a plug of cork, gone black. She did not pull it out. Not yet.
"Shut your eyes," she said.
Corin shut them.
He felt her lean over him. Felt the cold of the jar come close to his face. And then the cold glass, pressed gently against the middle of his forehead, just above his eyebrows, where the brow is. The glass was so cold it stung. He felt her thumb on the cork.
"Breathe," said Vespera. "Slow. In through the nose. Out through the mouth. Sit. Still. Breathe."
He breathed.
And in the dark behind his eyes, in the place where Malakor had been, very far off, so faint that it might have been the fire, a voice said:
Go on, boy.
Corin's breath caught.
Malakor?
Nothing.
Malakor, are you...
Nothing more. Only that. Three words, from a long way off. From the other side of a wall. And then gone again, back into the quiet.
But he had been there.
"Steady," said Vespera's voice, close. "Steady. What was that?"
"Nothing," said Corin. His voice was not quite steady. "Somebody I carry. Saying go on."
A pause.
"Well," said Vespera. "Then go on."
He felt her thumb push. Heard the soft wet sound of the cork coming out of the neck.
And something cold and blue and very old came up out of the jar against his forehead, and poured into him like water through a crack, and the tent and the fire and the furs and Lyra's face and Vespera's hand went away.
Oakhaven, Twenty-Eight Years Ago
Rain.
That was the first thing. Before light, before anything. The sound of rain. Soft and steady and close overhead, not on slate, not on tile, but on something thick and soft and dry. Thatch. Rain on a thatched roof, the sound of it soaked up, muffled, a long low hush like someone breathing out.
Then the smell.
Wet earth, coming in from outside, rich and dark, the smell of a spring night when everything is growing. Woodsmoke from a fire. And under that, close, two other smells. One sharp and clean: soap. Yellow soap, the hard kind, scrubbed into hands. And one he knew from long ago, from the Ministry's back rooms and the floors of cellars. Warm and thick and coppery.
Blood.
Then the light came.
He was in a room. A small room, low, with a ceiling of dark beams so close that he could have reached up and touched them. Rough white walls, the plaster uneven, bulging in places. A floor of stone flags, worn smooth, with a rag rug in the middle of it, red and blue and brown. A window, small and square and deep in the thick wall, with rain running down the dark glass and nothing beyond it but the night. A fire in a wide stone hearth, burning high, too high for a spring night, with a big black kettle hanging over it on a hook and steam pouring out of its spout.
And a bed.
It was against the far wall, under the low beams. A wooden bed, plain, with a straw mattress and a heap of blankets and a pillow, and on the pillow a woman.
He could not see her face.
He tried. He looked straight at her, the way you look at a face across a room, and he could see the shape of her. The dark hair, spread out loose and wet on the pillow. The pale skin. The line of the jaw. The hands on the blanket, gripping it. But the face itself would not come. It was blurred. Soft. Like a face seen through a steamed-up window, or through tears. Every time he looked at it, it slid away, the way a word slides away when you try to remember it.
It was not the light. The room was bright enough. It was the memory itself. The memory was keeping her face from him. On purpose. As if the person who had kept this memory had wrapped her face up in something soft before putting it away, so that it could not be seen.
He understood, then, that he was not in his own body.
He was standing. That was the first thing he noticed about the body he was in. Standing by the fire, light on his feet. Lighter than he had stood in years. No crutch. No bad leg. No ache in the knee. And quick. The body moved quickly, without thinking, the way a young body moves. It turned to the kettle and lifted it off the hook with a cloth round the handle, in one movement, and poured hot water out of it into a wide basin on the hearth, steam rising, and put the kettle back, and turned again.
Hands. He saw the hands. They were young. Thin and strong, with long quick fingers. White, with a few freckles on the backs. Not blue. Not blue at all. Clean, scrubbed pink, smelling of yellow soap, with the sleeves rolled up past the elbows.
And hair. It fell forward over his shoulder as the body bent to the basin. Long, and thick, tied back loosely with a bit of string and coming loose. Red. Bright red, like a fox's back, like a fire, catching the light from the hearth.
Vespera.
He was inside Vespera. Young. Years and years before the gray. Quick and light and red-haired, in a cottage, on a spring night, in the rain.
He did not drink. He remembered that. Don't drink. Stand in the river. Let the leaves go by.
He let them go by.
The woman in the bed cried out.
It was a low sound, long, dragged out of her. Not a scream. Deeper. The sound of someone pushing against something enormous. Her hands on the blanket went white at the knuckles. Her head went back on the pillow and the dark wet hair spilled over the edge of it.
And young Vespera's body moved. Fast. Two steps across the stone floor to the bed, and down on her knees beside it, and her quick freckled hands on the woman's hands, prying the fingers off the blanket and taking them in her own.
"There," said young Vespera's voice. It came out of Corin's own mouth, or seemed to. Light and quick and a little hoarse, with an accent he did not know, an accent from somewhere north, soft at the edges. Not the low dry voice from under the Dye-Works. A girl's voice. "There. That's it. That's it, my love. Nearly there. Nearly. One more like that and we'll have him."
Him.
The woman in the bed was breathing very fast. Her blurred face turned on the pillow toward the sound of the voice.
"Is he..." Her voice was hoarse and thin and tired, so tired. And Corin could not hear it properly either. Like the face, it was blurred. Soft. A voice heard through a wall. He could hear the words, but not the sound of them. Not what it was like. "Is he still there? At the door?"
"He's there," said young Vespera. "He's not moved. Don't you worry about the door. You worry about this. One more."
"I can't."
"You can. You can, love. You're stronger than you know." A hand pushing the wet hair back off the blurred forehead. "One more. Then it's done. Then you can sleep for a week."
The woman in the bed made a sound that might have been a laugh.
Then her face twisted, blurred as it was, and her hands crushed young Vespera's hands so hard that Corin felt it, the bones grinding, and she pushed.
And the rain came down on the thatch, soft and steady, and the fire roared, and somewhere outside in the dark, over the sound of the rain, there was another sound. A sound Corin knew. Water. Not rain. Running water. A river. Close. Just beyond the wall of the cottage, going over stones in the dark, fast and full with the spring.
Oakhaven, he thought.
He did not know how he knew the name. He did not know how he knew the sound of that river.
But he knew it.
