Chapter 39: The Slag Trade, Part 1 of 5: Shelf Forty
Shelf Forty
The ledge was no wider than Corin's two hands laid side by side.
He could feel the edge of it under his heels. Round, slimy stone, worn smooth by a hundred years of river, with a skin of green weed on it that was half frozen and half not, so that it was slippery in some places and rough in others, and he could not tell which until his boot found it. Behind him, the wall of the arch. Old brick, wet and cold, sweating river water, so cold it burned through his shirt and his coat into the skin of his back. In front of him, nothing. Air. And then, an arm's length below, the black water.
He could not see the water. It was too dark round this side of the arch, out of the gray of the Ash lamp and the yellow of the hanging oil lamp in the great square room. But he could hear it. It lapped against the stone just under his boots, slow and soft, slap, and slap, and a long sucking pull in between. He could smell it. Weed and oil and old rope and something rotten, a long way down. And he could feel it, coming up off the surface. A cold that did not move like air. A cold that sat.
His left hand held the iron chain. It came down out of the dark above him, from a ring somewhere up on the inside of the arch, and went down past him into the water. It was rough with rust, and so cold that his fingers had stopped hurting and gone dead, and he could not feel if he was holding it tight or not. He held it tighter, in case.
His right hand held Gideon's.
The boy stood on the ledge beside him, so close that their shoulders touched. He was not holding the chain. He did not need to. He stood with his back flat to the wet brick and his toes over the edge, the way a bird stands on a wire, as if falling was a thing that happened to other people. His thin fingers lay inside Corin's coal-black ones, quite still. They were not cold. Or if they were, he did not know it.
Corin's bad leg hung beside him. He had it bent a little at the knee, the foot just touching the ledge, with all his weight on the other one. He could not feel the bad foot at all now. He could feel the knee. It was a hot round knot of hurt, beating slowly, in time with his heart. And above it, through the tight strips of his shirt, he could feel the wet. Warm, at first. Then cooler. Then cold, running down the inside of his trouser leg into his boot.
He did not look down. There was nothing to see.
And inside his head, behind his left ear, where Malakor should have been, there was silence.
Not the silence of someone who is not talking. Corin had known that silence for twenty years. Malakor sulking. Malakor sleeping. Malakor thinking, and not wanting to be disturbed. Those silences had a shape. They had an edge, like the edge of a table you know is there in the dark. This was not like that. This was the silence of an empty room. Of a door left open, and a cold draft coming through, and nobody there.
The place behind his ear still throbbed where the Ash lamp had burned it. A slow, dull beat, like a bruise. That was all.
Malakor, he thought again, as hard as he could.
Nothing came back.
He made himself stop. He made himself listen to the room instead.
They were still there. Round the corner of the arch, in the great square room, twenty paces away. He could hear every sound they made. The stone of the room bounced the sounds out under the arch to him, and the water carried them, so that he heard them more clearly than he would have done if he had been standing among the crates.
The tap of a stick on the stone. Slow. Patient. Tap. And a step. Tap. And a step.
The swing and creak of a lantern on its handle.
The rustle of good cloth.
And the peppermint. It came round the corner of the arch on the cold air and sat in the back of his throat. Sharp. Sweet. So strong, in that wet rotten place, that it made his eyes water.
"Shelf forty," said Mistress Fane. Her small hard voice. "It's round on the north side, sir. By the hum. I'll take you. You'll want the light."
"Thank you, Fane. Thank you. You're very good." The kind voice. Soft and a little breathless. "And Mr. Drabble. Will you come with us? I'd like you to see. So you can tell your Nym people how careful we are. They do like to hear that we are careful."
"I've seen glasses before," said the rough voice. Drabble, then. He said it the way a man says it who wants to be somewhere warm with a drink. "Seen plenty."
"Not like these." A smile, in the voice. A small fond laugh. "Not like these, Mr. Drabble. These are very special. Come and see."
A pause. Then the heavy boots. Then the small quick shoes. Then the stick. Tap. And a step. Across the stone of the great square room. Up the three shallow steps, slowly, tap, step, tap, step, the kind voice saying "There we are," softly, at the top of them, the way an old man talks to his own knees. And into the ring passage. And away.
The yellow light went with them. Corin saw it go. A faint warm glow on the wet brick round the corner of the arch that had not been there before, and now was fading. Back to the gray. Back to the dark.
The voices went round the curve of the ring passage. East, and then north. Toward the middle. Toward the hum.
They grew small. They grew faint.
Corin breathed out.
He had not known he was holding it. It came out of him in a long shaky cloud, white even in the dark, and he felt his whole body sag against the wet brick, and his good knee nearly went, and he gripped the chain so hard that the rust bit into his dead fingers.
"They've gone round," said Gideon. In his ordinary voice. Not loud. Not a whisper. "To the north side. Shelf forty's the second door past the hum. I've been in it. With crates."
Corin turned his head and looked at him. A white smudge in the dark.
"We have to go," he whispered. "Now. While they're on the north side. West. Back the way we came. To the counting room, and the lamp, and the stairs." He swallowed. "Before they come back."
"Yes," said Gideon.
He did not move.
Corin did not move either.
They stood there on the ledge, the two of them, over the black water, and neither of them moved, and Corin knew why he was not moving, and it frightened him more than the water did.
Shelf forty, he thought. Fifty-two glasses. Such very good children.
He's going to look at them.
He's going to hold them up to the light and look at them, the way the Duke looks at his gallery.
And you are going to go home.
The Ledger was not here to tell him what to do. Lyra had it. Up in Cinder Lane, in a doorway, against her heart. And Malakor was not here to tell him what to do either. Malakor would have said go. He knew that. Malakor would have said you have seen the crates and you have the jar and you have the line from the counting room book, and that is a good night's work, and you are bleeding, and if you go round to the north side you will be caught, and if you are caught they will put you in the strap chair, and then you will tell them about the barge.
Malakor would have been right.
He could almost hear it. The old dry voice. Go home, Corin.
But he could only almost hear it. And the almost was not enough.
"I want to see him," he said.
It came out of him before he had decided to say it. Low and cracked. He heard it, and he knew that it was true, and he knew it was stupid, and he did not take it back.
"I want to see his face. So I'll know it. So that when I see it again, on the Hill, or in a carriage, or on the Fish Stairs on a Friday morning, I'll know." He took a breath. "I can't burn that. I need it."
Gideon thought about this.
"All right," he said.
He said it the way he had said it on the coal chute, and at the bottom of two nines and four, and at the crates. As if Corin had asked him to pass the bread.
"There's a door," he said. "Before shelf forty. A store room. Brooms and that. It doesn't lock. It's got a hole in it where the knot came out of the wood. You can see shelf forty from it." He paused. "I used to sit in it. When I was carrying. When Gant wanted me and I didn't want to go."
Corin looked at the white smudge of his face.
"You didn't want to go?"
Gideon thought about that too.
"I don't know," he said. "I think I knew I wasn't meant to want to. So I sat in the cupboard. It was nice in there. Quiet." He moved his hand a little, inside Corin's. "Come on. Before your leg goes."
They went back round the corner of the arch.
Sideways. Slowly. Heels on the ledge. Corin's left hand sliding down the iron chain and letting go of it, and finding the wet brick, and the edge of the arch. Round. Into the gray.
The gray took him again. The burning came back into his hands like a slap, and the smoke rose from his knuckles, and the place behind his ear flared up hot. He did not stop for it. He went up the three shallow steps from the water on his good knee and his hands, with Gideon pulling him by the collar, and out across the bare stone floor of the great square room. Ten paces. Out of the gray. Into the dark. The hanging oil lamp had gone with them; there was only the faint gray seep from the brass house on the far wall now, and the black shapes of the crates.
The burning stopped.
Nothing came into the silence behind his ear. He had not thought it would. He had hoped.
Up the three shallow steps on the far side. Into the ring passage. The Ash lamps on their brackets along the curved wall, thin and gray. He kept to the inner wall, away from them, where the light was thinnest. Left, toward the east. Round the curve. Past the doors he had passed before, going the other way. Past the store rooms. Past the bench by the lift.
It was empty. A long plain plank of black oak, worn smooth and dipped in two places, side by side, where two great weights had sat on it every day for a long, long time. Beside it, the lift. An iron cage in a shaft, with a big wheel and a rope.
Beyond the bench, on the inner wall, a door stood open. A narrow stone stair went up behind it, into the dark. The kitchen stair. A smell came down it. Old fat and onions and cold ashes and bread.
And beyond that, on the outer wall, side by side, at the bottom of a step in the floor, sat the Hollow brothers.
Corin stopped dead.
They had not gone to the bench. They had sat down here instead, on the floor, against the outer wall of the passage, where the stone was dry. Side by side. With their great bare gray feet out in front of them across the stone, so far across that there was only a narrow gap between their toes and the inner wall. Their backs were against the wall. Their spade hands lay on their knees, palms down, fingers spread. Their heads were bowed. They were not asleep. Their eyes were open. They looked at the floor between their feet.
Saul was humming.
Gideon did not stop. He walked past them, through the narrow gap between their toes and the wall, in his too-big boots, without hurrying. He did not look at them. Corin went after him. On his good leg, with his hand on the inner wall, dragging the other. His bad foot passed a hand's width from Big Gideon Hollow's huge gray toes.
The empty eyes did not lift.
The humming did not change.
They went on, round the curve, toward the north. The hum of the middle grew. It came up through the floor and into Corin's feet and into his teeth, until his whole skull rang with it, the way a bell rings after it has been struck. The breathing under the floor grew too. In. And out. So close now that he could feel the stone lift, ever so slightly, under his good boot. And sink. And lift.
Ahead of them, round the curve, there was a light. Yellow. Warm. Coming out of a door on the outer wall and lying across the passage floor in a long bright square.
And voices. And the peppermint.
Gideon put out his hand, without looking back, and touched a door on their right. A plain plank door, low, with an iron latch. It was a little way before the bright square. In the dark. He lifted the latch with one finger, so slowly that it made no sound at all, and drew the door open.
Brooms. Buckets. A smell of soap and old wet rags.
They went in.
Good Yield
The cupboard was very small and very dark, and it smelled of lye.
Corin had to stand with his back pressed into a corner, between a stiff broom and a stack of tin buckets, so that there would be room for Gideon too. His bad leg would not fit. He had to let it lean against the buckets, and the buckets tipped a little, and he caught the top one with his hand before it could ring against the others, and held it, and did not breathe until he was sure it was still.
Gideon pulled the door to behind them. Not shut. Nearly. He knew exactly how far. A thin line of gray from the Ash lamps in the passage lay down the edge of it, no wider than a thread.
Then the boy reached past Corin, in the dark, and found something on the back wall of the cupboard with his fingers. And moved aside. And a small round spot of light came on.
It was on the wall. Yellow, and as big as a farthing. Corin stared at it. Then he understood. The back of the cupboard was not stone. It was a board, an old plank partition, put up long ago to make a cupboard out of the end of a room. And there was a knothole in the plank. And on the other side of the plank was shelf forty, with a lantern in it.
Gideon touched his sleeve, and moved out of the way.
Corin bent his head and put his eye to the hole.
The room on the other side was long and narrow, the shape of a coffin, and the same stone as all the rest of the cellars, with a low curved roof. It was very clean. Somebody swept it. The floor was bare and pale, without a speck of coal dust on it, and the walls had been washed with lime, so that they were white, and the white threw the lantern light back and made the room brighter than any room down here had a right to be. There were no crates in it. There were shelves.
They went all down both long walls, from the floor to the curve of the roof. Narrow wooden shelves, close together, like the shelves in a shop where they sell spices. And on the shelves, in rows, in their hundreds, stood glasses.
Corin knew them. He had known them for twelve years. The squat round intake glasses of the Black Bank, as big as a fist, with a thick flat bottom and a wide lip. He had held a thousand of them. Ten thousand. He had cleared them in the cold room with his rod, one by one, every morning, while Briggs ticked them off in his ledger at the stand by the stairs. He would have known that shape anywhere. In the dark. In his sleep.
But these were not clean.
Every glass on every shelf had something in it. A haze. A cloud. A soft gray mist that hung inside the glass and moved, very slowly, like smoke in a still room, turning over and over on itself. Some were pale, almost clear, with only a thin thread of gray winding round near the bottom. Some were so thick that he could not see through them at all. And in some, the thickest, there were colors. Small and faint, deep in the gray, like the glints in a piece of coal. A flash of blue. A spark of yellow. A thread of red that came and went.
Slag.
The bits that are left. Pieces of people. Nobody's.
Shelves and shelves of it.
And in front of the shelves, at the far end of the room, by a small plain table with the lantern on it, stood three people.
Mistress Fane he knew. Small and thin and gray-haired, in a dark dress with a white collar, with a pencil behind her ear and a ledger open in her hands. She stood beside the table with her back very straight, like a clerk who has been called in by the head of the office.
The man beside her was Drabble. He was square and short and weathered, in a thick blue boat coat with brass buttons and a knitted cap. A river man. His face was red and wind-burned, with small pale eyes and a mouth that turned down. He stood with his hands in his coat pockets, a little apart from the other two, and looked at the shelves the way a man looks at somebody else's sheep. Bored. Counting.
And the third man stood at the table with his back half turned to the knothole, holding a glass up to the lantern.
He was thin. Very thin. Thinner than Corin had pictured him from the voice. A long narrow body in a long black coat, beautifully cut, that came down nearly to his ankles, with a high collar turned up at the back of the neck. He leaned on a stick. Ebony, with a silver head shaped like a closed flower. His left knee was bent a little and his weight was on the right, and Corin could see, even from here, the way the leg had been held like that for a long time. A bad knee. An old one.
His hair was white. Pure white, thick and soft, brushed back from a high forehead. His hands, holding up the glass, were in black kid gloves, very fine and very tight. And on the lapel of the long black coat, catching the lantern light as he moved, there was a small round badge. Blue enamel, with a white rim. Corin could not see the picture on it. He did not need to.
The man turned the glass slowly in the light.
"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, look at that. Look at that, Fane."
He turned a little further toward the lantern, and Corin saw his face.
It was a good face.
That was the worst of it. Corin had been waiting for something else. He did not know what. A cruel mouth. Cold eyes. A face like Gant's, or like Big Harl's, a face you could look at and know. But this was the face of somebody's grandfather. Long and thin and gentle, with soft pink cheeks, and a long nose with a pair of small gold spectacles perched on the end of it, and pale blue eyes behind the spectacles that were wet and bright and creased at the corners. The face of a man who had smiled a great deal in his life. Who smiled now. At the glass.
"You see the blue?" he said. "There, near the bottom. That's a mother. I'd stake my pension on it. The way it turns. They always turn like that, mothers. As if they're looking for something." He sighed, very gently. "Some little one at Saint Bride's has given us his mother, bless him. He won't miss her now."
Mistress Fane made a note in her ledger.
"Fifty-two in Friday's cart, sir," she said. "All from the doctor's crypt. All drawn very fast. Most in two days. Eleven in one." She turned a page. "It's the new drops. Mr. Gant says he's never had a yield like it. Not from the river."
"No." The man set the glass down on the table. Carefully. In a row with others. Corin saw there were a great many glasses on the table, in neat rows, ten and ten and ten. "No, I don't suppose he has. I was quite proud of this batch, Fane. I don't mind telling you. I had the boys in the dispensary boil it down twice as long. We lost two of the mice, testing." He smiled. "But the children are much stronger than mice."
Drabble moved his feet.
"The Nym people don't want slag," he said. "I keep telling Gant. They don't want this muck. They want cold. Clean cold, in jars. From the little ones. That's what keeps a miner going in the deep seams. That's what Brask pays for."
"I know, Mr. Drabble. I know." The man turned and patted the river man's arm with one gloved hand. Kindly. The way you would pat a dog that has barked at the postman. "And Mr. Brask shall have his cold. Twelve crates, Monday's tide. Fane has counted them. But the slag is not for the north. The slag is for here." He waved his hand at the shelves, a small soft wave, like a man showing off his garden. "Lady Vivienne blends it. Bulk lots. Kindnesses, small. Suitable for blending. The ladies on the Hill pour it into their tea. A little river kindness, to make them feel warm and sorry for an afternoon. They pay a great deal for it. And the Bourse pays me. And I pay the dispensary. And the dispensary makes more drops. And on Friday morning, I go down to the Fish Stairs." He smiled at Drabble over his gold spectacles. "And I give out sweets."
"Never seen the point of it," said Drabble. "All that walking."
"No? Ah, well." The kind voice was not offended. "I like to see them, Mr. Drabble. That's all. I like to see their little faces. It's so easy to forget, sitting in an office on the Hill, that they are real children. With names, and mothers." A pause. "For a little while."
Corin's hand had closed on the edge of the plank so hard that a splinter went in under his nail. He felt it. He did not move.
"And the doctor?" said Mistress Fane. "Mr. Gant asked me to ask. He's been writing again. He wrote to the Bank, Mr. Gant says. Not just to you. To the Bank."
The man sighed.
"Doctor Silas," he said, "is a good man. A very good man. I have the greatest respect for him." He took off his spectacles, and took out a white handkerchief, and began to polish them, slowly, one lens and then the other. "He came to see me, you know. In the autumn. Up the Hill, in the rain, in that dreadful coat. He sat in my office for an hour and told me about his poor children. He wept. It was very moving." He held the spectacles up to the lantern, and looked through them, and went on polishing. "I told him the matter was under review. And I gave him a peppermint for the walk home."
"Mr. Gant says—"
"Mr. Gant may say what he likes. The doctor does not know, Fane. That is the beauty of it. He thinks he is saving them. He puts the glass on them with his own two hands, every one, and sits up with them all night, and then he gives the glasses to my cart on Friday to be taken away and disposed of properly, because the Office of Public Health has told him that slag is very dangerous to keep." He put the spectacles back on. "Which it is. Which it is. And while he is doing that, he is not doing anything else. If we stopped him, someone else would start. Someone less good. Someone who might ask where the cart goes." He smiled. "No. We leave the doctor quite alone. He is the best farmer we have."
Mistress Fane wrote it down.
Corin took his eye from the knothole.
He had to. He could not have kept it there any longer. He stood in the dark cupboard with his back against the broom and the buckets and his face in his coal-black hands, and he shook. All over. From his teeth to his heels. Not from the cold.
He is the best farmer we have.
He thought of the crypt under the chapel with no roof. The two long rows of trestle beds. The flushed faces and the moving lips. The tall gray tired man going down the rows at six in the morning, touching foreheads, lifting wrists. The dented kettle. The brown sugar in the tea. I'm one doctor in a crypt with fifty-two children on the floor.
Beside him in the dark, Gideon touched his arm.
"You're doing it again," the boy whispered. "The water. On your face."
Corin wiped it away with the back of his hand.
"Is it for the doctor?"
Corin nodded.
Gideon was quiet for a moment.
"He cut my toes off," he said. "He was nice about it."
On the other side of the plank, a chair scraped. The stick tapped. Tap. And a step.
"Well," said the kind voice, "that's the glasses. Very nice. Very nice indeed. Put them on the book, Fane, and I'll sign. And then I should like to see the cold, before Mr. Drabble's people take it away. I do like to see the cold." A soft laugh. "And then home to bed. These stairs will be the death of me."
"Your bag, sir," said Mistress Fane. "You've left it on the counting room table. With the new drops. Shall I bring it down to you?"
"No, no, my dear. Leave it there. I'll take it up with me when we've seen the cold. Nobody's going to steal a bag of medicine from the Bourse on a Sunday night." The stick tapped again. "There we are."
The light moved.
Corin put his eye back to the knothole, in time to see the lantern lifted off the table, and the long thin black shape of the man turning away from it toward the door, leaning on his stick. And for a moment, as he turned, the badge on his lapel caught the full light and Corin saw it clearly. The round blue seal. The white rim. The white open hand, and the small gold lamp above it.
Then the room went dark, and the shelves of glasses with it, all their slow gray clouds turning and turning in the black.
The yellow light came out into the passage. Past the cupboard. Corin saw it go along the thread of gray at the edge of the door. Brighter. And then less bright. Tap. And a step. Heavy boots, and small quick shoes. Going back the way they had come. East, then south. Toward the great square room and the low black arch and the twelve crates for the Frost Reach.
And behind them, on the counting room table, a black bag. Left there, on a Sunday night, because nobody was going to steal a bag of medicine from the Bourse.
The counting room was west.
On the way out.
