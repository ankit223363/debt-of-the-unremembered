Chapter 39: The Slag Trade, Part 4 of 5: Lull
Lull
Doctor Silas did not take it at once.
He looked at it, held up in Corin's black fingers in the gray light. He looked at it for a long time, the way he had looked at the leg. Then he pulled off his gray woolen gloves, finger by finger, and put them in his coat pocket, and held out his bare hand.
His hands were long and thin and very clean. Cleaner than anything else in the arches. The nails were cut short and square. The skin over the knuckles was red and cracked from washing in cold water and vinegar a hundred times a day. Corin put the vial into the palm, and the long fingers closed round it.
The doctor turned it in the light.
He looked at the label first. He read it, slowly, moving his lips a little, the way a man reads who is tired. The Ministry. Office of Public Health. Tonic, for the river fever. Children. Then the seal. He tipped the vial back and forth under his crooked spectacles, close to his eye, until he could see the open hand and the lamp in the blue wax.
His face did not change.
"Where did you get this?"
"From a bag," said Corin. "In the Bourse cellars. Last night. On the counting room table." He swallowed. "There were twenty. In a wooden tray. I took one."
"Whose bag?"
"I'll tell you. After. Tell me what it is first."
Doctor Silas looked at him over the spectacles.
Then he took hold of the blob of blue wax with his thumbnail and broke it.
It came away in two pieces, with a small dry crack. He put the pieces carefully in his waistcoat pocket. He took the tiny cork between his finger and thumb and drew it out. It came with a little sucking pop.
And the peppermint came out into the arch.
It was not as strong as the bag had been. One vial, not twenty. But in that small brick place, with the smoke and the cabbage and the damp, it was as sharp and clean and sudden as a window thrown open on a winter morning. Lyra flinched back on her bucket. Corin felt it go into his nose and his throat and down into his chest, and the back of his neck went cold, and his hands closed on the sacking.
Doctor Silas did not flinch.
He held the open vial a hand's width under his nose, and breathed in. Slowly. With his eyes shut. The way a man who knows wine breathes in over a glass. He held the breath. He let it out.
Then he breathed in again. Shorter. Sharper. Twice. Like a dog.
He opened his eyes.
He was looking at nothing. At the far wall of the arch. His long gray face had gone very still, and very far away, the way a face goes when it is looking at something a long way back. Years back.
"Peppermint," he said. "Oil of peppermint. A good deal of it. To hide the other." He breathed in once more. "And under that, sugar. Burnt sugar, to hide it again. And under that—"
He stopped.
He put the cork back into the vial. Carefully. Pressed it down with his thumb. And sat there on his knees by Corin's pallet, holding it in both hands in his lap.
"Under that," he said, "grayroot."
The word meant nothing to Corin. He saw that it meant nothing to Lyra either.
"It's a plant," said Doctor Silas. "A weed. A little gray thing with a white flower, no taller than your hand. It grows in old graveyards and on battlefields. Anywhere the ground has been dug up and left. The farmers in the valleys pull it up and burn it, because the sheep that eat it go strange. They forget the way home. They stand in the middle of the field and bleat, and don't know where they are." He turned the vial over in his long fingers. "If you take the root, and boil it down, and boil it again, and strain it, you get a liquid. Gray-green. Like this. Bitter as gall. We called it Lull."
"We?"
"In the war." He said it quite simply. "In the field hospitals, behind the lines, in the north. I was a young man. Younger than you. A surgeon's mate, with a saw and a bag and no idea what I was doing." He was quiet for a moment. "They'd bring the men in on carts. From the front. Twenty, thirty a night. Some of them with their legs gone, or their arms, or their faces. And some of them we could save, and most of them we could not. And for the ones we could not, there was nothing to give. No laudanum. The Ministry needed all the laudanum for the officers. So we gave them Lull."
Lyra was holding the Ledger very tightly in her lap.
"What does it do?" she said.
Doctor Silas looked at her.
"It loosens," he said.
He held up the vial between them.
"You know how memory sits in a man," he said. "Mister Vane knows better than any of us. It sits in him the way a nail sits in wood. Driven in. Held tight. That's why it takes a harvester and a rod and a great deal of skill to draw it out, and why it hurts. The wood holds on." He turned the vial a little. "Lull rots the wood."
Nobody said anything.
"A little of it," said Doctor Silas, "and the nails loosen. Just the small ones. The newest ones. A man who's been shot forgets the shot. He forgets the pain of it, the cart, the night. He lies back on the straw and looks at the roof of the tent and smiles, and asks you if it's raining, and he doesn't know why he's there. And he dies like that, quite peaceful. Not afraid." A pause. "That's what we gave it for. That was the kindness of it."
"And a lot of it?" said Corin.
"A lot of it, and the big nails go too. The old ones. The deep ones. His mother. His wife's face. His own name." The doctor's voice was very flat. "And they don't go anywhere. That's the thing. They don't go into a stone, the way they do at the Bank, all neat and drawn. They just come loose. And they fall out of him. In bits. In flakes, like old paint off a wall. All the time. And if there's nothing there to catch them, they just—go. Into the air. Into the straw. Gone."
"Slag," said Corin.
"Slag." Doctor Silas nodded. "In the war, we called it the falling fever. A man on Lull would start to shed. You could see it on a cold night, if you knew how to look. A sort of mist round his head, gray, like breath. And a smell on his breath. Sweet. Like pears gone bad." He stopped. "Like the children."
The arch was very quiet. From the fifth arch, through the sack curtain, the low terrible sound had stopped. There was only the coughing now. And under it, very faint, a woman's voice, singing something to somebody, very softly, over and over.
"I smelled it in the autumn," said Doctor Silas. "On the first of them. A boy from the Fish Stairs. I knelt by him in the crypt and I smelled his breath, and I thought, I know that. I know that smell. From somewhere. And I couldn't think where. It was nearly twenty years ago. I'd burned the war hospitals to pay for my first set of instruments. Most of them." He looked down at the vial. "Not the smell. You can't burn a smell, not all the way. It stays in the bottom of you. It waits."
He put the vial down on the brick floor beside the pallet. Carefully. Upright. As if it might spill. And took his hands away from it, and laid them flat on his knees.
"It's not a tonic," he said. "It's not for the river fever. It is the river fever. Or it makes it. Or it makes it so much worse that it might as well." He looked at the blue wax on the floor. "A child takes a little of this, in a sweet, every Friday. Just a drop or two. And the small nails loosen. And the fever comes, the ordinary river fever, that they get every winter from the water, and that most of them get over. But this time it doesn't sit in them the way it should. It pulls. And everything that's loose comes away with it. Faster and faster. And they start to shed. And their mothers bring them to me." His voice went very quiet. "And I put them on the glass."
He stopped.
Corin watched him understand it.
It did not happen all at once. It happened the way frost comes over a window. A line, at the edge. And then another. And then the whole pane, white.
"I put them on the glass," said Doctor Silas again, slowly. "To catch it. To catch the slag. So it wouldn't just go into the air and be lost. So there'd be something left. So that I could—" He stopped. "And it comes out faster. The more I draw, the faster it comes. Because it's loose. It's all loose. It wants to come. It practically jumps into the glass." He was staring at the vial. "Fifty-two in two months. Eleven in a single day."
He lifted his head and looked at Corin.
"Whose bag was it?" he said.
Corin opened the Ledger on Lyra's knee. He did not take it from her. He turned the pages with his black burned fingers until he found the place, near the back, where a strip of torn newspaper was tucked in, folded small. He unfolded it. His own writing, in pencil, Coker's pencil, shaky and smudged with coal.
He held it out to the doctor.
Fri. Health. St. Bride's. 52 glasses, very fast. Good yield. Shelf 40. Paid: A. P.
Doctor Silas read it.
He read it a long time. Longer than it took to read. Then he took off his crooked spectacles, and folded them, and held them in his hand. And looked at the floor.
"Saint Bride's," he said. "That's me."
The Best Farmer
Corin had a choice then, and he knew it.
He lay on the sacking with the hot heavy log of his leg beside him, and looked at the doctor kneeling on the brick floor with his spectacles folded in his hand, and he knew that he did not have to say the rest. Not now. Not all of it. He could tell him about the shelves, and the glasses, and the bag, and the letters. He could leave out the voice in the white room, and what it had said.
He is the best farmer we have.
He could keep that. Fold it small. Put it away. Doctor Silas need never know it had been said.
And then Doctor Silas would go back to the crypt under the chapel with no roof, and kneel by the trestle beds, and put the glass on the next child. Because he would think there was still some good in it. Some small good. Catching the slag, so it was not lost.
Rule one was no lies between you, and it was for partners. Doctor Silas was not his partner. He was a tired man with red eyes who had cut off Gideon's toes, and held Corin's leg on Saturday, and lost a boy called Ned at the sixth bell this morning.
He looked at Lyra.
She was looking back at him. She did not know what he was going to say. But she knew he was deciding something. She had learned his face, the way he had learned the Ledger. She gave the smallest nod. Not yes. Just I'm here.
"There's more," said Corin. "It's worse. Do you want it?"
Doctor Silas did not look up from the floor.
"Yes," he said.
So Corin told him.
He told it plainly, the way the doctor had told Bet. He told him about the shelves in the long white room, the hundreds of glasses, the slow gray clouds turning in them. The fifty-two on the table in rows, ten and ten and ten. The blue in one of them, near the bottom, turning as if it was looking for something, and the kind voice saying That's a mother. Some little one at Saint Bride's has given us his mother. The new drops, boiled twice as long. Two mice lost in the testing. But the children are much stronger than mice.
The bulk lots. Kindnesses, small. Suitable for blending. The ladies on the Hill pouring it into their tea.
The letters in the side pocket of the bag. The neat small hand across the bottom.
And then the last of it.
"He said you came to see him," said Corin. "In the autumn. In the rain. He said you sat in his office for an hour and wept. He said it was very moving." He made himself go on. "And he said that you don't know. That that's the beauty of it. That you put the glass on them with your own hands, and sit up with them all night, and then give the glasses to his cart on Friday, because the Office has told you slag is dangerous to keep. And that while you're doing that, you aren't doing anything else. And if they stopped you, someone else would start. Someone who might ask where the cart goes."
He stopped. He did not want to say the last of it. He said it.
"He said, We leave the doctor quite alone. He is the best farmer we have."
Nobody moved.
The coal fire hissed in its brick hearth at the end of the arch. Somewhere further down, in the third arch or the second, a baby cried, and somebody hushed it. The snow-light came round the edge of the sack curtain, gray and soft.
Doctor Silas knelt on the brick floor with his spectacles in his hand, and did not move at all.
Corin had seen men take bad news. At the Bank, in the intake hall. Men who came in with a stone to sell, a wedding, a first child, a father's voice, and were told by the clerk at the brass grille that it was not worth what they had thought. He had seen their faces fall in. He had seen them shout, and weep, and argue, and walk out without a word with their shoulders round. He had seen Lord Thorne's face, a little, on the barge, when Lyra had said Bourse and he had tapped once and stopped.
This was not like any of that.
Doctor Silas's face did not fall in. It did not do anything. It stayed exactly the same, long and gray and tired, with the red-rimmed eyes looking at the floor. Only his hands moved. They opened. The spectacles slid out of the long thin fingers onto the brick, with a small click, and lay there. And the hands stayed open on his knees, palm up, empty, as if he had just put something down that he had been carrying for a very long way and did not yet know that he had put it down.
"Farmer," he said.
He said it quietly. As if he were trying the word, to see if it fit.
"Yes," he said. "Yes. That's the word, isn't it. That's the right word." He looked at his open hands. "I plant the glass, and I tend it, and I sit up with it all night, and on Friday I bring in the harvest and load it on his cart. And I thank the driver. I always thank the driver. He's a nice young man. He has a cough."
"You didn't know," said Lyra. Her voice was thick. "Doctor. You couldn't have known. Nobody could. You were trying to help them."
"Oh, I was," said Doctor Silas. "I was. I was trying very hard." He did not look at her. "That's what makes it good farming, you see. A bad farmer, a lazy one, he'd let the crop rot in the field. He'd lose half of it. I didn't lose any. I was very careful. I was proud of how careful I was." A pause. "Eleven in one day. I wrote it down. I was going to send the numbers to the Office. So they'd see how bad it was getting. So they'd send help."
He laughed.
It was the worst sound Corin had ever heard. Worse than the sound from the fifth arch. A short, small, dry laugh, with nothing in it at all. Like a stick breaking.
Then he put both his clean, cracked, empty hands over his face, and bent forward on his knees, slowly, until his forehead was nearly on the brick floor of the arch beside Corin's pallet, and stayed there.
He did not make any more sound.
His shoulders did not shake. He just stayed bent over like that, in his coat that was more mend than coat, with his hands over his face. The way a man bends when he has been hit very hard in the stomach and is waiting to find out if he can breathe again.
Lyra got up off her bucket.
She did not say anything. She went and knelt down on the brick floor beside him, in her blanket, and put her hand on his back. Flat. Between his shoulders. And left it there. The way she had put it on Corin's chest in the doorway.
Corin lay on the pallet and watched them, and could not get up, and could not do anything.
After a long time, Doctor Silas sat up.
He did it slowly. He took his hands away from his face. His face was dry. Quite dry. He felt about on the floor for his spectacles, without looking, and found them, and unfolded them, and put them on. They were still crooked. He did not straighten them.
"Thank you," he said to Lyra, without looking at her.
She took her hand away.
He looked at Corin.
"You could have kept that from me," he said. "The last part."
"Yes."
"Why didn't you?"
Corin thought about it. He wanted to give the true answer, and he had to find it first.
"Because you'd have gone back," he said at last. "And put the glass on the next one. And not known why it felt wrong. And it would have eaten you, a bit at a time, not knowing." He stopped. "I know what that's like. Not knowing why something feels wrong. I do it every morning."
Doctor Silas looked at him for a while.
Then he nodded. Once.
"Yes," he said. "Yes. That's fair."
He looked at the vial, standing upright on the brick floor by the pallet, with its broken blue seal.
"I've a question for you, Mister Vane," he said. "And I want you to answer it the same way. Straight."
"All right."
"The man in the cellars. With the kind voice. Who gave out sweets." The doctor's voice was very level. "What did he look like?"
Corin told him.
Thin. Very thin. Tall, in a long black coat, beautifully cut, with the collar up at the back. White hair, thick and soft. A long gentle face with pink cheeks. Small gold spectacles. Pale blue eyes, wet and bright. Black kid gloves. An ebony stick with a silver head like a closed flower. A bad left knee. A blue enamel badge on his lapel. About sixty.
And the smell of peppermint, all over him.
Doctor Silas listened to all of it without moving.
When Corin had finished, he took the vial off the floor and held it up again, unstoppered it again, and breathed in over it once more. Slowly. With his eyes shut.
"Yes," he said, with his eyes still shut. "Yes. That's his office."
A Bowl on the Desk
"I went in the rain," said Doctor Silas.
He did not look at either of them while he told it. He looked at the vial in his hands. He turned it slowly, round and round, so that the gray-green liquid inside it tilted and settled, tilted and settled, catching the snow-light from the door.
"The second week of the autumn. After the fourth letter. When the first ones started to drain fast and nobody had written back." He was quiet for a moment. "I'd never been up the Hill. Not in my life. Not past the Lantern Bridge. I didn't know the way. I asked a lamplighter. He looked at my coat and told me the tradesmen's door was round the side."
The Office of Public Health, he said, was a tall narrow building of white stone, halfway up the Hill, on a street of tall narrow buildings of white stone. It had a black front door with a brass knocker in the shape of an open hand. It had clean steps, scrubbed every morning. It had a porter in a blue coat behind a desk in the front hall, under a painting of a woman in white with a lamp, and a long corridor going back behind the porter, with a black and white tiled floor, and doors on both sides with names painted on them in gold.
"I sat on a bench in the corridor for three hours," said Doctor Silas. "With my hat on my knees. Wet through. The clerks went past me with papers. Young men. Clean collars. They didn't look at me. One of them gave me a cup of tea, near the end. A kind boy. He said the Under-Secretary was very busy but that he always saw everybody in the end. That he was famous for it. That he never turned anybody away."
He turned the vial.
"And then a bell rang somewhere, and the boy took me down to the end of the corridor. To the last door. And knocked. And a voice said come in."
He stopped.
Corin waited. Lyra did not move on her bucket.
"It was a nice room," said Doctor Silas. "Warm. That was the first thing. There was a fire. A big fire, in a white marble fireplace, with brass dogs. I hadn't been warm since the summer. I stood just inside the door, dripping on his carpet, and I could feel it on my face, and I nearly wept just for that." He was quiet for a moment. "There were books. A great many books, in glass cases. A painting of a ship. A clock on the mantel, gold, under a glass dome. And a desk. A big desk, by the window, with a green leather top, very neat. Pens in a stand. A blotter. Papers in piles, squared off at the corners."
"And a bowl," he said.
He looked at the vial.
"On the corner of the desk. Nearest the door. A glass bowl, cut glass, very fine, like a sugar bowl. Full of white sweets. Little round white ones. In twists of paper." A pause. "For the clerks' children, he told me later. When they came to the office at Midwinter with their mothers. He liked to have something for them. He said he couldn't bear for a child to go away from his door with nothing."
Nobody said anything.
"And the smell," said Doctor Silas.
He lifted the vial to his nose again. Not unstoppered, this time. He did not need to. He held it there, just under his nostrils, with his eyes closed.
"The whole room smelled of peppermint," he said. "The curtains. The carpet. The books. The chair he gave me, by the fire. It was in everything. Not strong. Soft. Clean. Like a room that's been washed with it. I remember thinking, sitting in that chair, with the fire on my legs: what a clean, kind room. What a clean, kind man." He opened his eyes. "And under it. Very faint. Under the peppermint. Under the fire and the books and the polish on the desk."
He held the vial out to Corin.
"Smell it again," he said. "Not the top. Underneath."
Corin took it. He drew the cork with his teeth, because his fingers would not do it. He breathed in over the open mouth of the vial. The peppermint. Cold and sharp and sweet. He breathed in again, longer, and made himself go past it. Under it, the way you look past your own face in a window to see the street.
And there, at the bottom, very faint, like a draft under a door, something else. Something dusty and bitter and old. Like the smell of a field where the ground has been dug up and left. Like ash after rain.
"Grayroot," said Doctor Silas. "It was in his room. Under the peppermint. I smelled it. Sitting by his fire. And I didn't know what it was. I'd burned the war. I thought it was the smell of the books. Of old paper." He laughed again, the small dry breaking sound. "I thought it was the smell of a learned man."
Corin put the cork back in. His hand was shaking.
"He listened to me for an hour," said Doctor Silas. "He sat behind his desk with his hands together, and his head on one side, and he listened. He asked good questions. How many. How fast. What ages. What did their breath smell of. He wrote it down. He was very interested in the breath." The doctor's mouth moved. "Of course he was."
"And when I'd finished, he came round the desk. With his stick. He has a bad knee. And he put his hand on my shoulder, and said I was a credit to my profession, and that the matter would be looked into with the greatest care. That it was under review. And that I should go home and keep doing exactly what I was doing. Exactly that. Because it was the best thing anybody could do for those children, and he would see that the Office knew it." A pause. "And then he gave me a peppermint out of the bowl, for the walk home. In a twist of paper."
"Did you eat it?" said Lyra. Her voice was very small.
Doctor Silas looked at her.
"No," he said. "I gave it to a child. On the Fish Stairs. On my way back."
The arch was quite silent.
Corin looked down at the vial in his black fingers. At the paper band. Care of the Dispensary, the Hill. At the broken blue seal.
"What was his name?" he said. "On the door. In gold."
"Pallister," said Doctor Silas. "A. Pallister. Under-Secretary." He said it slowly and carefully, the way you would say the name of a disease. "Ambrose Pallister."
On her bucket beside the pallet, Lyra made a sound.
It was not a word. It was the sound a person makes when they step down in the dark and the stair is not there. A small, sharp, caught breath. Corin turned his head and looked at her.
She had gone white. Not pale. White, like the frost in Cinder Lane, so that the hand-shaped smudge of coal on her cheek stood out on it black. She was holding the Ledger against her chest with both arms, the way Nell had held it. Her eyes were very wide.
"Lyra?"
"I know him," she said.
She said it in a whisper. Then she said it again, louder, as if she had to hear it.
"I know him. I know Mr. Pallister."
Doctor Silas had gone quite still.
"He used to come to the house," said Lyra. "To Crown Walk. When I was little. Before—before everything. Every Midwinter. To Father's dinner. He sat at the end of the table, by Mother. He was very old even then, I thought. He had white hair. He had a stick." She swallowed. "And after dinner, when the grown-ups went into the long room for the music, he used to come and find Ro and me on the stairs. Where we sat, to listen, in our nightgowns. When we were meant to be in bed."
She was looking at nothing. At the far wall of the arch. The way the doctor had looked, a little while before. Back. A long way back.
"He never told," she said. "He never told Mother we were on the stairs. He used to sit down with us, on the bottom step, with his bad leg out straight, and his stick across his knees. And he'd do coin tricks for Ro. Make a penny come out of Ro's ear. And then he'd take out a little silver box from his waistcoat pocket. Round. With a picture on the lid. And open it. And it was full of—"
She stopped.
She stopped as if somebody had put a hand over her mouth.
"Peppermints," she said.
Corin did not say anything. There was nothing to say.
"One each," said Lyra. Her voice had gone thin and high and strange. Like a much younger girl's. "He always gave us one each. And he said, There we are. There's a good girl. There's a good boy. Now off to bed, and I shan't tell." She was shaking. "And we ate them. Every year. I used to look forward to it. I used to sit on the stairs and wait for him. Ro and me. Mr. Peppermint, Ro called him. Is Mr. Peppermint coming this year?"
"Lyra," said Corin.
"I don't know what was in them." She was looking at him now. Her eyes were very wide and very dry. "I don't know, Corin. Do I? I don't know if they were just sweets. They tasted like sweets. I don't know if there was anything else in them, because if there was, I wouldn't know, would I? I wouldn't remember. That's the whole—that's what it does. That's what he said it does." She stopped. "I don't know what I've forgotten."
Doctor Silas spoke. Very gently.
"How often?" he said. "How often did you have them, Miss Thorne?"
"Once a year. At Midwinter. One each."
"Then they were sweets," said Doctor Silas. "Or if they weren't, one drop once a year would do no more than make a child sleep well. I promise you. You'd have lost a bad dream. Nothing more." He held her eyes. "Lull needs to be fed. Every week. Over and over. A child on the Fish Stairs eats one every Friday, for months. That's what it takes. Not one at Midwinter."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment.
Then she nodded, once. And put her face down on top of the Ledger, where she held it against her chest, and kept it there.
Corin lay on his pallet and looked at the curve of black brick overhead, and the long crack, and the dead brown weed.
He was thinking about a man sitting on the bottom stair of a great house on the Hill, at Midwinter, with his bad leg out straight and his stick across his knees, doing coin tricks for a little boy. While through the open doors of the long room came the music, and the lamplight, and the laughter of a lord and a lady from over the water in the west. Making a penny come out of a child's ear. And then taking out a small round silver box.
He liked to have something for them. He couldn't bear for a child to go away from his door with nothing.
He thought about the gold clock under its glass dome on the mantel, ticking. And the bowl of white sweets on the corner of the desk, nearest the door. And the soft clean smell of peppermint in the curtains, in the carpet, in the chair by the fire. Covering everything.
And under it, very faint, like a draft under a door, the smell of a field where the ground has been dug up and left.
