Chapter 39: The Slag Trade, Part 5 of 5: What We Do Now
What We Do Now
It was Doctor Silas who spoke first. And what he said was not what Corin expected.
"I have fifty-one children in the crypt," he said. "As of the sixth bell this morning. Fifty-one."
He had put the vial back on the floor. Upright. He had put his gloves back on, the gray wool ones with the fingers cut off, and he was pulling them straight, finger by finger, as if it mattered very much that they were straight.
"Nineteen of them are on the glass now," he said. "This minute. While I'm sitting here. The nurse is watching them. She's a good woman. She'll change the glasses at noon, when they cloud, and put the full ones in the box by the door. For Friday." He pulled the last finger of the glove straight. "For the cart."
Nobody said anything.
"So you see," said Doctor Silas, "the question is not what I do about Under-Secretary Pallister. I am one doctor in a crypt. I can do nothing at all about Under-Secretary Pallister, and we all know it. The question is what I do at noon." He looked at his gloved hands. "Whether I go back and change the glasses."
"Don't," said Lyra. Her face was still white. "Don't. Take them off. Take all of them off."
"And then what?" The doctor did not say it unkindly. "They're loose, Miss Thorne. All of them. The Lull's in them already. Months of it. Every Friday since the summer. If I take the glass off, it doesn't stop. It just goes into the air instead. Into the straw. Into the brick." He spread his hands. "Drawn, it's sold. Not drawn, it's lost. Either way, the child loses it. The only question is whether Pallister gets paid."
"Then he doesn't get paid," said Lyra.
"No. And on Friday, the cart comes, and there's nothing in the box. And the nice young man with the cough goes back up the Hill and tells somebody. And the somebody tells the Under-Secretary. And the Under-Secretary sits in his warm room and thinks: Why has the best farmer we have stopped farming?" Doctor Silas looked at Corin. "And on the same day, perhaps, he opens his bag to fill his twists for the Fish Stairs. And counts his vials."
Corin had thought of it. In the cellars, on the stairs, in the coal. He had thought of nothing else, between the other things.
"He'll know someone was in the bag," he said. "He'll know what they took. He won't know who."
"Not yet." Doctor Silas picked up his battered hat off the floor and turned it in his hands. "But a man who writes keep him busy across a doctor's letter is a man who thinks about doctors. And a doctor who stops sending glasses in the same week that a vial goes missing—" He stopped. "He'll think of me. If he's half as clever as you say."
"He is," said Corin. "He's cleverer."
"Then he'll think of me."
He said it quite calmly. The way he had said on Wednesday I'm holding a saw.
"So you'll keep sending them?" said Lyra. Her voice shook. "You'll keep putting the glass on them? Knowing?"
Doctor Silas did not answer for a long time.
From the fifth arch, through the sack curtain, the coughing came and went. The woman's voice had stopped singing. Somewhere further off, a man was chopping wood, slow, steady, chock, and chock. The snow-light at the door had gone a little brighter. The morning was going on, out there, in the ordinary way. Carts on the bridges. Bells. People buying bread.
"I don't know," said Doctor Silas at last. "I'm going to go back to the crypt and look at them. All fifty-one. One by one. The way I do every morning. And then I'll know." He put on his hat. "I'm not going to decide it here, in an arch, on my knees, because I'm angry. That's how you make a mistake you can't take back."
He looked at Corin.
"You'd know about that," he said.
Corin did.
"What about the proof?" said Lyra. "The vial. The letter. The line from the counting room. That's proof, isn't it? Of all of it? We could take it to—" She stopped.
Corin watched her look for the end of the sentence and not find it.
To the Watch, he thought. The Watch is the Ministry's. Moll's in the Watch, and they've summoned him to the Inquisition at the tenth bell today with his army papers.
To the Council. The Council is the Ministry's. Your aunt sits on it.
To the Archon. The Archon is the Ministry.
To the Bank.
He stopped there.
The Bank was not the Ministry. Not quite. The Bank and the Ministry were like two hands of one man, and sometimes the hands did not like each other. He had worked in the Bank for twelve years. He knew that much, in his body, the way he knew the shape of an intake glass. The Bank bought memory, and sold it, and kept the books. And the Bank did not like anybody else selling memory without its books knowing.
Lady Vivienne blends it. Bulk lots. The ladies on the Hill pour it into their tea. And the Bourse pays me.
Who at the Bank would care that the Bourse was selling children's slag, paid for by an Under-Secretary, through a door the Bank's ledgers did not show?
An auditor would.
The Duke's card, in Lyra's list pocket. A young woman from the Bank, with a gold tooth, who audits the Bourse each Midwinter.
He felt the Ledger move against Lyra's chest as she breathed. He thought of the first page. You have a sister. Her name is Elara. She works at the Fourth Desk. She has a gold tooth.
He did not say any of it. Not yet. It was not a thought yet. It was the edge of one, like the white edge of ice on a pond.
"We keep it," he said. "All of it. Folded small. Somewhere safe. Not here. Not the barge." He looked at Lyra. "In the gray gown. In the list pocket. Where the Duke's card is."
She looked back at him. He saw her see something in his face. She did not ask.
"All right," she said.
"And the boat," said Corin. "Tonight."
Doctor Silas's head came round.
"No," he said.
"High water at half past one. The wet door. A low boat with no lights, for the Frost Reach of Nym. Twelve crates of children's cold." Corin's voice was rough. "Gideon's in one of them. Thirteen jars of him. All he's got left. They'll take it north to a man called Brask who puts it in his miners, to keep them warm in the deep seams. And it'll be gone. Spent. Like coal." He swallowed. "I promised him. In the cellar. Without saying it, but I promised."
"You have a hole in your leg," said Doctor Silas, "that I can put my thumb into. With coal ground into the bottom of it, and a red road going up toward your groin. If you go down to the river tonight, in the cold, in the wet, you'll come back without the leg. If you come back." He held Corin's eyes. "And you'll not come back with any crates. Not with that leg. Not against men who load boats for a living, at the dead of night, with two great Slates to carry for them."
"Then somebody else—"
"Who?"
Corin opened his mouth.
Juno was on the barge, with her feet wrapped in goose grease and flannel, a day's walk north. Wat was on the barge, with twelve stitches in his shoulder. Oren was on the barge, guarding Lord Thorne, and could not leave him. Hessa had Robin. Moll had the Inquisition at the tenth bell.
Lyra could not go. She would go. He knew she would. She would put on Ellis's cap and walk down to the river in the dark, if he asked her. And she could not lift a crate, and she could not fight a river man, and if they caught her at the wet door, there would be nobody to sit in the gray gown on the Duke's arm on Midwinter night. Nobody to bid for Lot 41.
He shut his mouth.
There was nobody.
"It's all right," said a voice from the doorway.
They all turned.
Gideon stood in the gap between the fourth arch and the fifth, holding the sack curtain back with his good hand. His left arm was in a sling made out of a torn shirt, tied at the back of his neck, with Mother Grue's darning showing black through the bandage at the elbow. He was still wearing the too-big coat, buttoned up to the chin. His face was quite calm.
He had been there, Corin thought, for some time.
"It's all right," said Gideon again. "About the boat. You don't have to go." He came into the arch and stood at the foot of Corin's pallet, and looked down at him. "I heard what the doctor said. About your leg. You can't go with your leg."
"Gideon—"
"I've got one," said Gideon.
He put his good hand on the front of his coat. Over his chest. Over the place where a hard round shape pushed the cloth out a little.
"Two-nine-seven," he said. "From when I was eight. The first winter. I've got it here. I slept with it." He considered. "That's one more than I had on Saturday. Before you came."
Corin looked at him.
He wanted to say something. He wanted to say it's not enough. He wanted to say it's not fair, the way a child says it, with his whole chest. He wanted to say I'm sorry. None of it was any use to Gideon. Gideon could not feel any of it.
"One's enough to start with," said Gideon. "If you find out how to put it back. You only need one, to find out." He looked at the doctor. "Isn't that right?"
Doctor Silas looked at the boy for a long moment, through his crooked spectacles.
"Yes," he said quietly. "Yes, lad. That's right. You only need one."
Gideon nodded. As if that settled it.
"Then that's all right," he said.
And he sat down on the floor at the foot of the pallet, with his back against the brick and his knees drawn up and his good hand on his coat, over the jar. And looked at the wall.
Nobody said anything for a while.
Then Doctor Silas took off his coat. The long black coat that was more mend than coat. He folded it, and laid it on the floor. He rolled up the sleeves of his shirt, to the elbow, carefully, one and then the other. His forearms were long and thin and white, with the veins standing out blue.
He opened his cracked leather bag.
"I'm going to wash that leg out now, Mister Vane," he said. "Every grain. And then I'm going back to Saint Bride's to look at fifty-one children, and decide what kind of farmer I am." He took out a brown bottle, and a bowl, and a long thin pair of tweezers, and a roll of lint. "Miss Thorne, if you'd boil some water. A great deal of it. And Gideon."
The boy looked up.
"Come and hold his hand," said Doctor Silas. "He's going to need somebody to, and you're the only one of us who won't mind how hard he squeezes."
One Shiver
It was worse than Saturday.
Doctor Silas had said it would be, and he was a man who did not say things for the sake of saying them. It was a great deal worse. Corin would not remember most of it, afterwards, and he was glad. The Ledger would hold the rest for him, in Lyra's hand, in a few short lines that she wrote that night by the fire with her mouth pressed tight: The doctor took out the coal with tweezers, one grain at a time. It took the whole of the morning. You did not scream. You bit through the strap he gave you. Gideon held your hand the whole time and hummed.
He would read that, later, and nod, and not remember. That was the point of writing it.
What stayed in him, without the Ledger, was only a handful.
The smell of the brown bottle. Vinegar and something bitter and clean, poured into the open leg, cold for a moment and then not cold at all.
The crack in the roof, and the dead brown weed. He fixed his eyes on the weed and did not let them go. He learned every bent stiff leaf of it. He learned it the way he learned the Ledger, every morning.
The strap between his teeth. Old leather, soft and dark, with the marks of other teeth already in it. A great many other teeth. He wondered, through the red, whose they had been. Old soldiers. Dockers. Children. He bit down where they had bitten.
Gideon's hand.
It was a small hand, thin and cold and very still, and Corin held it so hard that he felt the boy's bones move under his fingers. Gideon did not pull away. He did not flinch. He sat on the floor by the head of the pallet with his back against the brick and his bandaged arm in its sling against his chest, and let Corin crush his good hand, and looked at the wall.
And hummed.
Corin did not know when he had started. He only knew that at some point, in the red, there had been a sound under the other sounds. Small and thin and a little off the note. Up a little, and down, and round again. Over and over. The tune from the top of the shallow steps. Saul's tune. Big Gideon's tune. The carrying song.
Gideon hummed it the whole morning, without stopping, without seeming to breathe, while the tweezers went in and out. He did not know why he was humming it. Corin was quite sure of that. If you had asked him, he would have thought about it, and moved one shoulder, and said I don't know. It's just a thing I do.
But he did it.
And once, near the end, when the tweezers went very deep and Corin's whole body came up off the sacking like a bow bent backwards, and the strap creaked in his teeth, and the arch went white, the humming changed. Just for a moment. It went a little louder. And a little faster. The way a mother's voice goes a little louder and a little faster when the child in her arms begins to cry.
Then it went back.
It was past noon when the doctor finished. Corin knew it was past noon because he heard the bells, very far off, over the roofs, through the brick. He could not count them. He did not try.
Doctor Silas packed the open leg with honey and lint, and bound it loosely, and laid it out straight along the pallet on a folded sack, with another folded sack under the knee. He washed his hands in the last of the boiled water. He rolled his sleeves down. He put on his coat.
He stood for a moment, looking down at Corin, with his battered hat in his hand.
"Three days," he said. "Not moving. I'll come back tonight, and tomorrow, and every day. If the red road goes up any further, I'll know." He paused. "And if it goes down, I'll know that too."
Corin took the strap out of his mouth. His jaw ached all the way up into his ears.
"And Saint Bride's?" he said. His voice was a croak. "The glasses. At noon. Did you—"
"I sent Nell," said Doctor Silas. "An hour ago. With a note for the nurse."
"What did it say?"
The doctor looked at him.
"It said, Take them all off," he said. "Every one. And break the full ones in the yard, on the stones, and wash the pieces into the river. All of them. And tell the cart on Friday there are none this week, and that the doctor is ill."
Nobody said anything.
"That's not a decision," said Doctor Silas. "Not yet. I've told you. I'll make that at the crypt, looking at them. It's only a week. One week where he doesn't get paid." He put on his hat. "And it may be the last week I'm let to have, so I'd like to spend it as a doctor."
He went to the sack curtain at the door. He stopped there, with his hand on it.
"Mister Vane."
"Yes."
"You said you saw his face. In the cellars." Doctor Silas did not turn round. "Will you know it again?"
"Yes."
"Good," said Doctor Silas.
And he went out into the snow.
The afternoon went by. Corin slept, and woke, and slept. Mother Grue came and looked at him and went away. Nell came back from Saint Bride's with her nose red and a heel of bread from the nurse, and sat by him for a while and told him in a whisper that all the glasses were off now, all of them, and the full ones were broken in the yard, and the nurse had cried, and then the nurse had sung while she swept up the pieces, and Nell did not know why. Lyra boiled water and made tea that tasted of the tin, and did not talk much.
When the light at the door had gone blue, and then gray, and then black, and the fire at the end of the arch was the only light, Corin asked for the Ledger.
Lyra brought it. She held it open for him on her knee, the way she had held it for him against the wall of the fifth arch three days ago, because he could not hold it himself. She put a stub of pencil in his fingers. He could just hold that.
He turned to a clean page near the back.
He wrote slowly. The letters were large and shaky and the pencil kept slipping in his burned fingers, so that some of the words went downhill. He did not mind. He was not writing it to be pretty. He was writing it so that tomorrow, and the day after, and every morning after that, a man who had forgotten would open the book and know.
AMBROSE PALLISTER.
Under-Secretary. Ministry Office of Public Health. On the Hill. Last door at the end of the corridor.
About sixty. Very thin. White hair. Gold spectacles. Pink face. Kind. Ebony stick, silver flower head. Bad left knee. Black coat, blue badge: white hand, gold lamp.
Smells of PEPPERMINT. All over. His office too. Under the peppermint: grayroot.
He gives out sweets on the Fish Stairs every Friday morning. The sweets have LULL in them. Lull loosens memory so it falls out as slag. The children get the fever and go to Doctor Silas. Doctor Silas draws the slag into glasses. The Ministry cart takes the glasses to the Bourse. Shelf 40. The Bourse sells the slag to the Hill for their tea. The Bourse pays A. P.
He called Doctor Silas "the best farmer we have".
He used to give Lyra and Rowan peppermints on the stairs at Crown Walk every Midwinter. Ro called him Mr. Peppermint.
He says "There we are."
If you ever hear him say it, you will know.
He stopped. He looked at it for a while.
Then he turned back. All the way back, to the page near the front that he knew by its feel, where the edges were soft and gray with being turned. The page with the two words at the top, in his own hand, from long ago, and the list under it, that got longer.
THOSE I OWE.
He went down to the bottom of the list. Past Mrs. Marta. Past Juno. Past Wat, and Hessa. Past Old Jory Hobday, and Bramble.
He wrote:
Ned. Bet's brother. Fifth arch. Died at Saint Bride's, Monday, before the sixth bell. He forgot how to swallow.
And under it:
Gideon Hollow. H. 291 to H. 304. Thirteen jars of cold, gone north tonight to the Frost Reach of Nym. I could not stop them. I have one. H. 297.
And under that, after a long time:
Big Gideon Hollow, and Saul. On the floor of the ring passage. They do not know their names.
He gave the pencil back to Lyra.
She looked at the page. She did not say anything. She shut the book gently and put it inside his coat, on the left side, against his heart, where it went. And her hand stayed on it, on the outside of the coat, for a moment. Then she went back to her bucket by the fire.
The arch was quiet. The fire hissed and settled. Down at the far end, Mother Grue's people were asleep in a heap of sacking and shawls, children in the middle, old ones on the outside. Somebody snored. Somebody muttered in their sleep. From the fifth arch, no sound at all now. Bet's mother had stopped coughing. Corin hoped she was asleep. He hoped Bet was.
Gideon lay on the floor beside Corin's pallet, on his side, on a sack, with his knees drawn up and his bandaged arm in its sling against his chest, and his good hand inside his coat. Holding the jar. He was asleep. His face in the firelight was white and thin and very young, and quite peaceful, the way it always was.
Corin lay and watched him, and did not sleep.
He heard the bells come over the roofs. He heard the tenth, because he could feel it go past the edge. He heard the eleventh and the twelfth, and could not feel them at all. And then, a long time later, a single bell. And then another, at the half.
Half past one.
High water.
He could not see the river from here. He could not hear it. There was too much brick and earth and city between. But he knew it. He knew it the way he knew the shape of an intake glass. At this moment, two miles south of him, at the foot of the great green dome, a low black arch was open on the water. And a boat with no lights was lying in under it, against the three shallow steps, with its oars shipped. And two huge gray men with bare feet were walking down the steps with crates on their shoulders. Slow. Scrape. A long wait. Scrape. One of them humming.
BY THE NORTH WATER, VIA THE LOCKS.
He lay in the dark and held it. All of it. He did not burn any of it. He did not let himself reach for anything to burn. He just lay there, and held it, and let it hurt.
It was the only thing he could do for Gideon tonight. So he did it.
And beside him on the floor, on his sack, in his sleep, with his good hand inside his coat and his fingers round a cold gray jar, Gideon Hollow shivered.
Once.
A small hard shiver, from his shoulders to his heels. The way a child shivers in the night when the blanket has slipped off.
Then he was still again.
Corin did not move. He did not wake him. He did not say anything, to anybody, because there was nobody awake to say it to, and because he did not know what it meant, and he was afraid that if he said it out loud it would turn out to mean nothing.
But he took the pencil stub that Lyra had left on the edge of the pallet. And he took the Ledger out of his coat. And in the dark, by feel, very small, at the bottom of the page about Gideon, under I have one. H. 297, he wrote two more words.
He shivered.
Then he put the book back against his heart, and closed his eyes, and listened to the boy breathe until morning.
