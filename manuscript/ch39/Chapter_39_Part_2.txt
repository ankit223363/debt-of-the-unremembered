Chapter 39: The Slag Trade, Part 2 of 5: The Black Bag
The Black Bag
They followed the light.
Not close. Corin kept a long way back, so far back that the yellow glow of Mistress Fane's lantern was only a warm smudge on the curve of the wall ahead, coming and going as the passage bent. He went on his good leg and his right hand, with the left hand on Gideon's shoulder, and the boy took his weight on every step as if he had been doing it all his life. They did not speak. They kept to the inner wall. They went past the Ash lamps on the outer wall the way Gideon had taught him, in the thin dark between, with the gray light lying across the floor like water, reaching for them and not quite reaching.
Ahead of them, the tap of the stick. Slow. Tap. And a step. Tap. And a step.
It made the going easy. Corin had never been so glad of a bad knee in his life.
Past the kitchen stair. The smell of old fat and onions. The narrow stone steps going up into the dark. Corin looked up them as he went past and saw, very far up, a thin gold line under a door. The kitchen. The Bourse above. The whole great building sitting on top of them, all its stone and glass, with its green copper dome. He thought of Lyra in the doorway. He did not let himself think of her for long.
Past the Hollow brothers.
They had not moved. They sat against the outer wall with their great feet across the passage and their hands on their knees, and their empty eyes on the stone, and Saul hummed. The yellow light had gone past them a little while before. Corin saw that the stick and the boots and the shoes had gone through the gap between their toes and the wall, the same as he did now. Nobody had told them to move. Nobody had looked at them at all.
Past the empty bench. Past the lift.
The passage curved south. The voices ahead grew no louder and no softer. They were walking at the speed of a bad knee, and talking as they went. Corin could not hear the words. Only the shapes. The rough low voice of Drabble, saying something short. The small dry voice of Fane. The kind voice, answering both of them, with a little laugh at the end.
The store rooms began. The long dark mouths of the vaults on the outer wall, with their crates to the roof. Shelf 2. The blue chalk and the white H.
And then, on the inner wall, the low door into the counting room. And beside it on the outer wall, across the passage, the gray Ash lamp in its brass house.
The yellow light went on past the counting room door.
It did not stop. Corin had been afraid it would. He had stood still in the dark, with his hand tight on Gideon's shoulder, and watched it come up to the door, and hang there a moment, and then go on. Round the curve to the west. Toward the great square room, and the three shallow steps, and the crates.
"Now," breathed Gideon.
They went.
Along the inner wall to the counting room door. Past the gray lamp. The heat came up behind Corin's ear again, a breath of it, at the very edge of the gray, and his knuckles prickled. Then he was at the door, and it was not locked. Fane had left it on the latch. He lifted the latch and they went through.
The counting room was dark.
He had not thought of that. Fane had taken the lantern. Corin stood inside the door with the dark pressed up against his eyes and his heart going very fast, and smelled the room. Dust and ink and old paper. The sharp wax of sealing wax. And peppermint. Strong. Fresh. Coming from somewhere ahead of him, low down.
"The table's in the middle," whispered Gideon. "Four steps. Mind the chair."
Corin had Coker's lantern. He had kept it the whole time, inside his coat, against his chest, shuttered, hot through his shirt. He took it out now. He opened the shutter a crack. A thin blade of yellow light came out of it, no wider than a knife.
He found the table with it. The long plain table in the middle of the room, under the racks of jars that went up the walls. Fane's ledger was gone; she had it with her. Her pens were in their tray. Her ink pot. Her sand box.
And at the end of the table, by the chair, a bag.
It was a doctor's bag. That was the first thing Corin thought. Black leather, good and soft and old, gone shiny at the corners with handling. Shaped like a small long box, with a round top, and a brass clasp across the top, and a handle. The kind of bag that a doctor carries down a street at night, so that everybody knows to let him pass. The kind of bag that people are glad to see come to their door.
There were initials on the clasp. Stamped into the brass in small neat capitals.
A. P.
Corin put the lantern down on the table, with the thin blade of light falling across the bag. He put his hands on the clasp.
If you open it, he thought, in Malakor's voice, because Malakor was not there to think it for him, he will know. When he comes back for it. He will see that something has moved.
Then he'll know, he answered himself. Let him know.
He pressed the clasp. It opened with a soft, well-oiled click.
The peppermint came up out of the bag like heat out of an oven.
It was so strong that his eyes watered all over again. He blinked them clear. He held the bag open with both hands and bent over it and looked inside.
It was very neat. A doctor's bag, inside as well as out. There were straps on the inside of the leather, and in the straps, in rows, things that a doctor would carry. A wooden stethoscope, like a little trumpet. A pair of scissors. A roll of bandage. A small brass case of the kind that holds needles. All clean, all in their places.
And in the bottom of the bag, in a wooden tray with a lid, set into the leather, the vials.
There were twenty of them. Corin counted them with his eyes. Four rows of five. Each one standing upright in its own round hole in the tray, the way the jars stood in the racks round the walls. They were small. No longer than his little finger. Clear glass, very thin and fine. Each one held a liquid that was nearly clear too, but not quite. It had a faint tint to it, gray-green, like water in a pond in winter. And each one was stoppered with a tiny cork, and over the cork, sealing it down onto the glass, a blob of wax.
Blue wax.
Corin took one out of its hole and held it up to the thin blade of light.
The seal was stamped. Very small and very neat, the way a jeweler stamps a ring. A round seal. A rim. And inside the rim, the shape of an open hand, palm out. And above the hand, so small he had to tip the vial back and forth in the light to see it, a lamp.
The same as the badge on the long black coat. The same as the crest on the carriage door.
There was a label too. A paper band, wrapped round the middle of the vial and glued, printed in tiny black letters. Not handwritten. Printed. On a press. A press that printed hundreds of these, thousands, for an office with clerks and stamps and a long corridor.
THE MINISTRY. OFFICE OF PUBLIC HEALTH.
TONIC, FOR THE RIVER FEVER. CHILDREN.
FOR ISSUE TO THE RIVER PARISHES ONLY. NOT FOR SALE.
CARE OF THE DISPENSARY, THE HILL.
Corin read it twice. Then he put the vial into his pocket. The inside pocket of his coat, on the left side, next to his heart, where the Ledger usually went. It lay there, small and hard and cold, no bigger than a finger.
He looked at the empty hole in the wooden tray. Nineteen vials, and one hole.
"He'll see," whispered Gideon, at his elbow.
"I know."
There was something else in the bag. In a side pocket, against the leather, with the flap tucked in. Corin saw the white edge of it. He lifted the flap.
A paper twist. The kind a sweet-shop gives you. A square of white paper, rolled into a cone, and the top twisted shut. And in it, when he pressed it gently between his finger and thumb, small round hard things that clicked together.
He did not open it. He did not need to. He knew what was in it. He could smell it through the paper. He put it in his pocket with the vial.
And behind the paper twist, folded in half, there were letters.
Three of them. On cheap gray paper, the kind you buy in a stationer's on Comb Street for a penny a quire. Written in a tall, sloping, shaky hand, in brown ink gone pale, as if the ink had been watered to make it last. Corin unfolded the top one with his black fingers and held it in the light.
To the Office of Public Health, the Hill.
Sirs. I write for the fourth time this autumn regarding the children of the Lower Cut and the river parishes, who are taken in increasing numbers with the slag fever. I have now forty-one in my care at Saint Bride's. The fever is changing. They drain faster. Their breath has a sweet smell I have never known in it before. I beg you, for the love of the Saints, to send someone to see. I will show them anything. I ask for nothing for myself.
Your servant, J. Silas, Doctor.
And across the bottom of it, in a different hand, in a neat small black ink, very fine, like the stamp on the seal:
Under review. Keep him busy. A. P.
Corin stood very still.
He heard himself breathing. In the dark room, under the racks of jars, with the peppermint in his mouth and his nose and his eyes. In, and out. Too fast. Like the floor.
He folded the letter in half again. Then he folded it again. And again. Small. Very small. Until it was a hard little square of gray paper no bigger than his thumbnail. Fold it small. He put it in his pocket with the vial and the sweets.
He put the other two letters back behind the flap, the way they had been. He closed the bag. He pressed the clasp until it clicked.
"Now we go," he said.
And from the west, far round the curve of the ring passage, from the great square room, came the kind voice. Small with distance. Bouncing off the stone. Clear enough in the silence to understand.
"Thank you, Mr. Drabble. That's quite enough. I've seen the cold. It's very good cold. Now, Fane, my bag, and then I really must get to bed."
The tap of a stick.
Coming back.
Going Up
There were two doors in the counting room.
The low one on the ring passage, where they had just come in. And the other, in the side wall, which was the way they had first come in, an hour ago or a year ago, from the selling room with the strap chair and the foot of two nines and four beyond it.
Corin shut the shutter of the lantern. The thin blade of light went out. In the black, he found Gideon's shoulder, and Gideon found the side door, and they went through it into the selling room, and Gideon pulled the door shut behind them so softly that Corin did not hear the latch go down. He only felt the air change.
The selling room was black too.
Corin could not see the chair. He knew where it was. He had seen it once, with Coker's lantern, and once had been enough. A tall chair with a high back, in the middle of the room, bolted to the floor. With leather straps on the arms, and a leather strap for the forehead, and a small brass stand beside it with a hook, for hanging a glass. He felt it as they went past it in the dark. Not with his hands. With the skin of his face, the way you feel a wall.
Across the selling room. Through the archway at the far end. And then the gray.
It was ahead of them, at the foot of the stairs. Lying on the bottom steps of two nines and four. The light of the Ash lamp in its brass house, in the corner by the bottom step, just where it had been when they came down. Thin. Gray. Like a frost on the stone.
Corin did not stop. There was no other way.
He went into it.
The heat came up into his hands at once. Into the backs of them, where the paint had scraped away, through the coal he had rubbed into the scrapes, through Hessa's neckcloth. He saw the smoke come up off his knuckles in the gray light. Thin threads of it, curling. He smelled it. A smell like hair in a candle. And behind his left ear, the burning patch flared up hot as a coal laid on his skin. He set his teeth.
And the silence behind his ear did not change.
That was the worst of it. On the way down, at this lamp, Malakor had gone faint and far, like a man calling from across a field. At the wet door he had gone out. Corin had thought, without knowing he was thinking it, that if he came back past this lamp he would feel something. A flinch. A hiss. Anything. To show that somebody was still there to be burned.
There was nothing.
He got onto the stairs.
Two nines and four. He had counted them coming down. Sitting, one at a time, with his bad leg out in front of him. He could not go up them sitting. He went up them on his hands and his good knee, the way a baby goes upstairs, with the bad leg dragging behind him from step to step, the boot knocking on the stone edges. Knock. Up. Knock. Up. Gideon went ahead of him, one step above, backwards, with both hands round Corin's wrists, pulling.
One. Two. Three.
He could feel the numbers. Each one solid and sure, like a stone in a stream.
Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.
The edge.
And then the step after, and he could not feel it. It was just a step. He went up it. And the next. And the next. A step, and a step, and a step, with no number on them, the way the bells after nine had no number. He knew the count went on. He knew that it was nine, and then another nine, and then four. He had written it in the Ledger. Two nines and four. But his body could not hold it. His body only knew one nine, and then stairs.
The gray thinned behind them. Faded. Was gone.
The burning stopped. His knuckles stopped smoking. The patch behind his ear stopped flaring and went back to a dull, slow beat.
Silence. Still.
He went on up. He did not know how many steps were left. He only knew he was going up, and that each step was harder than the last, and that the leg dragging behind him was not hurting any more, and that this was bad.
Below him, far below, a door opened.
He heard it. A latch. A hinge. And then the voices, flat and small and muffled by stone, coming up the stairwell from somewhere under and behind. Not in the selling room. In the counting room beyond it. The kind voice.
"There it is. There's my bag. Thank you, Fane."
Corin stopped dead on the stair, on his hands and knee, and did not breathe.
Gideon stopped too, above him, and held his wrists.
A click. The soft, well-oiled click of a brass clasp.
A long pause.
Corin waited for it. He waited for the kind voice to change. To go sharp. To say Fane, someone has been in my bag. To say Fane, call Gorse. To say Fane, the doors.
"Here," said the kind voice. "Have a peppermint, Fane. For your trouble. No, go on. Take two. You work too hard."
A small dry sound from Fane that might have been thanks.
The clasp clicked shut.
He didn't look, Corin thought. He didn't count. He opened it for the sweets.
Not tonight. But he will. When he gets home. When he takes out his tray, and goes to fill the twists for Friday.
Nineteen. And one hole.
"Good night, Mr. Drabble. Monday's tide, then. Do give my regards to Mr. Brask. Tell him I hope the little ones keep him warm." A soft laugh. "There we are. Up we go. These dreadful stairs."
The tap of a stick, going away. Not this way. The other way. East. Toward the kitchen stair, and the carriage at the kitchen gate.
Corin let out his breath.
He went on up.
Steps with no numbers. A long time. Gideon pulling. His own breath loud in the narrow stairwell, sobbing a little at the bottom of each one, though he was not crying. His hands were. They had been burned three times tonight, and scraped, and they left marks on the stone where he put them. Black marks. And then, on the higher steps, where the coal dust had worn off, red ones.
The eight steps. He could count those. One, two, three. He held onto them like a rope. Seven. Eight. Flat.
The five. One. Two. Three. Four. Five.
And the iron door.
It stood at the top of the five, a little open, the way they had left it. Black iron, studded. Beyond it, the coal store. The smell of coal, wet and sharp, and the cold. A real cold, not the sitting cold of the cellars. A winter cold, coming down the chute from the street.
Corin crawled through the iron door into the coal and lay on his face on it.
He did not mean to. His arms just stopped. He lay there with his cheek on the cold wet lumps of coal and his eyes open in the dark and breathed. Above him, very high, he could see a tiny square of something that was not quite black. The peep hatch in the coal flap, at the top of the chute. Night sky through the iron bars. A star in it.
Gideon crouched beside him.
"Your leg's gone white," the boy said. "Where the rag ends. Above the knee." He said it the way he said everything. "Doctor Silas said if it went white, it's bad."
"I know."
"Do you want me to pull you up the chute?"
Corin almost laughed. The chute was steep and long and black, a slope of loose coal going up into the dark at the angle of a roof, with the flap at the top. He had come down it on his back, sliding, with his leg tearing open. Going up it would be like climbing a waterfall.
"In a minute," he said. "Let me lie here a minute."
And then, from above them, from the top of the chute, from Cinder Lane, there came a sound.
A dog.
Not barking. Snuffling. A big dog, by the sound. Snuffling and blowing at the edge of the coal flap, the way a dog does at a rabbit hole, short hard breaths through its nose. And a whine, high and eager, at the back of its throat. And the scrape of claws on iron.
The little square of night sky went dark.
Something was standing over the peep hatch, blocking it. Corin heard the claws again. He heard the dog's breath, close to the bars, huffing, and then a long, deep, greedy sniff, as if the dog was trying to pull all the air out of the coal store and into its own chest.
He knew what it could smell.
He had left a trail of it all the way down this chute two hours ago. Hot and fresh and red. He was leaving one now, in the coal under his leg.
"Here," said a man's voice, up in the lane. Not Coker's. Younger. Harder. "Here, Coker. What's he got? He's going mad for something."
And Coker's voice, thick with sleep, and the Bell, and beer.
"Rats," said Coker. "It's always rats."
The Dog
"Rats don't make him go like that," said the hard young voice. "Rats he just looks at. Look at him. Look at his tail."
"He's a dog," said Coker. "He's a fool. Like you."
The dog was digging now. Corin could hear its claws on the iron of the flap, scrabbling, and its breath coming in quick hot whines through the bars of the peep hatch. Something fell down the chute. A little scatter of frost and grit, knocked loose from the edge of the flap. It came rattling down the slope of coal in the dark and stopped near Corin's hand.
He did not move. He lay on his face on the coal with his bad leg out behind him and his hands flat, and kept still the way a hare keeps still in a field. Gideon was crouched beside him. He did not move either.
"Watchman's round," whispered Gideon. In his ordinary voice, as low as it would go. "Every two hours. Round the whole Bourse. Him and the dog. I forgot it was Sunday. On Sundays it's the young one. Pike." A pause. "The dog's called Tinker. He bit a boy once. On the arm."
Corin had not known that. It was not in the Ledger. Gideon had not drawn it on his map. It had not been the kind of thing Gideon thought to say, any more than he would have thought to say the stairs are made of stone.
"I'm going to open it," said Pike, up in the lane. "The flap. I'm going to have a look."
"You're not. That's my flap."
"It's the Bourse's flap. And I'm the Bourse's watch. And if there's somebody down there, and Mr. Gant finds out I walked past—"
"There's nobody down there. I've been sat on it all night."
A pause.
"You've been at the Bell all night," said Pike. "I saw you come back. Half past eleven, and singing."
Coker said nothing to that.
The flap moved.
Corin heard it before he saw it. A scrape of iron on stone. A grunt. And then a great gray square of light opened at the top of the chute, high above them, and the cold came pouring down the black slope of coal like water from a sluice. Not daylight. Lantern light, and starlight, and the white glow of the frost in the lane. But after the cellars it was as bright as noon.
A shape leaned over the edge of it, black against the sky. A man's head and shoulders, in a round hat. And beside it, lower down, another shape. Pointed ears. A long muzzle, pushed forward over the edge, nose working.
And a lantern. Held out over the chute, at the end of a long arm, swinging.
The yellow light came down the coal.
Slowly. The way water comes down a slope. Across the top of the chute, where the coal was loose and glittering. Down. Over the lumps and the dust. Toward the bottom, where the slope flattened out into the coal store, and the black mouth of the iron door was, and two figures lay in the coal.
Corin pressed his face into the coal and shut his eyes.
And beside him, Gideon stood up.
Corin felt him go. He felt the small weight lift off the coal beside him, and he opened his eyes and turned his head and saw the boy's thin back in the too-big coat, standing up straight in the lantern light, at the foot of the chute. With his hands in his pockets.
"No—" Corin breathed.
Gideon did not look round.
He began to climb.
He went up the chute on his hands and feet, quickly, the way a cat goes up a roof. His new boots slipped in the coal and he dug them in again. Coal went rattling down behind him in a little black stream. He did not hurry and he did not slow. He climbed straight up into the yellow light, toward the lantern and the round hat and the dog.
"Oi!" Pike shouted. "Oi! There! Coker! Look!"
The dog went mad.
It barked. Once, huge, a sound like a door slammed in the cellar, so loud that it went round the coal store and down the stairs behind Corin and came back up again. And then again, and again, a great deep baying bark, with its front feet on the edge of the flap and its whole body straining down into the chute.
Gideon reached the top.
Pike's hand came down out of the light and took him by the collar of the too-big coat and hauled him up and out, the way you would haul a sack out of a hold. Gideon came up out of the chute into the lane, and the gray square of light went half dark round him, and Corin saw his boots kick once in the air over the edge, and then they were gone.
And the dog was on him.
Corin heard it. He could not see it. He lay at the bottom of the chute and heard it. The scrabble of claws on frozen cobbles. A deep growl, wet and choking. And Pike shouting, Tinker! Tinker, leave! Leave it! And Coker's voice, suddenly not sleepy at all. Get him off. Get him off the lad, you fool!
And no sound at all from Gideon.
Not a cry. Not a shout. Not a word.
Corin had his hands under him. He had his good knee under him. He did not know he had moved. He was halfway to getting up, with his face turned to the top of the chute and his mouth open, before he knew what he was doing. And then he stopped. Because if he went up the chute now, with his leg, with his gray hands, with the vial and the letter and the sweets in his coat, then Gideon's climb was for nothing.
He stopped. He hung there on his hands and knee, shaking. And listened.
"Leave! Leave!"
A thud. A yelp. The growl stopped.
Silence. Breathing. Pike's, fast and frightened. The dog's, panting. Coker's, heavy.
"Saints," said Pike. His voice had gone high. "Saints. Look at his arm. Look at it."
"I'm looking," said Coker.
"He's not crying. Why's he not crying? Tinker had him right to the bone. Look at it. Why's he not—"
"Because he's one of Grue's," said Coker. "Aren't you, lad? You're one of Grue's hollow ones."
A pause.
"Yes," said Gideon. Calmly. In his ordinary voice. "I was looking for coal. For the arches. It's cold in the arches. I'm sorry about your flap."
"There. You see?" Coker's voice. "A coal rat. A little coal rat from Grue's. That's what your fool dog smelled. Him and his blood." A breath. "Are you hurt, lad?"
"I don't know," said Gideon. "It's bleeding."
"Saints," said Pike again. "Saints. He's mad. They're all mad down there."
"He's not mad. He's hollow. They sold his feelings when he was a babe. There's a hundred like him in the arches." Coker's voice was low and rough. "Go on, Pike. Get on with your round. Get that dog away. I'll see to the lad."
"Mr. Gant says if there's thieving—"
"Mr. Gant says a lot. And if you go and tell Mr. Gant that your dog took the arm off one of Grue's lads for the sake of a bucket of coal, on a Sunday night, with the Health's carriage stood at the kitchen gate and the Under-Secretary himself down in the cellars, what do you think Mr. Gant'll say?" A pause. "Do you think he'll thank you for the noise?"
Pike did not answer.
"Go on," said Coker. "Get on."
Corin heard the boots go. And the claws, clicking on the frost, and a whine, and the jingle of a chain. Going away down the lane, toward the river end, toward the lamp. Getting smaller.
Then it was quiet.
The gray square of light at the top of the chute stayed open. Nobody leaned over it. Corin could hear Coker breathing, heavily, just beyond the edge. And another sound, small and soft. Cloth tearing.
"Hold still," said Coker. "Hold still, now. Hold your arm up. Like that."
"All right."
"You're Big Gideon's lad. Aren't you."
A long pause.
"Yes," said Gideon.
"I knew your da. Before." The sound of cloth, being wound round and round. "I used to drink with him at the Bell. Him and Saul. Before they went carrying." Another pause. Coker's breath came out of him in a long white cloud. Corin could see the edge of it, drifting over the top of the chute. "He was a good man. Your da. He could carry a hogshead on each shoulder and sing at the same time. Couldn't hold a note, mind."
"Saul could," said Gideon.
"Aye. Saul could."
The cloth stopped. A knot, pulled tight.
"There," said Coker. "That'll hold till Grue sees it. Go on home now. And lad." His voice dropped. So low that Corin, at the bottom of the chute, could only just hear it. "Don't come round my flap again. Not on a Sunday. Not when that carriage is at the gate. You hear me? There's things in that cellar that a lad shouldn't see. And there's a man in that carriage who'd give you a sweet and smile at you while they put you on a glass."
Silence.
"I know," said Gideon.
Coker did not answer that for a long moment.
Then Corin heard the creak of the stool. And the rustle of a newspaper, opened and shaken out. And Coker's voice, quite loud and quite ordinary, the voice of a man talking to himself.
"I'm going to have a sleep now," said Coker. "I'm going to sit here on my stool and have a good long sleep, with my eyes shut and my hat over my face, till the half past one. Like I do every Sunday. Like I've done every Sunday for nine years." A pause. "And if a coal rat comes up out of my flap while I'm sleeping, I'll not see it. Will I? And if I don't see it, I can't tell anybody. Can I?"
The newspaper rustled.
And the flap, very slowly, from above, was pulled nearly shut. Not all the way. Leaving a gap at the top, a hand's width wide, where the cold came in and the stars showed.
Corin lay at the bottom of the chute in the dark, and put his face down on the coal, and could not stop shaking.
