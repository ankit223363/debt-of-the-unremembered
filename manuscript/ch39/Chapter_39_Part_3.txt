Chapter 39: The Slag Trade, Part 3 of 5: Cinder Lane at Midnight
Cinder Lane at Midnight
He climbed the chute alone.
Afterwards he could never remember most of it. The Ledger would tell him only what Lyra wrote down for him the next morning, because he could not hold a pen: You came up the chute on your elbows. It took you as long as a quarter bell. Coker did not move once. He would read it and nod and not remember. There was only a handful of it left in him, like a handful of coal. Black lumps, with nothing between them.
The coal moving under his elbows, sliding, so that for every two pulls up he slid one back down.
The smell of it. Wet and sharp, in his nose and his mouth and the back of his throat, until he could taste nothing else.
The gap of stars at the top, a hand's width wide, that did not seem to get any closer. And then did.
His own voice, very small, saying something over and over under his breath. He did not know what. Numbers, perhaps. One to nine. And then one again.
The edge of the iron flap, very cold, under his fingers. Pushing it. It swung up, slow and heavy, and the frost air came over him like a bucket of water.
And the lane.
He lay on the frozen cobbles beside the coal flap with his cheek on the stone. The cobbles were white. Everything was white. The frost lay over all of Cinder Lane, thick and soft and glittering under the single lamp at the river end, and on the iron railings, and on the roofs. It was so bright after the cellars that it hurt. He could see his own breath going up off the cobbles in small quick puffs. He could see, very close to his face, a single dark spot in the white. Then another. Then a line of them, going away up the lane toward the Bourse corner. Red-black. Frozen already.
Gideon's blood.
Beside him, a yard away, on a low wooden stool against the wall, sat a big man in a long coat with a newspaper over his face. His hands were folded on his stomach. His boots were stretched out in front of him across the cobbles. He was breathing slowly and loudly through his nose.
Corin looked at him.
Coker did not move. The newspaper rose and fell. A corner of it lifted in the small wind off the river, and settled again.
Corin got up onto his good knee. Then onto his good foot. He could not stand up straight. He stood bent over, with his hand on the wall, and his bad leg hanging. He turned his head and looked at the man on the stool once more.
He wanted to say something. He did not know what. Thank you, perhaps. Or I'm sorry. Or How can you sit here, every Sunday for nine years, and know, and keep the door?
He did not say any of it. Coker was asleep. He had said so.
Corin went up the lane.
He went with his hand on the wall the whole way, step and drag, step and drag, along the line of frozen red spots on the white. The warehouse doors went past him on the other side, black and shut. The coal flaps. The iron railings. The frost crunched under his good boot and under the dragging one. He did not look back.
Halfway up, a shape came out of a doorway.
Small. Quick. With something held against its chest in both arms. It came across the lane toward him at a run, and stopped a yard off, in the frost, and he saw a white thin face under a mass of tangled hair, and two enormous eyes.
"Mister," said Nell.
She was holding the Ledger. She had both arms wrapped round it, crossed over it, the way you hold a baby that is crying. The lantern hung from her wrist by its handle, forgotten, knocking against her knees.
"I flashed," she said. Her voice shook so much that the words came out in pieces. "I flashed, mister. Three times. Like a knock. When the man with the dog came. I did it right. Did I do it right? I didn't know if you could see it, down there. I didn't know."
"You did it right," said Corin.
His own voice came out of him like a stranger's. Cracked and low.
"The lady took it," said Nell. "Your stick. The lady went and got it, before the fat man came, and pretended she was lame, and he gave her a penny. And then she went round the corner, and she didn't come back, and then the dog came, and I flashed, and the boy came up out of the hole, and the dog—" She stopped. Her mouth worked. "The dog," she said again.
"Where is he?"
"With the lady. Round the corner. She came running back when she heard the barking. She's got him. She's tied his arm up in her scarf over the old man's rag." Nell held out the Ledger to him, suddenly, in both hands, as if it were burning her. "I didn't let go of it, mister. She said don't let go of it whatever happens. I didn't."
Corin took it.
It was warm. Warm from her arms. He held it against his chest for a moment with both his black, burned, shaking hands, and something in him that had been clenched tight for three hours like a fist let go, a little.
"Thank you, Nell," he said.
She stared at him.
Then she burst into tears.
She did it all at once, the way children do, with her whole face. Standing in the frost in Cinder Lane with the lantern knocking at her knees, and her hands empty, and her mouth wide open. Not quietly. Corin did not tell her to be quiet. He could not have.
They went up the lane together. Nell holding his coat. Corin holding the wall, and the Ledger.
At the corner by the Bourse, in the deep shadow of the great building, Lyra was waiting.
She was sitting on the frozen step of a side door, with her back against the wall, and Gideon was sitting beside her. She had her arm round him. He was letting her. He sat with his left arm held up across his chest, the way Coker had told him, wrapped in a mess of Coker's torn rag and Lyra's gray wool scarf, both of them black and wet. And his other hand in his pocket. And his pale calm eyes on the street.
The crutch lay across Lyra's knees.
She saw Corin and stood up. Too fast. The crutch fell off her knees and clattered on the step. She did not pick it up. She came across the frost to him in two steps and stopped, and put up her hands, and did not touch him. Her face was gray in the lamplight. Her short hacked hair stuck out from under Ellis's flat cap. Her lips had gone blue with cold.
"You're alive," she said.
"Yes."
"Is that your blood? On your face?"
"I don't think so. Coal." He thought about it. "Some of it."
She put her hand flat on his chest. On the Ledger, where he held it. Over his heart. Just for a moment. Then she took it away.
"I broke the plan," she said. "I went for the crutch. And I gave the book to Nell. I know I promised. I'm not sorry."
"Good," said Corin.
She looked at him.
"There's a carriage," she said, low and fast. "Round the back. At the kitchen gate. With the Health's crest on the door. A white hand and a gold lamp. And it smells—"
"Of peppermint," said Corin. "I know."
They looked at each other in the lamplight.
"I saw him," said Corin. "Lyra. I saw him. I saw his face."
And from behind the Bourse, round the corner, in the back street that ran down to the river, there came the sound of a carriage door shutting. A soft, solid, expensive sound. And a man's voice, kind and a little breathless, saying something to the driver. And then hooves on frozen cobbles, slow at first, and then quicker, and wheels. Coming this way.
Lyra moved first.
She took Corin by the arm and pulled him back into the side doorway, and pushed Nell in after him, and Gideon came in by himself and stood with his back to the door. They were packed in so tight that Corin could feel Lyra's heart going against his arm, quick and hard. The doorway was deep and black.
The carriage came round the corner.
It passed them so close that Corin could have touched the wheel. Black, and well kept, with the two gray horses blowing white clouds and their hooves striking sparks off the frost. The lamp on its side swung. The light swept across the doorway, across the four of them, and was gone.
And on the door, as it went past, Corin saw the round blue crest, with its white rim and white open hand and the small gold lamp.
And in the window above it, for one moment, a face. Long and thin and pink, with white hair, and small gold spectacles. Looking out at the frost, at the empty street, at the stars over the Bourse dome. Smiling a little. A peaceful, tired, old man's smile. The smile of a man going home to bed after a good day's work.
It did not see them.
The carriage went up the street toward the Hill, and the sound of it got smaller, and smaller, and was gone.
The smell of peppermint hung in the doorway for a long time after.
They went home the long way. Not to the barge. The barge was a day's walk, and Corin could not walk an hour. To the arches.
Along the river. Under the bridges, where the frost had not reached, and the ground was black and wet. Up the steps by the dye-works, where the water in the Mill Cut ran blue even at night, steaming a little in the cold. Through the gap in the wall, into the dark. Corin on the crutch and on Lyra's shoulder. Gideon on Corin's other side, with his good hand on Corin's belt, holding him up, saying nothing. Nell in front with the lantern, lit now, swinging.
Corin did not remember most of that either.
He remembered one thing. Halfway along the river, under the second bridge, in the dark, where the water slid past black and quick and smelled of the city.
A voice.
Behind his left ear. Very faint. Very far. Like a man calling from the other side of a frozen lake, in the wind, so that only one word comes across.
Corin.
He stopped.
Lyra stopped with him. "What is it?"
He stood under the bridge with the water sliding past, and held his breath, and listened with his whole body.
Nothing more came.
But it had come. Once. He had heard it. The old dry voice. Thinner than paper. But there.
"Nothing," he said. "It's nothing. Come on."
They went on.
At the gap in the wall below the dye-works, at the top of the steps into the arches, his good leg went. Not slowly. All at once, the way a rope goes when the last strand breaks. He felt the frost come up and hit him in the side of the face. He heard Lyra say his name. He heard Nell scream, and a door bang open somewhere below, and Mother Grue's voice, sharp as a slap, shouting for lights.
And the last thing he felt, before the dark, was the Ledger, still against his chest, under his arm, where he had held it all the way.
The Doctor Comes Late
He woke because somebody was reading to him.
It was Lyra's voice. Low and steady and a little hoarse, as if she had been reading for a long time. He did not open his eyes. He lay and listened to it, the way you listen to rain on a roof.
"Your name is Corin Vane. You are twenty-eight years old. You were a harvester at the Black Bank of Veridia, in the Third Basement." A pause. A page turned. "You have a sister. Her name is Elara. She works at the Fourth Desk. She has a gold tooth. You have not spoken to her. You think she may know who you are."
He opened his eyes.
Brick. A curved roof of old black brick, very close above him, with the wet coming through it in dark patches and a long crack running from one side to the other with a weed growing out of it, a small brown dead thing, stiff with frost. Smoke. The smell of a coal fire, and of boiled cabbage, and of a great many people sleeping close together in a small space. And under all of it, faint and sharp, a smell of vinegar and something else that bit the inside of his nose. A doctor's smell.
He was lying on a pallet of sacking stuffed with straw, in the fourth arch. He knew it by the crack in the roof. He had lain here before, with the fever, three days ago. He knew the crack better than his own hands.
Lyra sat beside him on an upturned bucket, with the Ledger open on her knees.
She had a blanket round her shoulders, a gray army blanket with a burn in one corner. Her face was very white and very tired, and there were dark marks under her eyes like bruises. Her short hair stuck up at the back where she had slept on it. There was coal dust on her cheek, in the shape of a hand. His hand, he thought. From the doorway. When she had put her hand on his chest, and he had touched her face, and he did not remember doing it.
She saw his eyes open and stopped reading.
"There you are," she said.
And he flinched.
He could not help it. It went through him like a shock from a cold iron rail. There you are. There we are. The kind voice, in the cellars. There we are, all down safely. He felt his whole body go stiff on the pallet, and his burned hands close.
Lyra saw. Her face changed.
"I'm sorry," she said quickly. "What did I say? Corin. What did I say?"
"Nothing." He made his hands open. "Nothing. It's what he says. That's all. There we are." He swallowed. His throat was raw, as if he had swallowed coal. Perhaps he had. "It doesn't matter. Say it. It's yours too."
She did not say it again.
"How long?" he said.
"All night. It's morning. Monday. Past the eighth bell." She shut the Ledger. "I read you the first page at the sixth bell, and again at the seventh. In case you woke and didn't know. I was going to do it every bell until you did."
"I know," he said. "I knew. When I woke."
It was true. For once, it was true. He lay on the sacking and went through it in his head, the way a man goes through his pockets after a fall. His name. Lyra. The barge. Hessa and Wat and Robin. Old Jory. Gideon. The Bourse. Coker. The crates. The jar. The ledge. The cupboard. The knothole. The face.
It was all there. Nothing had gone. He had burned nothing in the night.
He had promised Malakor he would not. And he had not.
Malakor, he thought.
Silence. The empty room.
And then, very faint, so faint that he might have been making it up, a sort of rustle. The sound a sleeping man makes when he turns over in his bed in another room. Nothing more. But not nothing.
He let out his breath.
"Gideon?" he said.
"Asleep. In the fifth arch, with Mother Grue's lot. She sewed up his arm herself last night, with a darning needle, by the fire. Twelve stitches." Lyra's mouth went tight. "He watched her do it. He asked her if she could do a pattern."
Corin shut his eyes.
"And the doctor?"
"Nell went for him at first light. To Saint Bride's. Mother Grue sent her." Lyra looked at the doorway of the arch, where the gray morning came in round the edge of a sack curtain, with snow-light in it. "He hasn't come."
"He always comes."
"I know. That's why I've been watching the door."
He tried to sit up. He got as far as his elbows. Then the leg woke up, and he lay down again, very fast, with the arch going round him in a slow gray wheel.
It was not the hurt. Or it was not only the hurt. It was the heat. He could feel his left leg from the hip down as if it were a separate thing laid along beside him on the pallet, a log out of the fire, burning. Hot and heavy and huge. Beating. And above the heat, in his head, a strange high light feeling, like the feeling just before the fever came, three days ago.
"Don't," said Lyra. "Mother Grue looked at it. She said not to let you move till the doctor comes. She said—" She stopped.
"What did she say?"
Lyra did not answer.
The sack curtain at the door moved.
It was pushed aside by a long thin hand in a gray woolen glove with the fingers cut off. And a man came in, stooping under the low brick arch, with snow on his shoulders. A tall man, thin and gray, in a long black coat that had been mended so many times that it was more mend than coat. A battered black hat. Crooked spectacles. A cracked leather bag in one hand.
Doctor Silas.
He straightened up, as far as the arch let him. And stood there for a moment, just inside the curtain, with the snow melting on his shoulders, and did not come any further in.
Corin looked at his face. And knew.
It was the face of a man who has been up all night. That was nothing; Doctor Silas always looked like that. But this was more. This was the face of a man who has been up all night and has lost. The long gray face had gone slack round the mouth. The eyes behind the crooked spectacles were red at the rims and very dry, the way eyes go when they have finished crying and there is nothing left to cry with.
"I'm sorry I'm late," he said.
His voice was perfectly steady.
"I couldn't leave until it was over. And then there was the paper to write, for the Watch. They like a paper." He took off his hat and looked at it, at the snow on its brim, as if he did not know what it was. "And then I had to come here anyway, so I thought I would bring the news myself, and not send it with the girl. It's not a thing to send with a girl."
Lyra had stood up. "Doctor—"
"Is Bet here?" said Doctor Silas. "Little Bet. From the fifth arch. With the mother who coughs."
Nobody answered.
Then, from the doorway between the fourth arch and the fifth, where the sack curtain hung that Corin had pushed aside four days ago on his crutch, there came a small sound.
Bet was standing there.
She was holding the curtain back with one hand. She had a heel of bread in the other, the end of a loaf, Gideon's loaf, that she had been eating. She was very thin and very small, in a dress made of a man's shirt, with a shawl crossed over her chest and tied at the back. Her enormous eyes went from Doctor Silas, to Lyra, to Corin on the pallet, and back to the doctor.
She did not ask anything.
She did not need to. She looked at the doctor's face, and she knew. Corin saw her know. He saw it go into her and down, like a stone into a well.
Doctor Silas went down on one knee in front of her, on the cold brick, so that his face was level with hers. He did it slowly, because his knees were stiff with the cold and the night. He did not touch her.
"Bet," he said. "It's Ned."
Bet looked at him.
"He stopped eating," said Doctor Silas. "Yesterday. At dinner. He was sitting up, and the nurse gave him his broth, the way she always does, and he held the spoon. And he looked at it. And he didn't know what it was for." The doctor's voice did not change. He said it the way you tell a child the plain truth, when you have decided that the plain truth is all you have left to give. "We tried. All night, we tried. We held his head, and we put the broth in his mouth, and we stroked his throat, the way you do for a baby. But it didn't help. He'd forgotten how. He'd forgotten how to swallow."
Bet said nothing.
"He went just before the sixth bell," said Doctor Silas. "It was very quiet. He wasn't frightened. He didn't know enough to be frightened, by then. He was just tired." A pause. "I was holding his hand. I'm sorry, Bet. I'm sorry it wasn't you."
The bread fell out of Bet's hand.
It did not make a sound. It fell onto the brick floor, onto the cold dirt and straw, and lay there. She did not pick it up. She stood in the doorway, holding the curtain, and looked at the doctor, and her face did not do anything at all.
Then she turned round and went back into the fifth arch. The curtain fell behind her.
A moment later, Corin heard her mother begin to cough. And then stop coughing. And then a sound that was not coughing, low and long and terrible, that went on and on.
Bet made no sound at all.
Doctor Silas stayed where he was, on one knee, on the brick floor, for a long time. Then he picked up the heel of bread. He brushed the straw off it, carefully, with his gloved fingers. He set it on the edge of the bucket beside the curtain, where Bet would find it.
Then he got up. Slowly. And came across the arch to Corin's pallet, and set down his cracked leather bag, and knelt beside it, and began to unbuckle it. His fingers were shaking. He did not seem to notice.
"Now then," he said. "Let me see that leg."
He did not look at Corin's face.
He cut the rags off the leg with his long scissors. Corin did not watch. He looked at the crack in the roof, and the dead weed, and listened to the scissors going snip, snip through the wet black cloth, and to Lyra's breathing, and to the low terrible sound from the fifth arch, going on.
The scissors stopped.
The doctor did not say anything for a while.
"Well," he said at last. Very quietly. "You've done your best to kill yourself, haven't you."
"How bad?"
"Bad." Doctor Silas bent closer, and sniffed, and drew back. "It's opened all the way. Both ends. And it's dirty. Coal. Coal in the wound, ground right in, the whole length of it. And there's heat in it that wasn't there on Saturday." He laid the back of his gloved hand against Corin's thigh, above the knee, where it had gone white. Then lower. Then lower. "And here. And here. You see the color? The red, going up the line of the vein. Toward the groin. Like a little red road."
Corin did not look.
"If that road gets to the top," said Doctor Silas, "it'll be in your blood. And then it won't matter what I do to the leg." He sat back on his heels. "I'm going to open it all again, and wash it out. All the coal. Every grain. It will be worse than Saturday. A great deal worse. And then I'm going to pack it with honey and lint and leave it open to drain, and you are going to lie on this pallet for three days and not move. Not to the barge. Not to the Bourse. Not to the privy. Not anywhere."
"I can't," said Corin. "Tonight. There's a boat. At the wet door. At high water, half past one. They're sending Gideon's cold north. I have to—"
"You have to lie still," said Doctor Silas, "or I take the leg off."
He said it without heat. Without any feeling at all, almost. The way he had said he'd forgotten how to swallow.
"Not today," he said. "But by Wednesday, if you get up. I've seen it go like this a hundred times. In the war hospitals, and the Cut, and the docks. A brave fool with a hole in his leg who won't lie down. On Monday he says I'll be all right. On Wednesday I'm holding a saw." He looked at Corin at last, through the crooked spectacles. His eyes were very tired and very red and very steady. "I've had a bad night, Mister Vane. I have lost a little boy who forgot how to swallow. I am not going to lose a man's leg as well, this week, because he wanted to go and look at a boat. I won't. Do you understand me?"
Corin looked back at him.
Then he put his hand inside his coat, into the inside pocket on the left, next to his heart. His fingers were stiff and burned and he could not feel much with them. But he found it. Small and hard and cold, no bigger than a finger.
He took it out and held it up between them. In the gray light from the door.
The little glass vial. The gray-green liquid. The tiny cork. And the blob of blue wax, with the small round seal stamped into it.
"Before you do anything," said Corin, "look at this. Please. And tell me what it is."
