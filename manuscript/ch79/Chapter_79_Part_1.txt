Chapter 79: The Ridges, Part 1 of 5: Running
Running
They ran.
Lyra did not remember deciding to. Nobody decided. Tansy said Run and the word went through all of them at once, the way a spark goes through dry grass, and then they were out of the square stone door frame and onto the bright scoured glass, and running.
The silent man went first. He did not let go of Tansy's hand. He went out of the doorway with her trotting at his side, half-dragged, her little blue lamp swinging wildly on its wire, and turned north, away from the sun, toward the long low line of walls that Tansy had pointed at. He did not hurry. Lyra saw that even then. He did not hurry. He only went, at his long even walking pace, and somehow that was faster than anybody else's run.
Then Kestrel. Then the litter.
Brin and Oda had the front poles, and Sef and Marrit the back, and they came out of the doorway sideways, the way they had gone in, and straightened, and ran. Arran swaying between them on the stretched hide. Holding on to the edges with both hands. His wrapped foot bouncing on its rolled coat. His face, as the litter went past Lyra, white as paper under the gray line, and grinning. Grinning. Of course grinning. As if this were the best game in the world.
Then Vespera. Old as she was, she could move when she had to. Her bag clutched to her chest in both arms. Her coat flapping round her thin legs.
Then Oren. He did not run past Lyra. He stopped beside her in the doorway, with his sword out, and looked back west. At the two shapes coming out of the red light.
"Go," he said.
"Corin—"
"I've got him. Go."
But she did not go. Because Corin was coming out of the doorway behind her, on his crutch, and he was so slow.
She had known he would be. She had known it all day. She had watched him walk the whole night before, from the iron horse, through the dark, and he had been slow then, and that had been walking. This was running. He could not run. He did not have a leg to run on. He had one good foot, and one stiff one that did not bend, and a stick of ash wood wrapped in felt. And with those he could go fast for a man with a stick. Faster than she would have believed, three weeks ago. But not fast. Not fast enough.
He came out onto the bright glass. Crutch, swing, step. Crutch, swing, step. His face was set. His lips pressed white. She could see him trying to go faster and not being able to, and the trying made it worse. His good foot slipped on the scoured glass, which was smooth now, smooth as ice, with all the grit blown off it. He nearly went down. She caught his arm.
And then the silent man was there.
Lyra had not seen him come back. He had been at the front. He had been fifty paces ahead, with Tansy. And now he was here, at Corin's left side, the bad side, the stiff side. As if he had never left it. Tansy was running on ahead alone, with her lamp, after Kestrel. And the silent man had come back for Corin.
He did not say anything. He never did. He just put one long thin hand under Corin's left elbow. Under the arm with the crutch. Not holding it. Not lifting it. Just there. Underneath. The way you put your hand under a cup somebody else is carrying, so that if it slips you will catch it.
And Corin went faster.
Lyra saw it happen. It was not that the man pulled him. He did not pull. It was that with the hand under his elbow, Corin did not have to be afraid of falling. And without the fear, his body knew how to go. Crutch, swing, step. Crutch, swing, step. Longer. Quicker. The crutch going down further ahead each time, and the stiff leg swinging further, and the good foot coming down surer, until he was not hobbling anymore but moving, in great long ugly heron strides, across the bright glass, with the man at his side matching him step for step.
Lyra ran on his right.
Oren ran behind.
And behind Oren, behind all of them, far back and getting nearer, came the ticking.
Counting Strides
Lyra counted.
She did not mean to. She never meant to. It was a thing her body did when it was frightened, the way some people's hands shake, or their mouths go dry. Her mind started counting. Anything. Stairs. Steps. Breaths. Buttons. It did not matter what. It was something to hold onto. A rope in the white.
She counted Corin's strides.
Crutch, swing, step. One. Crutch, swing, step. Two. Each one long and ugly and fast, with the silent man's hand under his elbow and his face set white. Three. Four. Five.
She counted the ticking.
It was behind them. To the west, and a little south, out of the red light. She could not look back. If she looked back she would slow down, and if she slowed down Corin would look at her, and if he looked at her he would slow down too. So she did not look. She only listened. And counted.
Tick. Tick. Tick. Two of them. A little out of step with each other. Like two clocks in two rooms.
And faster than Corin.
She could hear that. She did not need to look. Corin's strides, with the man's hand under him, were long and quick for a man with one leg and a stick. But the ticking was quicker. Not much. A little. Each tick a little closer together than each stride. So that for every eight of Corin's strides, there were nine of theirs. Or ten.
Twenty-one. Twenty-two. Twenty-three.
They were gaining.
She looked up. Ahead. North. Past Corin's shoulder, past the swaying litter and Kestrel's thin running shape and Tansy's little blue bobbing light far in front.
The Ridges.
She could see them now. A long dark line across the north. Not a hill. Not a swell. A line of walls. Low, from here. Like a row of teeth. Or a fence, at the bottom of a long field. Standing up out of the bright scoured glass. And their tops were red. The sun was on their tops. Only their tops. Everything below was in shadow.
A mile, Tansy had said, once, in the chapel. A mile from the chapel to the Ridges.
A mile.
Lyra had walked a mile a thousand times. In the city. In the snow. In the hills. A mile was nothing. A mile was the walk from Crown Walk to the river, on a summer morning, with her mother, counting the lamp posts.
A mile was a very long way, on one leg and a stick.
Forty. Forty-one.
She looked west. She could not help it. Just once. Just with her eyes. Not turning her head.
The sun.
It was sitting on the edge of the world. Huge and red and swollen, so big it seemed to fill half the sky. Its bottom edge was touching the glass. Flattening against it, the way an egg yolk flattens in a pan. And above it, a little way above it, there was a gap of sky, between the top of the sun and the place where the dark began.
She held up her hand. Without stopping. Without thinking. At arm's length, the way Miss Penhallow had taught her when she was eight, in the garden at Crown Walk, to tell the time by the sun. Fingers together. Flat. Under the sun's top edge.
One finger. Less.
One finger of sun. Before it was down.
And then the dusk. And then the dark. And then, Kestrel said, the cold. And the iron would freeze.
But not yet. Not for a finger of sun, and then the dusk after it. Which was not nothing. Which was a long time. On the Glass, the dark came fast, but not that fast.
She put her hand down.
Sixty. Sixty-one. Sixty-two.
"Lyra," said Corin. Breathing hard. Not looking at her.
"What?"
"You're counting." He was gasping between words. Crutch, swing, step. "Out loud."
She had not known.
She shut her mouth. And went on counting inside, where nobody could hear.
Sixty-three. Sixty-four.
Behind them, the ticking. A little louder.
Ahead of them, the Ridges. A little bigger. The red on their tops going thinner, like paint wearing off a fence.
And in between, the bright scoured empty glass, and the four bearers running with the litter, and Vespera clutching her bag, and Oren at the back with his sword out, running heavily, with his bad back, not looking round.
Seventy.
They ran.
Vespera Falls
Halfway, Vespera fell.
Lyra saw it happen. She was not looking at Vespera. She was looking at the Ridges, and the sun, and Corin's feet. But she saw it out of the corner of her eye. A small dark shape ahead and to the left, running with its bag clutched to its chest. And then not running. Down. All at once. The way a bird comes down when a stone hits it.
The old woman's foot had gone out from under her on the scoured glass. That was all. The glass was smooth now, smooth as a polished floor, with all the grit blown off it, and her felt-wrapped boot had slid, and she had gone down on her side with the bag under her and her thin arms round it.
And did not get up.
Lyra stopped. She could not help it. Corin stopped beside her, swaying, with the silent man's hand under his elbow.
"Go," said Vespera. From the glass. Not loudly. "Go on. Don't be fools."
She was trying to get up. Lyra could see her trying. One hand on the glass, pushing. Her old knees bending. Her face, in the red light, gray and set. And the hand slipping on the smooth glass. And her going down again.
The ticking. Behind them. Louder.
"Go," said Vespera again.
And Oren went past Lyra.
He did not stop. He did not slow down. He came up from the back of the line at his heavy run, with his sword in one hand, and as he passed Vespera he bent, without breaking his stride, and put his other arm round her. Round her middle. Under her bag. And lifted her off the glass. The way you would lift a child who has fallen over in the street. All of her. Bag and coat and thin old legs. And tucked her against his hip, on his left side, with her legs hanging, and ran on.
Lyra heard the breath go out of him when he lifted. A grunt. Low and deep. From his back. From the place where the throw had bruised him to the bone. She saw his face go white.
He did not stop.
"Put me down," said Vespera. From under his arm. Furious. "Put me down, you great fool. Your back."
"No," said Oren.
"I told you. I told you not to carry anything heavy."
"You're not heavy." He was breathing hard. "You're a bag of sticks."
"I'll remember that."
"Good," said Oren. "Remember it. Remember all of it. Then you'll have something to shout at me about. Later."
And he ran on. With his sword in one hand and the old woman tucked under the other arm, and his face white as paper, and his teeth set. Past the litter. Past Kestrel. Toward the Ridges.
Lyra went after him.
She did not count, for a while. She found she had lost her place. She had been at seventy-something. She did not know where. It did not matter. She started again.
One.
Corin's crutch on the glass.
Two.
The Ridges ahead, taller now. The red on their tops thinner.
Three.
The ticking behind.
Four.
Vespera's voice, ahead, muffled under Oren's arm, saying something sharp that Lyra could not hear. And Oren's voice, grunting back: "Saints and all their candles, woman. Hold still."
And for one mad moment, in the middle of running for her life across the burning-cold glass with the iron coming behind, Lyra wanted to laugh.
She did not. She ran.
The Ridges
They grew as she ran toward them.
That was the thing about the Ridges. From a mile off they had looked like a fence. A low dark line, like the teeth of a comb laid flat on a table. And then, as she came closer, they got taller. And taller. And they did not stop getting taller until she was right at the foot of them, with her neck bent back, looking up.
Walls.
Walls of glass. Standing up out of the ground in long straight lines, one behind another, running east and west as far as she could see on either side. Each one twice the height of a man. More. Oren could not have reached the top of one with his sword held up at arm's length. Thin, they were, for their height. A pace thick at the bottom, perhaps, and less at the top, so that they leaned in a little toward each other as they went up, the way the walls of a deep street lean. And they were not one wall each. Each line was broken. Broken into sections, a dozen paces long, or twenty, or five, with gaps between them. Narrow gaps. Some wide enough for two people side by side. Some so narrow you would have to turn sideways to get through. Some no wider than a hand.
Like the teeth of a comb. Yes. A comb as big as a city, lying on its side in the glass, with its teeth sticking up.
And they were not smooth.
The ground glass, out on the open, had been smooth. Scoured. These were not. They were ridged. Rippled. Their faces were covered in long wavering lines, running along them from end to end, the way sand is rippled on a beach where the tide has gone out. As if they had been made by something flowing. As if the glass, when it was hot, had run along here in great waves, and piled up, and set.
The last of the sun was on their tops.
Only the tops. A thin red line along each one, running from end to end, like a thread of fire laid along the edge of each wall. Below the thread, the walls were dark. Dark green, nearly black, the color of deep water. And the gaps between them were darker still.
And as she came close, the wind began to sing.
She had not known there was a wind. There was hardly any. Only the last breath of the storm, going away south. But it was coming through the Ridges. Through the gaps between the walls, the narrow ones and the wide ones. And as it came through, it made a sound. A sound like breath blown across the top of a bottle. Low, in the wide gaps. High, in the narrow ones. And all the gaps at once, all along the line of the Ridges, east and west, as far as she could hear. A great soft many-voiced humming. Rising and falling. Like a choir in a church a long way off, singing with their mouths shut.
It went under her skin. It went into her teeth, on top of the hum of the Glass.
She hated it.
And then, under the singing, she heard the ticking. And it was everywhere.
That was the worst thing about the Ridges. The thing Tansy had not said. The walls threw sound. Every wall caught every noise and threw it at the wall next to it, and that wall threw it back, and the next one caught it, so that a sound made in one place came at you from ten. Lyra heard the ticking behind her. And then in front of her. And then to her left. And then from above, off the top of a wall. Tick. Tick. Tick. From every side at once. As if there were not two iron men out there in the dusk but twenty. A hundred. Coming at them from every direction through the singing walls.
She stopped. She could not help it. She stopped dead at the foot of the first wall and turned round and round, trying to find where it was coming from.
"Don't," said a voice. Small and sharp, at her elbow.
Tansy. With her lamp. Grabbing Lyra's sleeve.
"Don't listen to it," said Tansy. "It lies. The Ridges always lie. You can't tell where anything is by listening. You have to look." She tugged. "Come on. In. Kestrel says in."
Lyra went in.
Through a gap. A wide one. Between two walls that rose up on either side of her, dark and rippled and leaning, so close she could have touched both with her hands out. The red thread of light far above, along their tops. The air in here cold already. Colder than outside. As if the walls had been holding the cold all day and were letting it out now, into the gaps, the way a cellar holds the cold.
And the singing louder. All round. In the walls themselves.
Corin came in behind her. On his crutch, with the silent man's hand under his elbow. Breathing in great sobbing gulps. His face gray. His good leg shaking so hard she could see it through his trousers.
Then Oren. He had set Vespera down just inside the gap, on her feet, and turned straight round and gone back out to the mouth of it. And now he came in last of all. Backing in. With his sword up. Looking out the way they had come.
And behind Oren, out on the open glass, in the very last of the red light, Lyra saw them.
Not shapes this time. Not far off. Close. Two hundred paces. Less.
Two iron men. Tall and gray, with their collars up, and their blank oval heads turned toward the gap. Walking. At the speed of a man. Not faster. Never faster.
They did not need to be faster.
They were here.
Hold Till the Stars
Kestrel was waiting for them further in.
The gap they had come through did not go straight. It ran in a little way between its two walls, and then turned, sharply, left, round the end of one wall and into the space behind it. A long narrow passage between that wall and the next line of walls behind. Like an alley between two rows of houses. And then it turned again, and opened out, a little, into a sort of yard. A place where three sections of wall stood close together round a patch of open glass, the way three people stand together round a fire. With gaps between them. One gap where they had come in. One on the far side, narrow, going north. And one to the right, wider, going east.
The litter was down. In the middle of the yard. Arran was sitting up on it, on his elbows. The bearers were standing round it with their chests heaving. Marrit was bent double, her hands on her knees, coughing.
Kestrel stood in the middle of them all with her spear in her hand.
She had a spear. Lyra had not seen it before. Or she had, and not noticed. The Ashborn all carried them, she remembered now. Bone spears. Long shafts of pale old wood, with a point at the end made of bone, ground sharp and bound on with gut. Brin had one. Oda had one. Sef. Even Marrit had one, though she leaned on it more than she held it.
They had all broken their spears on the iron at Last Thorn. Lyra remembered that too. These must be new ones. Made since. At the Edge, perhaps. Or carried spare.
She hoped they were stronger.
"Listen," said Kestrel.
Everybody listened.
She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. The yard was quiet, in the middle of the singing. The walls round them seemed to hold the sound off, a little. Like a room inside a house.
"When it is dark," said Kestrel, "they freeze. You know that. You have seen it. When the sun goes and the cold comes, they stop. Wherever they are. Whatever they are doing." She looked round at all of them. At Oren. At Lyra. At Corin, holding on to the silent man's arm. "We do not need to beat them. We cannot beat them. We do not need to run from them. We cannot run from them. Not with the litter. Not with him." She nodded at Corin. Not unkindly. "We need only to hold. Till the stars."
"How long?" said Oren.
Kestrel looked up.
The sky above the yard was a long thin strip between the tops of the walls. Lyra looked up too. It was still light up there. Still a pale blue, going purple at the edges. And along the tops of the walls on the west side, the red thread was still there. Thinner. Much thinner than it had been. But there.
"When the red is gone off the walls," said Kestrel, "they slow. When the first stars come, they stop." She looked back down. "Not long. A little while. Not long."
"How long is a little while?"
Kestrel looked at Oren.
"Long enough," she said, "to die in. If we do it wrong."
Nobody said anything to that.
"So we do not do it wrong," said Kestrel.
She began to place them. Quickly. Pointing with her spear.
The litter, against the north wall. In the deepest shadow. Arran on it. Marrit and Vespera beside him, their backs to the wall. "Stay down," said Kestrel to them. "Stay still. Do not move unless this one says."
Corin next to them. The silent man beside Corin. Tansy beside the silent man, with her lamp. Kestrel looked at the lamp. "Put it out," she said. Tansy looked at her. Then cupped her hand round the small blue flame, and blew, and it went out. And the yard went darker.
Oda and Brin at the gap they had come through. One on each side of it. Spears ready. Sef at the narrow gap on the far side, going north. Kestrel herself at the wide gap going east.
And Oren in the middle. In the open. Between all of them. Where he could go to any gap.
"And you," said Kestrel to Lyra. "With him." She jerked her spear at Oren. "Where he goes, you go. You are his other hand."
Lyra nodded.
She drew her sword.
She had not drawn it since the dusk at the iron horse. Since the lesson. Since the shadow and the Sun Road and the thing Oren had shown her. The Vance cut. She had done it a hundred times that afternoon, under his eye. A hundred times badly, and then, once, on the very last of the light, not badly.
She felt it in her hip now. Waiting. Like a word on the tip of her tongue.
She went and stood beside Oren in the middle of the yard.
He did not look at her. He was looking at the gap where they had come in, where Oda and Brin stood with their spears. His sword was up, in both hands, the point a little forward. His feet were apart. His weight was on the balls of his feet. His face, in the dusk, was very still.
"Remember," he said. Low. Without turning his head. "From the hip. Not the arm."
"I remember."
"And don't go for the body. The body's iron. Go for the joints. The knees. The back of the knee."
"I know."
"I know you know." He was quiet a moment. "I'm saying it anyway."
The ticking came round the corner of the wall.
