Chapter 79: The Ridges, Part 5 of 5: Three Left
Three Left
Corin was looking at the iron.
Lyra found him standing over the second one, on his crutch, when she came back from the first with her bent sword. He had got up, after the shaking stopped. She had not seen him get up. He was standing by the litter, by the north wall, looking down at the long gray shape lying on its face beside it. At the open hole in its back. At the dead wheels.
"How many were there?" he said. Not looking at her. "At the start. In the city. How many?"
"Six," said Lyra. "Wren said six. Six crates."
He was quiet. She saw his lips move. Counting. On his fingers, she thought, inside his coat, where nobody could see. Six was small enough. He could hold six.
"One at Last Thorn," he said. "You broke one at Last Thorn. Or somebody did. The book says."
"Yes."
"And two here."
"Yes."
He counted again. Lips moving.
"Three," he said. "Three left."
"Three left."
He looked down at the dead iron. For a long time.
"Three," he said again. Very quietly. "And it cost Arran. Two of them cost Arran." He shut his eyes. "What will three cost?"
Lyra did not answer that. She did not have an answer.
Oren did. He came and stood on Corin's other side, with his sword in his hand. He looked down at the iron too.
"Less," he said. "Now we know how."
Corin opened his eyes and looked at him.
"Knees to hold it," said Oren. "Something in the back of each knee. Spear, sword, anything that'll jam. Hold it still. Then somebody up its back for the spring." He jerked his head at the silent man, sitting cross-legged across the yard with Tansy. "Him, if he's got any hands left. Somebody else, if not." He paused. "We didn't know, before. At Last Thorn we were hitting them in the chest like fools. Now we know."
"And Arran?"
Oren was quiet for a moment.
"Arran didn't know," he said. "Arran just went. He went in front of the little one because he saw she was in the way and nobody else was close enough. That's not knowing how. That's just—that's just being the kind of man who goes." He looked at the litter. At the still shape on it, under the hide, with its face to the stars. "You can't plan for that. You can't teach it. You can only be glad when somebody has it. And sorry."
Nobody said anything.
Kestrel had come up behind them. Lyra had not heard her. She was standing a little way off, with her spear in her hand and her face in the shadow of the wall, and her brother's flute buttoned inside her coat.
"They are not dead," she said.
They all turned.
"The iron," said Kestrel. She nodded at the long gray shape on the glass. "You think they are dead. They are not dead. They are clocks. Broken clocks. A clock can be mended. A spring can be put back. A plate can be riveted on." She looked at Oren. "Somebody made them. Somebody can make them again."
"Out here?" said Oren.
"The man with the silver stick," said Kestrel. "And the one who walks in the dark. They have hands. They have tools, maybe. They have time, if we sit here and wait for them." Her face did not move. "We do not wait for them. We go. Tonight. Now. Away from here. Into the Ridges. Where they cannot follow by the sound."
"And Arran?" said Lyra.
Kestrel looked at the litter.
"Arran comes," she said. "Arran always comes."
And she went to tell the others.
The Fold
It was Tansy who saw to the silent man's hands.
Nobody else had. Lyra realized that later, with a kind of shame. Nobody had thought of him at all. He had been standing all that time over the second iron man, with the dead spring at his feet and the blood running off his fingers, drip, drip, onto the starry glass. Not moving. Not making a sound. And everybody had been looking at Arran. And nobody had looked at him.
Except Tansy.
She got up from Arran's feet, when the breathing was done and Kestrel had closed his eyes. She got up and went across the yard to the silent man. And took him by the wrist. Just above the cuts. And pulled.
"Sit down," she said.
He sat down.
Right where he was. On the glass. Beside the fallen iron. Folding himself down the way he always did, long legs and long arms, until he was sitting cross-legged with his ruined hands lying palm up on his knees.
Tansy knelt in front of him.
She looked at his hands. Lyra, sitting a little way off with Corin leaning against her, saw her look at them. Saw her face go white. Even in the starlight. Even under the dirt and the grit and the fine red cuts of the storm. Saw her swallow.
Then saw her set her jaw.
"You stupid," said Tansy. Fiercely. "You stupid, stupid, stupid."
She began to pull at her own shirt.
Under her uncle's coat, she had a shirt. An old one, gray, once white, much too big for her, with the sleeves rolled up and up. She pulled the tail of it out of her belt. And tore it. With her teeth, to start the tear, and then with her hands. A long strip. Then another. The cloth ripping loud in the quiet yard, over the singing of the wind.
"Putting your hands in it," she said, tearing. "Your bare hands. In all the wheels. Like a baby putting its hands in the fire. Do you know what you look like? You look like you've been eaten. You look like a dog's dinner." Rip. "And you didn't even say. You didn't say ow. You didn't say anything. You just stood there bleeding on everything." Rip. "Hold still."
He was holding still. He had not moved at all.
She wrapped his right hand first. Round and round. Over the white of the bone across the back of it, so that Lyra could not see it anymore. Round the fingers, one at a time, the ones that were still whole, and round the ones that were not. Tight. Tighter than Lyra would have thought a child could pull. Tying the end off with a knot, and then another knot on top, the way Arran had taught her to tie the felt.
The blood came through the gray cloth at once, dark. She did not stop.
"And your face," she said, starting on the left hand. "Look at your face. That spring hit you right in the face. You've got a cut from here to here." She did not have a hand free to show him. She jerked her chin at her own cheek instead. Ear to jaw. "You'll have a scar. A big ugly one. You'll look like the Captain."
Oren, sitting against the wall with his sword across his knees, made a sound that might have been a laugh. It did not get very far.
"Hold still," said Tansy again. Though he was holding still. "Hold still, hold still, hold still."
Her voice was shaking. Her hands were not. She wrapped the left hand. Round and round. Tied it off. Knot on knot.
Then she sat back on her heels and looked at what she had done.
Two great lumpy gray bundles of torn shirt, where his hands had been. Already going dark.
"There," she said. And her voice cracked in half. "There. Now you're done."
And she put her face in her own hands and began, at last, to cry.
The silent man did not move to comfort her. He did not move at all. He sat cross-legged on the glass with his bandaged hands on his knees and his still pale eyes looking at nothing.
Then he did.
Lyra saw it. She was the only one who did, she thought afterward. Everybody else was looking at Tansy, or at Arran, or at nothing. Lyra was looking at his hands.
There was a scrap of cloth on the glass beside his knee. A small torn square of Tansy's gray shirt, left over from the strips. No bigger than the palm of a hand. Lying there in the starlight.
And the silent man picked it up.
With his bandaged fingers. Clumsy. Slow. They were wrapped so thick he could hardly bend them. But he picked it up, between the thumb and the first finger of his right hand, and held it up in front of him. And looked at it.
And folded it.
In half. Carefully. Corner to corner. Pressing the fold flat with the side of his thumb.
Then in half again.
Then again.
Lyra watched him do it. She could not stop watching. He did not hurry. He did not look at anybody. He sat in the starlight beside the crying child, with his ruined bound hands, and folded the scrap of gray cloth smaller. And smaller. Each fold pressed flat, slow and careful, with the side of his thumb. Each fold making it half the size. A square. A smaller square. A strip. A smaller strip. A tight hard little knot of folded cloth.
And smaller.
Until it was no bigger than a fingernail.
Until she could hardly see it, between his bandaged fingers. A tiny gray square. Folded so many times it was nearly solid. Like a seed.
Then he stopped.
And held it. Between his finger and thumb. Up in front of his face. And looked at it. With his pale still deep-well eyes. For a long, long time.
As if it was something he had made before. A long time ago. And had forgotten how. And had just, for the first time, remembered.
Lyra did not know why it made the hair stand up on her arms.
She only knew that she would remember it. The starlight. The crying child. The bound bloody hands. And the tiny gray square, folded and folded and folded until there was almost nothing left of it, held up to those pale eyes as if it were the most important thing in the world.
She did not say anything at all.
Sef
Sef sat with her for a while, after.
Lyra did not know why. He had never sat with her before. He had hardly spoken to her. He was the quiet one of the Ashborn. Quieter than Brin, even. He walked lightly and said little and looked at everything with his narrow dark eyes, and in all the nights since the Edge Lyra had heard perhaps a dozen words from him.
But he came across the yard, when the binding of the silent man's hands was done, and sat down on the glass beside her. Not close. A little way off. With his knees up and his arms round them. The way she sat.
And for a long time he did not say anything.
Then he said, in the southern tongue, slowly, finding each word:
"We walked. Together. Him and me."
Lyra looked at him.
"The trail," said Sef. "Yesterday. The false one. With the goatskin. We walked it together. All day." He was looking at the litter, across the yard. "He talked. All day. All the way. He never stopped. I never said anything. He did not mind. He said I was the best person to talk to in the whole world, because I never argued."
Lyra did not say anything.
"When the felt wore through," said Sef, "on his foot. In the afternoon. Far out. He did not tell me. I did not know. He went on talking and limping and I thought it was still the pretend limp. For hours. And then I saw the blood. On the glass. Behind him." He stopped. "I said, Arran, your foot. And he looked down at it and he said, Oh. So it is. And laughed. And kept walking."
He was quiet for a moment.
"I held him up, the last mile," he said. "With my arm round him. He leaned on me. He was heavy. He talked the whole way. He told me stories about the old man, Hask, and the goats. He told me about a girl at the Salt Wells he liked. He told me the stupidest joke I ever heard, about a goat and a priest. Three times. Because I did not laugh the first time. Or the second."
"Did you laugh the third time?"
Sef looked at her. And something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The place where a smile would be.
"Yes," he said. "It was worse the third time."
Lyra put her bandaged hand over her eyes.
They sat like that for a while. The wind sang in the Ridges.
"I have some of him," said Sef. Very low. "Now. In here." He touched his chest. "Not much. A little. Like all of us." He paused. "I got the joke."
Lyra took her hand away from her eyes and looked at him.
"The goat and the priest," said Sef. "I have it. I can feel it. Not like I heard it. Like I made it up. Like it is mine." He shook his head slowly. "It is a very bad joke. And now I will have it forever."
And he put his face down on his knees.
Lyra did not know whether he was laughing or crying. She thought perhaps he did not know either.
She did not ask him to tell her the joke. She thought she understood why. It was not hers. It had been Arran's. And now it was Sef's. And some things, you only tell to the person who did not laugh the first two times.
She sat beside him on the glass and did not say anything at all, and after a while, without looking, he moved a little closer. Until his shoulder was against hers. And stayed there.
Through the Gaps
They left the yard a little before midnight.
It was slow. Everything was slow now. Brin could not carry, and Oda could not carry, and Kestrel would not let Oren carry, and then let him anyway, because there was nobody else. So it was Oren and Sef on the front poles of the litter, and Lyra and Marrit on the back, with Lyra's bandaged hand burning on the pole. And Arran between them, under the hide, with his face to the stars.
He was lighter than she had thought. That was the first thing. She had carried him for an hour the night before, on the walk to the chapel, and he had been heavy then. Warm and heavy and talking, all the way, making jokes about lords and goats. Now he was light. As if something had gone out of him that had weighed more than his body. She did not like to think about that.
The north gap was narrow. Too narrow for the litter to go straight. They had to tip it. Up on its side, almost, with Oren going through first sideways and Lyra coming last, and the hide slipping, and Kestrel walking beside it with one hand flat on her brother's chest to hold him on. Nobody spoke. The glass walls were so close on either side that Lyra's shoulders brushed them. Cold. Rippled. Like the walls of a deep well.
Then a passage. Long and dark, between two lines of walls, with the strip of stars far above. The wind singing in it. Their feet, in their felt, going shff, shff on the glass, and the sound running ahead of them and coming back.
Tansy led. With the silent man. She had lit her lamp again, cupping it in her hands, and its small blue flame went ahead of them down the passage like a will-o'-the-wisp, and the light of it ran up the rippled walls and made them shine. She knew the way. Lyra did not know how. Every passage looked the same. Every gap. Every yard. But Tansy went through them without stopping, left, and right, and through, and left again, the way you go through the rooms of your own house in the dark.
The silent man walked beside her with his bound hands held a little out from his body, the way a man holds wet hands. He did not let them touch anything. Lyra saw the dark stains spreading through Tansy's torn shirt, slow, as he walked.
Corin came behind them. On his crutch. Alone, now. Nobody's hand under his elbow. The silent man's hands were no good for that anymore. He went slowly, and his face, when the blue light caught it, was very white and very still. But it was his own stillness. Not hers. Lyra had learned the difference.
She counted the gaps.
She could not help it. One. A narrow one. Two. A wide one, with a little drift of grit in it that the storm had left. Three. Four. A yard. Then another passage. Five.
At seven, Kestrel said, Here.
And they stopped. And set the litter down. And the new yard was round them, small and dark and quiet, with its three close walls and its strip of stars.
Kestrel stood beside the litter for a long time after they put it down. Looking at the yard. At the walls. At the sky.
"He would have liked this one," she said at last. To nobody. In the southern tongue. "He would have said it was very roomy."
Nobody laughed.
But Lyra heard, from somewhere behind her in the dark, a small sound. Short. Bitten off. Like a fox, very far away.
She did not turn round. She knew whose throat it had come out of. And whose laugh it was.
The Door
By midnight, they had moved.
Not far. Kestrel would not let them stay in the yard, with the two iron men lying on the glass. So they had picked up what they had and gone through the north gap, the narrow one, sideways, one at a time, and along a passage between two lines of walls, and through another gap, and found another yard. Smaller. Three walls close together, like the first. With nothing in it but glass and stars.
They had carried Arran.
On the litter. The way he had come. Brin had tried to take a pole and could not, because of his ribs, and Oren had taken it instead without a word, bad back and all. And they had laid him in the new yard against the north wall, with his hands folded on his chest and a hide over him, and his face uncovered, to the stars. Kestrel had wanted his face uncovered. She did not say why. Nobody asked.
And then they made the heap.
They made it beside him. Close beside him. So close that the edge of it touched the edge of the litter. And they lay down in it. All of them. Under the hides, in the dark. Back to front. Knees in the backs of knees. The way they did every night.
One less.
Lyra lay on the edge of it. She could not sleep. She had known she would not. She lay on her side on the hide with her back to the cold and her face to the heap, and Corin in front of her, and the silent man beyond him, and Tansy curled up small against the silent man's chest with her face in his coat. And beyond them, Kestrel. And beyond Kestrel, the edge of the litter. And Arran.
She listened.
She counted.
She did not mean to. It was the ticking. She was listening for the ticking. All night, since the yard, she had been listening for it. For a third one. For another one. For any of them. For the sound of the clocks being wound.
She counted the silences instead. The places where there was no ticking. One. Two. Three. A long one. Four.
There was none.
None near. None far. Nothing. Only the wind singing in the Ridges, all along their dark lines, east and west. And the hum. And under the heap, the small sounds of people trying to sleep. Brin's breath, catching on his ribs. Oda, very still. Marrit, murmuring her ward-words, very low, the same few words over and over, so that they were more like a hum than words. Sef, crying without a sound. Lyra could feel him shake. Somewhere in the middle of the heap.
And Corin.
Corin was awake. She could tell. His breathing was wrong for sleep. Too careful. Like a man who is afraid of what might come out of him if he lets go.
She put her hand on his back. Between the shoulders. Through the coat. And left it there.
After a while, his breathing went a little easier.
She counted the silences. Nine. Ten. Eleven.
Then, from the far side of the heap, out of the dark, a voice.
Small. Thick with crying. Muffled in a coat.
Tansy.
Not talking to anybody. Lyra knew that at once. Not to the silent man. Not to Kestrel. Not to any of them. The way you talk to yourself, when you are very tired, and very small, and something terrible has happened, and you need to say a thing out loud to make it real.
"Tomorrow's the door," said Tansy.
Lyra lay very still.
"The round one," said Tansy. "In the middle. The iron one. Where he goes." A sniff. "He'll knock. He always knocks. Every time we go. He knocks and knocks, all those knocks, with the long stops and the short ones. And nobody ever comes." A long, shaky breath. Into the coat. "Nobody ever answers."
She was quiet for a moment.
The wind sang in the Ridges.
"But we've got lots of people now," said Tansy. Smaller. Very tired. "We never had lots of people before. It was only ever me and him. And now there's lots."
Another breath. Slower. Going toward sleep.
"He'll knock," said Tansy. "Maybe this time somebody answers."
And then nothing.
Only her breathing. Slowing. Going down. Into sleep, against the silent man's chest.
Lyra lay on the edge of the heap with her hand on Corin's back and her eyes open, looking at the stars between the walls, and the stars in the glass below, and the dark shape of the litter at the edge of everything.
She did not count anymore.
She only listened. To the wind. And to the hum. And to the silence where the ticking was not.
Beyond Corin, the silent man had not lain down like the rest. She could see him, a little, against the stars, where the hides had slipped. Sitting up, half under them. With Tansy asleep against his chest and his bound hands held out from his sides, away from her, so the blood would not get on her coat. Facing north. Toward the middle. Toward the round iron hump that Tansy said was there, and the door in it, and whatever was behind the door.
He was not asleep. She was sure of that. His head was up. He was waiting. The way you wait the night before a long journey home, sitting up in the dark with your bags packed, too full of the morning to close your eyes.
Lyra watched him for a long time.
She thought of all the knocks Tansy had talked about. The long stops and the short ones. Two winters of knocking on an iron door in the middle of the Glass, and nobody ever coming.
She thought she would like, very much, for somebody to come.
And waited for the morning, and the door.
