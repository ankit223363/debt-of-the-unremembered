Chapter 79: The Ridges, Part 4 of 5: Two in Him
Two in Him
Lyra got to him first.
She did not decide to. She was outside the ring, on her knees, and then she was beside him, inside it, though the ring was broken now and it did not matter. She put her arms round him. Round his shoulders. From the side. And pulled him against her.
He was shaking. All of him. She could feel it through his coat, through her own. A deep hard shaking, like a man with a fever. His gray hand was still pressed over his mouth.
"It's all right," she said. She did not know if it was. She said it anyway. "It's all right. It's all right."
He took his hand away from his mouth. Slowly. As if he was afraid of what would come out if he did.
Nothing came out. Only his breath. Quick and ragged.
"He's laughing," said Corin.
Very low. So low that only she could hear.
"In me. Arran. He's laughing. Not at anything. He's just—laughing. The way he does. Did." His voice cracked on it. "I can feel it. Right here." He put his hand flat on his own chest. Over the black words. "Like something trying to get out. Like a bird in a box. And I can't stop it. I don't want to stop it. But I can't—he's dead, Lyra. He's dead and he's laughing and it's coming out of me and he's dead."
She held him tighter.
"I know," she said.
"I promised him." He was shaking harder. "In the chapel. This afternoon. He asked me if I remembered. Last Thorn. The promise. And I didn't. And I said I'd do it anyway. And he grinned at me and said, good, I only wanted to know. In case." He made a sound. Not a laugh. Not Arran's laugh. His own. Broken. "In case. He knew. He knew, Lyra."
"He didn't know."
"He knew something."
She did not argue. She did not know. Maybe he had. Maybe the Ashborn did. Maybe, after a whole life on the edge of the Glass, carrying your dead, you got so that you could feel the cold coming up before it came.
"You kept it," she said. "The promise. You kept it."
He did not answer for a long time.
Then he said, even lower:
"I didn't stand up."
"What?"
"When the second one came. When it came across the yard at me. I didn't stand up." He was looking at his own hand on his chest. "She did. Sola. I felt her do it. I was sitting there against the wall with Vespera, and I saw it come through the gap, and I saw it turn toward me, and I couldn't move. I couldn't move at all. I was so frightened I couldn't breathe." His fingers curled on his chest. "And then I was standing. And I didn't do it. She did it. She stood up in me and stood there in front of it like—like she'd done it all her life. Like it was nothing. And I was behind my own eyes, watching. Watching her do it with my leg. With my hands."
He stopped. Swallowed.
"And Arran was already down," he said. "By then. While she was standing up in me. He was already down on the glass. He went in front of Tansy on his knees with a spear and nobody made him. Nobody stood up in him. He just went."
He turned his head and looked at her. In the starlight. His eyes were wet. His own eyes. Gray and tired and his.
"He was braver than me," said Corin. "And she was braver than me. And now they're both in here. And I'm the only one in here who was afraid."
Lyra did not say anything for a moment.
Then she took his face in her hands. Both hands. The way she had in the heap, the night he came back. And made him look at her.
"Listen to me," she said. "You were afraid. Good. You should have been. It was a great iron thing coming to kill you. Anybody who isn't afraid of that is a fool or a dead woman." She held his face. "Sola wasn't afraid because Sola's been dead for sixty years. Arran wasn't afraid because Arran was Arran, and he was never afraid of anything in his life, and look where it got him." Her voice cracked. She made it stop cracking. "You were afraid. And you stayed. You didn't run. You didn't crawl away behind the litter. You sat there, and you were afraid, and you stayed. That's yours. That's not hers. That's not his. That's yours."
He looked at her.
"No lies," she said. "Rule one. Not even the kind ones. I'm not being kind. It's true."
He looked at her for a long time.
Then he put his forehead against hers. The way she had, in the heap, in the dark. And shut his eyes.
And they stayed like that, on their knees on the starry glass beside the body, until the shaking stopped.
The Flute
Afterward, Kestrel took the flute.
She did it when everybody had moved back. When the ring had broken up, and the Ashborn had sat back on their heels, one by one, and wiped their faces, and looked at each other, and looked away. When Brin had lain down on his back on the glass beside the body, very slowly, with his hand pressed to his ribs, and shut his eyes. When Oda had let Vespera come and look at her shoulder. When Corin had taken his hand away from his mouth, at last, and sat back, and Lyra had come and put her arm round him, and he had leaned against her, shaking.
Then Kestrel bent over her brother again.
The flute was on its cord round his neck. Where he had hung it that evening in the chapel, when she gave it back. The storm stopped. You said. It was lying on his chest, on his torn shirt, in the starlight. Small and yellow and smooth. A little bone flute, no longer than a hand, with its holes worn smooth by his fingers.
Kestrel lifted his head. Gently. With one hand under it. The way you lift the head of a sleeping child to turn the pillow.
And with her other hand she drew the cord up over it. Over his short shaved scalp. Over the plait at the back, tied with red thread. And laid his head down again on the glass.
She held the flute in her hand.
She looked at it for a long time. Turned it over. Ran her thumb down the row of holes. The way, Lyra thought, you would run your thumb down the face of somebody you love, to learn it by heart.
She did not play it.
Lyra had thought she might. For a moment. When she held it up, close to her face, in the starlight. Lyra had thought she was going to put it to her mouth and play the three notes. Low, high, a little lower. Stop. The way he had played them in the chapel, nine times, until she took it away.
She did not.
She held it a moment longer. And then she put it inside her coat. On the left. Over her heart. The same place she had put it that afternoon, in the chapel, when she said Until the storm stops.
And did up her coat over it. Button by button. All the way to the top.
Then she sat back on her heels again beside him, with her hands in her lap, and looked at his face.
That was all.
She did not say anything. She did not cry. Her face did not move. She sat there beside her brother in the yard between the singing walls, under the stars, above and below, with his flute over her heart, and did not move for a long time.
Nobody went near her.
Nobody needed to be told not to.
The Hide
After a while, Marrit brought a hide.
She brought it across the yard from the litter, slowly, leaning on her spear, with the hide folded over her other arm. An old one. Brown and white, the hair worn thin in patches. Lyra thought it might be the one Arran had lain on all day in the chapel, under the green streak, sulking about his flute.
Marrit did not give it to Kestrel. She did not say anything to Kestrel at all. She knelt down on the other side of Arran, the way Kestrel had knelt, slowly, one knee and then the other, with her old bones cracking. And she unfolded the hide, and laid it over him. Over his chest and his folded arms and his legs and his wrapped bad foot. Gently. Smoothing it flat with her spotted hands, the way you smooth a blanket over a sleeping child.
She did not cover his face.
She left that bare. To the stars. And she sat back on her heels and looked at him, and her lips moved. The ward-words. The same few words she said every night, very low, over and over, in the tongue that Lyra did not know. More like a hum than words.
Kestrel did not look at her. But after a while, Lyra saw Kestrel's lips begin to move too. Very slightly. The same shapes. Without any sound.
And then Sef's. Kneeling by the north gap.
And Oda's, against the wall, with her arm held to her side.
And Brin's, on his back on the glass, with his eyes shut.
All of them. All the Ashborn. Saying the same few words without sound, in the starlight, while the wind sang in the Ridges round them.
Lyra did not know the words. She did not know what they meant. She thought perhaps they were names. Or a prayer to the Saints, in the old tongue of the valleys. Or perhaps they were only the words you say over somebody when you have carried them, and they are in you now, and you want them to know they are not alone in there.
She found her own lips moving.
Not the same words. She did not have them. Only numbers. One. Two. Three. Over and over, very low, without any sound. The only words she had that were hers.
She did not stop herself.
And beside her, she felt Corin's breathing slow, and slow, and fall into the same rhythm. Not counting. Something else. Three notes. Up and down and round. Hask's. Under his breath.
Nobody said anything. Nobody needed to. They knelt and sat and lay round the boy under the hide, each saying the words they had, and the stars came down to the edges of the glass and went on underneath, and for a little while, in the yard between the singing walls, it was almost like a church.
What It Cost
Vespera went round them all. In the dark. With no lamp. Kneeling by each one in turn, and feeling with her old cold hands, and saying nothing until she had finished.
Lyra went with her. She did not know why. Because she could not sit still. Because if she sat still she would look at Arran. Because somebody had to hold the bag.
Brin first.
He was lying on his back on the glass where he had lain down, by the first fallen iron. Breathing in small short gasps, through his teeth, with his big hand pressed to his left side. When Vespera knelt and moved his hand away and put her own there instead, under his torn shirt, he made a sound like a dog that has been kicked, and then bit it off.
"Ribs," said Vespera. "Two. Three. Cracked, not through. Nothing sticking out. Nothing in the lung, or you'd be coughing red." She took her hand away. "You'll live. You'll hate it, but you'll live."
Brin said something in the round speech. Lyra did not understand it. Vespera snorted.
"And you," she said to him. "Don't carry anything. Don't lift anything. Don't laugh."
"He won't laugh," said Lyra. Before she could stop herself.
Vespera looked at her. In the starlight. And did not say anything. And then looked back at Brin, and patted his big shoulder, once, and got up.
Oda next.
She was sitting against the wall of the west gap, where she had fallen, with her right arm held tight across her body by her left. Her face was gray under the gray line. Her lips were pressed white. When Vespera reached for the arm she flinched, the whole of her, like a horse.
"Out," said Vespera. Feeling. Gently. Round the top of the arm, round the shoulder. "It's out. The ball's come out of the cup. When that iron hand hit you." She sat back. "I can't put it in. Not with these." She held up her own thin hands. "I've not the strength anymore. It wants somebody heavy."
She looked round.
Oren was already coming.
He had been sitting against the north wall with his sword across his knees. Lyra had thought he was not watching. He had been watching. He came across the yard and knelt down by Oda without a word, slowly, with the long breath and the hand on the knee, and looked at the shoulder, and then at Oda's face.
"I've done this," he said to her. In the southern tongue. Slowly, so she would follow. "Many times. In the war. And once for your friend Brin, at the Edge. Remember?"
Oda looked at him. Then nodded. Once. Short.
"It hurts," said Oren. "Very much. For a moment. Then it stops hurting so much." He sat down on the glass beside her. Took off his left boot. "Lie down."
She lay down. On her back. On the glass. Oren lay down beside her, the other way round, with his feet toward her head. He took her right wrist in both his big hands. And put his bare foot, his left one, in her armpit. Gently. Into the hollow under the arm.
"Breathe out," he said.
She breathed out.
And he pulled.
Not a jerk. A long, steady, slow pull. Leaning back. Pulling her arm out, away from her body, with his foot braced in her armpit. Lyra saw Oda's face go from gray to white. Saw her mouth open. Saw her not scream. And then there was a sound. A dull thick clunk, deep inside the shoulder, like a door shutting in a far room.
Oda's whole body went loose.
"There," said Oren. He let go of her wrist. Gently. Laid it across her chest. "There. It's in."
Oda lay on the glass with her eyes shut. Breathing. In. Out. And then she opened her eyes and looked up at Oren, sitting up beside her with one boot off and his scarred face sweating in the starlight.
She said something in the round speech.
Oren did not understand it. Lyra could see he did not. But he nodded anyway, as if he did. And put his boot back on.
Vespera bound the arm to Oda's side with a strip of hide. Then she went to Kestrel.
Kestrel would not let her look. She held up one hand, flat, without turning her head from her brother. And Vespera stopped. And looked at her for a long moment. And then went away and left her.
Then Corin. His hands. The storm cuts had opened again, some of them, when he came up off the glass. Vespera put salve on them in the dark, by feel. He did not seem to notice.
Then Sef. Nothing. A bruise on his cheek. Then Marrit. Nothing. Then Tansy, who would not let go of the silent man's sleeve long enough to be looked at, and said she was fine, and was.
Then Lyra.
"I'm not hurt," said Lyra.
Vespera looked at her. Up and down. Then took her right hand and turned it over. The palm. Lyra had not looked at it. It was raw. All across the inside of the fingers and the heel of the hand. The skin torn and blistered, where she had held on to the hilt of her sword while the iron knee ground on it and the great thing swayed and would not fall.
She had not felt it. She felt it now.
Vespera put salve on it. And wrapped it. And did not say anything. And then, last of all, before she got up, she put her old cold hand flat on Lyra's cheek. Just for a moment. And took it away.
"Your sword's still in that thing's knee," she said. "You'll want it."
Lyra had forgotten.
The Sword
She went to get it.
The first iron man lay on its face in the middle of the yard where it had fallen. Long and gray and still under the stars. Its arms flung out. Its egg turned a little to one side, so that the thin dark slit lay against the glass. Its back open, the square hole where the brass plate had been, and inside the hole the dead wheels, still. Not ticking. Not anything.
And in the back of its right knee, standing up out of the brass crease at an angle, her sword.
She looked at it for a long time before she touched it.
It was the sword she had carried since she was a girl. Through the yard at Crown Walk, and the long gallery, and the back streets of the Ink Quarter, and the dark of Old Joss's press room with its point at a stranger's throat. Through the snow and the hills and the fire at Last Thorn. It knew her hand. She knew its weight. She had never once in all those years left it behind anywhere.
And now it was stuck in the knee of a dead clock in the middle of the Glass.
She put her left hand on the hilt. Her right was wrapped now, in Vespera's linen, over the raw palm. She did not want to use it. She put her left hand on the hilt and pulled.
It did not come.
She put her foot on the back of the iron calf, the way you would put your foot on a log to pull out an axe, and pulled again. Harder. With her whole weight.
It did not come.
The brass had closed on it. That was what it felt like. As if, when the iron fell and the leg went straight, the joint had shut on the blade like a mouth. Like a trap. She could feel the steel bending a little as she pulled, the way it had bent when the knee tried to fold. She was afraid it would snap.
"Let me."
Oren. Behind her.
She let go. She stepped back. And Oren crouched down by the dead iron, slowly, with the long breath and the hand on the knee, and looked at the sword where it went into the brass. Looked at it for a long time. Then put out one big hand and touched the joint. Felt round it. The way Vespera felt round a shoulder.
"It's not the joint," he said. "It's the leg. It's lying on it. The weight of the whole thing. Pressing the knee shut." He stood up. "Get the other side."
She got the other side.
They could not lift it. It was much too heavy. Two of them could not have lifted it. But they did not need to lift it. They only needed to roll it. A little. Oren got his hands under the iron hip, on one side. Lyra got her good hand and her shoulder against the iron thigh, on the other. And Oren said, Now, and they pushed.
It moved. Not much. A hand's width. Rolling, heavy and slow, onto its side, with a long grinding scrape of iron on glass.
And the knee came open.
Just a little. Just enough. The brass crease at the back of it loosened, the way a fist loosens when you pry up one finger. And Oren reached across with his free hand and took hold of the hilt of her sword and drew it out. In one long smooth pull. Like drawing it from its sheath.
He held it up. In the starlight.
The point was notched. Not badly. A small bite out of one edge, a finger from the tip, where it had ground against the brass. And there was a long bright scratch down the flat of it. And the last two fingers of it, near the point, were bent. Not much. A little. Out of true. She could see it when he held it up and sighted down it, the way he had sighted down his own sword on the hoof of the iron horse.
"It'll straighten," he said. "Not here. With a smith. And a fire. It'll straighten."
He held it out to her. Hilt first.
She took it. With her left hand. And held it. And looked at the notch, and the scratch, and the little bend.
"I did it," she said. Very quietly. "From the hip."
Oren looked at her.
"I know," he said. "I saw."
"It went in. It went right in. It didn't skid."
"No. It didn't."
"It didn't stop it, though," said Lyra. "It didn't do anything. It just stuck. And the thing kept going. And then he—" She looked across the yard. At the silent man, sitting cross-legged on the glass by the second iron, with Tansy kneeling in front of him. "It was him. Not me. It was him that stopped it. I just got my sword stuck."
Oren was quiet for a moment.
"You held it," he said. "You and Brin. You held it still. So he could get up its back." He looked at the dead iron at their feet. "If you hadn't held it, it would have walked right across this yard and through that old woman and the boy and out the other side, and he'd have been chasing it." He paused. "That's what a cut's for, sometimes. Not to kill. To hold. To make the thing stop for one breath, so somebody else can do what needs doing."
Lyra looked at him.
"Is that in the family too?" she said. "The Vance cut? To hold?"
Oren almost smiled. In the starlight. With his scarred face.
"That's in every family," he said. "That's just fighting."
She put the sword back in its sheath. It did not go in all the way. The bend caught at the mouth of the scabbard. She left it like that. A hand's width of bent steel sticking out of the top.
It would straighten. With a smith. And a fire.
She would have to remember to find one.
