Chapter 79: The Ridges, Part 2 of 5: Inch by Inch
Inch by Inch
It did not come at once.
That was the worst of it. The ticking came round the corner of the wall, into the alley behind the first line of walls, and then it went on. Along the alley. Past the turning. And Lyra stood in the middle of the yard with her sword up and listened to it go past, on the far side of the wall, so close she could have put her hand on the glass and felt it through. Tick. Tick. Tick. Going east.
And then, a long way off, turning. And coming back.
It did not know where they were. That was what it meant. The walls were lying to it too. Throwing its own sound back at it. Throwing their sounds. It had come into the Ridges after them and now it was walking up and down the alleys between the walls, looking, the way a dog casts about for a scent.
It would find them. Lyra knew that. It did not need to hurry. It did not need to be clever. It only needed to walk, at the speed of a man, up every alley and through every gap, until it came to the right one.
All they could do was wait.
She looked up.
The strip of sky between the tops of the walls was going dark. Blue to purple. Purple to a deep plum color, like a bruise. And along the tops of the walls on the western side of the yard, the red thread was still there.
She watched it.
She had never watched light go before. Not like this. Not as if her life depended on it. She had watched sunsets, of course. From the windows at Crown Walk, with the whole city going gold below, and the river on fire. She had watched the light go out of a room, at the end of a winter afternoon, and the lamps come on. But she had never watched it the way she watched it now. Inch by inch.
The red thread lay along the top of the western wall. Thin as a hair. And it was going.
Not all at once. It went from the bottom up. That was strange. She had thought it would go from one end to the other. It did not. It went from below. As if the dark was water, rising in the yard, and the red was the last dry stone at the top of a wall, and the water was coming up over it. The red got thinner. Thinner. A line. A thread. A hair.
One. Two. Three.
She was counting again. She did not stop herself.
On the far side of the wall, the ticking came back. Closer this time. It had come back along the alley and turned into the passage behind the second wall. It was in the passage that led round to the gap where Oda and Brin stood. Coming.
Four. Five. Six.
Oda's spear came up. Brin's. Lyra saw them shift their feet. Set themselves. One on each side of the gap. Spears low. Pointed in.
Oren did not move. He stood in the middle of the yard with his sword up and his eyes on the gap and his breath going slowly in and out through his nose. Lyra could hear it. In. Out. Steady. As if he were standing guard at a door at Crown Walk on a quiet night. As if nothing at all was coming.
Seven. Eight.
She looked up again.
The red was a hair. Less than a hair. A shine. A glint. Just at one end of the western wall, the far end, the highest part. Hanging on.
And the sky was going. Plum to deep blue. Deep blue to something darker. And in the darker, near the top of the strip of sky, far up, she thought she saw something. A tiny prick of light. White. So faint that when she looked straight at it, it went away, and only came back when she looked to one side of it.
A star. The first.
Not yet. Not quite. But nearly.
Nine.
The ticking turned the corner.
And came through the gap.
The First One
It stepped round the end of the wall into the gap.
Lyra had seen them before. At Last Thorn, in the fire, in the dark, with the tents burning and the goats screaming. She had seen four of them walk through the thorn bushes in a line. But that had been at night, by firelight, in smoke, with everything moving. She had not seen one close. Not like this. Not standing still for a moment in a narrow gap between two walls of glass in the last of the dusk, ten paces from her, with nothing between them but Oda and Brin and their two bone spears.
It was tall. Taller than Oren. Much taller. Its head nearly reached the place where the walls of the gap began to lean in toward each other. It was made of iron plates, gray and dull, overlapping like the scales of a fish. Fitted together so closely you could hardly see the joins, except at the shoulders and the elbows and the knees, where the plates stopped and there was brass. Brass joints, round, like the joints of a doll. And through the gaps in the brass, here and there, she could see wheels. Small ones. Turning.
Its arms hung down at its sides, long, longer than a man's, down past its knees. And at the ends of them, the hands. Not hands. Three fingers each, flat and broad and iron, like the blade of a shovel split into three. Hanging open.
And its head.
Its head was an egg. A smooth gray iron egg, a little longer than it was wide, with nothing on it at all. No eyes. No nose. No mouth. No face. Only, across the front of it, where eyes would be, one thin dark slit. The width of a finger. Going from one side of the egg to the other.
And in the slit, something glinted.
Lyra did not know what. Glass, perhaps. Or a lens, like the ones in Old Rook's spectacles. Something that caught the last light from the sky and threw it back, a cold thin gleam, as the egg turned.
The egg turned. Slowly. Toward the yard. Toward her.
No. Not toward her. Past her.
Toward the north wall. Toward the deepest shadow. Toward the litter and the three people sitting with their backs to the glass beside it. Toward Corin.
It had found him.
Tick. Tick. Tick. It took a step. Out of the gap. Into the yard.
Oda's spear went into it.
She was so fast that Lyra did not see her move. One moment she was standing on the left of the gap with her spear low. The next she had driven it in, hard, with her whole body behind it, low down, at the brass joint behind the knee. The bone point hit the brass and there was a sound like a stone hitting a bell, and the spear shaft bent, and bent, and did not break. And the point did not go in. It skidded off the round brass and slid down the iron of the shin and stuck in the glass of the floor.
The Eraser did not even look at her.
It took another step. Its long arm swung, as it walked, the way a man's arm swings. Not at her. Just swinging. And the back of the shovel hand hit Oda's shoulder as it went past, and she flew. Sideways. Off her feet. Into the wall of the gap, with a crack that Lyra felt in her own teeth. And slid down it. And lay there.
Brin roared.
A great deep wordless sound, like a bull. And came at it from the other side with his spear held in both hands like a pike, and drove it at the other knee. And Lyra heard the bone point hit the brass, and skid, and Brin's whole weight go behind it, and the spear shaft snap.
The Eraser took another step.
Brin was in the way. He was standing right in front of it with the broken shaft in his hands. Big and slow and brave, with the gray line down his face and his mouth open, roaring. And the Eraser walked into him. Not hitting. Walking. The way it had walked through the thorn bushes at Last Thorn. Its iron chest met Brin's chest and Brin went backward, off his feet, and down, on the glass, and the Eraser's foot came down on the broken spear beside his head and crushed it flat.
And walked on. Into the yard.
Oren met it.
Lyra had not seen him go. He had been beside her. And then he was not. He was in front of the Eraser, between it and the litter, between it and Corin, with his sword up. And as the iron came at him he struck.
She had seen him strike before. In the yard at Crown Walk, against the posts. At Last Thorn, in the fire. In the horse's shadow that afternoon, slow, to show her. She had never seen him strike like this. With everything. With all of him. A great overhand blow, down, at the place where the iron neck met the iron shoulder. Where there should have been a joint.
The sword hit the iron.
And sparks went up. A spray of them. Orange and white. Bright as a firework in the dusk, lighting up the whole gap for a moment, the two walls and the iron egg and Oren's face, scarred and set, with his teeth bared.
And the blade skidded. Off the shoulder plate. Screaming. With a long silver scratch left on the gray iron where it had gone.
The Eraser did not stop.
It did not even slow. Its long arm swung, and Oren saw it coming and was not there when it arrived. He had stepped back. One pace. Two. Giving ground. With his sword up again. His face had not changed.
"Joints," he said. Not loudly. To her. "Now."
Low
She went low.
She did not think about it. There was no time to think. Oren had drawn it. That was what he had been doing. Giving ground, step by step, backward across the yard, with his sword up and his eyes on the egg. Drawing it after him. Toward the middle. Away from the litter. Making it turn. Making it give her its back.
And it did. It turned to follow him. Its long arm swinging. Its great iron back toward her, the gray scaled plates, the square brass plate set in the small of it, with the hole in the middle where the key went.
And below that, the backs of its knees.
Brass. Round brass joints, like the knees of a doll. With a dark crease in each one, where the leg bent. Where the upper part met the lower part. A gap. Not wide. The width of a blade. Less.
She dropped her point.
Low. Down by her left hip. Point down and back, almost touching the glass behind her heel. The lie. The beaten man. The tired one. Who has given up.
The Eraser did not see. It did not have eyes in the back of its head. It did not have eyes at all.
And she rose.
From the hip. Not the arm. The arm was a stick on the end of her. The hip was the stroke. She felt it go. The turn, low down. The push of the left foot on the glass. The spring of it, coming up through her like a bow let go. And the sword riding up on top of it, across and up. Under. Up. Into the dark crease at the back of the right knee, where the brass met the brass.
It went in.
She felt it go in. Not far. Not deep. But in. The point found the crease and went into it, between the two round brass edges, a finger's depth, two fingers', with a grinding scrape that went up the blade into her hands and up her arms into her teeth.
And stuck.
She pulled. It did not come. She pulled again, harder, with her whole weight. It did not come. The sword was stuck in the joint like a knife in a frozen loaf, and the Eraser was still walking, and its knee was trying to bend, and could not, because there was a sword in it, and the sword was bending instead.
She heard the steel creak.
The Eraser stopped.
Not because of her. Because its knee would not bend. It stood there in the middle of the yard on one straight leg and one stuck leg, swaying. Its ticking went wrong. Lyra heard it go wrong. Tick. Tick. Tick-tick. Tick. Uneven. Like a clock with a stone in its works.
And its egg turned. All the way round. On its neck. Further round than any neck should go. Until the thin dark slit was looking straight down at her, over its own shoulder, from high above. With the glint in it.
Lyra looked up into the slit.
She had her hands on the hilt. She could not let go. If she let go she lost her sword. If she did not let go, its arm was coming round. She saw it start. The long iron arm, swinging back toward her, low, the three-fingered shovel hand coming at her head like a door slamming.
She did not let go.
And Brin hit it.
He came up off the glass behind her. Lyra had thought he was finished. He had gone down so hard. But he came up, roaring again, with blood on his mouth, and in his hands was Oda's spear. The one that had bent and not broken. That had stuck in the glass by the gap. He had pulled it out of the floor. And he came across the yard at a run, big and slow and terrible, and drove it with all his weight into the back of the Eraser's other knee.
The left one. The one that was not stuck.
The bone point found the crease. And went in.
And the Eraser staggered.
Both knees. Both legs. One with a sword in it and one with a spear. Neither of them able to bend. It lurched forward. Sideways. Its swinging arm went wide, over Lyra's head, so close she felt the wind of it, and hit the wall behind her with a crack like a gun going off, and a long split ran up the dark glass of the wall from the place where the iron hand had hit.
It swayed. On two straight stiff legs. Like a man on stilts in a high wind.
It did not fall.
Lyra hung on to her sword. Brin hung on to the spear. And between them the great iron thing swayed and ticked, wrong, uneven, tick-tick-tick, and its egg turned, and turned, from her to Brin and back, and its arms swung, and it did not fall.
"It won't go down," Brin gasped. In the southern tongue. "It won't go down."
"Hold it," said Oren. From in front of it. With his sword up. "Just hold it."
They held it.
And then the silent man moved.
The Man Moves
Lyra would try, afterward, to tell it. To Corin. To herself. And she could never get it right.
Because it did not look like fighting.
She had seen a great deal of fighting. She had been taught it since she was ten. She had seen Oren fight, and the masters in the yard at Crown Walk, and men in the street, and Enforcers, and the iron at Last Thorn. Fighting had a shape. It was fast and then slow and then fast. It was hurry and fear and breath and noise. Even the best of it. Even Oren at his best, that afternoon, in the horse's shadow. You could see the work in it. The wanting.
This had none of that.
The silent man got up from beside the litter. Lyra saw him get up. Out of the corner of her eye, hanging on to her sword. One moment he was sitting with Corin and Tansy against the north wall. The next he was standing. Like water standing up. And then he was walking. Across the yard. Toward the Eraser. Not fast. Not slow. At his own long even walking pace. As if he were crossing a room to look out of a window.
No hurry. No fear. Nothing in his face at all.
And every move he made was already finished before it started.
That was the only way she could ever say it. Every move was finished. He did not reach for the Eraser. His hand was simply on it. On the back of its iron hip, where the plates overlapped. He did not climb it. He was simply going up it. The way a boy goes up a tree he has climbed every day of his life, without looking, without thinking, hand and foot and hand, his long body going up the iron back as if there were steps there that only he could see. Foot on the stuck knee. On Lyra's sword, even, on the flat of the blade, light as a bird, so that she felt it dip and rise in her hands. Hand on the plate edge. Knee on the brass plate in the small of its back.
The Eraser felt him. Its egg turned. All the way round, over its shoulder, the slit glinting. Its arm came up and back, the long iron arm, reaching behind it for the thing on its back the way a man reaches for a wasp.
The silent man was not where the arm went.
He had moved. Before it came. To the other side. His long fingers found the edge of the square brass plate in the small of the iron back, the plate with the keyhole in it.
And tore it off.
With his fingers. Bare. Lyra heard it go. A shriek of metal, of rivets tearing out, like a nail pulled out of a board. The plate came off in his hand, bent, and he dropped it, and it fell past Lyra's face and hit the glass with a clang.
And there was a hole. Square. Dark. Into the iron chest. With the edges of the iron plates round it, where the brass had been, sharp. And inside the hole, wheels. Turning. Brass wheels and steel wheels, big and small, dozens, all turning, all ticking, wrong now, uneven, tick-tick-tick-tick, faster and faster, as if the thing knew.
And in the middle of the wheels, a spring.
Lyra saw it. A great coil of dark steel. Wound tight. Thick as a man's wrist where it was widest, curling round and round on itself, round a center post, gleaming with oil.
The silent man put his hands into the hole.
Both hands. Bare. Into the wheels. Lyra saw the edges of the iron plates cut into his wrists as they went in. Saw the wheels catch at his fingers. Saw dark lines open on the backs of his hands, and then more, and then blood, running down into the works, black in the dusk.
He did not seem to notice.
His long hands closed round the spring. Round the outside coil of it, where it was thickest.
And he pulled.
The Eraser screamed.
Lyra had not known it could make a sound like that. None of them had. It was not a voice. It was the sound of the spring. The great coiled steel, wound tight for days, coming loose all at once. A long, high, tearing, metal shriek, rising and rising, as the coil uncurled out of the iron chest in the silent man's hands. Unwinding. Lashing. Whipping round and round in the air above the yard like a snake, like a whip, so fast it blurred. It hit the wall. It hit the iron. It hit the silent man across the face and opened a long line on his cheek, from his ear to his beard. He did not let go.
The wheels inside the chest spun. Faster. Faster. With nothing to hold them. A whirring. A whine. Higher than the shriek.
And then stopped.
All at once. Every wheel. Every tick. Silence. Out of the iron chest, nothing at all.
The Eraser stood in the middle of the yard on its two stiff stuck legs, with a sword in one knee and a spear in the other and a hole in its back, and the silent man standing on its hips with the dead spring hanging out of his bloody hands in long loose coils.
It swayed.
And fell.
Forward. Slowly at first. Then all at once. Like a tree going over. Lyra let go of her sword at last, and jumped back, and it went down past her onto its face on the glass with a crash that she felt in her feet. And lay there. Long and gray and still. With its arms flung out. Its egg turned a little to one side. As if it had gone to sleep.
The silent man stepped off it as it fell. Lightly. Onto the glass. And stood there with the spring in his hands.
His hands were cut to the bone. Lyra could see the bone. White, in the black blood, across the back of the right hand. Across the fingers of the left. He was bleeding from the wrists, and from the face, and from places she could not see.
He looked down at the spring in his hands. As if he did not know what it was.
Then he let it fall. And it lay on the glass beside the Eraser in long loose coils, like a dead snake.
And from the gap on the east side of the yard, the wide one, where Kestrel stood, came more ticking.
The Second
It came through Kestrel's gap.
Lyra saw Kestrel see it. Saw her go still, with her spear up, at the mouth of the wide gap on the east side of the yard. Saw her set her feet. And then the second iron man came round the end of the wall into the gap, tall and gray and ticking, with its egg turned toward the yard, and Kestrel did not wait for it to step out. She went at it. In the gap. Where it could not swing its arms wide.
She was fast. Faster than Oda, even. Lyra saw her spear go in at the knee, and skid, and come out, and go in again, higher, at the joint of the hip, and skid again. Saw Kestrel duck under the swing of the first arm, and turn, and strike at the other knee. And the bone point scraped on the brass and did not go in.
And the Eraser walked.
It did not fight her. It did not seem to know she was there. It simply walked. Out of the gap, into the yard, the way the first one had. And Kestrel was in the way, and it walked into her, and its iron hip hit her side and she spun away, off her feet, and hit the end of the wall, and fell.
She was up again at once. On one knee. With her spear. But it was past her.
It was in the yard.
And its egg was turning. Slowly. Toward the north wall. Toward the litter. Toward the deep shadow where Corin sat with his back to the glass, and Vespera, and Marrit.
And Tansy.
Tansy was in the middle.
Lyra saw it all at once. The whole yard, in the last gray light, as if somebody had stopped every clock in the world and let her look. The fallen Eraser on its face in the middle of the glass, with her sword still stuck in its knee. Brin beside it, on his knees, with blood on his mouth. Oda against the wall of the west gap, not moving. Oren, a few paces off, turning, his sword coming up. The silent man, standing by the fallen iron with the dead spring at his feet and his hands hanging down at his sides, black with blood.
Kestrel on one knee at the east gap.
And the second Eraser, walking. Out of the east gap. Across the yard. Straight across it. Toward the north wall. Toward Corin.
And between the Eraser and Corin, standing in the open glass in the middle of its line, Tansy.
She had got up. When the silent man went across the yard, she had got up to follow him. Lyra had seen her do it, out of the corner of her eye, and had not had time to shout. And she had stopped halfway. In the middle. And now she was standing there, small and thin in her uncle's coat, with her dead lamp in her hand, looking at the iron coming toward her.
She did not run.
Lyra did not know why she did not run. Afterward, she thought perhaps Tansy did not believe it would hurt her. Perhaps she thought it would stop. The way they stopped for a man in a gate. The way, Kestrel had said once, they stopped and waited when there was a person in the way, until somebody told them.
But somebody had told them.
On the open glass, before the storm. Before the chapel. Before any of this. Far back on the trail, somebody had said a word to them. A soft polite worn word that Lyra had heard in the dark at Last Thorn and never quite caught.
Through.
And the word said through. Through whatever is in the way. Through gates. Through walls. Through men.
Through a girl with a lamp.
The Eraser did not stop.
It did not slow. It did not turn aside. Its egg was not even looking at Tansy. It was looking past her. Over her head. At Corin. And it walked, at the speed of a man, in a straight line, and Tansy was in the line, and in three steps it would walk into her the way it had walked into Brin and into Kestrel. Except that Brin was a big man and Kestrel was quick.
Tansy was neither.
Lyra ran.
She had no sword. It was in the fallen one's knee. She ran anyway. Across the glass. She would not get there. She knew she would not get there. It was too far, and the iron was too close, and Tansy was too small. She ran anyway.
Oren ran. She heard him. Behind her, his heavy feet.
The silent man turned his head.
And from the north wall, from the deep shadow, from the litter, somebody moved first.
