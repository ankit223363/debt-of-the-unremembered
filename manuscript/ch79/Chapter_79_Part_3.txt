Chapter 79: The Ridges, Part 3 of 5: Arran
Arran
Arran.
He came off the litter. Lyra did not see how. He could not walk. He could not stand. His foot was raw from the heel to the toes and wrapped in linen and honey and he had not put it to the ground in a day and a night. He came off the litter anyway. Rolled off it. Sideways. Onto the glass. Onto his knees. With his spear in his hand.
He had had his spear beside him on the litter all day. Lying along the hide. Lyra had seen it. She had thought it was for comfort, the way a child keeps a toy in its bed. Something to hold.
It was not for comfort.
He came across the glass on his knees. On his knees and one hand, dragging the bad foot behind him, with the spear in his other hand. Fast. Faster than Lyra would have believed a man could go on his knees. Like a dog. Like a fox. Low to the glass, and quick, and silent, and his face, as he went, white as paper under the gray line, and his teeth bared.
Not grinning.
He got there first.
He got there before Lyra. Before Oren. Before the silent man had finished turning his head. He came across the last of the glass on his knees and he did not stop. He went past Tansy. Round her. And he put out his free arm as he went and swept her back, hard, behind him, off her feet, so that she sat down on the glass with a thump and her dead lamp went skittering away.
And then he was in front of her.
On his knees. In the line. Between Tansy and the iron. Between the iron and Corin.
And he drove his spear into its knee.
Not at the back. He could not reach the back. At the front. Upward. Into the crease of the brass joint at the front of the right knee, where the leg bent forward. With both hands now. With his whole body behind it. Kneeling, and driving upward, the way you would drive a stake into the ground.
The bone point went in.
Lyra heard it go in. The scrape and the crunch. It went in and it went deep, deeper than her sword had gone, deep into the works of the knee, and stuck.
And the Eraser walked.
It did not stop. It could not. The word said through. Its knee was stuck, with the spear in it, so its knee did not bend. But its leg still moved. Swinging forward from the hip. Straight. Stiff. Like a man on a wooden leg.
Like Corin.
And Arran was in front of it.
And it walked through him.
Lyra saw it. She would see it for the rest of her life. She would see it with her eyes shut, in the dark, on nights when she could not sleep. The great iron leg swinging forward, stiff, with the spear standing out of its knee. And Arran kneeling in front of it, with his hands still on the spear shaft. Not letting go. Not getting out of the way. Not even trying. Holding on.
And the iron leg coming forward. And the iron body coming after it. And Arran in the way.
The sound.
She did not want to remember the sound. She did.
And then the Eraser was past. One step. Two. Its stiff leg swinging, dragging the spear with it, the shaft broken now, snapped off a hand's length from the point and the rest of it lying on the glass. Past Arran. Past the place where Arran had been. Past Tansy, sitting on the glass behind him with her mouth open. Toward Corin.
Toward Corin.
And Arran was on the glass behind it. On his back. With his arms out.
Not moving.
The Second Spring
Corin was standing.
Lyra saw that as she ran. He had come up off the glass by the north wall. All at once. In one movement. The way Oren came up. The way he had come up out of the hides at the iron horse. Onto the ball of his good foot, with the stiff leg swung out for balance and the crutch already under his arm, and his eyes on the thing coming at him.
Not running. He did not run. He could not have run, and he did not try. He stood. On the ball of his foot. Weight forward. With his back to the glass wall and Vespera and Marrit crouched behind him and the empty litter at his side.
And his face was not his face.
Lyra saw that too. Even across the yard. Even in the dark. The stillness in it. The flat far calm. The level look of somebody who has stood in front of a great many things coming at her and is not going to step aside for this one either.
Sola.
Sola was standing in front of the iron, on Corin's one good leg, with nothing in her hands but an ash stick wrapped in felt.
The Eraser came on. Stiff-legged. Dragging. The broken spear in its knee. Its egg turned toward him. Its long arm beginning to swing up.
And the silent man hit it.
He had not been running. Lyra had seen him turn his head. That was all. And now he was there. On its back. Already. Already up it, the way he had gone up the first one, foot on the stiff dragging knee, hand on the edge of the plate, knee in the small of the iron back. As if he had been there all along. As if he had never been anywhere else.
His hands were black with blood. They were cut to the bone. He did not seem to notice. They found the edge of the second brass plate, and tore it off, with the same shriek of rivets, and dropped it.
And went in.
Into the wheels. Into the works. Bare. Lyra saw the iron edges open his wrists again, in new places. Saw the wheels catch at what was left of his fingers. Saw him close his ruined hands round the second spring.
The Eraser's arm was coming up. At Corin. The three-fingered shovel hand rising, swinging, toward the still flat face that was not Corin's face.
And the silent man pulled.
The shriek again. The long tearing scream of steel uncoiling. And the second spring came out of the iron chest in the silent man's hands, still turning, still alive, whipping and lashing round him in great loose loops of dark steel, round his arms, round his shoulders, cutting. He held it. Out from his body. Away from Corin. The way you hold a snake by the neck, at arm's length. And it turned and turned in his hands, unwinding, slower, slower, the coils going loose, until it hung from his fists like a dead thing.
The wheels inside the Eraser spun. Whined. Stopped.
The shovel hand stopped. In the air. A hand's width from Corin's face.
The Eraser stood there. Leaning forward. One arm up. Its egg tipped toward him, the dark slit glinting a hand's width from his eyes.
Then it fell.
Forward. Past Corin. Not onto him. Lyra saw the silent man do it. Saw him, as it began to go, put one foot on its iron shoulder and push, sideways, hard, so that it went down to one side. Onto the glass beside Corin. Beside the empty litter. With a crash that went round and round the singing walls.
And lay there. On its face.
Ticking.
Still ticking. Faintly. Somewhere deep inside it, a last wheel. Turning. Slower. Tick. Tick. Tick.
Slower.
Tick.
Tick.
Tick.
And then nothing.
The silent man stood over it with the dead spring hanging from his hands.
And Corin stood with his back to the glass wall, on the ball of his foot, with his crutch under his arm and the stillness in his face. Looking down at the fallen iron. Looking at it the way you look at a thing that is over.
Then he blinked.
And it was Corin again. Lyra saw it come back. The stillness went out of his face like water out of a basin. And he swayed. And put the crutch down hard on the glass. And stared at the iron at his feet, and at the silent man, and at his own empty hand, as if he did not know any of them.
"What—" he said. Hoarse. "What happened? What—"
And then he looked past the iron. Across the yard.
At Arran.
Stars
The last light went.
Lyra did not see it go. She was on her knees on the glass by then. Beside Arran. She did not remember getting there. She did not remember the last steps across the yard, or kneeling down. She was just there. On her knees, with her hands out over him, not touching. Not knowing where to touch.
And while she knelt there, the red went off the tops of the walls. The last of it. The very last shine at the high end of the western wall. It went, and nobody saw it go, because nobody was looking.
And the stars came out.
All at once, it seemed. The way they always did on the Glass. The sky above the yard, the long thin strip of it between the tops of the walls, went from deep blue to black. And in the black, stars. One, and then a dozen, and then too many to count. Thick and white and cold. Coming out the way frost comes out on a window, all over at once.
And below. In the glass of the yard floor. Under her knees. The stars again. Faint. Blurred. Upside down. Under Arran's back. Under his flung-out arms. As if he was lying on the sky.
The wind sang in the Ridges.
It had never stopped. All through the fight it had been singing, in the gaps, in all the gaps along all the walls, east and west. Lyra had not heard it. Now she heard it. Low and high, rising and falling. The great soft many-voiced humming, like a choir with their mouths shut. Singing on and on over the yard and the two fallen iron men and the people kneeling and lying and standing in the dark.
Nobody moved.
For a long time, nobody moved at all.
Kestrel was on one knee at the mouth of the east gap, where she had fallen. With her spear across her knee. Looking at her brother.
Oren was standing a few paces off, with his sword hanging from his hand. Point down. Breathing hard.
Brin was kneeling by the first iron man, with his big hands hanging between his knees and blood on his mouth.
Oda was sitting up now, against the wall of the west gap. Holding her shoulder. Her face gray.
Sef had come in from the north gap. He was standing just inside it. Very still.
Vespera and Marrit were by the north wall, by the empty litter, with their hands over their mouths.
Tansy was sitting on the glass where Arran had pushed her. With her knees drawn up. Looking at him. Her dead lamp lay on its side a little way off, where it had skittered.
The silent man stood over the second iron man with the dead spring hanging from his hands and the blood running off his fingers onto the glass, drip, drip, black, and did not move.
And Corin.
Corin was coming across the yard.
Lyra heard him. Crutch. Swing. Step. On the glass, in the singing dark. Slow. Not the long fast heron strides of the run. Slow. As if every step was a long way. As if the yard was a mile wide.
He came and stood over her. Over Arran. And looked down.
And then, slowly, with his hand on her shoulder, he let himself down. Onto his good knee. With the stiff leg out to the side. Beside her. On the starry glass.
Arran was breathing.
Lyra had not known. She had not dared to look for it. But he was. Small. Shallow. Wet. A little bubbling breath, in and out, through his open mouth. His eyes were open too. Looking up. At the strip of stars between the walls.
They moved. His eyes. They came down from the stars and found Corin's face. Then Lyra's. Then went past them. Looking for something.
"Kestrel," said Lyra. Not loud. She did not need to be loud. The whole yard was listening. "Kestrel. Come."
And Kestrel got up off her knee at the east gap and came.
What He Gives
Kestrel knelt down on the other side of him.
She did not say anything. She put her spear down on the glass beside her, carefully, the way she put everything down. And then she put out her hand and touched his face. The gray line on it. Forehead to nose. Two fingers. The way she had touched it at the iron horse, when he came back from the false trail with his foot cooked through, grinning.
He was not grinning now.
He was looking up at her. His eyes had found her when she knelt, and stayed. And his mouth moved. And a sound came out of it. Small. Wet. Lyra could not tell what it was. Not a word.
Kestrel bent down. Close. Her ear near his mouth.
He said something again. In their own tongue. Lyra did not understand it. Two words. Three.
Kestrel did not answer. She stayed bent close, with her ear to his mouth and her hand on his face, for a long time.
Then she sat up.
Her face did not change. Lyra watched it not change. It stayed as it always was. Narrow and sharp and brown, with the gray line down it, and the dark eyes, very steady. She did not cry. Her mouth did not shake. Her eyes did not fill. She sat back on her heels on the starry glass beside her brother and looked at him with that face, and it did not move at all.
Lyra had seen faces like that before. Once. In a mirror. The morning after her father burned.
"He says," said Kestrel. In the southern tongue. Quite steady. For the city people. "He says it doesn't hurt."
Nobody said anything.
"He's lying," said Kestrel. "He always lies about that."
Arran's mouth moved again. And this time Lyra saw it. The corner of it. Going up. Just a little. Just at one side.
The grin.
Even now.
He said something else. Lyra heard it this time. In the southern tongue. Very faint. A whisper with blood in it.
"Is she—" A breath. Bubbling. "The little one. Is she—"
"She's here," said Lyra. "She's all right. You pushed her. She's all right."
Tansy had come. Lyra had not seen her come. She was kneeling at Arran's feet, now, small, in her uncle's coat. Holding on to the edge of his wrapped bad foot with both hands. Not crying either. Looking at his face with her big eyes.
Arran's eyes went to her. Found her. And the grin got a little bigger.
"Good," he whispered.
And then his eyes went back to Kestrel. And stayed there. And he said something to her in their own tongue again. Longer, this time. Slower. With stops in it, for breath. And every stop longer than the last.
Kestrel listened. With her face not moving.
When he was done, she nodded.
Then she looked up. Round at all of them. At the Ashborn. Brin, kneeling by the iron, with blood on his mouth. Oda, against the wall, holding her shoulder. Sef, in the gap. Marrit, by the litter. At Vespera. At Oren. At Lyra.
At Corin.
"He says," said Kestrel, "that here, on the Glass, nothing can be taken."
Her voice was very steady.
"You know this," she said. "Nobody can take. Not the Ministry. Not the Bank. Not the iron. Not us. Not here. If he dies here, nobody can take him. He will go into the glass, like all the others. Under our feet. A light. Alone." She paused. "Unless."
"Unless," said Lyra.
"Unless he gives," said Kestrel. "Giving works. Here. Only giving." She looked down at her brother. "So he is giving. He says so. He says he gives it. All of it. Everything he is. To us. To whoever will take it. While he still can. Before he goes." She swallowed. Once. The only sign. "He says he does not want to go into the glass alone. He says it is very cold, down there, and very quiet, and he never liked quiet."
Arran's mouth moved. The grin.
Kestrel looked at Corin.
"And he says," she said, "that you promised."
Carrying Arran
They did it there. On the glass. In the yard between the walls. Under the stars, above and below.
There was no fire. No thorn heap. No brown herb to throw on the coals, no thick white burnt-honey smoke to open them. No paste for the gray lines. There was nothing. Only the glass, and the cold coming up out of it, and the wind singing in the Ridges, and the stars.
They did it anyway.
The Ashborn came. All of them that could. Kestrel at his head. Brin, on his knees, dragging himself across the glass from the fallen iron, leaving a dark smear behind him. Oda, getting up from the wall with her arm held tight against her side, her face gray, and coming, and kneeling. Sef, from the north gap. Marrit, from the litter, leaning on her spear.
They knelt round him. In a ring. Close. Shoulders touching. The way they lay in the heap at night. And they put their hands on him.
Bare hands. Skin to skin. Not through cloth. Kestrel's on his face. Brin's on his chest, over the place where the iron had gone through. Oda's, the one that still worked, on his wrist. Sef's on his forehead. Marrit's old spotted one on his bare ankle, below the wrapped foot.
Lyra moved back. She did not know the rules. She knew one. From the night at Last Thorn. Don't enter. Don't touch. She moved back out of the ring, on her knees, and sat on her heels just outside it, and watched.
Corin did not move back.
He was kneeling where he had knelt. Beside Arran. On his good knee, with his stiff leg out. Inside the ring. And Kestrel looked at him, across her brother's body, and did not tell him to move.
She nodded at him instead. Once.
Corin put his hand out. His left one. The gray one, with the black tips and the scabbed red lines of the storm across its back. He put it flat on Arran's chest, beside Brin's. Bare. Skin to skin.
And they breathed.
Lyra had seen it once before. At Last Thorn. The night they breathed Vivienne out of Corin. But that had been the other way. That had been taking. Twelve of them, round a fire, breathing the dark out of him into themselves.
This was not taking. This was being given.
She could see the difference. She did not know how. But she could see it. At Last Thorn, the gray threads had come out of Corin slowly, reluctantly, pulled. Here they came on their own.
They came up out of Arran like breath on a cold morning. Gray. Fine. Rising off his face and his chest and his open hands. Not one thread. Many. Dozens. A soft gray mist of them, coming up out of him in the starlight, the way steam comes up off a pond at dawn. Drifting. Rising. And then turning. Each one. Toward a face.
Toward Kestrel's. Toward Brin's. Toward Oda's and Sef's and Marrit's. Toward Corin's.
And going in. With their breath. As they breathed in.
A little each.
That was how they did it. Lyra understood that, watching. Nobody took it all. Nobody could. Nobody should. A whole man was too much for anybody. So each of them took a little. A thread each. A handful of threads. And in the morning there would be six people carrying Arran instead of one Arran lying in the glass alone.
Arran's eyes were open. Looking up at the stars. And as the gray came up out of him, his face changed. Not much. A little. The pain went out of it. Lyra saw it go. The tightness round the mouth. The white. It went out of him with the gray, and what was left was quiet. And young. Very young. Younger than she had ever seen him.
He was not grinning anymore. He did not need to.
The mist thinned. Slowly. Fewer threads. Fainter. The last of them coming up off his hands, very slow, like the last wisps off a candle when it has been blown out.
And then there were none.
Arran's chest went up, under all their hands. Once. And down.
And did not go up again.
Nobody moved. Not for a long time. They knelt round him in their ring with their hands on him and their eyes shut, breathing slowly, the way you breathe when you are carrying something heavy up a hill.
Then Kestrel opened her eyes.
She took her hand off her brother's face. Slowly. And put it on his eyes instead. And closed them. One. Then the other.
And one by one, round the ring, the others took their hands away.
Corin was the last.
Lyra watched him. He knelt there with his gray hand on Arran's still chest, and his eyes shut, and his face very white in the starlight. Breathing. In. Out. As if he was listening to something.
Then he opened his eyes.
And he laughed.
A short sharp sound. Out of nowhere. A bark. Like a fox. Exactly like a fox. Exactly like the laugh Arran had laughed a hundred times since the Edge. On the march, in the dark. Under the shades at noon. On his litter in the chapel, that very afternoon, with his arm flung over his eyes.
Arran's laugh. Coming out of Corin's mouth.
Corin's face went white. Whiter.
He put his hand over his mouth.
And knelt there, on the starry glass, beside the body, with his gray hand pressed over his own mouth, and his eyes very wide above it, and did not make another sound.
