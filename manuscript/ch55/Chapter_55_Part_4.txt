Chapter 55: The Vault Under the Dome, Part 4 of 5: Remy's Price
Remy's Price
They stood by the lift shaft, the three of them, in the small yellow light of the carter's lantern, with the frost creeping up their boots.
Gideon was shaking. He had come the whole way round the ring passage on his knees, he said, from the grating in Cinder Lane where Oren was waiting, following his own chalk marks in the dark. Two hundred yards of old brick drain, half full of black ice, so low he had to lie flat in three places and pull himself along by his elbows. He had marked every turn on Sunday night. A circle with a line through it. Left. A circle with two lines. Right. A circle on its own. Straight on. He had found every one of them again tonight, by touch, with his bare fingers on the wet brick, because the candle had gone out in the first ten yards.
"My hands have stopped working," he said. "I don't know why. They just won't bend." He held them out in the lantern light. They were white, the fingers stiff and curled. "My feet are the same. And my nose. I'm not certain I've still got a nose."
"You've got a nose," said Corin.
"Is it a good one?"
"No."
"Then it's mine." Gideon blew on his fingers. "Where's the case?"
Remy had not said anything since Gideon came out of the hole. He stood with the lantern in his hand, by the open mouth of the lift shaft, looking up into the dark at the flat brass-bound bottom of the empty lift. Listening to the voices forty feet above. The fiddle and the horn. The crack of the hammer.
"Not yet," he said.
He turned round.
And Corin saw his face, and knew that something was coming. Something Remy had been carrying all evening. Carrying down the back stair, and across the cold stone, and through the glass. Carrying the way Corin had carried the case on Sunday, from the crypt. Close. Careful. Afraid to put it down.
"Before I give it you," said Remy, "there's my price."
Gideon stopped blowing on his fingers.
"Your price," he said. "We've paid your price. Vespera's paint. The gloves. The forty crowns from the Duke. You said—"
"I said I'd get you down. I've got you down." Remy did not look at Gideon. He looked at Corin. "I didn't say what it'd cost to get you out again with the thing you came for. I'm saying it now."
Corin waited.
He had learned, in the last two weeks, that the people who wanted something from you very badly did not ask for it quickly. They walked round it. They came at it from the side. Lucian had done it on Saturday night, in the gray window, with his three questions. Linus Hale had done it in the crypt, with his book. Not until after Monday. They walked round and round the thing they wanted, the way a dog walks round and round a spot before it lies down, and you had to let them.
Remy walked round it.
"She's dying," he said.
Corin did not have to ask who.
"She's been dying since the autumn. Hollis knows. Mrs. Pratt in the kitchen knows. The doctor from the Hill comes every Thursday with his black bag and goes away again with a face like a wet Monday and a jar of something in his pocket, which is how she pays him. She's eighty-one years old and she's been burning since she was eleven, and there's—" He stopped. He started again. "There's not much left in her now. You saw her upstairs. The hair, the gown, the vials. That's all paint. Like your face. Hollis does the paint for her every evening and it takes him two hours. Underneath it she's a little old woman who doesn't always know what year it is."
"And you think it's tonight," said Corin.
"I know it's tonight." Remy's voice was very quiet. "Or tomorrow. Or the day after. But I think tonight. She's been holding on for Midwinter. For the auction. For the Night of Masks. It's the last one, and she knows it's the last one, and she wanted to see the floor sink one more time and hear the Weigher say sold one more time and stand in the dome with all the lords bowing to her. And then she'll go up to her room, and lie down, and Hollis will take the paint off. And that'll be it."
He looked down at the lantern in his hand.
"I've seen it before. With the old ones. They hold on for a thing. A birthday. A wedding. A ship coming in. And then the thing comes, and they let go. All at once. Like a rope."
The fiddle, forty feet above them, came to the end of its slow dance. There was a patter of clapping, very faint, like rain on a roof. And then the Weigher's voice, too faint to make out the words. Another lot.
"And when she goes," said Corin.
"When she goes, there'll be Ash." Remy lifted his head. "There's always Ash. You know that better than me. When a burner dies, whatever's left of her goes out of her the way smoke goes out of a candle when you blow it. And it goes up. And it hangs. And if nobody's there, nobody who knows how to hold it, it goes away into the air and it's gone. Or it settles. Into the walls. Into the floor. Into whoever's nearest." He was looking at Corin very hard now. "You know what happens when it settles into whoever's nearest."
Corin knew.
He had stood in rooms where it had happened. In the wet house in Gutter Lane, where a burner had died alone in the night and the Ash of him had settled into the rats, and the rats had gone mad. In the attic over the dyer's shop, where an old woman had died with her granddaughter asleep beside her, and the girl had woken up in the morning remembering a husband she had never had and a war she had never seen, and had not stopped screaming for three days.
That was why there were harvesters. That was what harvesters were for. Before they were anything else. Before the Ministry gave them badges and sent them after rogue mages. They were the men who came in the night, when a burner died, and held out their hands, and took the Ash into themselves before it could settle into anyone else.
"Who's nearest?" he said.
Remy did not answer.
He did not have to.
"She sleeps with me in the room," he said at last. "Since I was seven. On a little bed by the door. In case she wakes in the night and doesn't know where she is. I hold her hand till she goes back to sleep. Hollis sleeps in the next room. Mrs. Pratt on the floor above. But I'm the one in the room."
Since I was seven.
Corin thought of the boy on the seventh step of the back stair, an hour ago, waiting for him. Thin and quick and sharp-faced. I've opened it four hundred and three times since. Eleven years old, perhaps. Twelve. Who had been carried into this house as a baby, Hollis had said, on Tuesday, in a basket, and given to the Lady because she had asked for a child to keep her company.
"She's all I've got," said Remy. "She's not kind. She's never been kind. She bought me. Like a lot. Like a jar off a shelf. I know that. But she's all I've got. And she's eighty-one years of things nobody else in the world remembers. And when she goes, if there's nobody there to hold it—" He stopped.
"It'll go into you," said Corin.
"All of it. Eighty-one years. Into me." Remy's mouth twisted. "And I'll wake up in the morning thinking I'm an old woman in a dome of hair who built a cellar for fish. And I won't remember Hollis, or Mrs. Pratt, or the back stair, or the door, or how to open it. I won't remember me. There won't be a me. There'll be her, in a boy, for a week or two, screaming. And then the doctor from the Hill with his black bag."
The cold came up through the floor.
Gideon had gone very quiet. He was standing with his white hands pushed into his armpits, looking at Remy with his mouth a little open.
"So that's my price," said Remy. "When she goes, I want you there. In the room. By the bed. To take it. All of it. Before it gets to me."
He held up the lantern, so that the light fell full on Corin's painted face.
"I know what it costs. I'm not a fool. Hollis told me. Eighty-one years of somebody else, pouring into you all at once. On top of whatever you've got already. I know you might not come out the other side of it the same. I know you might not come out the other side of it at all." His voice did not shake. "I'm asking anyway. Because there's nobody else in this city I'd ask. Because you came down that passage tonight thinking of something so hard the glass wanted it, and you still walked out the bottom. And because I've got your case."
There it was. The thing he had walked round and round. Set down at last, on the frosty stone between them, in the yellow light.
Corin looked at it.
He thought of the Ledger, inside Fenton's coat, against his ribs. Of all the pages in it. Of how many of them were already not memories but only words. You knew a man named Pell. He was kind to you. Of how many more pages there would be, after tonight. And of what eighty-one years of a woman who had built a cellar for fish would do, pouring into a head that was already half empty. Whether there would be room. Or whether it would push things out, the way water poured into a full cup pushes out what was there before.
What it would push out.
And behind his left ear, in the dark, Malakor said nothing at all. Not no. Not yes. Nothing. Which was, Corin had learned, sometimes the worst thing he could say.
"Tonight, you think," said Corin.
"Tonight, I think."
"If I'm still in the house."
"If you're still in the house." Remy's eyes did not leave his. "And if you're not, I'll know you tried. And I'll go up to her room anyway, and hold her hand, the way I always do. And whatever comes, comes."
Forty feet above them, the hammer cracked on the wood.
Sold.
"All right," said Corin Vane.
He said it quietly. He did not let himself think about it first. If he had thought about it, he would not have said it. He knew that.
Remy let out a breath.
It went out of him in a long white cloud in the lantern light, and hung there between them, and did not drift away. And for a moment, behind the sharp quick face, Corin saw something that looked very much like a boy of eleven who had not slept properly for a long time.
Then it was gone.
"Right," said Remy. "Right. Good." He turned away and wiped his nose on the back of his wrist. "Then let's get your case."
And behind Corin's left ear, very faint, an old dry voice said one word.
Fool.
But it did not sound angry. It sounded, if anything, almost fond.
Gideon's Cold
But Gideon did not move.
He was looking past them both. Past the lift shaft and the wall of ice. Down the long narrow walks between the shelves, toward the great stone pillar in the middle of the room and the back rows behind it, where Remy had said the House of Rest jars were kept. And there was something in his face that Corin had not seen there before.
Gideon did not feel things.
That was the first thing you learned about him. Corin had learned it in the fifth arch, two weeks ago, by the light of Mother Grue's fire. The Lady had taken it out of him, over four winters, from the time he was eight. A jar a week. Fear and joy and grief and anger and love, one after another, poured off into glass and labeled in a neat hand and sold to the Hill, until there was nothing left in him but a thin quick boy with a dry voice who did what needed doing and did not care. He did not cry. He did not laugh. When Doctor Silas had taken off his two big toes, black with frost, on the table in the crypt of Saint Bride's, Gideon had watched the knife go in with his chin on his fist, the way you watch a man mend a boot.
And he did not feel the cold.
That had been the worst of it, Silas said. Worse than the rest. Fear and grief, a boy could do without, if he had to. But a boy who did not feel the cold would walk barefoot in the snow till his feet went black and never once think to go inside. Would sleep on a stone floor in the winter with his coat off. Would put his bare hand on an iron rail in a frost and leave the skin of his palm behind and wonder why it bled.
He knew it was cold, Gideon. With his head. He could see the frost. He could see his breath. He could see his fingers go white and stop bending. He knew, the way you know a sum, that when the puddles had ice on them a person ought to wear boots. But he did not feel it. Not in his body. Not the way other people did, the shrinking and the shivering and the wanting to be by a fire.
Only once had he shivered. In his sleep, on the floor of the crypt, eight nights ago. Corin had seen it and written it down in the Ledger, because it seemed like something that ought to be written. He shivered.
And on the Saturday after, when Gideon had told them his price for the vaults, the third part of it had been: Look for my cold.
It's a jar, he had said. Same as the others. In the lower rooms, or the cold room, or gone. I don't know. But I watched her put it in. The last winter. The last one she ever took. She held it up to the lamp after, and laughed, and said, "There. Now you'll never know when to come in." And she put a mark on the lid in blue chalk. An H. For Hollow. My real name.
And if I find it? Corin had said.
Bring it out. Gideon had shrugged. I don't want it back. I don't think you can put them back. I just want to have it. In my coat. With the other one.
The other one was jar H.297. Corin had taken it off a shelf in the lower rooms on the night they first went down, with a crowbar, with the Hollow brothers standing over them in the dark. Gideon had carried it inside his coat ever since. Against his chest. Under his shirt, at night, when he slept. He had never opened it. He had never said what was in it. But he touched it, sometimes, through the cloth, when he thought nobody was looking.
Now he stood by the lift shaft in the cold room under the Memory Bourse, with his white hands that would not bend held against his chest over the place where it was, and looked down the long walks toward the back rows.
"It's here," he said.
"You don't know that," said Remy.
"It's here." Gideon's dry voice did not change. But his eyes had gone very bright. "I've been in every store room in the lower rooms. Every crate. Every shelf. On Sunday, with the chalk. It's not down there. So it's up here, or it's gone. And she wouldn't sell it. She said so. I'll keep this one for myself." He looked at Remy. "Where does she keep the ones she keeps for herself?"
Remy looked at Corin.
Corin looked at the lift shaft. At the flat brass-bound bottom of the empty lift, a man's height above their heads. Listening. The fiddle had started again, forty feet up. A quick dance now. The lords were dancing round the hole in the floor while they waited for the last lot.
"How long?" he said.
"Till they want the last tray?" Remy thought. "Lot 39 is up there now. A War. They'll sell that slowly. Lots of bids. Then 40. Then the hammer goes three times, and the bell rings down here, and the brothers turn the handle, and the lift comes down for 41." He chewed his lip. "A quarter of an hour. Not more."
"Then show him," said Corin.
Remy swore.
But he showed him.
He took them down the narrowest of the twelve walks. The one that ran from the lift shaft straight to the great stone pillar in the middle of the room. The shelves went up on either side of them into the dark, black iron, frosted white along every edge, with the jars glowing faint and slow on them, gray and blue and green. Corin could not go fast. His stick slid on the frost. Twice he had to stop and lean on a shelf with his gloved hand, and feel the cold of the iron come straight through Fenton's white cotton into his fingers. Gideon went ahead of him, quick and silent on his boots with no big toes in them, and did not look at the jars on either side. He looked only ahead.
They came to the pillar.
It was huge. Close to, it was wider than the three of them standing with their arms out. Rough gray stone, old, with water running down it in thin bright lines and freezing as it ran, so that the whole of it was hung with little white beards of ice. And round the bottom of it, against the stone, all the way round, was a ring of small wooden cupboards. Low, the height of a man's knee. Each with a little door, and on each door a little brass plate.
"Hers," said Remy.
He held the lantern down to the plates.
They were not numbers. They were names. Small, neat, cut into the brass. Corin read them as Remy's light went round.
Mother.
The Ball at Ashcombe.
Alistair.
The Glass Garden.
My First Lot.
The Boy.
Remy's light stopped on that one.
He did not say anything. He just held the lantern there, on the little brass plate, and his hand was quite still.
The Boy.
And then he moved the light on, quickly, and it went round the pillar, and Corin followed it, and Gideon followed Corin. Round to the back of the pillar. To the side that faced away from the lift and the glass passage. Where the light from the lantern fell on the last of the little cupboards in the ring, and on its brass plate.
There was no name on this one.
Just a letter. Cut into the brass very deep, the way a child cuts his letters into a desk at school.
H.
Gideon went down on his knees on the frost.
He did it all at once, like a puppet when you let go of the strings. His knees hit the stone with a crack Corin heard. He did not seem to notice. He put out his white curled hands to the little wooden door, and they would not bend, and he could not get his fingers round the knob. He tried. He tried three times. And then he leaned forward and took the knob in his teeth, and pulled, and the little door came open.
Inside it was one jar.
Small. No bigger than a hen's egg. Sitting by itself on a little shelf of frosted wood, in the dark. With a gray wax seal on its lid. And on the wax, still there after all this time, faint and smudged but quite clear, a letter in blue chalk.
H.
And inside the glass, the light.
It was not gray like the others. Not blue. Not green. It was white. Pure, clean, hard white, like the sun on snow. And it did not move slowly, folding and unfolding, the way the other jars moved. It moved fast. It went round and round inside the glass, quick and bright, like something trying to get out.
Gideon looked at it.
He knelt on the frosted stone in front of the little open door, with his useless white hands in his lap and his breath going out of him in short quick clouds, and looked at the small bright jar on its shelf. For a long time. He did not take it. He did not touch it. He only looked.
"What does it say?" he said. "On the side. There's a paper."
There was. Tied round the neck of the jar with string. Corin bent down, slowly, his bad leg stuck out behind him on the frost, and turned it toward the lantern with one finger.
The hand was the same neat hand as all the labels in the room. But there was no lot number. No reserve. Only a line.
The cold. Taken from the Hollow boy, his fourth winter. For myself. So that I shall always have something of his that he will never miss.
Corin read it aloud.
He did not want to. But Gideon could not read, and Gideon had asked, and Corin had a rule about that, an old one, older than Lyra's. When they ask, you tell them. So he read it, in Fenton's voice, in the cold, every word.
Gideon listened.
Then he leaned forward, and took the jar off the shelf in his two curled white hands, held together like a cup, the way you hold water. And brought it to his chest. And held it there. Next to the other one.
He did not cry. He could not. That was in a jar somewhere too, on a shelf, or gone.
But his shoulders shook.
Once. Twice. A hard, small shaking, all through him, from the neck down. Like a wet dog. Like a boy who has come in out of the snow.
He shivered.
Corin did not write it down. He did not need to. He would remember it.
Or he would not. But either way, it had happened.
"Right," said Gideon, at last. His dry voice was quite steady. He put the small white jar inside his coat, against his chest, and buttoned the coat over it with his teeth, one button at a time. "Right. That's that." He got up off the frost. "Now your case."
He turned to go back round the pillar.
And stopped.
Corin had already turned, with Remy and the lantern, and was going back round the pillar the way they had come. His knee was screaming. His whole mind was on the frost under the tip of his stick, and the walk back to the lift, and the quarter of an hour. So he did not see what Gideon saw. He would think of that afterward. A long time afterward. That he had been three steps away, and had not turned his head.
Gideon saw it because he was low. Because his eyes were still down at the height of the little cupboards, from kneeling. And because the lantern, swinging in Remy's hand as Remy turned, threw its light for a moment into the gap between the pillar and the end of the nearest shelf. A narrow dark gap, a hand's width, where nobody would ever look.
There was a crate in it.
An empty one. Wooden. Long and narrow, the kind of crate the porters used for the Bulk Lots, with rope handles at each end. It had been pushed in there, on its side, as if somebody had wanted it out of the way. Its lid was gone. It was quite empty, except for a little straw, gray with frost.
And on its side, in black paint, through a stencil, there were letters.
Gideon looked at them.
He could not read them. He did not know any letters but V. He knew chalk colors, and numbers up to twelve, and the shapes of the marks the porters made. But these were not porters' marks. These were words. Three of them, in a line. Big black square letters. And under them, smaller, three more.
He looked at the first letter of the first word.
It was a circle. A plain round circle, like his own chalk mark for left in the ring passage, but without the line through it.
Straight on, he thought.
That was all. That was all it meant to him. A circle on its own. Straight on. And then some other shapes after it that meant nothing at all.
"Gideon," said Remy, sharp, from round the pillar. "Come on."
Forty feet above them, the hammer cracked three times on the wood.
And somewhere close, on the other side of the wall of ice, deep and slow, a bell began to ring.
Gideon turned away from the empty crate and ran.
He did not look back. He did not ask what it said. There was no time to ask, and he had what he came for, inside his coat, white and bright and fast against his chest. And by the time he thought of it again, it was much too late to ask anyone at all.
The letters on the crate said:
OAKHAVEN. BRIGHT.
TO THE VAULT.
