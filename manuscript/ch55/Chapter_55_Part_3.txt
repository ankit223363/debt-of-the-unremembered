Chapter 55: The Vault Under the Dome, Part 3 of 5: Glass
Glass
It was the third time Corin had walked the glass passage, and it was the worst.
The first time had been on Tuesday night. He did not remember much of it. He had been a lamp boy then, in Vespera's paint, and Remy had taken him through it at a run, holding his hand so hard the bones ground together, and he had thought of nothing because he had been too frightened to think of anything. The second time had been the same night, coming back, with his knee gone and Remy and Gideon carrying him, and he had been in too much pain to think at all.
This time he could think. That was the trouble.
The passage was twelve steps long. Remy had told him. Twelve steps, down a slope, very gentle, from the iron door to the room under the dome. And all of it glass.
The floor was glass. Thick and greenish and very smooth, like the bottom of a bottle, with light coming up through it from somewhere far below. The walls were glass. Curved, like the inside of a tube, so that the passage was round, a great round glass pipe going down into the dark, only just wide enough for two people side by side. The roof was glass. And through all of it, through the floor and the walls and the roof, the gray light came, steady and cold, from everywhere at once, so that there were no shadows at all.
And it was not clear glass.
Corin had not seen that the first two times. He saw it now. The glass was full of things. Deep inside it, as if they had been poured in while it was still hot, and set there. Faint shapes. Pale. Moving, very slowly, like fish under ice. He could not see what they were. They went away when he looked straight at them. But when he looked at the floor in front of his feet, or at Remy's back, they came back, at the edges of his eyes, all round him, in the walls and the floor and the roof. Faces. Hands. Rooms. Trees. The shapes of things people had carried down this passage and left behind.
It reads you, Remy had said, on Tuesday, in the arches. Every step. It reads what's in you, and if you're not the Lady's, or one of hers, it closes you.
Closes you?
Shuts you up. Like a book. Like a door. You stop on the sixth step and you can't go on and you can't go back and you can't remember why you came. And in the morning the porters carry you out and put you on the Friday cart.
Corin held Remy's hand.
He held it in his scorched white cotton glove, and Remy held it back, bare, with his cut thumb pressed against the back of Corin's gray fingers through the cotton. And they went down the first step together.
Think of nothing, Remy had said.
Corin thought of nothing.
He thought of the floor. Green glass. Smooth. His stick on it, the silver tip ringing, very faint. The bad leg swinging out stiff, the heel coming down. The good leg after.
The second step.
The glass under his feet was cold. He could feel it through Fenton's buckled shoes. Cold the way ice is cold. Cold that went up through the soles of his feet into his bones.
The third step.
And the glass looked at him.
He felt it. Not with his eyes. With all of him. The way you feel somebody standing behind you in a dark room. The whole passage, the floor and the walls and the roof, turning toward him. Not moving. Just turning. Its attention. Like a great cold eye opening very slowly, and finding him, and looking.
He kept walking.
The fourth step.
And the shapes in the glass came closer.
He saw them now. He could not help it. At the edges of his eyes, in the walls, all round him, the pale moving shapes were not pale any more. They were coming up through the glass toward him, the way fish come up through dark water toward a light. And they were not other people's things. They were his.
A ledger, open on a table, with a page in square capitals. READ THIS FIRST.
A narrow room on Tallow Street, with a window over a lane, and a stove, and a jar on the sill with gray light moving in it.
A dead man on a heap of rubbish in Gutter Lane, in the gray rain, with his eyes open.
The glass knows you now, Remy had said. But it'll still look.
The fifth step.
And then a face.
It came up through the glass of the left-hand wall, right beside him, so close that he could have touched it. A girl's face. Thin and pale and sharp-chinned, with dark eyes, and dark hair cut short and ragged just below the ears. Looking at him.
Lyra.
Not as she was tonight. As she had been in the autumn, the first time he saw her. In the rain, at the door of the Inkwell, in a gray cloak with the hood back, holding a letter. Her face set like a door. You're the harvester.
And then another. Beside it. Lyra in Vespera's shop, on the floor, by candlelight, with her chin up. No lies between us. Not even the kind ones. And another. Lyra on the barge, in the dark, with her father's hand in hers, tapping. And another. Lyra in the arches, with Silas's knife in her hand and her hair falling round her in long dark locks onto the floor. And another. Lyra in the crypt, in her mother's gown, under the grating, asleep against the wall. Lyra holding his hands while Vespera's needles went in. Lyra at the foot of the stairs on Crown Walk, an hour ago, in a white mask, saying It's still there. Under this. I promise.
They were all round him.
In the walls. In the floor. In the roof. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Every face of hers he had. Every one he had read in his Ledger every morning to be sure it was still there. Coming up through the green glass toward him, pressing close, so many that he could not see the passage any more, only her. Lyra. Lyra. Lyra.
The glass was reading her.
He felt it. The great cold eye of the passage, turning, and turning, and finding her in him, and stopping. Looking. Not at Fenton. Not at Corin. At her. At every piece of her he carried. Turning each one over, slowly, the way a man turns over a coin to see if it is good.
As if it were weighing them.
As if it were deciding what they were worth.
The sixth step.
Corin stopped.
He did not mean to. His feet stopped. His good leg would not lift. His bad leg hung, stiff, in the air, and would not come down. He stood on the sixth step of the glass passage with Remy's hand in his and his stick on the green glass and could not move. And all round him, in the walls and the floor and the roof, her faces. Looking at him. Hundreds of them.
You stop on the sixth step and you can't go on and you can't go back and you can't remember why you came.
He could remember why he came.
He could remember. He held on to it. A black wooden case with a silver clasp. A folded square on the clasp. Her father's last evening, inside. Gently, Linus. If you would. He held on to it the way you hold on to a rope in the dark. And he could not move.
"Vane," said Remy.
His voice was very far away.
"Vane. You're thinking of something."
He was. He could not stop.
"Stop it," said Remy. His hand had gone very tight. "Whatever it is. Stop thinking of it. It's looking at it. It's looking at it too hard. It wants it."
It wants it.
Corin looked at the faces in the glass.
And then, very far away, behind his left ear, behind the door, in the dark room where the held breath was, something moved. Something old and dry and sharp and furious. Something that had been silent for a week except for one word, and then three, and was not silent now.
NOT THAT, said Malakor.
It was not a whisper. It was a shout. The loudest Corin had ever heard him. Louder than the wet door. Louder than anything. It went through him like a bell.
Not that. You can't have that. Not yet. NOT YET.
And the glass flinched.
Corin felt it. The great cold eye of the passage, all round him, flinched. The way an eye flinches from a sudden light. And the faces in the glass went back. All of them. All at once. Down, and away, into the green deep, the way fish go down when a stone hits the water. Lyra in the rain. Lyra on the floor of Vespera's shop. Lyra with the knife and her hair falling. Lyra in the mask. Down. Gone. Pale shapes at the edge of his eyes again, and then not even that.
The glass was just glass.
His bad leg came down onto the seventh step.
The good one followed.
"Go," said Remy, very low. "Go, go, go."
They went. The eighth step. The ninth. Quick now. As quick as he could go, with Remy pulling him by the hand and his stick ringing on the glass and the bad leg swinging. The tenth. The eleventh. And the glass did not look at him again. He felt it not looking. He felt it lying very still all round him, the way a dog lies still when it has been struck, and is waiting to see if it will be struck again.
The twelfth step.
And then stone. Rough, wet, ordinary stone under Fenton's buckled shoes. And the gray light behind them. And the cold. And a smell of ice.
Corin stood on the stone and shook.
Remy was looking at him. In the gray light from the passage behind them. His face was quite white.
"I've never seen it do that," he said. "Four hundred and three times. I've never seen it stop on anybody and then let go." He did not let go of Corin's hand. "What was it? What did it want?"
Corin did not answer.
He could not have. He did not know how to say it. He stood on the stone at the bottom of the glass passage under the Memory Bourse with his hand in Remy's and his heart going like a drum, and inside him, behind his left ear, an old man's voice that had shouted NOT YET was breathing hard, like a man who has run a long way. And then going still again. And then, very faint, one more time:
Not yet, boy. Not here. Not for this.
And then nothing.
Corin opened the Ledger, there, on the stone, with his shaking gloved hands. To the first page. And looked at it, in the gray light, to be sure.
She was still there. Every line. In his own square capitals, and in hers, small and quick and leaning forward. Lyra Thorne. Partner. Rule one: no lies between us. Not even the kind ones.
He shut it again.
"Nothing," he said to Remy. "It wanted nothing. Let's go."
The Cold Room
The room under the dome was the coldest place Corin had ever been.
He had been cold before. He had been cold all his life, it felt like. Cold in the narrow room on Tallow Street with the stove gone out. Cold on the barge on the black river with the fog coming off the water. Cold in the crypt of Saint Clement's, under the grating, with the snow coming down through the bars. But that had been the cold of winter. Of weather. Of a world that would, some day, be warm again.
This was not that.
This was a cold that had been made. On purpose. By people. And kept, day after day and year after year, the way a miser keeps his gold. It came up out of the stone floor and down out of the stone roof and out of the stone walls on every side, and it did not move, and it did not change, and it had no smell but one. Ice. Old ice. The clean, sharp, empty smell of ice that has never once in its long life been touched by the sun.
Corin's breath came out of him in a cloud. It hung in front of his face, white, and did not drift away. There was no air moving to carry it.
"Don't touch the walls," said Remy. "Your glove'll stick. I lost the skin off two fingers my first winter."
He let go of Corin's hand at last. He went a little way ahead, along the wall, not touching it, and found something on a hook, and there was a scrape and a hiss and a small flame, and then a lantern. Not an Ash lamp. A plain tin lantern with a candle in it, the kind a carter hangs on his cart. Its light was yellow and weak and very small. But after the gray light of the glass passage it looked as warm as a kitchen fire.
Remy held it up.
And Corin saw the room.
It was round. Like the dome above it. Like the passage. Everything under the Memory Bourse was round, as if the men who built it had not liked corners. It was perhaps forty paces across, with a low roof of stone, curved, held up in the middle by one great thick pillar of stone like the trunk of a tree. And from the pillar to the walls, like the spokes of a cart wheel, ran the shelves.
Rows and rows of them. Iron shelves, black and very plain, from the floor to the roof, with narrow ways between them just wide enough for one man to walk. Twelve rows. Corin counted. Twelve spokes, from the pillar out to the wall, with the narrow walks between. And on every shelf, from the floor to the roof, in the yellow candlelight, the jars.
Thousands of them.
He had known. Remy had told him on Tuesday. He had seen the trays go up through the floor of the dome on the brass lift, three hundred jars and more in one evening, each one shining. He had known there must be a place where they were kept. But he had not known it would be like this.
They were packed close, side by side, in lines, on the black iron shelves. Glass jars of every size. Little ones no bigger than a thumb. Big ones the size of a man's head. Tall thin ones like bottles. Short fat ones like the jars a cook keeps her jam in. Each one sealed with gray wax. Each one with a small white paper label tied round its neck with string. And each one with something in it.
Light.
Not much. Not like the jars on the trays upstairs, shining under the great lamps of the dome. Down here, in the cold, the light in the jars had gone dim and slow, the way a fire goes dim when you bank it for the night. Each one glowed, faint, gray or blue or a pale greenish white, and moved, very slowly, inside its glass. Turning over on itself. Folding and unfolding. Like something asleep, breathing.
Thousands of them. Row on row. Shelf on shelf. All round him in the dark, from the floor to the roof, glowing very faintly, breathing very slowly, in the cold.
It was like standing in a field at night with all the stars come down out of the sky to sleep on the ground.
Corin stood quite still and looked at them.
"It's the cold," said Remy, beside him. "They keep better in the cold. Like meat. Like milk. A memory in a jar on a warm shelf in a shop goes off in a year, two years. Fades. Goes thin. Down here they'll keep for twenty. Thirty. There's jars on the back shelves older than me."
"Who brings the ice?"
"Carts. From the north. Every week, from autumn to spring, the ice carts come down from the lakes in the hills and unload in Cinder Lane, at the back. There's a shaft. They tip it down. And the ice men cut it and pack it in the walls." He held the lantern closer to the wall beside them. "Look."
Corin looked.
The wall was not stone. Not here. The stone came down from the roof and stopped, a man's height above the floor, and below that the wall was something else. Gray-white. Rough. Shining a little in the candlelight, with a wet shine. Ice. Great blocks of it, square-cut, set one on another like bricks, all the way round the room, behind the ends of the shelves. A wall of ice.
"Two feet thick," said Remy. "Behind it there's straw. Behind the straw there's the stone. They change the ice from the inside, a block at a time, so it never gets warm. Not once. Not in forty years."
"Forty years."
"The Lady's mother built it. Before the dome. Before the Bourse, even. It was a cellar for a fish market once, Hollis says. They kept the ice for the fish." Remy almost smiled. "Then somebody found that memories keep better cold, and here we are."
Corin looked at the shelves.
The label on the nearest jar was turned toward him. It was small and square and very neat. He could read it in the candlelight.
LOT 2207. A summer afternoon. River, boat, a girl's laugh. Clear. Strong. Reserve: four crowns.
The next one.
LOT 2208. The smell of a bakery, early. Bread and sugar. A father's hand. Fair. Reserve: one crown, six.
And the next.
LOT 2209. A wedding. Whose not known. Bells. Fair to poor. Reserve: one crown.
Corin stopped reading.
He had read enough of these. In the Bourse catalogue, in the arches, on Tuesday. In Lucian's black book, on Saturday night, in the gray light of a window on Ferrule Street. Three hundred and twelve admitted. One hundred and eight died. One hundred and sixty-three discharged. To the Bank.
The Bank.
This was the Bank.
"Where are the House of Rest jars?" he said.
Remy did not answer at once.
He lifted the lantern, and turned, and looked down the long narrow walk between two rows of shelves, toward the great stone pillar in the middle of the room. In the yellow light Corin could see the shelves going away into the dark, row on row, with the jars glowing faintly on them, gray and blue and pale green. And at the far end of the walk, by the pillar, something else. Something that was not a shelf.
"There," said Remy. "The far side. The back rows. Behind the pillar." His voice was very flat. "They don't sell those upstairs. Not on a night like tonight. Not with the lords watching. Those go out the back, by the ice shaft, on the Friday cart. To the Hill."
"To the Hill."
"To the Health. To the Fourth Desk. Sundries." Remy looked at him. "You read the abstract. I saw your face on Sunday when Gideon told me. You know where they go."
Corin did know.
To the lamps. To the Crescent Gas. To the cold blue light in the street lamps of the Hill, that never flickered and never needed a lamplighter, that burned all night in the fine wide streets where the lords lived, so that the lords could walk home from their suppers without seeing their own shadows. One tenth to the Treasurer. D.S. And the rest to the lamps.
He had stood under one of those lamps on Saturday night. On Ferrule Street, outside the Lamplighter's Rest, waiting for Lucian's window to go dark. He had thought how clean it was. How steady.
"Later," he said.
His voice was not quite his own.
"Later," Remy agreed. "Not tonight. Tonight there's one jar and one case and one lift." He turned the lantern. "This way."
He led Corin round the outside of the room, between the ends of the shelves and the wall of ice, with the lantern held low so that the light fell on the floor. The floor was stone, and wet, and very slippery, with a thin skin of frost on it that crackled under Fenton's buckled shoes. Corin went slowly. His stick slid. His bad knee, in the cold, had gone from stiff to something worse, a deep slow ache like a rotten tooth, all the way up into his hip. He gritted his teeth and went on.
They came round to the far side of the room. To a place where the wall of ice stopped, and there was a gap in it, a square of black, a man's height and twice as wide. And in the gap, the shaft.
Corin looked up.
He could see it in the lantern light. A square shaft going straight up into the dark, lined with wood, with iron rails running up two sides of it. And a little way up, a man's height above his head, the bottom of something. Flat. Square. Made of wood, bound with brass, with a great iron chain hanging from each corner, going up into the dark.
"The lift," said Remy. "It comes down here between lots. They load the next tray from the shelves, the porters, and it goes up through the floor into the dome, and the Weigher sells it, and it comes down again." He pointed with the lantern. "Above that, forty feet up, the brass box. Above the brass box, the floor of the dome. Above that, the lords."
Forty feet up. Corin listened.
And he heard them. Very faint, through all that wood and stone and brass. Voices. A great many voices, muffled, like the sea a long way off. And music. A fiddle and a horn, playing a slow dance. And once, sharp and clear, coming straight down the shaft as if it had been dropped, the crack of a small hammer on wood.
The Weigher.
Sold.
He thought of Lyra. Somewhere up there. Forty feet above his head, in her mother's gown and the white mask with the thread of quiet sewn into it, standing among the lords with Wren beside her. Watching the lift. Waiting for the floor to sink.
The floor had already sunk. At a quarter to midnight. He had felt it, coming down the back stair, the whole house shivering very faintly, the way a ship shivers when the anchor goes down. Door three was open. That was how he had got in. And now the lift was down here, empty, and above it the hole in the floor of the dome stood open too, with the lords crowding round the edge of it in their masks, waiting to see what came up next.
Lot 41 went up last.
"Where is it?" said Corin.
Remy did not answer.
He was looking past Corin. Past the shaft. At the wall of ice, to the left of it, a few paces along. At a place where one of the great square blocks had been cut away at the bottom, low down, near the floor, leaving a black hole no bigger than a dog's kennel. And in the hole, very faint, something white.
A mark. In chalk. On the wet stone just inside.
A circle, with a line through it.
"He's early," said Remy.
And from inside the hole, out of the black, very quietly, a voice said, "I'm not early. You're late. And Oren says it's bitter out there tonight, so I expect it's bitter in here too. I'll take his word."
And Gideon came crawling out of the ring passage on his hands and knees, with his coat white with frost and his face gray with cold and a stub of chalk still in his fingers, and stood up, and looked round at the room full of sleeping jars, and swore very softly under his breath.
