Chapter 55: The Vault Under the Dome, Part 5 of 5: Lot 41
Lot 41
The bell went on ringing.
It was not a church bell. It was small and harsh and iron, the kind of bell that hangs over a shop door, and it rang on and on, on the other side of the wall of ice, in a quick hard clatter, like somebody shaking it on a string. Corin heard it as he came round the pillar. He heard it all the way back down the narrow walk between the shelves, with his stick sliding and his knee on fire and Remy's lantern swinging ahead of him.
"The porters' bell," said Remy, over his shoulder. "The Weigher pulls a cord by the box. It rings in the drum room. It means bring her down."
"The drum room."
"Behind the ice. Where the handle is." Remy did not slow down. "Where they are."
They came out of the walk by the lift shaft.
And as they did, the iron bell stopped. And in the quiet after it, very close, on the other side of the wall of ice to the left of the shaft, Corin heard another sound.
A long, low groan of wood. And a clank. And another clank. And then a slow steady rattle, like a great chain being wound in over a drum, link by link by link.
And under it, a man humming.
Corin knew the tune.
He had heard it in his fever, in the fifth arch, with Gideon holding his hand while Silas washed the coal out of his leg. He had heard it on the floor of the crypt at night, from the place by the wall where Gideon slept. He had heard it an hour ago, Lyra told him afterward, coming up out of the hole in the floor of the dome when the floor sank. A slow, heavy, going-on tune, with no real end to it. The kind of tune a man hums when he has a load on his back and a long way to go. A carrying tune.
Big Gideon Hollow's tune. Hummed now by his younger brother, Saul. On the other side of a wall of ice two feet thick. Turning a handle.
Gideon stood by the shaft and listened to it with his hands over the two jars inside his coat.
He did not hum along. Not this time.
Above them, in the dark of the shaft, the flat brass-bound bottom of the lift moved.
It came down slowly. Very slowly, with the chain rattling over the drum behind the ice and the four iron chains at its corners swinging and knocking against the wooden walls of the shaft. A hand's width. Another. The bottom of it went past the top of the gap in the wall of ice. Then it was level with Corin's eyes. Then with his chest. And then it touched the stone floor of the cold room with a soft heavy bump, and stopped, and the rattle of the chain stopped, and the humming stopped too.
It was a platform. That was all. A square of thick wood, bound at the edges with brass, with a low brass rail round three sides of it and the fourth side open. Big enough for two men to stand on. Or for one tray.
It was empty.
"Now," said Remy.
He put the lantern down on the frost.
And he went to the wall of ice beside the shaft, to the right of it, the side away from the drum room, and knelt down, and put both his bare hands flat on one of the great gray-white blocks near the floor. Corin saw him wince as the cold bit. Then he pushed. And the block slid.
It was not ice. Not all of it. The front of it was a skin of ice, two fingers thick, frozen onto something behind. And behind the skin was a box. A wooden box, the size of a bread bin, set into the wall in the place of a block, with the ice frozen over its front so that you could not tell it from the others unless you knew. Remy slid it out a hand's width on its runners, and the skin of ice cracked across, and he put his hand in.
"I made this," he said, not looking round. "Two years ago. For keeping things. The ice men change the blocks from the inside, a few at a time, every week, and they've never once changed this one, because I always tell them I've done it already."
He took his hand out.
And in it, in the yellow light of the carter's lantern, was a case.
Corin had never seen it before.
He knew it at once.
It was black. Black wood, so dark it seemed to drink the lantern light, polished smooth and hard as stone. A little bigger than a large book. Not deep. The kind of case a man might keep a set of fine pens in, or a pair of dueling pistols, or a necklace. Its corners were bound in silver, worn thin, and at the front there was a silver clasp, shaped like a closed hand. And tucked under the fingers of the closed silver hand, so small that you could miss it, a little square of paper. Folded. And folded again. And again. Very small. Very tight. As small as a thing can be folded.
Lord Thorne folded something small, Linus Hale had said, in the crypt, on Sunday, with his blue fingers shaking on his knees. While I took it. With his free hand. On his knee, under the table, where she could not see. I did not know what it was. I thought it was a habit. A thing a man does with his hands when he is afraid.
Remy held it out.
Corin did not take it.
Not at once. He stood on the frost by the empty lift with his stick in one hand and the other hand held out, in Fenton's scorched white glove, and could not make it close.
Inside that case was the last evening of a man's life. Not the end of his life. Lord Thorne was alive. He was sitting in a reed hut on Sedge End tonight, with Moll Brask, or he was not, Corin did not know. He was alive, and he could not speak more than one word at a time, and he tapped his daughter's palm, and he had not known his own name for a year. But his last evening was here. The last whole evening he had had. Before the fire. Before Linus. Gently, Linus. If you would. The whole of it, taken out of him in one long careful pull and sealed into a black wood case that opened only to Thorne blood, and given to a woman in white. And then to Cressida. And then to here.
And whatever Lord Thorne had known on that last evening was in it. Whatever he had seen. Whatever had made a hooded man with long thin white hands want it taken out of him whole, and kept, and never sold. For sale by sealed bid. And Lyra was standing forty feet above Corin's head in her mother's gray gown, waiting to bid for it, with the Duke's money, against men in masks who would kill to have it. Or kill to make sure nobody else did.
Whoever buys it will not live to open it.
Remy's book. The last column. AFTER.
Corin took the case.
It was heavier than it looked. And it was cold. Colder than the ice it had come out of, it seemed to him. Cold right through Fenton's cotton glove and into his gray fingers, all the way up his arm. He held it against his chest. He could feel it there, against his ribs, through Fenton's coat. Next to the Ledger.
And behind his left ear, Malakor drew in a long, slow breath, and let it out, and did not say anything at all.
"Now the other one," said Remy.
He put his hand back into the box in the wall, and took out a second case.
It was black. Black wood, polished. A little bigger than a large book. With silver corners, and a silver clasp shaped like a closed hand. And under the fingers of the silver hand, a little square of folded paper.
Corin looked from one to the other.
He could not see any difference between them. None. Not in the lantern light, not in the cold. The same wood. The same silver. The same worn corners. The same small tight square of folded paper.
"Old Rook made it," said Remy. "The cabinetmaker on Vellum Row. The one who does the Lady's boxes. I took him the real one to look at, on Wednesday night, an hour, while the Lady slept. He made this in four days. He didn't ask why. He wouldn't take any money." He turned it over in his hands. "The wood's from the same tree. He said he still had a board of it, from when he made the first one. Thirty years ago."
Thirty years ago.
Corin opened his mouth. And shut it. Not now.
"What's in it?" said Gideon.
"Nothing." Remy turned the second case so the lantern caught the clasp. "It won't open. Not to anybody. There's no clasp, really, under the silver. Just a pin, set in solid. You'd need an axe." He almost smiled. "I wanted to put something in it. Something rude. A jar of me telling them all what I think of them. But Hollis said no. Hollis said if it was empty they might spend a week trying to open it before they knew it was empty. And a week is a long time."
He stepped onto the platform of the lift.
There was a tray on a hook on the brass rail, flat and wide, lined with plum-colored velvet. He took it down, and set it in the middle of the platform, and put the second case on it, in the center, square, the way the porters set out every lot. He straightened the folded square of paper under the silver fingers with the tip of one finger, very carefully. He stood back and looked at it.
And then he took a small white card from his pocket, and tucked it under the edge of the case.
Corin could read it from where he stood.
LOT 41. A Lord's Last Evening. Taken whole, before the fire. C. T., by proxy. Sealed bid.
"There," said Remy.
He stepped off the platform.
He picked up the lantern. He went to the wall of ice to the left of the shaft, the drum-room side, and put his mouth close to a small round hole in it, low down, that Corin had not seen before. A speaking hole, lined with a little copper pipe.
"Up," he said into it.
On the other side of the ice, there was a pause.
And then a deep voice. Very slow. Very flat. A voice like a stone being rolled across a floor.
"Up."
Just that. Just the one word, said back, the way a child says back a word it has been told without knowing what it means.
Gideon's face did not change. But his hand closed, as far as it would, over the front of his coat.
The chain began to rattle over the drum.
The humming began again.
And the lift went up.
Slowly. With the four iron chains at its corners swinging and knocking. Up past Corin's chest. Up past his eyes. Up into the dark of the shaft, with the second black case sitting square and small and alone in the middle of its velvet tray, its silver clasp shining for one last moment in the lantern light. And then only the flat brass-bound bottom of the platform, going up. And then only the dark.
Forty feet above them, Corin heard the voices change.
They had been loud. Laughing. Talking. A crowd's noise, like the sea. Now they went quiet, all at once, the way a crowd goes quiet when the curtain goes up. A great held breath, coming down the shaft. And in it, the fiddle stopped. And the horn stopped. And then one voice. High and thin and very clear, coming straight down the shaft as if it stood at the top of it.
The Weigher.
"My lords. My ladies. The last lot of the evening. The last lot of the Fourth Tier. Lot forty-one."
A pause.
"A Lord's Last Evening. Taken whole. Before the fire."
And then a sound Corin had not expected.
Not a gasp. Not a murmur. Something lower than that. A kind of shifting. A long soft rustle of silk and velvet and feathers, all at once, forty feet up, the way a field of tall grass rustles when the wind goes over it. As if every person in the dome had leaned forward at the same moment to look.
And somewhere in that rustle, Corin knew, were a black half-mask and black gloves. And a woman in a white mask with a thread of quiet in it, at the very edge of the hole, counting. And a man in gray, at the edge opposite her, who had smiled.
"Sealed bids," said the Weigher. "In the box. My lady will open it at the bell."
Remy turned to Corin.
In the yellow light, his sharp face was quite white. But his eyes were very steady.
"Now you go," he said. "Now, before they open the box, and somebody opens the case, and finds they can't. East. The ring passage. Past the drum room. Gideon knows the way."
"And you?"
"I go back up the glass. The way I came. Up the back stair, to her." He picked up the lantern. "And when she goes up to bed tonight, I'll be in the room. On my little bed by the door. Like always." He looked at Corin. "And so will you, if you can."
"If I can."
"You promised."
"I promised."
Remy nodded once.
Then he turned and went away across the frost toward the dark mouth of the glass passage, quick and light, with the carter's lantern swinging. And the cold room went dark behind him, all but the faint slow glow of the sleeping jars on their shelves. And Corin stood in the dark with the real Lot 41 held against his chest, cold as death, and Gideon's hand found his sleeve.
"This way," said Gideon. "Mind your feet. There's a step."
The Brothers on the Stair
The ring passage east was not like the ring passage Gideon had crawled through.
That had been an old drain, he said. Brick, half full of black ice, so low you lay on your belly. This was the porters' way. It ran round the outside of the cold room, behind the wall of ice, in a great slow curve, the way the rim runs round a wheel. It was tall enough for a big man to walk upright, and wide enough for two big men to walk side by side with a crate between them. Its floor was flagstones, worn into shallow dips down the middle by a hundred years of bare feet. Its walls were bare stone, wet and black. And every thirty paces, high on the wall, in an iron bracket, there was an oil lamp, burning low and yellow, so that you went from dark to dim light to dark again, over and over, like walking through the stripes on a cat.
And low on the wall, at the height of a man's knee, every ten paces, there was a mark in blue chalk.
A circle on its own.
Straight on.
"I did those on Sunday," said Gideon. "In case. You never know who's going to be carrying who."
He went ahead. Corin followed, as fast as he could, which was not fast. The case was against his chest under Fenton's coat, cold as a stone from the bottom of a well, and he held it there with his left arm and leaned on the stick with his right, and every step on the bad leg was a long white flash of pain from the knee to the hip. He stopped thinking about it. He thought about the chalk circles instead. One. Ten paces. Two. Ten paces. Three.
The drum room was at the fourth.
It opened off the inside of the curve. Corin saw the light from it first, warmer and yellower than the lamps. Then the doorway, wide, with no door. And then, as they came level with it, the room.
It was small and square and hot. There was a brazier in the corner, glowing red, the first warmth Corin had felt since the kitchen gate. And in the middle of the room, taking up almost all of it, the drum. A great wooden drum as tall as a man, on its side, on an iron axle, with the lift chain wound round and round it, link on link, thick as a wrist. And from each end of the axle, a long iron handle, worn bright where hands had held it.
Nobody was holding them.
The room was empty.
There was a bench along the wall, under the speaking pipe. Bare wood. With two hollows worn in it, side by side, the shape of two big men who had sat there a very long time. And on the floor under the bench, two pairs of big bare footprints in the coal dust, very clear.
Gideon looked at the empty bench.
"They've gone up," he said.
"Up where?"
Gideon did not answer. He turned and went on along the passage, faster.
The fifth circle. The sixth. And then the passage stopped curving, and went straight, and narrowed, and Corin saw ahead of them, at the very edge of the next pool of lamplight, the foot of a stair.
Stone steps. Steep. Going up into the dark between two walls, so narrow that a big man would brush both walls with his shoulders. Corin knew this stair. He had come down it on the first night, two weeks ago, behind Gideon, with Vespera's paint running down his wrists. Eight steps, then a turn, then five steps, then the low iron door where Coker sat on his stool. And beyond Coker's door, the black coal cellar, and the iron flap, and the chute, and Cinder Lane, and the cold clean air, and Oren.
The Hollow brothers were standing on it.
Corin saw their feet first. In the lamplight, on the third step and the fourth. Big bare feet, gray, with the toenails thick and yellow and the skin cracked like old leather. Then their legs, in canvas trousers rolled to the knee. Then the leather aprons. Then the rest of them, going up into the dark of the stair, huge, filling it from wall to wall, one behind the other, so that there was no way past. No way at all.
Big Gideon was in front. On the third step. Corin knew him by the scar on his cheek, a white hook, from the corner of the eye to the corner of the mouth, and the nose that had been broken and set crooked a long time ago. Saul was behind him, on the fourth, a step higher, so that his head was above his brother's. No scar. A rounder face. The same eyes.
The same eyes. Pale and flat and quite empty. Like windows in a house where nobody has lived for a long time.
They did not move. They stood on the stair with their great gray hands hanging at their sides and looked down at Corin, and at Gideon, and did not seem to see either of them.
"Stair's shut," said Big Gideon.
The stone voice. Rolled across a floor.
"Stair's shut till the bell. Nobody up. Nobody down. Till the bell."
It was not a threat. There was nothing in it. It was the voice of a man saying a thing he had been told to say, a long time ago, and had said every Midwinter since, in the same words, and would go on saying until somebody told him to stop. Stair's shut till the bell. The way a clock says the hour.
Corin looked past them, up into the dark.
Forty steps, perhaps. Fifty. To Cinder Lane. And between him and the first of them, two men each as big as a door, who had once carried crates of the Lady's jars up and down this stair for a shilling a night, and had been emptied for it, a jar at a time, over years, until there was nothing left in them but the stair and the drum and the bench and the tune.
And the rules.
Nobody up. Nobody down. Till the bell.
The bell was the one Vivienne would ring, upstairs, at midnight. When she opened the brass box and read the sealed bids and named the winner of Lot 41. And somebody went forward to take a black wood case off a velvet tray. And tried to open it.
How long? Ten minutes? Five?
Corin had his hand on the stick. He had the case against his chest. He had a dead harvester's dark behind his left ear and a half-empty head, and a leg that would not bend, and nothing else. He could not fight them. Nobody could fight them. He could burn. He could reach into himself and find something and set fire to it and push. He had done it before. In the wet house. On the barge. But what would he burn? And what would it do to two men who had nothing left in them to push against?
And they were Gideon's.
Gideon had not moved.
He was standing at the foot of the stair, a step in front of Corin, in the yellow lamplight, with his white hands over the two jars inside his coat. Looking up at the two huge gray men on the stair. At his father. At his uncle.
He was thirteen. Small for it. He came up to Big Gideon's belt.
And then he did a thing Corin had not expected.
He began to hum.
Not loudly. Very softly. Under his breath, the way he hummed in his sleep on the floor of the crypt. The carrying tune. Slow and heavy and going-on, with no end to it. The tune a man hums when he has a load on his back and a long way to go.
Saul's head moved.
Just a little. Just the way a dog's head moves when it hears its name from a long way off. His pale empty eyes came down from the dark above and found the small thin boy at the foot of the stair.
And he hummed too.
The same tune. His voice deep and slow under Gideon's thin one, like a river under a reed. And for a moment the two of them hummed it together, in the cold stone stairway under the Memory Bourse, the boy and the man, the tune going on and on with no end to it, and Corin stood behind them and could not breathe.
Big Gideon did not hum.
He never had. Couldn't hold a note, Coker had said. Saul could.
But he looked down.
His pale eyes came down off the dark too. Slowly. And found Gideon. And stayed there. And his face did not change, not at all, and nothing came into his eyes, nothing, they stayed as empty as before. But his great gray right hand lifted a little way from his side. As if it were going to do something it had done a long time ago. Before. And had forgotten what.
And Big Gideon Hollow said, in his slow flat stone voice:
"Not the boy."
Corin's heart stopped.
Not the boy. Take it out of me.
Remy's book. The fourth column. What Big Gideon had said, coming up on the lift into the dome nine years ago, when the Lady had sent for his son. The last thing he had said with anything left in him to say it. Not the boy. Take it out of me. And she had. All of it. And then she had taken the boy anyway, for four more winters, and Big Gideon had stood at the drum and turned the handle and never known.
"Not the boy," said Big Gideon again.
The words had nothing in them. Nothing at all. They were just words, the way stair's shut was just words. A thing he had said once, so hard and so deep, that it had worn a groove in him. And the groove was still there. After everything else was gone.
And he stepped aside.
He did it slowly. Like a great stone door swinging on old hinges. He turned his huge gray body sideways on the narrow stair and pressed his back to the wet wall, as flat as a man that size could press, and stood there. And above him, on the fourth step, still humming, Saul did the same.
There was a gap. Narrow. Between their great gray bodies and the other wall. Just wide enough for a thin boy. Just wide enough, perhaps, for a thin man with a stick, if he turned sideways and held his breath.
Gideon stopped humming.
He stood at the foot of the stair and looked at his father for a long moment. At the scar. At the crooked nose. At the great gray hand, still lifted a little way from his side, doing nothing.
Then he reached out, with his own white curled hand that would not bend, and touched it.
Just touched it. The back of it. The way you touch a wall in the dark to know it's there.
"I'll come back for you," he said. "Both of you. I'll find out how. Juno says somebody should."
Big Gideon's hand did not close. His eyes did not change.
Gideon took his hand away and went up the stair, sideways, through the gap.
Corin followed him.
He went up past the two Hollow brothers with his back to the far wall and the case held against his chest and the stick rattling on the stone, so close to them that he could smell them. Coal dust and old sweat and something else, something faint and sweet and dry, like a room where apples have been stored too long. He did not look at their faces. He could not have. He looked at the step in front of him, and the next, and the next. Third step. Fourth. And then he was past them, and above them, on the fifth step, and the sixth, and the stair was dark ahead of him and empty, and below him, behind him, Saul was still humming. Softly. On and on. With no end to it.
The seventh step. The eighth. The turn.
And somewhere above them, far off, muffled through stone and wood and glass, Corin heard a bell. A big bell. Deep and slow. Ringing once.
Midnight.
The bell. Vivienne's bell. In the dome, forty feet over the cold room, she was opening the brass box.
Five steps more. Coker's low iron door, standing open, his stool empty, a pot of beer cold on the floor beside it. The black coal cellar, and the smell of coal, and the dark. And then the square of the chute, faint gray above them. And at the top of it, at the iron flap, a big dark shape against the night sky, leaning down, with one hand held out. A big square hand, with a sword belt round the wrist.
Oren.
"Gideon first," said Corin. "Then the case. Then me."
Gideon went up the chute on his elbows and knees, with Oren's hand round his collar. Corin pulled the black case out of Fenton's coat and held it up, as high as he could reach, and felt Oren take it out of his fingers. Then the hand came down again, for him.
He put his gloved hand in it.
And from far above them, from the top of the great glass dome of the Memory Bourse, high over Cinder Lane and Exchange Street and all the sleeping roofs of Veridia, thin and sharp and clear on the frozen air, a whistle blew.
Short.
Short.
Short.
Juno.
Run.
And under Corin's feet, under the coal and the stone and the cold, the whole of the Memory Bourse shook, once, as if something very heavy had been dropped on its roof. And from Exchange Street, on the other side of the building, there came a sound he knew from his years at the Ministry. The sound of a great many men in heavy boots, all running at once. And a voice, a calm and quiet voice that carried over all of it, the way a voice carries when it has never once in its life needed to shout.
"In the name of the Ministry," said Silas Kross. "Nobody leaves."
Oren pulled.
Corin came up out of the dark.
