Chapter 49: Lilies, Part 1 of 5: The Grays
The Grays
The carriage was waiting at the bottom of Comb Street, where the steps ran out into the cobbles of the lane behind the Corn Exchange, under the one gas lamp.
Lyra saw the horses first. Two of them. Gray. Dappled, the way the sea is dappled under a cloudy sky, with long pale manes combed out and tied with ribbon, and blankets over their backs against the cold, and their breath going up in white clouds in the lamplight. They stood very still in the falling snow with their heads down, the way good horses stand when they have been told to wait and know they will be fetched.
And beside them, at their heads, a small bent old man in a long caped coat and a tall hat with snow on its brim, holding both their bridles in one gloved hand and stamping his feet.
Tibbs.
He saw her coming down the last of the steps, in her coat with the gray silk showing under it, between the big man with his arm in a black sling and the boy with his arm in a white one. He saw her face. And he did a thing she had seen him do a thousand times, in the stable yard at Crown Walk, when she was small and came running out to see the horses. He took off his hat.
He held it in his free hand, against his chest, in the snow. His bald head was pink with cold. He did not say anything.
Lyra stopped in front of him.
"Tibbs," she said.
"Miss."
That was all. He had never said much. He had been her father's groom for thirty years, and in thirty years she did not think she had heard him say more than a dozen words at a time to anybody but a horse. He looked at her, with his hat against his chest, and his old pale eyes went over her once, quickly, the way he would look at a horse that had come back from a long hard ride, to see where it was hurt.
They stopped on her hair.
Something moved in his face.
Then he looked past her. At Oren.
The two old men looked at each other in the snow by the carriage. The captain of the Thorne guard and the Thorne groom. Twenty-two years they had worked in the same yard, the same house. Lyra had seen them stand side by side at the gate of Crown Walk on a thousand mornings, waiting for the carriage to come round, and never say a word to each other. They did not say a word now. Tibbs looked at Oren's sling. At the brown stain down the front of Terrence's coat. At his face.
And then, very slightly, he nodded. Once. The way one soldier nods to another across a field.
Oren nodded back.
"His Grace is inside, miss," said Tibbs. "Waiting. He came himself. He doesn't, as a rule." He put his hat back on. "Said he wanted to see you before the lilies did."
He opened the carriage door.
It was a big carriage. Bigger than any Lyra had ridden in since the auction. Dark blue, nearly black, with the Marrow arms painted small on the door in gold, a ship and a key, and the lamps lit on either side of the box. And the inside was dark, and warm, and smelled of leather and woodsmoke and something sweet and heavy, like a cake.
She turned round.
Oren was standing one pace behind her and one to the left, in the snow, the way he always stood.
"You'll go back," she said. "To the crypt. Both of you. Now."
"I'll wait."
"Oren—"
"I'll wait here." He did not move. "At the bottom of the steps. Under the lamp. Till you come back."
"It'll be midnight. Later."
"Then I'll wait till midnight." His face did not change. "I can't read the clock on the Corn Exchange. But I can count the bells."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment, standing there in the snow in another man's coat with his arm in a sling.
Then she looked at Gideon.
"Take him home," she said. "If he's still here at the tenth bell. Make him."
Gideon looked up at Oren. Oren was a head and a half taller than him, and three times as wide. Gideon looked at him the way he looked at a crate, working out how heavy it would be.
"All right," he said.
Tibbs held out his hand to help her up.
She took it. And as she put her foot on the step, she heard Oren, behind her, say something. Not to her. Low. To himself, she thought. Under his breath, in the snow.
She did not catch it all. Only the end of it.
"...a gate with a belly. A tent."
Then she was inside, and the door shut behind her, and the dark warm smell of leather and cake closed round her.
And a voice in the dark, soft and slow and sleepy, like a great cat purring in a warm room, said:
"Good evening, Lady Lyra. I'm so glad you came. Do sit down. Mind my feet. They take up more of the carriage than I'd like."
His Grace
He filled the whole of one side of the carriage, the way he had filled the gold chair in his drawing room. A great soft dark shape, in a coat of dark blue velvet that drank the faint light from the lamps outside, with a rug of gray fur over his knees and his feet, in soft gray boots, stretched out across the floor of the carriage so that Lyra had to step over them to reach the other seat.
She sat down. The seat was soft, and deep, and warm, as if a stone had been heated and put under the cushion. Perhaps one had.
The carriage moved. Gently. The two gray horses leaned into their collars, and the wheels turned in the snow with a soft hiss, and the lamp at the bottom of Comb Street slid past the window, and with it, for a moment, a big man in a too-short coat with his arm in a sling, standing under it, watching. Then he was gone.
The Duke of Marrow did not say anything for a while.
Lyra could not see his face. Only the shape of it, large and pale, above the white fall of lace at his throat. And his hands, folded on the fur rug over his belly. Large and white and soft, with dimples where the knuckles should have been. And a ring on every finger, catching the light from outside as the carriage passed under the lamps, one after another. Gold. Gold. A square blue stone. Gold. And on the little finger of his left hand, a plain band of silver, with nothing in it at all.
"You're thinner," he said at last. "Than on Saturday."
"Yes."
"And your hair." A pause. "I heard about your hair. Comb Street. Fothergill had it in his window. The whole Hill went past it to look." The soft sleepy voice did not change. "And then on Thursday it wasn't in his window any more. And on Thursday evening Agnes Hesper was dead."
Lyra did not move.
"I had my gloves from her," said the Duke. "For twenty-two years. She knew the size of my hands better than my valet. She used to say I had the hands of a baker and the taste of a bishop." The rings caught the light. "I heard at six. My steward came up to my dressing room and told me, while I was choosing a waistcoat. Madame Hesper's shop on Velvet Street, Your Grace. Broken into. Robbers, they say. The old woman held her back door against them with her shears." He was quiet. "I put on the gray. It seemed right."
The carriage turned a corner. Lyra felt it lean, and right itself.
"Robbers," she said.
"That is what the Watch says." The Duke's head turned, a little, in the dark, toward her. "The Watch also says there was a girl in the shop. In gray. Who went out over the roofs. And a big man with a sword, who did something to the window that nobody can explain, so that it is full of a kind of gold dust that will not sweep up. The Watch is very puzzled by the gold dust." A pause. "I was not. I have seen gold dust like that once before. A long time ago. In a field."
Lyra looked at him.
She could see his eyes now. As they passed under another lamp. Pale, gray-green, heavy-lidded, the color of the sea under fog. Sleepy. Half closed. Looking at her through the dark of the carriage the way a cat looks at a mouse it has decided not to eat.
"Your aunt will have heard too," he said. "Everyone on the Hill has heard. They'll talk about nothing else tonight. Agnes Hesper dressed half of them." He folded his ringed hands the other way on the fur. "And they will all look at you. In your gray. And wonder."
"I know."
"Do you want to go back?"
Lyra thought about it.
She thought about it honestly, the way she had promised herself she would think about things now, since the tower. She thought about a white hall full of lilies, and a staircase wound with them, and a tall woman in white with a red mouth coming down it with her hands held out. Oh, darling. She thought about the lily paper, folded small in the pocket in the left side of her waist, against her ribs, where Madame Hesper had sewn it.
"No," she said.
The Duke was quiet for a moment.
"Good," he said. "Then I'll tell you three things, and then I'll go to sleep until we get there, because I always go to sleep in carriages, and I find it saves a great deal of talking."
Lyra waited.
"The first," said the Duke, "is that your aunt will be very kind to you. In front of everybody. She will hold your hands and cry a little and call you darling, and the whole Hill will see her do it, and that is why she will do it. And then, at about the tenth bell, she will take you upstairs to the white room, alone, for tea." A pause. "Go. You'll want to. And when you get there, do not drink from the cups."
"The Marrowmere cups."
"You've seen them."
"In the autumn. She told me they were my mother's."
"They were." The soft voice did not change. "They came with your mother from Marrowmere, in a box lined with blue velvet. Twelve of them. Your mother never used them. Your aunt uses them for special guests." The rings caught the light. "A cup that has a mask in the bottom, my dear, is a cup that is watching you while you drink. Remember that."
Lyra did not say anything.
"The second," said the Duke, "is that there will be a man there tonight that you won't expect. In gray. Quiet. Standing near the fire, eating very little and listening a great deal." He paused. "He comes every year. Your aunt invites him every year. He is the only guest on the Hill she is afraid of, and she has never once failed to ask him."
Lyra's hands went cold in Madame Hesper's wedding gloves.
"Kross," she said.
"I didn't say a name."
"You didn't need to."
"No." The Duke's eyes, half closed, rested on her. "He won't touch you. Not at your aunt's. Not on my arm, in front of the whole Hill, on the Friday before Midwinter. He's far too careful for that." A pause. "He'll only watch. And perhaps tap a little, on the side of his glass, while he listens to the music. He likes the music at your aunt's. It's always very good."
The carriage began to climb.
Lyra felt it. The horses leaning harder into their collars. The wheels slowing. The Hill. They were going up the Hill, out of the Lower Docks and the snow-choked lanes and the smell of the river, up into the wide clean lamplit streets of the High Quarter.
"And the third?" she said.
The Duke did not answer at once.
He turned his large pale head and looked out of the window. At the houses going past, taller and whiter and grander with every street. At the gas lamps, city-paid, in their neat iron rows.
"The third," he said slowly, "is that she will ask you about the harvester. Your Mister Vane. She will ask you very nicely, as if it were nothing, as if she were asking after a dog you used to have. And whatever you say to that, Lady Lyra, she will believe the opposite." He turned back. "So decide before you get there what you want her to believe."
Then he closed his eyes.
And as far as Lyra could tell, all the rest of the way up the Hill, with his ringed hands folded on the fur and his great soft head nodding a little with the carriage, the Duke of Marrow slept.
Crown Walk
She knew when they turned into it. She did not need to look.
She knew it by the sound of the wheels. On every other street on the Hill the wheels went over cobbles, a hard quick rattle, even under the snow. On Crown Walk they went quiet. Because Crown Walk was not cobbled. It was laid with long smooth slabs of pale stone, cut to fit, the way the floors of churches are laid, so that the carriages of the great houses could come and go at night without waking anyone. Her grandfather had paid for a third of it. She had been told that so many times as a child that she could hear her governess saying it now, under the soft hiss of the wheels. Your grandfather paid for this street, Lyra. Walk on it as if you know.
She looked.
Crown Walk curved away ahead of the carriage in a long shallow arc, like the edge of a plate, along the very top of the Hill. Tall white houses on one side, all joined together in a single sweeping row, five floors each, with tall windows and iron balconies and white steps going up to white doors. On the other side, a long garden behind iron railings. Trees, bare, black, heavy with snow. A frozen fountain in the middle with a stone fish on it, its mouth open, a long icicle hanging from its lip where the water had been. And beyond the garden, beyond the railings, nothing. The edge of the Hill. And the whole of the city below, in the dark, in the snow, a great spread of small yellow lights going down and down to the black curve of the river.
The gas lamps along Crown Walk were lit. Every one. Tall and black and elegant, on fluted iron posts, with glass heads like tulips. They threw their soft white light on the snow and the steps and the white house fronts, so that the whole long curve of the street glowed, faintly, like the inside of a shell.
And halfway along, between two blazing houses, one that did not.
It was dark. Every window. From the area railings at the bottom to the little round attic windows under the roof. The shutters were closed on the inside, all of them, gray, so that the windows looked like eyes with the lids down. There was snow on the steps. Deep, untouched, smooth as a sheet, from the bottom step to the top. Nobody had swept it. Nobody had walked on it. And the white front door at the top of the steps had a pale square on it, a little higher than the middle, where something had been taken off and the paint underneath had not faded.
The knocker.
It had been a brass hand. Holding a ball. Her father had said, when she asked, that it was the hand of a Thorne holding the world, and her mother had laughed and said it was the hand of a Thorne holding an apple he had stolen, and her father had laughed too. She had been six. She had been allowed to polish it, once, on a Saturday, with Tibbs's rag and a tin of paste, standing on the top step on tiptoe.
There was a pale square on the door where it had been.
And on the edge of the top step, where the snow came up to her knees, the stone girl. Small. Gray. Kneeling, with a stone jug held out in front of her in both hands, as if she were offering somebody a drink. She had been on the step of Crown Walk since before Lyra's grandfather was born. The snow lay on her head and her shoulders and in her jug.
The carriage went past.
Lyra did not turn her head. She looked at it out of the side of her eyes, the way you look at something that you are afraid will look back. The dark house. The shut eyes. The pale square. The stone girl with her jug full of snow.
Then it was behind her.
She found she was holding her breath, and let it out, slowly, so that the Duke would not hear.
"Two years," said the Duke of Marrow.
He had not opened his eyes. He was sitting exactly as he had been, with his ringed hands folded on the fur rug and his great head back against the cushions.
"Two years this winter," he said, "since the auction. I bought it. The house, and the gallery furniture, and the mare. Your aunt bid against me for the house till the very last. She wanted it badly." A pause. "I shut it. Nobody has been in since. Not to clean. Not to air it. Not a single servant. I keep the only key myself."
Lyra looked at him.
"Why?"
The Duke did not open his eyes.
"Because I was asked to," he said. "A long time ago. By somebody who stood in my gallery one evening, eleven years ago, in a traveling cloak, and said: Ansel, if it ever comes to that, if they ever sell this house, buy it. And keep it shut. And don't let her in." The rings caught the light from a passing lamp. "I didn't ask which her. I thought I knew. I have thought, since, that I may have been wrong."
The carriage slowed.
And Lyra saw Number 11.
It was blazing. There was no other word. Every window on every floor was lit, from the area to the attic, gold and warm, so bright against the snow that it hurt her eyes. The white steps had been swept and swept again, and sanded, and covered with a long runner of white felt that went from the curb to the door. There were torches on iron stands on either side of the steps, real flames, wind-blown, throwing their orange light up the white front of the house and making the shadows of the balconies jump and dance.
There were carriages. A line of them, along the curve of Crown Walk, all the way to the corner. Black and blue and green and dark red, with crests on the doors and lamps on the boxes and horses steaming under blankets in the cold. Coachmen standing about in their caped coats, stamping, smoking. Footmen running.
And on the top step, beside the white door, very straight and very still, a footman in white.
White coat. White breeches. White stockings. White gloves. A white powdered wig. He stood with his hands at his sides and his chin up and his eyes on the air above the street, the way they stand. And on the white door behind him, at the height of a man's heart, the knocker.
Brass. In the shape of a lily.
The door was open. And out of it, down the white steps, over the white felt, past the torches, into the cold street and through the shut window of the carriage, so strong that Lyra smelled it before the carriage had stopped, came the smell.
Thick. Sweet. Heavy. White. Like a room full of flowers with the windows shut. Like a blanket soaked in honey, laid over your face.
Lilies.
The carriage stopped.
The Duke opened his eyes.
He did it all at once, the way he had shut them. One moment asleep. The next, awake, with the sleepy gray-green eyes fixed on her face in the dark.
"Breathe through your mouth," he said. "For the first quarter of an hour. After that you won't notice it." He began, slowly, to push the fur rug off his knees. "That's the worst of lilies. After a while you stop noticing. And then you can't smell anything else for a week."
