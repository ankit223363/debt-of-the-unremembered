Chapter 49: Lilies, Part 4 of 5: By Proxy
By Proxy
"And Lot Forty-one," said Lyra.
She said it before she could stop herself. Before she could think about whether it was wise. It came out of her the way her hand had gone across the tower room to Rowan's, without asking.
Cressida did not move.
"A Lord's Last Evening," said Lyra. "Taken whole, before the fire. In the Lady Vivienne's catalogue. In a black box with a silver clasp, kept cold, under her house, behind three doors." She held her aunt's gray eyes. "C. T., by proxy."
The fire ticked.
"You knew those letters," said Cressida softly.
"I've known them since I was nine. On the bottom of every card that came with every present."
"Yes." Cressida looked at her bare hands. "I always signed them like that. Your father used to tease me. He said it looked like a shopkeeper's mark." She was quiet for a moment. "I suppose it was, in the end."
"Did you sell him?"
Cressida looked up.
"Did you sell Father?" said Lyra. "Two years ago. To whoever came for him. Did you sell him, the way you're selling his last evening now, to whoever bids the most on Monday night?"
She heard her own voice go up. She did not stop it. She had stopped lying. She would not stop this either.
"Ro thinks I did it," she said. "You told him I did it. In the hall at Crown Walk, with your hand on his shoulder. Your sister has done this, darling. I've been carrying that for two years, Aunt Cressida. I'd like to know what I've been carrying. I'd like to know if it's yours."
Cressida Thorne looked at her niece for a long time.
Then she got up.
She did it slowly, beautifully, the way she did everything, the white silk pouring off the chair and down to the white carpet like cream. She stood for a moment by the fire with her bare hand on the white marble of the mantel. Then she went to the cabinet.
She opened one of its glass doors. With a small key, from a chain round her wrist. And reached inside. And took something down off the blue velvet of the fourth shelf.
She brought it back to the fire, and sat down, and held it out across the little table.
It was a card.
A small square of thick white card, very good, with a gilt edge. Like the one the Duke had sent. Like the one on the box. On it, in black ink, in a hand Lyra did not know, a neat clerk's hand, a few lines.
Received of C. T., by the hand of L. H., harvester, for consignment at the Midwinter sale: one sealed vessel, black, silver clasp, maker's mark a folded square. Contents declared: one evening, whole. To be held cold. Reserve: none. Proceeds: none. The seller asks no money and takes no part.
And under it, a seal. Plum-colored wax. A V.
Lyra read it twice.
"Proceeds: none," she said.
"None."
"You're not selling it."
"I'm giving it away," said Cressida. "To whoever wants it most. In front of the whole of the Hill. Under the dome. At midnight. With the Bank's Fourth Desk standing over it, and the Inquisitor in the gallery, and the Treasurer at a quarter to midnight in a little room he thinks nobody knows he has a key to." She took the card back, and looked at it. "I'm giving it away in public, darling, because it is the only way I could think of to make sure that whoever ends up with it cannot do so quietly."
The fire ticked.
"I don't understand," said Lyra.
"No." Cressida put the card in her lap, on the white gloves. "You wouldn't. You weren't there." She looked at the fire. "You asked me if I sold your father. I'll tell you. I didn't sell him. I wasn't brave enough to sell him, and I wasn't brave enough to save him. I did the thing in between, which is what people like me always do. I stood in the hall of Crown Walk, the night before they came, and I listened to men's voices in his study, and I did nothing. And the next night, when it was done, and he was sitting by the fire tapping, and the harvester came out of the study with a black box under his arm, I held out my hand and said, Give it to me."
"Linus Hale."
Cressida's eyes came back from the fire.
"You know his name."
"I know a great many names now."
"Yes," said Cressida slowly. "I see you do." She looked at Lyra a moment longer. "Linus Hale. With his blue fingers and his books. He took your father's last evening out of him whole, at somebody's order, and sealed it, and came out of the study with it. And I held out my hand. And he looked at me for a long time, and then he gave it to me. I don't know why. I've never known why." She turned the card over in her bare fingers. "I kept it for two years, darling. In the cold. In the cellar of this house, under the wine, in a box packed with ice that my steward changed every morning without knowing what it was for. And every night of those two years I sat in this room, in this chair, and thought about going down to the cellar and opening it."
"Why didn't you?"
"Because I was afraid of what was in it."
The fire ticked.
"And then this winter," said Cressida, "three men came to see me. Separately. In this room. In that chair." She nodded at the chair Lyra was sitting in. "Each of them very charming. Each of them very kind. Each of them asking, in the nicest possible way, whether I had anything of your father's that I might like to part with." She smiled her thin small smile. "One of them tapped on the arm of the chair while he asked. One of them looked at his watch and didn't. And one of them sent his card up from the hall and never came up at all, because he never comes up anywhere, he only sends."
Lyra sat very still in the chair.
Kross. Sterling. And the third.
"The third," she said.
"The third sends," said Cressida. "The third has a dome on his ring, darling, and everybody on the Hill knows who wears that ring, and nobody can remember that they know." She folded the card in half. "After the third card came up from the hall, I knew I couldn't keep it any longer. So I sent for Linus Hale again. And I gave it back to him. And asked him to carry it to the one house in this city where nobody could take it quietly. The Lady's. Because the Lady sells everything, in public, to the highest bidder, under the dome, in front of everybody. And because the Bank would put a clause on it. And because whoever wins it on Monday will have to stand up in front of the whole of the Hill and be seen to have wanted it."
She looked at Lyra.
"That was my plan," she said. "It isn't brave. It isn't pretty. It's a sealed bid and a public room and a woman too frightened to open her own brother's box." Her red mouth moved. "I had not, I'm afraid, counted on you."
The Twelfth Cup
The fire had burned down. Cressida did not ring for anyone to mend it. She sat with the folded card in her lap and looked at the red coals, and Lyra sat in the other white chair and looked at the cabinet.
She could not help it. She had been not looking at it since she came in. Now she looked.
It stood against the far wall beside the fire, tall and pale, white and gilt, with its two glass doors. One of them still a little open, where her aunt had taken the card from the fourth shelf. Seven shelves behind the glass, lined with deep blue velvet, the blue of the sky just after the sun has gone. And on the velvet, laid out in rows with space round each one, the way jewelers lay out rings, the things.
She knew some of them. Corin had written them in the Ledger, in the autumn, on the night he came here in a green footman's coat and stood in front of this cabinet with his gloved hand lifted toward the catch. A silver thimble. A child's coral teething ring. A lock of fair hair, tied with thread. A small key. A glass vial, very small, with something faint and gold moving inside it.
And on the third shelf, on its own square of velvet, the white shell mask. Curved, polished, glowing faintly pink where the firelight came through it. With tiny white pearls round the eye holes, sewn in a pattern like the curl of a wave.
And beside it, the silver cup. Plain and heavy, like a big egg cup, with a wide shallow bowl. And inside the bowl, carved round and round toward the center, a spiral.
Lyra looked at the spiral for a long time.
Then she looked at the shelf below. The second shelf. Because there was something on it she did not remember from the Ledger.
A box.
Long and flat, of dark polished wood, with brass corners. Its lid was open, folded back against the blue velvet of the shelf behind it. And inside, the lining was blue velvet too, the same deep blue, but older, worn pale in places. Pressed into the velvet, in two rows of six, twelve hollows. Round. Each the size of the bottom of a small cup.
Ten of the hollows were empty. The cups that belonged in them were here, she understood. On the tray. In the kitchen. In the long room downstairs, perhaps, for special guests.
One of the hollows was full. A cup sat in it. Small, thin, white, with a band of silver round its rim. Upside down. Its mask hidden.
And one of the hollows, the last, at the end of the second row, was empty in a different way.
The velvet in it was not worn. It was not pale. It was dark, and deep, and new-looking, the way the paint on the front door of Crown Walk was new-looking in a square where the brass hand had been. As if nothing had ever sat in that hollow at all. Or as if something had sat in it for a very short time, a long time ago, and then been taken out, and never put back, so that the velvet had never had a chance to wear.
"There are eleven," said Lyra.
Cressida did not look up from the fire.
"You told me in the autumn there were twelve," said Lyra. "In a box lined with blue velvet. That she brought them all the way from the water." She looked at the dark new hollow at the end of the row. "There are eleven. And one place where there never was a cup. Or not for long."
Cressida was quiet for a long time.
Then she said, without turning her head: "You count like she did."
Lyra waited.
"She came to Crown Walk with twelve," said Cressida slowly. "In that box. I saw her unpack them, the day after the wedding, in the long gallery. She took each one out and held it up to the window and looked at the mask in the bottom and put it back. Twelve. I counted, too. I was very young, and very jealous, and I counted everything she had." She turned the folded card over in her bare fingers. "And for years and years they sat in that box in a cupboard in the gallery and she never used them. And then one night, the night before she went to the coast, she came to my room. Late. In her traveling cloak, already. And she had one of the cups in her hand."
The coals ticked.
"She held it out to me," said Cressida. "And she said, Cressida, I'm taking one. Just one. If anyone ever asks, there were always eleven. And I said, Why? And she said—"
She stopped.
"What did she say?"
Cressida looked at the fire.
"She said, Because where I'm going, I'll need one for a special guest." Her red mouth moved. "And she laughed. The way she always laughed, as if the joke was on all of us and she was sorry for it. And she put the cup inside her cloak, against her chest. And went out."
She put the folded card down on the white lace cloth beside the teapot.
"When the box came to me," she said, "after the auction, the twelfth hollow was empty, and the velvet in it was new. The way it is now. As if a cup had only sat there for a day. I've never known what that meant. I've never known where she meant to go that she would need a cup with a mask in the bottom of it, for a special guest." She looked at Lyra at last. "Have you?"
Lyra thought of a carriage going out of the east gate in the gray early morning, past the old customs house, toward the salt flats. And a long dark shape over the flats, low in the sky, like a fish, with a light under it.
"No," she said.
It was the first lie she had told since the tower.
She felt it go out of her, small and hard and cold, like a pebble dropped down a well. And she watched her aunt's gray eyes rest on her face, for a long moment, and believe it. Or not believe it. She could not tell.
Whatever you say, she will believe the opposite.
Cressida looked away.
"No," she agreed softly. "Neither have I."
The Lily Paper
Lyra put her gloved hand to her waist.
To the left side. To the seam. She felt for the little pocket that Madame Hesper had sewn there on Saturday, and that Mrs. Doyle had sewn round this afternoon, and slid two fingers into it, and took out the paper.
She did it slowly, in front of her aunt, so that her aunt could see every movement. Like Wren, Corin had told her once. Wren in her attic, getting up to fetch a book, moving slowly so that he would see she was not reaching for a knife.
She unfolded it on her knee.
Thick cream paper, soft from a month of folding and unfolding, creased, smudged with soot, one corner scorched brown. And the smell. Coming up off it into the warm white room, where it was already thick with the same smell, so that for a moment it was like pouring water into the sea.
She held it out across the little table.
Cressida looked at it.
She did not take it at once. She looked at it in Lyra's gloved hand, over the silver teapot and the two thin cups with the masks in their bottoms, and her long pale face did not move. But her bare right hand, in her lap, on the white gloves, closed. Slowly. Into a fist.
Then she opened it, and reached out, and took the paper.
She read it. Lyra watched her read it. Watched the gray eyes go along the clerk's lines, one after another, down the page, the way Corin's had gone along them on the barge, and Oren's, every afternoon, by the stove. I, Aurelian Thorne, being of sound mind. All the way down. To the end.
She stopped at the end.
She read the last two lines again. Lyra saw her do it.
I remember that I have two children only. A son and a daughter. I remember there were never any others.
Then Cressida Thorne lifted the cream paper toward her face, a little, and breathed in.
She breathed in her own lilies, off a piece of paper that had been through the sewers and the Saltmarsh and a barge full of hay and a crypt full of children, in the inside pocket of a dead man's—a live man's—coat. Lyra watched her do it. And watched something come into her face that had not been there before, all evening. Not sorrow. Not fondness. Not the tired stillness of the truth.
Fear.
Plain and small and sharp, like the fear on the face of a child who has broken something and hears footsteps on the stair.
"Where did you get this?" said Cressida.
Her voice had changed. It was not soft any more. It was quick, and low, and very quiet.
"From a drawer," said Lyra. "In the autumn. A drawer that was never supposed to be opened by anybody. Corin opened it." She held her aunt's eyes. "It was in there. With Father's signature on it. And your scent on it. And a line at the bottom that he never wrote."
Cressida looked at the paper.
"He signed it," she said. "I saw him sign it. I stood behind his chair."
"I know he signed it. I know his hand. That's his name." Lyra did not look away. "But the rest is a clerk's. And the clerk wrote what he was told. And somebody told him to write there were never any others." She leaned forward. "Why would anybody need to write that, Aunt Cressida? Why would anybody make a man remember that there was never something? Unless there was?"
The fire ticked.
Cressida did not answer.
She sat in her white velvet chair with the cream paper in her bare hands, and for the first time since Lyra had known her, all her life, since the end of a long table at Midwinter with a face like thunder because there were no more plums, Cressida Thorne looked as if she did not know what to say.
"There were two chairs at the nursery table," said Lyra. "At Crown Walk. When I was small. Mine and Ro's. And a third one, pushed back against the wall, with a cup on it that nobody used. Scratched. I thought it was for a doll." She swallowed. "There's a height chart on the inside of the nursery cupboard door. Ro's marks and mine. And another one. Older. Measured from two to six, while I was a baby. With the name gouged out."
Cressida's hands, on the paper, had gone white.
"Who was she?" said Lyra.
She did not know why she said she. She had not known she was going to. It came out of her the way a word comes out of you in your sleep, from somewhere under everything, before you can stop it.
Cressida looked at her.
And Lyra saw her decide something. The way Corin had said he saw people decide things. Standing at the top of a stair, deciding whether to go down.
"No," said Cressida Thorne.
She folded the cream paper. Small. Along its old creases. Her bare fingers did not shake.
"No," she said again. "Not that. Not tonight. Not in this house." She held the folded paper out across the table. "I've told you about the nurse, darling, and I've told you about the box, and I've told you more than I've told anyone in two years, and I'll tell you nothing about that. Because that is the one thing in this whole city that a man with a dome on his ring is still afraid of. And as long as nobody says it out loud, he stays afraid, and she stays—" She stopped.
"She stays what?"
Cressida held the paper out.
"Take it," she said. "Put it back in Agnes's pocket. And don't show it to anyone else on the Hill. Not Ansel. Not Dorian. Not anyone." Her gray eyes held Lyra's. "And if you love your brother, darling, don't show it to him either."
Lyra took the paper.
She put it back in the pocket at her waist. Her hands were shaking now. She did not try to stop them.
"You said she," said Lyra.
"You said it first."
"You said she stays."
Cressida picked up her cup, and looked down into it, at the little silver mask looking up at her through the cold tea.
"Did I," she said. "How careless." And drank.
The Hatpin
Cressida put down her cup and stood up.
That was the end of tea. Lyra knew it the way you know, at a dance, when the music is about to stop. Her aunt stood up out of the white velvet chair with the white silk pouring down off her, and took up her long white gloves from the chair, and began, slowly, to put them on. One finger at a time. The way the woman in the gray apron had put hers on, on the stair at Madame Hesper's, Oren said. The way a lady takes off her glove at a ball, and puts it on again after.
She stood by the fire to do it. In the light. With her back to the little white grate and her face turned down to her own hands.
And Lyra, sitting in the other chair, looked up at her.
At the crown of old-gold hair, piled high on the back of her head. At the long silver pins pushed through it at angles, holding it, with their heads standing out of it like the heads of flowers. At the beads on the ends of them. Small. Round. White glass. Milky, like pearls, but brighter. Catching the firelight as her aunt bent her head over her gloves, and flashing, and going dark.
She counted them.
She could not help it. She counted everything now. Bells. Steps. People. Memories. Pins.
One. Two. Three. Four. Five.
Five.
And on the left side of the crown, low down, just above the nape of the neck, a place where the gold hair was a little looser than the rest. Not much. A strand or two, slipping, catching the light. As if something had held it there once, and had been taken away, and nothing quite as good had been found to take its place.
Lyra looked at that place for a long time.
And something that had been standing very still at the back of her, all evening, since she first saw her aunt at the top of the lily stairs, turned round.
She knew those pins.
Not these. Not these five, in this hair, in this room. She had never seen them before tonight. But she knew one like them. She had read about it. In the Ledger. In Corin's square capitals, on a page from the autumn, a page from the very first days, when she had sat on the floor of Vespera's shop in Hook Lane with the book on her knee and read it from the beginning, all of it, every word, because he had asked her to and because she wanted to know who he was.
Note on my door this morning. STOP LOOKING. In my hand, very nearly. Not mine. Pinned with a hatpin. Long. Silver. White glass bead on the end.
And she remembered, too, what else he had written on that page. That all the other notes round his door were pinned with ordinary hatpins, with colored beads, cheap ones from a stall. And that this one was not. That this one was silver. And the bead on it was white, and milky, and fine. A lady's pin, he had written. Not from round here.
Lyra sat in the white velvet chair with her hands in her gray silk lap and did not move.
Cressida finished the last finger of her left glove, and did up the tiny pearl button at the wrist, and looked up. And saw her niece's face.
And saw where her niece was looking.
Her gloved hand went up. Not fast. Slowly. The way the Duke had said she put her hand to her pearls. To the back of her head. To the loose place in the gold hair, low on the left side, above the nape of her neck. And rested there.
"Six," said Lyra.
It came out very quietly.
"There should be six. Shouldn't there. A set."
Cressida's hand stayed where it was.
"They were your grandmother's," she said, after a moment. Her voice was the soft one again. The stairs voice. Warm. "Six white beads from the glassworks at Ost, on silver. She gave them to me on my wedding day, which was the only good thing about my wedding day." Her red mouth smiled. "I lost one. In the autumn. I've never found it."
"Where?"
"Oh, darling. If I knew where, it wouldn't be lost."
"On a door," said Lyra. "In the Lower Docks. Number fourteen, Tallow Street. The top floor. Ninety-one steps up." She did not look away. "Holding a note that said stop looking. In a hand that was very nearly his."
The fire ticked.
Cressida took her hand down from her hair.
She did not say anything for a long time. She stood by the little white grate in her white silk and her long white gloves, with the firelight on one side of her face and the five white beads flashing in her hair, and looked down at her niece in the white chair. And her face was not soft, and not sorrowful, and not afraid. It was only very, very tired.
"He was going to find it," she said at last.
"Find what?"
"Whatever it was he was looking for. In the autumn. In the drawers at the Ministry. In the scribe's room. In the lane." Cressida's voice was very low. "He was going to find it, and when he found it, they were going to empty him, the way they emptied your father. Only worse. Because your father was a lord and a Thorne, and they had to be careful with him. And your harvester was nobody." She looked at the fire. "I thought, if I told him to stop. In his own hand. Nearly. He might think it was himself. Some part of himself he'd forgotten, trying to warn him. And stop."
"He didn't."
"No." Cressida's mouth moved. "No. He went on looking. And he found you, instead." She turned back from the fire. "And now look at the pair of you."
Lyra stood up.
She did not know she was going to. She stood up out of the white velvet chair in her mother's gray gown, and stood in front of her aunt, close, the way she had stood in front of her brother in the tower. Close enough to smell the lilies on her. Close enough to see the fine lines at the corners of the Thorne eyes, that she had not seen from across the table.
"You stood over a man in a lane," said Lyra. "In the autumn. In a white shell mask. With a silver cup. While the Lady's people held him, and a big man held the lamp. And he ran four steps, and died." Her voice did not shake at all. "And the mask is in your cabinet. On blue velvet. Behind me. With the cup beside it."
Cressida did not look at the cabinet.
She looked at Lyra.
"Yes," she said.
And then, before Lyra could say anything else, before she could breathe, before the room could finish tilting, her aunt put out one gloved hand and laid it, very lightly, on Lyra's cheek. The way Lyra had laid hers on Corin's, in the crypt, an hour before. On the cheekbone. Under the eye.
"There were things in that scribe's head," said Cressida Thorne softly, "about your sister, darling. And I could not let anybody else have them."
She took her hand away.
And went to the white door, and opened it, and stood aside, the way the footman had.
"The Duke will be wanting you," she said. "He doesn't like to stay past the eleventh bell. It's bad for his digestion." The red mouth smiled. "Good night, darling. Mind the stairs. The lilies drop their pollen on everything."
