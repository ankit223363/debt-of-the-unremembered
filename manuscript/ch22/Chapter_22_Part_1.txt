Chapter 22: The Den on Cinder Lane, Part 1 of 5: Mister Gant
Mister Gant
Corin did not answer.
He stood on the last step, with the rod in his right hand and the dark lantern in his left, and the warm sour air of the cellar all round him like dirty bathwater, and he did not say anything at all. He did not trust his voice. He did not trust what it would say.
Behind him on the steps, Wat had stopped. Corin could feel him there. A great warm stillness at his back, like a wall of a house.
The soft voice spoke again. Round the corner. At the end of the passage.
"Harl?"
A pause. A small sound. A pen being laid down on wood. Carefully. The way a tidy man lays down a pen.
"Harl, I do wish you'd answer when you're spoken to. It's so tiresome, having to say everything twice."
Corin stepped off the last step onto the stone floor of the cellar.
It was a passage. Gideon had been right. Long and low and arched, made of old brick gone black with damp, and so narrow that two people could hardly have passed each other in it. The roof curved down so low that Corin had to bend his head. Water dripped from it, here and there, with a small cold sound, plink, onto the flagstones, and the flagstones were wet and slippery and shone in the lantern light.
On the right, the barrels.
They stood in a long row against the wall, on wooden cradles, on their sides. Beer barrels. Huge ones. Taller than a man, some of them, and as round as a cartwheel, with black iron hoops and wooden taps in their ends. The smell of them came off the wood, sour and yeasty and old. Some of them had chalk marks on their ends, numbers and dates. One had a cat asleep on top of it, curled up in a tight gray ball. It opened one yellow eye as Corin went past, and shut it again.
On the left, a little way along, a door.
Not a wooden door. A door made of bars. Iron ones, thick and black and rough with rust, set into a frame of iron in the brick of the wall, from the floor to the low arched roof. Behind the bars, darkness. And the smell, stronger. The sweat and the old straw and the bucket and the fear.
And the crying. Louder now. Close. Just behind the bars, in the dark. A girl's voice. Thin and high and hopeless, going on and on, not loudly, the way a child cries when it has been crying for so long that it has forgotten how to stop.
Beside the barred door, on a stool, with his back against the wall and his chin on his chest, an old man was asleep.
He was very old. Small and shrunken and gray all over, gray hair and gray stubble and a gray coat and a gray muffler, like a heap of ash in the shape of a man. His hands were folded on his round stomach. On a string round one wrist, a ring of keys. Big black iron ones. His mouth was open and he was snoring, very softly, a little whistling snore, in and out.
Lom, thought Corin. He's old. He sleeps.
He went past him.
The passage went on. Past the barred door. Past three more barrels. And then it turned, sharply, to the right, round a corner of old brick, and the yellow light was coming from round the corner. Bright. Steady. A good lamp. An expensive one. Not tallow.
Corin stopped at the corner.
He put his back against the wet brick wall, and turned his head, slowly, and looked round.
The passage beyond the corner was short. Ten paces. Perhaps twelve.
On the left side of it, halfway along, set low in the wall, there was a hatch. A square wooden hatch, about as big as a small window, on iron hinges, with an iron bolt across it. Frost on it. A thick white rime of frost on the wood all round the edges, and a little white mist curling out of the cracks and sinking to the floor.
The cold room. Where they keep the ice.
And at the end of the passage, a room.
The door of it was open. It was a proper door, wooden, painted dark green, with a brass handle. It stood wide open against the inside wall. And through it, Corin could see the room.
It was small. Smaller than his own room at the top of No. 14 Tallow Street. A cellar room, with an arched brick roof and brick walls, whitewashed once and gone yellow with smoke. But it was not like the passage. It was clean. Swept. There was a rug on the floor. A thick red rug, with a pattern of birds. There was a little iron stove in the corner, glowing, with a kettle on it. There was a shelf on the wall with a row of books on it, all the same, tall and thin and bound in black. And there was a desk.
A good desk. Dark wood, polished, with brass handles on its drawers and a green leather top. On the desk, a lamp. A brass lamp with a glass chimney and a green glass shade, burning clear and bright and steady. A pen in a brass stand. A bottle of ink. A blotter. And a book. Open. Tall and thin and bound in black, like the ones on the shelf.
And behind the desk, in a high-backed wooden chair with a cushion on it, a man.
He was small. That was the first thing. Corin had thought, from the voice, and from what Gideon had said, Gant sits, that he would be big. Fat, perhaps. Lazy. He was not. He was small and neat and thin, in a gray knitted cardigan buttoned up to the top over a white shirt with a stiff collar, and a dark tie. He had thin gray hair, combed very carefully over the top of his head from one side to the other, to hide where it had gone. Small round spectacles with gold rims, pushed up onto his forehead. A small gray mustache, clipped very short. A face like a clerk's. Like Toby's, almost, if Toby had been fifty years old and had never once in his life been kind to anyone.
He had his elbows on the desk and his fingertips pressed together under his chin, and he was looking at the doorway.
Not at Corin. Corin was round the corner, in the dark. Mister Gant was looking at the empty doorway of his room, with his head a little on one side, and a small, polite, puzzled frown on his neat face. Waiting for Harl.
And in the corner of the room, by the stove, on a stool, there was another man.
This one was big.
He was so big that the stool under him seemed to be a child's stool, and the corner seemed too small for him, and the whole room seemed to lean a little toward him, the way a boat leans toward the heavy side. Not as big as Wat. But nearly. Broad and thick and heavy, with shoulders like a bull's and a neck as thick as his head. He had a round, red, shining face, with small eyes in it, very pale blue, almost white, and a thick red nose, and a great shapeless mouth. His hair was cropped close to his skull. He wore a leather jerkin over a striped shirt with the sleeves rolled up above the elbow, and his forearms, resting on his knees, were as thick as Corin's legs, and covered in reddish hair.
He was eating. Bread and an onion. He held the onion in one enormous hand, raw, peeled, white, and he was biting into it like an apple, slowly, with his eyes half shut, the way a cow chews. There was a mug of beer on the floor by his boot.
Dace, thought Corin. He doesn't sleep.
And then the smell reached him, across the little room, through the doorway, round the corner. Over the sweat and the straw and the fear and the yeast of the barrels. Strong and sour and sharp.
Beer. And onions.
Something went through Corin then. Like cold water. Like the needle of ice that had gone through him on the jetty, when Fenn Briggs said it couldn't take it off him either. It went from the top of his head to the soles of his feet, and he could not have said what it was, because he did not remember. He did not remember anything. But his body did. His hands did. The hand holding the rod had gone white at the knuckles. And under the Ledger, against his ribs, his heart was going like something trying to get out.
A big man held him, he had said, in a lane behind a printing house, to a sergeant of the Watch with a boiled-ham face. Huge hands. Beer and onions.
It was in the Ledger. He had read it a hundred times.
He had never smelled it.
He was smelling it now.
Boy, said Malakor. Very low. Very fast. Be careful. Be very careful. Do not do anything you cannot take back.
Mister Gant sighed.
He took his fingertips away from his chin. He pulled his gold spectacles down off his forehead onto his nose, and picked up his pen again, and dipped it, neatly, in the ink.
"Dace," he said, without looking round. "Go and see what Harl wants, would you? He's being tiresome again. I expect he's lost his hat, or his nerve, or both." He wrote something in the black book, in a small careful hand. "And tell him that if he's come back for another muffler, he can't have one. I'm not made of mufflers."
The big man in the corner stopped chewing.
He put the onion down on his knee. Slowly. He wiped his hand on his leather jerkin, also slowly. Then he picked up the mug of beer by his boot, and drank from it, a long drink, tipping his head back so that Corin could see his great thick throat working. And put the mug down.
And stood up.
He went on standing up for a long time. When he had finished, his close-cropped head nearly touched the arched brick roof of the little room. He had to bend his neck as he came round the desk, past Mister Gant's chair, toward the door.
Toward the corner.
Corin heard Wat breathe in, behind him. One long slow breath. The way a man breathes before he lifts something very heavy.
Gideon did not breathe at all. Corin did not hear him. He did not know if Gideon was even still there.
The big man came to the doorway of the room. He filled it. The light from the green-shaded lamp went out behind him, all at once, the way the sun goes behind a cloud, and the short passage was suddenly dark, with only the thin line of yellow leaking from the bottom of Corin's shuttered lantern, and the faint white mist creeping out of the cracks round the frosted hatch.
He stood in the doorway and looked down the passage toward the corner. His small pale eyes, almost white, went along the brick. Past the hatch. To the corner.
To the thin line of yellow on the wet floor.
He stopped.
"Harl?" he said.
His voice was deep and thick and slow, and there was something in it that was not from the city. A roll to the r. A heaviness at the end of the word, as if the word were being pressed down into the ground.
Nobody answered.
The big man's small pale eyes narrowed.
And then his great right hand went down to his belt, to the back, behind his hip, where the leather jerkin hung loose, and came out again with something in it. Short and thick and black. A cosh. A bag of leather, the size of a man's fist, stuffed with lead shot, on a short whippy handle.
"You are not Harl," said Dace.
And he came down the passage.
Something from Today
He came slowly.
That was the most frightening thing about him. He did not rush. He did not shout. He came down the short dark passage from the green door the way a big dog comes across a yard toward a stranger. Head low. Shoulders forward. One heavy step, and then another, and his boots on the wet flagstones making a slow soft sound, slap, and slap. The leather cosh hung from his right hand, swinging very gently against his thigh.
He passed the frosted hatch. The white mist curled round his boots and he did not look down at it.
Corin stood at the corner with his back to the brick and the rod in his hand and could not move.
It was not fear. Or not only fear. It was something under the fear, something older and colder, that had nothing to do with the man coming down the passage and everything to do with the smell coming in front of him. Beer and onions. It filled the narrow space. It filled Corin's nose and mouth and throat. And somewhere very deep in him, below the Ledger, below the place where he kept the things he had written down, something was screaming. Not in words. In a young man's voice. A scribe's voice. Thin and high, in a dark lane, with a huge hand over his mouth.
Pell's dying, thought Corin, very clearly, from a long way off. I took it out of him. And I gave it back. I breathed it into the jar. Malakor made me.
But some of it stayed.
Some of it always stays.
The big man was five paces away. Four.
Something small, boy, said Malakor. Sharp and close and urgent, the way he had said it once before, on a bridge, in the snow. Something from today. Quickly. Quickly!
Corin reached inside himself.
He did it the way Vespera had taught him. The way the Ledger said he had done it a hundred times, and forgotten every one.
He went in. Past the cold. Past the screaming that was not his. Past the beer and onions and the slow soft slap of boots. Down, into himself, into the place where the day's memories were still lying on the surface, new and loose, like leaves on water, with no roots yet.
Today.
There was so much of today. It was the longest day he had ever lived. He could feel it all, lying there on the surface, crowded, jostling. Dawn in the crypt, with Lyra asleep on his shoulder. Juno's fist on the door. Wick, on his sack, with his combed wet hair. The tide weighs it. Hessa Doon by her pillar. The bird market. A wren going out through a hole in the roof. A plaque on a bridge with a smooth place in the middle. A room full of small black books. An oak leaf on a yellowed page. A fourth note, coming down.
He could not burn any of those. He would not.
Something small. Something with no roots.
He went past them. Further. To the end of the day. To an hour ago. Less. To a narrow lane, steep as a gutter, behind a great round building, and a red door with steam curling out round its edges.
And a smell.
Roast meat. And onions, fried golden in the pan. And gravy, thick and brown and rich, bubbling. Coming out round the edges of a red door into the freezing dark, so warm and so strong that it had gone into him like a blow, and his empty stomach had turned over, and for one moment, one single moment, standing in the frost, he had been so hungry that he had forgotten everything else.
That.
Not the door. Not the lane. Not Mrs. Pratt, with flour on her nose and her burned red hands on the bars of the grating, crying for a boy who kissed her on the cheek. He would keep her. He would keep her forever, if he could.
Just the smell. Just that one warm, rich, hungry moment, on the coldest night of the year.
He took it.
And he burned it.
The light came out of his hands.
It was not white this time. It was gold. Warm, deep, rich gold, the color of gravy in lamplight, the color of a kitchen fire seen through a window on a winter night. It came out of his chest and down his arms and out through his fingers, through the rod, all at once, in one great silent burst, and the narrow brick passage was suddenly full of it from end to end. Every brick. Every drop of water on the arched roof. Every flagstone. The frost on the hatch blazed like a sheet of gold leaf. The white mist curling out of its cracks turned to golden smoke.
And the big man in the middle of the passage threw his arm up in front of his face and roared.
It was a terrible sound. Not a word. A bull's sound. He staggered, blind, with his arm over his eyes and the cosh swinging wildly in his other hand, and his shoulder hit the brick wall, hard, and he bounced off it, and swung the cosh at nothing, at the light, at the air, and it cracked against the brick a foot from Corin's head and sent a shower of red dust and mortar into his face.
Corin did not feel it.
He felt the burn.
It was not like the stew. He had read about the stew. I burned the taste of Mrs. Marta's stew, and the light came out of my hands, and it did not hurt, and I did not know what I had lost until I tried to remember it. This was different. This hurt. It hurt the way a tooth hurts when it is pulled. A deep, wrenching, tearing pull, from somewhere just under his breastbone, and then a hole. A small, clean, empty hole, where something had been a moment ago.
He did not know what.
He stood in the golden light with the rod in his hand and tried to remember what he had just burned. And could not. There was nothing there. Only the shape of a hole. Only the edge of something, the way you feel the edge of a gap where a tooth was with your tongue.
It's gone, he thought. Whatever it was. It's gone.
And then the light began to fade, and Wat came past him.
Wat came round the corner like a falling wall.
He did not have the boat hook. He had left it, Corin saw afterward, leaning against the barrels. He did not need it. He came round the corner with his great spade-hands open in front of him and his bald head down, and he hit the big man in the middle of the chest with his shoulder, at a run, and the sound it made was like two barrels of beer being dropped off a cart.
Dace went backward.
He went backward three steps, four, with his arms flying out, and his boots skidding on the wet flagstones, and the cosh flying out of his hand and clattering away along the floor. He hit the frosted hatch with his back. The wood cracked. The iron bolt bent. White mist burst out round him in a cloud.
But he did not go down.
He stood against the hatch, blind and roaring, with the golden light dying round him, and he put his head down, and he came off the hatch at Wat like a bull coming out of a gate.
They met in the middle of the passage.
Corin had never seen two men that size fight. He would not want to see it again. It was not like the fights in the penny storybooks. It was not quick or clever. It was slow, and close, and terrible. They had hold of each other. Each had his arms round the other, like two bears, and they leaned on each other, chest to chest, with their boots sliding on the wet stone and their faces red and their breath roaring, and neither of them moved. For a long time. A very long time. They just stood there, in the narrow brick passage, shoving, the way two oxen shove at a cart that is stuck in the mud.
Then Dace got one hand free.
He put it in Wat's face. Flat. His whole great palm, over Wat's nose and mouth and eyes. And pushed. Wat's head went back. His bald head hit the arched brick roof with a sound like a dropped stone, and he grunted, and his hold slipped.
And Dace had his other hand free too.
It went to his belt. To the front. And came up with something short and bright.
Corin saw it in the last of the golden light. A knife. Not long, like Harl's. Short and broad and curved, like a little sickle, with a bone handle. It caught the light and flashed.
And Corin moved.
He did not think about it. There was no time to think. He took two steps forward along the wet passage and swung the silver rod. Not at the knife. At the hand. At the thick, red-haired wrist above it. With everything he had.
The rod hit the wrist with a crack.
Not the crack of bone. The crack of something else. Something Corin had never felt before and did not understand. When the silver touched the big man's bare skin, there was a jolt. Up the rod and into Corin's hand and up his arm, like lightning, like the shock you get touching an iron gate in a dry frost, only a hundred times stronger. His whole arm went numb from fingers to shoulder. His teeth slammed together.
And Dace screamed.
Not a roar. A scream. High and thin, like a child's. He dropped the curved knife. It rang on the flagstones. He let go of Wat. He fell back against the brick wall, clutching his wrist, staring at it with his small pale eyes gone huge.
Corin stared at it too.
On the big man's wrist, where the rod had touched it, there was a mark. Not a bruise. Not a cut. A line. A thin, bright, silver line, curling round the thick red-haired skin. A spiral. Exactly like the spiral on the rod. As if the rod had printed itself onto him.
It glowed, faintly, for a moment. Then it faded. And was just a red mark, like a burn.
Ah, said Malakor, in the back of Corin's head. Very quietly. With something in the dry old voice that Corin had never heard there before, and did not like. Ah. It remembers him. Doesn't it.
"What does?"
The rod, boy. It remembers every man it has ever drawn Ash from. And every man whose Ash it has ever touched. A long pause. You drew Pell Dorran's last minutes with this rod, in a lane behind a printing house. And this man's hands were on Pell Dorran. That night. I can taste it.
Corin stood very still.
In front of him, against the wall, the big man was sliding slowly down onto the wet flagstones. Holding his wrist. Staring at it. His great red face had gone the color of cheese.
And Wat, behind him, with blood running out of his nose into his beard, took one step forward, and picked up the curved knife off the floor, and stood over him, and said, in his voice like a cart on a bridge:
"Stay down."
Dace stayed down.
In the little room at the end of the passage, behind the open green door, Mister Gant had not moved.
He was still sitting at his desk. In his gray cardigan, in his high-backed chair, with his pen in his hand and his gold spectacles on his nose. He had not got up. He had not called out. He had not reached for anything.
He was looking down the passage at Corin. Over the top of his spectacles. With his neat gray head a little on one side, and a small, polite, interested expression on his clerk's face.
The way a man looks at a column of figures that has, unexpectedly, added up to something.
"Ah," said Mister Gant softly. "The harvester."
He put down his pen. Neatly. In its brass stand.
"You'd better come in," he said. "Do mind the rug."
