Chapter 22: The Den on Cinder Lane, Part 5 of 5: Midnight
Midnight
Everybody in the room heard it.
Corin saw it go through them. Through the eleven, packed round the walls in the warm, with Gideon's tea in their hands. One moment they were sitting there, dull and gray and still, staring at the stove. The next, every head had come up. Every face had turned toward the arched brick roof. And on every face, the same thing. The same look. The look of a field of rabbits when the shadow of a hawk goes over.
The old woman with the white hair began, very softly, to moan.
Tilly's hand closed on Corin's sleeve so hard that he felt her nails through the cloth.
"That's her," she whispered. "That's her carriage. It always stops there. Right over the counting room. You can hear the horses stamp."
Above them, faint, through the brick and the earth and the floor of the Bell, a horse stamped. And then another. And a jingle of harness. And a carriage door, opening, and shutting, with a soft expensive thud.
Behind his desk, tied to his chair, Mister Gant had stopped watching them. He was looking up too. At the roof. And over the top of the torn muffler, his small pale eyes had gone bright. Not frightened. Pleased. Like a dog that hears its master on the stair.
Corin stood up.
"Now," he said. "All of you. Now."
It was not quick.
He had known it would not be quick. He had thought about it, kneeling by Tam, for half an hour. He had made a plan. But plans were things you made in the warm, by a stove, with a clock ticking. And now the clock said seven minutes to twelve, and there was a carriage on Cinder Lane, and nothing went the way the plan said.
Wat took the old woman with the white hair. He did not ask. He bent and picked her up off the floor, in her rags, as if she were a child, and held her against his great chest, and her thin white arms went round his neck and held on, and she stopped moaning.
John Rennick took Tam. With another man, a thin bald docker with a broken nose, called Pegg. They made a chair of their hands, the way children do, and lifted Tam onto it in his blanket, and he put an arm round each of their necks, weakly, and his head fell against Rennick's shoulder, and he did not wake.
The rest walked. Or shuffled. Or held on to each other. The young man with the black eye, with his eyes shut, holding the coat of the man in front. The two other women, one on each side of a third. An old man with a stick, who had to stop every three steps and cough.
And Tilly. Who would not let go of Corin's sleeve.
Gideon went first. With the dark lantern. Down the short wet passage, past the open hatch with its white breath, past the corner, past the barrels and the cat and old Lom, still snoring on his stool with the wax in his ears. To the bottom of the ice steps. And stood there, holding the lantern up, small and thin and pale in his huge boots, lighting the way.
Corin went last.
He stopped at the green door. He looked back into the little room. At the red rug with its ring of black birds. At the glowing stove. At the shelf of black books. At the small neat man in the gray cardigan, tied to his high-backed chair, with his gold spectacles crooked on his nose and his small bright eyes on the roof.
And at Dace.
They had brought him in from the passage, before. Tied hand and foot. He was sitting against the wall by the door, on the floor, with his great legs out in front of him and his pyre-marked arm twisted behind his back. He was not looking at the roof. He was looking at Corin.
"She'll know," he said. Very low. "She'll know I talked. Gant will tell her. The first thing. Before she's off the bottom step."
Corin looked at him.
"And then she'll get out her cup," said Dace. His pale eyes did not blink. "And I'll go the way the clerk went. And the doctor. And I'll never see the sky-lamps. Not even from inside one."
Corin stood in the doorway with the clock ticking behind him.
No, said Malakor. Sharp and quick. No, boy. He held the lamp. He pulled the boot. He put a hand on the girl's arm while Gant read her yield. Leave him.
Corin took Harl's long knife out of his belt.
He knelt. He cut the rope round Dace's ankles. Not his wrists. His ankles. The leather belt came away. Dace's great legs jerked.
"Walk," said Corin. "In front of me. Up the steps. If you run, Wat will break you in half. If you shout, I will put this rod on you again and you'll feel the clerk's foot in your hands for the rest of your life."
Dace stared at him.
"Why?" he said.
Corin did not know. He did not answer. He stood up and stepped back, with the knife in one hand and the rod in the other, and waited.
Dace got up. Slowly. Clumsily, with his hands still tied behind him. He stood swaying in the doorway, with his close-cropped head bent under the lintel. And then he went past Corin, out into the passage, toward the steps.
Behind the desk, Mister Gant had turned his head. He was looking at Corin now. Over the gag. With his small bright eyes.
Corin looked back at him for one moment.
Then he took the black ledger from under his arm, and held it up, so that Mister Gant could see it. Accounts. This Year. And he put it inside his coat, against his ribs, next to his own.
And turned. And went.
The steps were the worst.
They were steep and narrow and worn into hollows and covered in frost, and there was no rail. The old man with the stick fell twice. Wat went up them sideways, slowly, one at a time, with the old woman held against his chest and his free hand flat on the slimy wall. Rennick and Pegg, with Tam between them on their crossed hands, had to stop on every third step and shift their grip, gasping.
And all the time, above them, through the wall of the Bell on their left, Corin could hear.
A door. The front door of the Bell, opening, and the noise of the tavern changing, all at once. Going quiet. Not silent. Quiet. The way a room goes quiet when someone comes in that everyone has been waiting for, and nobody wants to look at.
Footsteps. Light. Quick. A woman's. On bare wooden boards. And with them, heavier ones. A man's. Big. Slow.
And a voice.
Corin could not hear the words. Only the shape of it. A woman's voice. Low and pleasant and amused. Saying something, and then laughing, very softly. A little laugh, light as a feather.
And then another voice, that he did know. Big and tired and cheerful and a little too loud.
"Evening, ma'am! Evening! Terrible night, terrible night to be out. Can I get you something? A hot gin? Bert! A hot gin for the lady! Oh—oh, saints, I'm so sorry, ma'am, I'm so sorry, I've gone and—here, let me—"
A crash. A great crash, of glass, a lot of glass, and a tray, and something heavy falling. A shout. Bert's voice, shouting. And the woman's voice again, not amused now, sharp and cold and quick. And a man's, deep and slow, growling.
And Moll's, over all of them, louder than ever, cheerful and stupid and endless. "Sorry, ma'am, oh, I'm so sorry, it's my stomach, I've had a bad stomach all day, here, here, let me help you, I've got a handkerchief, it's clean, mostly—"
I'm good at getting in the way, Moll had said.
Corin found that he was smiling, on the frozen steps, in the dark, with Tilly clinging to his sleeve. He did not know he could.
They came up out of the ground into the yard.
The cold hit them like a slap. The eleven stood there in the frozen yard, in the yellow light of the lamp on the back wall, blinking, gasping, in their rags, with their breath going up in clouds. Some of them looked up at the sky. At the thin crack of stars between the houses. As if they had forgotten it was there.
The old woman in Wat's arms looked up at it for a long time.
"Oh," she said. Just that. "Oh."
Gideon was already at the mouth of the alley. Holding the lantern up. Waiting.
"Which way?" said Wat.
"The arches," said Corin. "Under the Dye-Works. Mother Grue. She'll take them. She said she'd put a poker through anybody who comes." He looked at Gideon. "Can you find the way? From here? Not by the streets. By the back."
"Yes," said Gideon.
"Then take them. All of them. Wat goes with you."
"And you?"
Corin looked back at the Bell.
At its back wall. At the lamp on its bracket. At the dirty glass of a small window, low down, beside the back door, with yellow light in it. The kitchen, perhaps. Or a passage. And through the dirty glass, moving, shapes.
One of them was tall. And white.
He went to the window.
He did not mean to. Malakor said no in his head, very sharp, and he heard it, and he went anyway. Three steps across the frozen cobbles. And he put his face close to the glass, at the corner, where it was cleanest, and looked in.
It was a passage. A narrow one, with a stone floor and whitewashed walls, running from the tavern at the front to a door at the back. And at the end of it, by the top of a wooden stair that went down, there was a woman.
She was standing with her back to him. Tall. Straight. In a long coat of some soft white stuff, with a high fur collar, silver-gray. White gloves. And over her head and shoulders, falling to her waist, a veil. White. Fine as mist. So fine that he could see, through it, the shape of her hair, dark and piled up high, and a flash of something at her throat. Small lights. Tiny vials, on a chain, glowing faintly. Gold, and green, and blue.
She was looking down the stair. Toward the cellar. Toward the passage, and the barrels, and the counting room, and the little warm room with the red rug, where a small neat man sat tied to his chair.
And beside her, a big man. Huge. In a long dark coat, with a round red face and a bowler hat, and a paper bag in one hand, with grease on it. A meat pie.
Gorse.
The woman in white lifted one gloved hand. She held something in it. Small. Silver. It caught the lamplight from the passage, and flashed.
A cup.
And the smell came through the glass. Through the dirty window, through the cold, through everything. Thick and sweet and heavy. So strong that Corin's eyes watered.
Lilies.
He stood with his face against the cold glass and did not breathe.
She did not turn round. She did not lift her veil. She stood at the top of the cellar stair with the silver cup in her hand, and looked down, for a long moment. Very still.
Then she began to go down.
Corin stepped back from the window.
He turned. Wat was still in the yard, with the old woman in his arms. Waiting. The others had gone. Into the alley, after Gideon's lantern, shuffling, holding on to each other, into the dark.
"Go," said Corin. "Go, Wat. Now. She's going down. She'll find Gant in two minutes. And then they'll come out of that door."
Wat looked at him.
"You first," he said.
And behind them, from somewhere very far off, on the hill, Saint Agatha's began to ring midnight.
Dace's Last Word
They ran.
Not fast. Nobody could run fast, with an old woman in his arms and a frozen boy on a chair of hands and nine thin gray people in rags shuffling in front of him in the dark. But they ran as fast as they could. Down the black slot of the alley, between the backs of the houses, with the frozen slops in the gutter cracking under their feet. Gideon's lantern bobbing ahead, small and yellow, like a firefly. The bells of midnight rolling and ringing over the roofs, all round them, from every church in the city, so loud that nobody could have heard them if they had shouted.
Twelve.
The last stroke of Saint Agatha's went out over the river and died.
And behind them, a long way back, from the yard behind the Bell, there was a sound. A door, banging open. Hard. Against a wall. And a man's voice, deep and slow, shouting something. And a woman's voice, cold and quick, saying one word.
Corin did not hear the word. He did not need to.
Run, said Malakor. Run, boy.
He ran.
Dace was waiting at the mouth of the alley.
Corin had forgotten him. He had sent him up the steps first, with his hands tied behind his back, and then there had been the yard, and the window, and the lilies, and he had forgotten. He had thought, when he thought anything, that the big man would be long gone. Off into the dark, with his hands tied, running for his life.
He was not gone. He was standing at the end of the alley, where it came out into Fuller's Yard, in the shadow of a shed. Waiting. With his hands still tied behind him and his close-cropped head bent and his breath going up in great white clouds.
When Corin came up, he lifted his head.
"You didn't run," said Corin. Gasping.
"Where would I run?" said Dace. "With my hands tied, in this cold. In this city." His pale eyes went past Corin, back up the alley. "She'll have me by morning. Wherever I go. Gant will tell her what I said, and she'll tell Gorse, and Gorse will find me. Gorse always finds people." A pause. "So I waited."
"Why?"
"To tell you something." Dace swallowed. "Before she has me. Something I didn't tell you before. In the passage. Because I was frightened."
Corin looked at him. At the great round red face. The pale, almost white eyes. The sweat, freezing now, on his forehead.
Ahead, across Fuller's Yard, Gideon's lantern had stopped by the little bridge over the dye stream. Wat's great shape stood beside it, with the old woman in his arms. Waiting.
"Tell me," said Corin. "Quickly."
"The girl," said Dace. "In the broadsheet. The one who said she saw the Thorne girl kill the doctor."
"Agnes Fell."
"That's not her name." Dace's mouth twisted. "That's just a name. Like all of them. The white one buys them girls from the laundries, from the workhouses, from the street. Girls nobody will miss. And she takes them somewhere. And when they come back, they believe things. Things that never happened. They believe them so hard they'd swear to them in front of a magistrate." He looked at Corin. "You know that. You found one. The one with the wrong writing on her arms."
"Hester," said Corin. "Hester Dunn."
"The white one was very angry about Hester." Dace almost smiled. Almost. "She said the man who did her was careless. She said he'd be sorry."
"Hollis Marsh," said Corin. "Needle."
Dace's pale eyes widened, just a little.
"You know him?"
"I know his hand. The hook on the g." Corin's own hand had gone to his coat. To the Ledger. "I don't know where he works. Nobody does. Vespera's been looking for him for ten years."
Dace was quiet for a moment.
Then he jerked his close-cropped head. Back. Over his shoulder. Not at the alley. At Fuller's Yard. At the wide dark square of frozen mud, with the row of old wooden sheds along one side, and the backs of tall black buildings round the other three.
At one building in particular. Long and low and very old, with a sagging roof and a row of tiny dark windows high up under the eaves, all along its length, like the portholes of a ship. It ran the whole length of the far side of the yard. A hundred paces long. More. And at the near end, at the top, under the gable, one small square window had a light in it.
Very faint. Very small. Yellow.
"The old rope-walk," said Dace. "They used to make ship's rope there. Long ago. You need a long room to make rope. As long as the rope." He looked at the faint yellow window. "There's nothing there now. Rats. Pigeons. And a loft at the top, at the end, up a ladder, with a trap door."
Corin's heart had begun to go hard.
"I carried her up there," said Dace. "Twice. The white one. Up the ladder, when the snow was bad and she didn't want her shoes wet. And I waited at the bottom, in the dark, with the rats, while she was up there. An hour. Two." His voice dropped. "And I heard him. Through the trap. Talking to her. A thin voice. Like a needle on glass. And her laughing." He paused. "And other things. A girl. Crying. Then not crying. Then saying, very calm, over and over, like a lesson, I saw her. I saw her face. I saw her do it."
Corin looked at the faint yellow light under the gable.
Two hundred paces away. Across Fuller's Yard. At the end of an old rope-walk. While he had been walking past it, three hours ago, with Gideon and Moll and Wat, on his way to the Bell.
Everything runs into everything else.
"He's there now?" he said.
"He's always there," said Dace. "He never goes out. Not since the girl with the wrong arms. He's frightened. Of the ink woman. Of you." He looked at Corin. "Of her, most of all. The white one. She said he'd be sorry."
Corin stood for a long moment in the shadow of the shed.
Then he took Harl's long knife out of his belt again. He stepped behind Dace. And he cut the rope round the big man's wrists.
It fell away into the frozen mud.
Dace did not move. He stood with his great arms hanging at his sides, and his hands open, and looked down at them. At the red marks on his wrists where the rope had been. At the other mark, on the right one. The burn. Where, for one moment, there had been a thin bright silver spiral.
"Why?" he said again.
"Because you waited," said Corin.
Dace looked at him.
"Go south," said Corin. "Tonight. Now. Before Gorse. Get on a barge, or a cart, or walk. Go round the Glass the long way, with a caravan. Go home." He did not know why he was saying it. He said it anyway. "Go and stand under your mother's lamp."
The big man's face did something then that Corin had not thought it could do.
It crumpled. All at once. Like a child's. The great shapeless mouth went square, and the small pale eyes screwed up tight, and his whole huge body shook, once, from head to foot, the way Gideon's had shaken in the white mist by the ice door.
Then it was gone.
Dace wiped his face with the back of one enormous hand. He looked down at Corin. For a long moment. With those pale, almost white eyes, the color at the very edge of a candle flame.
"You've got a keeper's rod," he said. Very low. "From my country. And you don't know where it came from." He nodded slowly. "Find out, harvester. Find out who carried it north. Because nobody carries a keeper's rod out of Kesh-Amaran. Nobody. Not in a thousand years. Unless the fire told them to."
And he turned, and went away across Fuller's Yard, south, toward the river. Big and slow and dark, with his hands in his pockets and his head bent. Not running. Walking. The way a man walks who has a long way to go and knows it.
Corin watched him until the dark took him.
That, said Malakor, very softly, was either the kindest thing you have done all week, boy, or the stupidest. A pause. I find, more and more, that they are usually the same thing.
They were at the little bridge when Juno found them.
She came down off the roof of the nearest shed in one long drop, landing on her toes in the frozen mud with a small soft thump, and straightened up, and stood there with her chest heaving and her brass key bouncing on its string and her sharp face white as paper.
"Pruett's," she said, before Corin could open his mouth. "I got there. I told them. Oren went. Lyra went. I went." She was talking so fast that the words tripped over each other. "The Watch on the gate wouldn't let us in, and Oren said move, and they didn't, and he moved them. Both of them. Into the water trough." Something like a grin went across her face and was gone. "But we couldn't get his lordship out. He wouldn't go. When Oren picked him up, he screamed. Like a baby. He held on to the window. He held on to the box with the blue stone in it. Mrs. Hobb said if we took him out in this cold, in the night, he'd die before the bridge."
"So you stayed."
"We stayed." Juno swallowed. "And they came. At eleven. Four of them. Over the yard wall. A little one with a red scarf who never stopped talking, and a big one with a club with nails in it, and two more." She took a breath. "And Oren went out in the yard to meet them. On his own. With his sword. And Lyra went with him, and he told her to go back, and she didn't."
Corin could not breathe.
"They're alive," said Juno quickly. "Both of them. Lyra's got a cut on her arm. It's not bad. Mrs. Hobb sewed it. And Oren—" She stopped.
"What?"
"Oren did something," said Juno. Her voice had gone strange. "When the one with the club came at Lyra. He put his hand up. And he said a word. I didn't hear what. And the air went hard. In front of them. Like glass. And the club hit it and broke." She stared at Corin. "And they ran. All four of them. Over the wall. And the little one with the red scarf was shouting, all the way, We'll be back! At first light! With more! With the whole downstairs!"
She stopped. She stood there in the frozen mud by the little bridge, with the dye stream steaming and stinking under it in the dark, and her sharp chin trembling.
"And Oren's sitting on the stairs now," she said. "In the stable. With his sword across his knees. Waiting. And he can't remember what you call the thing you sit on. He keeps patting the stair and frowning at it."
Corin looked across Fuller's Yard.
At the old rope-walk. At the faint yellow light under the gable.
Then back the other way. Toward the river. Toward the river road, and Linden Walk, and a livery stable with a big man sitting on the stairs in the dark with a sword across his knees, waiting for first light.
He took out the Ledger. He wrote, standing up, by the light of Gideon's lantern, very fast.
Monday, just past midnight.
Out from under the Bell. Eleven + Tam. To Mother Grue in the arches, with Wat and Gideon.
Gant's ledger: I have it.
The white one went down the stair with the cup. Gorse with her.
Dace let go. South. "Find out who carried the rod north."
NEEDLE: the loft at the end of the old rope-walk, Fuller's Yard. Light in the window now.
Pruett's: Oren and Lyra held the yard at 11. Lord Thorne will not be moved in the night. They will come back at first light, "with the whole downstairs." Oren has lost the word for the thing you sit on.
He looked at the last line. Then he wrote, under it, small:
Chair.
And underlined it, so that if Oren ever asked, it would be there.
"Juno," he said. "Can you find Vespera? On Hook Lane? Now?"
Juno looked at him. Then across the yard, at the faint yellow light in the old rope-walk.
Her black eyes went very wide.
"Is that him?" she said. "Is that Needle?"
"Tell her," said Corin. "Tell her I've found him. Tell her I'll wait for her at the bottom of the ladder. And tell her—" He stopped. He thought of a woman with a thousand fine lines of true writing on her arms, and an old half-name on her wrist that was older than the man it named. HOLL—. "Tell her to bring her needles."
Juno was already gone. Up the side of the shed, onto the roof, into the dark.
And from the far end of the alley, from the direction of the Bell, Corin heard a man coming. Heavy. Slow. Limping a little. Whistling, through his teeth, out of tune. A song about a girl and a boat and a fisherman.
Sergeant Moll came out of the alley into Fuller's Yard with his collar turned up and his hat gone and a long cut across the back of one big hand, wrapped in a bar towel. He saw them by the bridge. He stopped whistling.
"Spilled a whole tray on her," he said. "Gin. All down that white fur." His boiled-ham face split into a grin. "Best night's work I've done in twenty-two years."
