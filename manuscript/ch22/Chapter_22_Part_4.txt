Chapter 22: The Den on Cinder Lane, Part 4 of 5: The Pyre on His Arm
The Pyre on His Arm
Corin did not say anything for a long time.
He crouched in the wet passage in front of the big man, with the rod in his left hand resting across his knees, and the white mist from the open hatch creeping round his boots, and he looked at Dace's face. At the sweat on it. At the small pale eyes, shut tight. At the great shapeless mouth, trembling.
I've heard that water every night for two years.
He believed him. He did not want to. But he did.
Of course you do, said Malakor quietly. He is telling the truth. Men always tell the truth to the rod, boy, once it has touched them. They cannot help it. It is like holding a hand over a candle. A pause. That is why harvesters were feared, once. A long time ago. Before they were clerks.
Corin looked down at the rod across his knees.
He did not like what Malakor had said. He did not like it at all. He put it away, in a corner of himself, to think about later. To write down.
Then he looked up again.
"Dace," he said. "Look at me."
The big man opened his eyes.
"Where are you from?" said Corin. "Not the city. Your voice. The way you say your r. You're not from here."
Something happened in the big red face. Something that was not fear. It was older than fear. And sadder.
"South," said Dace.
"How far south?"
Dace did not answer at once. He looked down at his own arms, tied behind him. Then, slowly, he shifted his weight against the wall. Heaved himself round, a little, onto his side, grunting, so that his left arm came out from behind his back as far as the rope would let it. And turned it, so that the inside of the forearm, below the rolled-up striped sleeve, was toward the lantern light coming from Gant's door.
"There," he said. "That's how far."
Corin had not seen it before. He had seen the big arms, thick as his own legs, covered in reddish hair. He had not looked at them closely. There had not been time.
He looked now.
Under the red hair, on the inside of the left forearm, from the wrist almost to the elbow, there was a picture.
It had been put there with ink and a needle, a long time ago. Corin could tell it was a long time. The lines had gone soft and blurred and spread a little into the skin, the way old writing spreads on wet paper. And the colors had faded. But they were still there. Black. And a deep, rusty red, like dried blood, like brick. And here and there, in the middle, a thin bright line of something that was not quite either. Something that caught the lantern light and shone, very faintly, like a thread of gold.
It was a fire.
No. Not just a fire. Corin bent closer. It was a pile. A square pile of logs, laid crossways, one layer on top of another, like the walls of a little house with no roof. Very carefully drawn. Every log had its rings. And on top of the pile, lying flat, there was a figure. Small. A person, lying on their back, with their arms folded on their chest, the way the dead are laid out in the Ink Quarter, with coins on their eyes.
And out of the pile, round the figure, up the arm toward the elbow, rose the flames.
Three of them. Three long, curving tongues of rusty red, twisting round each other as they went up, the way flames twist in a wind. And at the top, where the three tongues met, they turned to gold. That thin, faint, shining thread. And the gold went on up, past the flames, in a long thin line, toward the elbow. Toward the sky, Corin thought. As if the fire were carrying something up out of the pile, out of the dead figure on top of it, up and up, into the air.
And all round the picture, in a ring, very small, there was writing.
Not letters Corin knew. Not the letters of the city, or the Ministry, or the old tall letters on the bridge plaque. Something else. Little curling marks, like hooks and seeds and tiny birds' feet, all joined together in one long unbroken line that went round and round the pyre without a beginning or an end.
He looked at it for a long time.
"What is it?" he said.
"My mother," said Dace.
Corin looked up.
"Her pyre," said Dace. His voice had changed. The fear had gone out of it, a little. What was left was very low, and slow, and far away. "In my country, when somebody dies, we don't put them in the ground. Not like here. Here you put them in the ground, in the cold and the wet, and the worms have them, and they're gone." His great face twisted. "We burn them. On a pyre. Like that. High. In the open, under the sky. And we stand round all night, and sing, and watch them go."
"Watch them go where?"
"Up," said Dace. He jerked his chin at the arm. At the thin gold line rising from the flames. "When you burn the dead right, on a proper pyre, with the right wood and the right songs, what's in them comes up out of them. All of it. Everything they knew. Everything they were. It comes up in the smoke, like light. Gold light. And the pyre-keepers catch it. In glass. In great glass lamps, on poles, as tall as houses." He was quiet a moment. "And they hang the lamps over the city. Over the streets. Thousands of them. So the dead light the way home for the living. Every night. For a hundred years."
Corin did not say anything.
He was thinking of the gemstone lamps of the city. Blue and cold and humming, on their tall iron posts, all down every street. Burning other people's memories. Pale, thin, borrowed light, that lit nothing but the frost.
"The sky-lamps," said Dace. "That's what we call them. In my country. My mother's in one. Over the Street of Weavers, in the old city, by the river. She was a weaver. She made carpets. Red ones." His pale eyes went past Corin, down the passage, toward Gant's door, and the red rug with its ring of black birds. "Like that one. Only better. Much better."
He looked back down at his arm.
"When she died," he said, "I was ten. They burned her on the hill above the river. And I stood and watched her go up. All night. Till the sun came. And in the morning the old keeper put my arm on a stone and did that." He nodded at the pyre on his skin. "With a bone needle. And ash. Her ash, from the pyre, in the ink. So I'd carry her. So she'd always know where I was." He swallowed. "The writing round it is her name. And mine. And the name of the hill."
"What's it called?" said Corin. "Your country."
Dace looked at him.
"Kesh-Amaran," he said.
He said it in a different voice. Not his own deep slow city voice, with its heavy rolling r. Softer. Higher. With a kind of song in it. The way you say the name of a place you love and have not seen for a very long time. Kesh-Amaran. The second part went up, and then down, like a hill. Like a bird's wing.
"It's south," he said. "Far south. Past the end of the country. Past the mountains. Past the Glass."
"The Glass?"
"The wastes." Dace's face closed a little. "The dead country. Between here and there. Where nothing grows, and the ground's all glass, black and broken, and if you walk on it too long you forget your own name." A pause. "The caravans go round it. The long way. Three months. Four." He looked at his arm. "I came north with a caravan when I was twelve. After my mother. With my uncle. He owed money. Here. To the Bank. And when he died, the debt came to me, the way debts do." His mouth twisted. "So I've been paying it ever since. To her ladyship. In the only coin I've got."
He looked at his own great red-haired hands. Tied behind him.
"My mother would spit on me," he said. "If she saw. She'd come down out of her sky-lamp and spit on me, and go back up, and never light my way again."
Corin took out the Ledger.
He wrote it all down. Crouched in the passage, with the Ledger on his knee and the lantern light from Gant's door falling across the page. The pyre. The three flames, twisting. The gold line going up. The ring of writing like hooks and seeds and birds' feet. Kesh-Amaran. Far south. Past the Glass. Sky-lamps. They burn their dead and keep their light.
He drew the pyre too. As well as he could. The square pile of logs. The figure on top. The three flames. He was not good at drawing. It came out crooked. But he drew it, carefully, every log, and the gold line rising.
Then he looked at the smell again. In his head. The one under the beer and onions. The one he had not known.
Smoke. Burnt sugar. A spice he had no name for. Old dry bones.
Pyre smoke, he wrote. He smells of it. Still. After all these years. It's in his skin. Or in the ink.
Ash in the ink, he wrote, and underlined it. His mother's Ash. In a tattoo. So she'd always know where he was.
He stopped. He thought of Vespera, in her shop on Hook Lane, with her thousand fine lines of writing all over her own arms, and her needles, and her pots of ink. Write only what is true. He thought of Hollis Marsh, who wrote lies into people's skin, and paid for every lie with a true memory of his own.
And he thought of a lady in white, with a silver cup, and a necklace of tiny glowing vials, and Marrowmere gold.
Everything runs into everything else, he thought. Like the dye in the stream.
"Dace," he said.
The big man looked at him. His pale, almost white eyes. The color at the very edge of a candle flame.
"The pyre-keepers," said Corin. "In Kesh-Amaran. The ones who catch the light. What do they catch it with?"
Dace was quiet for a moment.
Then he looked down. At the rod. Lying across Corin's knees, in the lantern light. Long and thin and silver, with its spiral winding round and round it, and the worn word near the thick end.
"With that," he said. Very low. "With rods like that. Silver. With the turning in them." His breath came out of him in a long shaky cloud. "That's why I screamed. When it touched me. I haven't seen one since I was ten years old. Since the hill. Since the morning they burned my mother." He looked up at Corin. "The old keeper held one over her, all night. Over the pyre. And the gold came up out of her and went into it, round and round, like water down a drain. And when it was full, he held it over the glass, and it all poured out."
He shut his eyes.
"Where did you get it?" he said. "That's a keeper's rod. That's from my country. Where did a harvester in a cold city in the north get a keeper's rod from Kesh-Amaran?"
Corin looked down at the rod in his hand.
He did not know.
It had been his for as long as the Ledger went back. Six years. Longer. He had never once wondered where it came from. It was simply his rod, the way his hands were his hands. Long and thin and cold, with a spiral winding round it, and a word cut into the silver near the thick end, worn almost too smooth to read.
MALAKOR.
In his head, the dry old voice had gone completely silent. Not the silence of a closed book. The silence of someone holding their breath.
Tam
Tam spoke at a quarter to twelve.
Corin was kneeling beside him on the red rug, in front of the stove, when he did it. The little brass clock on Mister Gant's desk said a quarter to twelve, in its small neat ticking voice. Above them, through the arched brick roof, the noise of the Bell had changed. The singing had stopped. Corin could hear Moll's big voice, still, rumbling on, telling some long story, and people laughing at it, but fewer people now. Chairs scraping. The front door banging, and banging again, as people went home.
Soon there would be nobody left up there but Moll. And the one on the beer.
And after midnight, the Lady would come down.
Corin had been thinking about that. He had been thinking about it for half an hour, kneeling by the stove with Tam's hands in his, rubbing them, and the eleven packed round the walls of the little room, drinking Gideon's tea and staring at nothing. He had been thinking that they must go. Now. All of them. Up the ice steps and out into the alley and away, before the bells rang twelve. And he had been thinking that half of them could not walk, and the old woman with the white hair could not walk at all, and Tam could not even sit up on his own.
And then Tam's hand moved in his.
Not much. A twitch of the fingers. Then they closed. Weakly. Round Corin's thumb.
Corin looked down.
Tam's eyes were open. Properly open, this time. Not a slit. Brown and wet and tired, under the frost-damp lashes. Looking up. Not at the stove. At Corin.
"Harvester," he said.
It came out rough and cracked, like ice breaking. But it was a word. It was his voice.
"I'm here."
"You came." Tam's mouth moved. Not quite a smile. "Mrs. Pratt said you would. If anybody did." He swallowed. It seemed to hurt him. "She said, That one'll come. He's got a face like a wet week and he never gives up."
"Don't talk," said Corin. "Not yet. You're too cold."
"I have to." Tam's fingers tightened on his thumb. "I have to talk. Before she comes. Before I forget again." His brown eyes went past Corin, quickly, to the desk. To Mister Gant, tied in his chair with the muffler round his mouth. Watching. "He asked me. All day. And all last night. He asked me over and over. And I didn't tell him." His voice cracked. "But I wanted to. By the end. In the ice. I wanted to so much."
"What did he ask?"
Tam looked at him.
"Where we keep the copies," he said.
Corin did not understand. Not at first.
Then he did.
V. K. U. On the corner of a chalkboard in a pen shop on Quill Row. A tiny open book, and an eye, not closed. Veritas Keeps Unbroken. Bram, with his huge red scarred hands, sewing brown cloth coats onto six Ledgers by the light of a lamp. All the Keepers ever wanted kept.
Clerks. Copyists. Men and women with ink on their fingers in every office in the city, in the Ministry, in the Bank, in the Watch house, in the counting houses, who sat at their desks all day and copied what they were told to copy. And at night, in secret, copied what they were told to burn.
"The Keepers," he said.
Tam closed his eyes. Opened them.
"Thirty years," he whispered. "Thirty years we've been copying. Everything they burn. Every file the Ministry sends to be pulped. Every page the Bank tears out of a book. Every name they scratch off a list." His voice was very faint. "We copy it first. Before it goes in the fire. And we put the copies somewhere safe. Where they'll never find them."
"Where?"
Tam's mouth moved.
"I don't know," he said.
Corin stared at him.
"That's the point," said Tam. And this time it was a smile. Small, and cracked, and very tired. "Nobody knows. Not all of it. I know my bit. I copy at the Bourse, at night, in the vault, what the Lady sells and who to. And I give my copies to a man. And he gives them to a woman. And she gives them to someone else. And somewhere at the end of all of it, in the dark, there's a place." He breathed. "I've never seen it. I never will. Nobody sees it but the last one. And nobody knows who the last one is."
He looked past Corin again. At Mister Gant.
"So I couldn't tell him," said Tam. "Even when I wanted to. Even in the ice. Because I didn't know." His eyes came back to Corin's. "That's how we keep it unbroken. Nobody can give it away. Not even if they're frozen. Not even if they're emptied."
Corin did not say anything.
He was thinking of a woman in an attic on Mercer Street, with two thousand people on her shelves. Writing them down, one by one, so they would not be lost. Alone. With nobody to give her copies to.
She should know, he thought. She should know there are others.
He did not say it. Not here. Not with Mister Gant's small pale eyes over the top of the gag.
"Tam," he said instead. "The fiddler. The old man with the red scarf, who plays on the quays. A young fellow paid him a silver, last week, by the Bourse. To learn a tune properly and play it everywhere there's people. The fisherman and the moon."
Tam's eyes changed.
"That was me," he said.
"Why?"
Tam was quiet for a long time. So long that Corin thought he had gone to sleep again. His eyes had closed. His breath had gone slow.
Then, without opening his eyes, very softly, he said:
"Because it stays."
Corin went very still.
"You've seen it," said Tam. "Haven't you. You must have. The ones who've sold everything. Or been emptied. The Slates. They've got nothing left. Not their names. Not their mams. Nothing." His fingers moved on Corin's thumb. "But some of them hum it. That tune. If you play it near them. Not all. Some. They hum the end of it. The bit that comes down. And they don't know why. And for a moment—" He stopped. Breathed. "For a moment they look like somebody's home."
I know that one. Don't I.
Felix, on the plank, with ice on his shoulder.
"Why?" said Corin. Hardly aloud. "Tam. Why does it stay?"
Tam opened his eyes.
"I don't know," he said. "Not really. Nobody does." A pause. "But there's a saying. The old ones say it. The Keepers who've been at it longest. They got it from somewhere north. From the mountains. A long time ago." His voice had gone so faint that Corin had to bend right down, with his ear almost against Tam's cold lips, to hear it.
"What is sung," whispered Tam, "cannot be stolen."
The stove ticked. The little brass clock on the desk ticked.
Ah, said Malakor, very quietly, in the back of Corin's head. And nothing else. Just that. Like a man who has heard a door open somewhere in a house he thought was empty.
"That's why," said Tam. "That's why I paid him. The fiddler. So it's everywhere. So it's on every quay and every street and every bridge. So that every Slate in the city hears it, again and again, until—" He stopped. His face twisted with something that might have been pain. "Until I don't know. Until something. Until somebody wakes up."
"And the Lady found out."
"The Lady found out." Tam's mouth moved. "She found out about the fiddler. And the copies. And the eye on her kitchen wall." A breath. "And Gant asked and asked. And I didn't know where the copies were. And I didn't know why the song stays. And I didn't know where Bram keeps your books, though I do, Saints forgive me. I said I didn't. I said it two hundred times."
He was crying. Corin saw it. Thin cold tears, running down out of the corners of his eyes, into his damp dark hair. He did not seem to know.
"And then he put me in the ice," said Tam. "And after a while I didn't want to tell him anything. I didn't want anything at all. It was so quiet in there. So quiet. And I thought, this is what it's like. For them. For the Slates. This is what it's like to have nothing." His fingers tightened. "And it wasn't so bad. That's the worst of it, harvester. It wasn't so bad. It was peaceful."
Corin held his hand.
"And then I heard it," said Tam.
"Heard what?"
"The song." Tam's eyes went wide. Wet and brown and wide, in the firelight. "In the ice. In the blue. Just at the end. Before you came. Very far away. Somebody humming it. The end of it. The bit that comes down." His voice cracked. "And I didn't want to be peaceful anymore. I wanted to be cold. I wanted to be cold, and frightened, and me."
Corin's hand went to his coat.
To the right side. To the deep inside pocket. To the bandaged brown jar, still warm, humming its one low note, steady and soft, the note it had hummed since the crypt. The fourth note. The one that came down.
He had crawled into the ice with it against his ribs.
He had not even thought about it.
What is sung cannot be stolen.
Tam's eyes had closed again. His breathing had gone slow and deep. His hand was still round Corin's thumb, but loosely now. Asleep.
Corin sat on the red rug, in front of the stove, with Tam's hand in his and his own hand flat on his coat over the humming jar, and he did not move for a long time.
On the desk, the little brass clock said ten to twelve.
And from above, very faint, through the arched brick roof, through the floor of the Bell, through everything, Corin heard wheels on the cobbles of Cinder Lane. Slow. Heavy. Stopping.
