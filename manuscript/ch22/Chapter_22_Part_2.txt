Chapter 22: The Den on Cinder Lane, Part 2 of 5: Do Mind the Rug
Do Mind the Rug
Corin went in.
He did not mean to. He meant to go back down the passage, to the barred door and old Lom asleep on his stool with the keys on a string round his wrist. To the girl who was crying. He meant to leave this small neat man in his cardigan sitting at his desk, and let Wat watch him, and go and do the thing he had come to do.
But his feet took him in. Past Dace, slumped against the wall with his wrist in his lap. Past Wat, with the curved knife in his hand and blood running into his beard. Along the short wet passage, past the cracked hatch with the white mist pouring out of it. Through the green door.
Onto the rug.
It was thick and soft under his boots, after the stone. Red, with a pattern of little birds on it. Blackbirds, he thought. Or crows. Flying round and round in a ring, beak to tail, beak to tail, all the way round the edge.
The room was warm. The little iron stove in the corner glowed red, and the kettle on it had begun to sing, very softly. The green-shaded lamp on the desk made a pool of clear light on the green leather, and the open black book, and the brass pen stand. And on the shelf on the wall, the row of tall thin black books stood shoulder to shoulder, perhaps twenty of them, each with a white label on its spine. Corin could read the nearest. Accounts. Year of the Wet Spring. And the next. Year of the Late Thaw. And the next.
Mister Gant watched him look.
"Thirty-one years," he said. "Those. Every day of them." He folded his small neat hands on the open book in front of him. "I've never missed one. Not even the day my wife died. I wrote her in, that night, in the column at the end. Mrs. A. Gant. Closed. It seemed only right."
He smiled. A small, dry, polite smile, like a crack in a plate.
"Do sit down," he said. "You look tired. There's a chair. I'm afraid it's a hard one. I don't encourage visitors to stay."
Corin did not sit down.
He stood in the middle of the red rug with the silver rod in his numb right hand and the dark lantern in his left, and he looked at the small neat man behind the desk. At the gray cardigan. At the thin gray hair combed so carefully across the top of the head. At the gold spectacles. At the hands.
They were small, clean hands. Pale and soft. With a ridge of hard yellow skin on the inside of the middle finger of the right hand, where a pen had rested for thirty-one years.
The same as Wren's.
Corin looked away from them.
"You know who I am," he said.
"Of course I know who you are." Mister Gant took off his gold spectacles and polished them, slowly, on a little square of gray cloth that he took from his cardigan pocket. "Everybody who matters knows who you are, Mister Vane. That is rather your trouble. You are the most famous man in the city who cannot remember his own breakfast." He held the spectacles up to the lamp, and looked through them, and went on polishing. "Her ladyship has talked of nothing else since Thursday. It has been very tedious. One gets so tired of hearing about other people's harvesters."
"Where's Tam?"
Mister Gant put his spectacles back on.
"Keeping," he said.
Corin's hand tightened on the rod.
"He's quite well," said Mister Gant. "Quite unharmed. More or less. I asked him some questions, last night and today, and he was very good about answering some of them and very stubborn about answering others, and in the end I got tired, and so did he, and I put him somewhere cool to rest." He nodded, very slightly, at the open door. At the passage beyond it. At the hatch. "Her ladyship likes them rested, when she comes down. She says it makes them easier to read."
"In the cold room."
"In the cold room." Mister Gant tilted his head. "It's not as bad as it sounds. Truly. It's very clean. No rats. Rats don't like it. And the cold, after a while, is quite soothing. People stop struggling. They stop crying. They become—" he searched for the word "—still."
The kettle on the stove sang a little louder.
"How long?" said Corin. His voice did not sound like his own. "How long has he been in there?"
Mister Gant looked at a small brass clock on the corner of his desk.
"Since six o'clock," he said. "Four hours and a quarter." He looked up. "He'll be quite cold by now. But he'll keep. They always keep. That's the beauty of north ice."
Corin turned to go.
He did not decide to. His body decided. It turned on the red rug toward the green door and the passage and the hatch, and his legs were already moving.
"Before you go," said Mister Gant.
Corin stopped. He did not know why. He hated himself for it.
"Before you go, Mister Vane," said Mister Gant, quite gently, "and pull that poor boy out of the ice, and carry him up the steps, and unlock my counting room, and let out all my people, and run away with them into the night, as I'm sure you're going to do, being the sort of man you are—" He laid one small pale hand flat on the open black book in front of him. "I wonder if you'd like to see what you're stealing."
Corin turned round.
"I'm not stealing anything."
"Oh, but you are." Mister Gant's small dry smile did not change. "You are stealing eleven debts. Freely entered into. Witnessed. Signed. Written down." He tapped the book. "Look. Come and look. I'd like you to. I've always wondered what a harvester would make of my accounts."
Corin did not move.
Then, slowly, he came across the red rug to the desk, and looked down.
The book lay open under the green lamp.
It was a ledger. A real one. A clerk's ledger, with ruled columns down each page, in faint red and blue ink, and a narrow column on the left for the date, and wide ones across the middle, and a narrow one on the right at the end. The paper was thick and good and creamy. And the writing was small and neat and even and very black. A careful hand. A tidy hand. Every letter the same size. Every line the same distance from the next.
Corin read the top line of the open page.
Sunday. 3rd bell after noon. Rennick, J. (docker, aged 40). Fear of the dark (renewed, good), 2c. Grief, for wife (still strong), 4c. Hunger (poor, thin), 1c. Total taken: 7c. Owed this day: 3s. 4c. Balance owing: 4g. 11s. 2c.
And under it:
Sunday. 3rd bell after noon. Prewitt, M. (washerwoman, aged 33). Fear for children (very strong, excellent crop), 6c. Shame (good), 3c. Total taken: 9c. Owed this day: 2s. 8c. Balance owing: 7g. 2s. 0c.
And under that:
Sunday. 3rd bell after noon. Tilly (no other name; aged about 12). Fear (very strong, constant), 8c. Homesickness (strong), 5c. Loneliness (strong), 4c. Total taken: 17c. Owed this day: 1s. 0c. Balance owing: 0g. 19s. 5c. Note: girl's yield remains high. Keep.
Corin stopped reading.
He stood over the desk with his hand on the edge of it and the green light on his face and he could not read any more. The words had gone strange and swimming, like words seen through water.
Note: girl's yield remains high. Keep.
"You see," said Mister Gant, in his soft, pleasant voice, "it's all quite proper. All quite fair. Every copper accounted for. They each owe a sum. A sum they agreed to, when they borrowed. And every day, I take a little of what they have, at a fair price, and I write it down, and I take it off what they owe." He turned the page back, and back again, so that Corin could see the columns going on and on, line after line, name after name. "And every day, they owe a little more. Because a debt is a living thing, Mister Vane. It grows. Like a child. It has to be fed." He smiled. "So the balance never quite goes down. But it isn't my fault. It's arithmetic. I don't make arithmetic. I simply write it down."
Corin looked up from the page.
He looked at the small neat man in the gray cardigan for a long, long moment. At his soft clean hands. At his gold spectacles. At his small dry polite smile.
And he thought of Gideon. Eight years old. On a glass. With Big Harl's hand holding his hand down, laughing. Being hungry, and being cold, and being frightened. Three years of it. Every morning and every night. Until there was nothing left.
There's nothing left in this one. He's hollow as a bell.
"You write it all down," said Corin.
"Every word."
"So somebody knows it happened."
Mister Gant blinked.
Just once. Behind his gold spectacles. A small, quick, puzzled blink. As if Corin had said something in a foreign language.
"So that it balances, Mister Vane," he said. "So that it balances. What else would one write things down for?"
Corin took the book.
He did not decide to do that either. His hand did it. It reached out over the green leather and closed on the open ledger and shut it, with a soft thick clap, and picked it up. It was heavy. Heavier than the Ledger over his heart. Thick creamy paper, and a binding of black leather, and a brass clasp.
Mister Gant watched him do it. He did not try to stop him.
"That's this year's," he said mildly. "There are thirty more on the shelf."
"I know."
"You can't carry them all."
"No." Corin put the ledger under his arm, the left one, with the dark lantern. "But I can carry this one. And I can tell people the others are here."
"Which people?"
Corin did not answer.
Mister Gant looked at him for a moment longer. Then, slowly, he picked up his pen again from its brass stand. And dipped it. And drew toward him, from a drawer in the desk, a fresh sheet of creamy paper.
"Do give my regards to Gideon," he said. Without looking up. "I saw him on the steps behind you, when Dace went out. I'd know those ears anywhere." He began to write. "He was a very good crop, once, you know. One of my best. It's such a pity he dried up so young."
Corin stood on the red rug with the ledger under his arm and looked down at the top of the small neat gray head, bent over the paper, writing.
Do not, said Malakor. Very quietly. I know what you want to do, boy. Do not. He is not worth the Ash.
Corin turned and walked out of the room.
The Cold Room
"Tie him," said Corin to Wat, in the passage. "Gant. To his chair. Gag him. He'll shout the moment we're gone."
Wat looked at him. Then at the green door. Then down at Dace, slumped against the brick wall with his wrist in his lap and his face the color of cheese, staring at the red mark that had been a silver spiral.
"And this one?"
"Him too. But not yet." Corin's voice came out hard and flat. "I want him able to talk."
Wat nodded slowly. He wiped his bloody nose on the back of his great hand. He went into the little warm room with the red rug, ducking under the lintel, and Corin heard Mister Gant say, mildly, "Oh, really, is this necessary?" and then the scrape of a chair, and the sound of a muffler being torn, and then nothing.
Corin knelt in front of the hatch.
The iron bolt was bent where Dace had hit it with his back. Bent and jammed. Corin pulled at it with his left hand. It did not move. The frost on the iron burned through his glove.
"Gideon," he said.
Gideon was there. Corin had not heard him come. He was standing at Corin's shoulder in his huge stuffed boots, looking down at the hatch.
"Hold the lantern. Please."
Gideon took the dark lantern. Corin opened the shutter all the way, and the yellow light jumped out across the wet floor and the frosted wood. Then he took the silver rod in his left hand, the good one, and put the thin end of it into the bend of the bolt, and leaned on it.
The rod did not bend. Silver should have bent. It did not. It was as stiff as iron and colder than the frost, and it went into the bend of the bolt like a key into a lock, and Corin leaned, and leaned, and something in the bolt gave with a squeal, and the bolt slid back.
He pulled the hatch open.
The cold came out like a wall.
It hit him in the face and the chest and knocked the breath out of him. Not the cold of the night. Not even the cold of the white mist creeping out round the edges. Something past cold. Something so cold that it felt, for one moment, like heat. Like putting your face into an oven. His eyes watered and the water froze at once on his lashes. His nose and his lips went numb. The skin of his face tightened, all over, like wet leather drying in the sun.
And the smell came with it. Brass. Oil. Dust. And under it, faint and sweet and far away, rain on a summer road.
He pushed the lantern forward into the hole.
It was a room. A small one. Long and low, like a cupboard dug into the earth. The walls and the floor and the low curved roof were all brick, like the passage, but you could not see the brick. It was hidden under ice. A thick rough skin of white rime, an inch deep, over every surface, so that the whole room looked as if it had been carved out of a block of snow. Long icicles hung from the roof, as thick as Corin's arm, as long as his leg. When the lantern light touched them, they lit up from inside, pale and blue.
And along both sides of the room, there were shelves.
Wooden shelves, rough planks, white with frost. And on the shelves, packed close in straw, the ice.
Wat had been right. It was not river ice.
River ice was gray. Cloudy. Full of bubbles and bits of grass and muck. This was not gray. This was blue. A deep, clear, glowing blue, all the way through, like the color at the bottom of the sea, like the color of the sky on a winter evening just before the stars come out. Blocks of it. Square ones, as big as loaves of bread, stacked on the straw in rows. And when the lantern light went into them, it did not stop at the surface. It went in. Deep in. And the blocks lit up from inside, one after another, all down the room, as if every block had a small blue candle burning in its heart.
And in the heart of every block, there was something.
Corin saw it in the nearest one. Frozen into the middle of the blue ice, the way a fly is frozen into amber. A jar. A small glass jar, no bigger than a thumb, with a cork in its neck. And in the jar, very faint, a glow. Not violet, like the crates. Not blue, like the ice. Gray. A thin, cold, wavering gray, like breath on a window.
And tied to the cork of the little jar, on a thread, frozen into the ice with it, a label.
Corin could read it through the blue. He bent close. The cold burned his face.
Cold. Boy. Aged 10. Winter. Strong. 3c.
He straightened up.
He looked along the shelf. At the next block. And the next. And the next. All down the long low room, on both sides, row after row after row. Blue blocks, glowing. And in every one, a small jar. And on every jar, a label.
Fear of dogs. Woman. Aged 30. Strong. 2c.
Cold. Old man. Aged 70. Very strong. 4c.
Shame. Girl. Aged 14. Good. 3c.
Cold. Child. Aged 6. Excellent. 5c.
Hundreds of them. Frozen. Kept. Like fruit in a cellar.
At the far end of the room, against the back wall, there were crates. Wooden ones. Long and plain, nailed shut, with rope handles, and straw sticking out of the cracks. Three of them, stacked. And stenciled on the end of each one, in black paint, in square letters, frosted over but still clear:
FOR THE REACH. CARE OF THE OVERSEER. NYM.
Corin did not know what it meant. He wrote it down later, word for word. For the Reach. Care of the Overseer. Nym. And under it, Where is Nym? What overseer? He did not know then that he would carry that line in the Ledger for a long time before he found out.
Beside him, Gideon had gone very still.
He was holding the lantern up, the way Corin had asked. His thin bare arm was steady. But he was not looking at the crates. He was looking at the first shelf. At the first block of ice. At the small glowing jar frozen in its heart, with its label.
Cold. Boy. Aged 10. Winter. Strong. 3c.
He looked at it for a long time.
Then he put out his free hand. Slowly. And laid it flat on the side of the blue block.
Corin waited for him to snatch it back. The ice was so cold that it would burn, he thought. It would stick to the skin and tear it.
Gideon did not snatch it back.
He left his hand there, flat on the ice. His thin blue fingers spread out against the glowing blue. And Corin saw, in the lantern light, that every small hair on the boy's bare forearm was standing on end.
Gideon did not say anything. He did not shiver. He just stood there, with his hand on the ice and his pale eyes on the small gray jar inside it, and the tiny crease between his eyebrows, very deep now.
Then he took his hand away.
There was skin on the ice. A thin pale print of a small hand, frozen to the blue, where he had touched it. And on Gideon's palm, when he turned it over and looked at it, a raw red patch, shining.
He looked at that for a moment, too. With mild interest.
"It doesn't hurt," he said.
"Gideon—"
"It doesn't hurt." He lowered the hand. "I just wanted to see. If it would."
He held the lantern higher.
"Tam's at the back," he said. "Behind the crates. I can see his boots."
Corin went in.
He had to crawl. The roof was too low to stand, and the floor was a sheet of ice. He went on his hands and knees between the shelves of glowing blue blocks, with the frozen labels on either side of him, and the cold going into his knees through his trousers like knives, and his breath going out in front of him in clouds so thick he could hardly see. The rod was in his coat. His bad hand, on the ice, screamed at him. He did not listen to it.
Behind the three crates, in the corner, where the lantern light only just reached, there was a shape.
Sitting. On the floor. With its back against the iced brick wall and its knees drawn up to its chest and its arms wrapped round its knees. Very small. Folded up tight, the way a child folds itself up in the cold, to keep the last of the warm inside.
It was white. All over. A fine white fur of frost, on the dark hair, on the shoulders of the dark footman's coat, on the backs of the hands clasped round the knees. On the eyelashes. On the eyebrows. On the small white scar through the left eyebrow, like a tick on a page.
The eyes were shut.
"Tam," said Corin.
Nothing.
He crawled the last of the way. He knelt in front of the folded white shape on the ice and put out his good hand and touched the face.
It was like touching marble. Hard and smooth and cold. So cold. He held his hand there and could not feel anything at all, not warmth, not breath, not the faintest movement, and the cold went up his arm and into his chest and closed round his heart like a fist.
No, he thought. No. No. Not this. Not after Wick. Not this.
And then, under his hand, very faint, so faint that he thought at first he had made it up, he felt something.
A breath.
One. Slow and shallow and thin as a thread. Against the side of his palm. Out of the frozen nose.
And another.
Corin took his hand away. He took off his coat.
He did not think about Malakor, or about walking, or about running, or about holding a rod. He took off his gray coat, with the Ledger in it and the jar in it and the rod in it, and he wrapped it round the folded white shape on the ice, round the shoulders, round the knees, as tight as he could. And then he got his arms under it, one under the knees and one under the back, and lifted.
Tam was light. Much lighter than Corin had thought. Like lifting a bundle of sticks.
He could not stand up. The roof was too low. So he went back the way he had come, on his knees, shuffling backward, with Tam in his arms, between the glowing blue shelves with their hundreds of small frozen jars and their hundreds of labels. Cold. Fear. Shame. Cold. Cold. Cold. The way you carry something out of a burning house.
At the hatch, Wat's great hands came in and took Tam from him, gently, the way you take a baby, and lifted him out into the passage.
And then Gideon's thin blue hand came in, and took hold of Corin's wrist, and pulled.
He pulled very hard. For a boy who did not care.
