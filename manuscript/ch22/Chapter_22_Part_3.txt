Chapter 22: The Den on Cinder Lane, Part 3 of 5: The Counting Room
The Counting Room
They put Tam in front of Mister Gant's stove.
On the red rug, with the ring of little black birds flying round the edge of it. Wat laid him down there, very carefully, still folded up tight in Corin's gray coat, with his knees to his chest and his white-frosted head bent. And Corin knelt beside him and pulled the coat tighter, and rubbed his hands, his frozen hands, between his own, the way the Ledger said you should do for a man brought in out of the snow. Not too hard. Not too fast. Just rubbing, slowly, over and over, the way you rub a cold dog.
The frost on Tam's hair began to melt. It ran down his face in thin cold drops, like tears.
Behind the desk, Mister Gant sat in his high-backed chair with his arms tied to the arms of it with his own cardigan sleeves, and Wat's torn muffler round his mouth, and his gold spectacles still on his nose, a little crooked. He watched them. He did not struggle. His small pale eyes went from Tam to Corin and back, over the top of the gag, with mild interest. The way a man watches someone else do a sum wrong.
The kettle on the stove had begun to boil. Gideon took it off. Nobody asked him to. He just took it off, with his bare hand round the hot iron handle, and stood holding it, and looked at Corin.
"Do you want tea?" he said. "There's tea. In the tin. He keeps it for himself. The good kind."
Corin looked up at him.
"For Tam," he said. "Hot. Not too hot. With sugar. A lot of sugar."
Gideon nodded. He found the tin. He found a cup. He found the sugar, in a blue paper bag in the desk drawer, beside a little silver bell with a wooden handle, the kind you ring for a servant.
Gideon took the sugar and left the bell. He made the tea. He did it slowly and carefully and exactly, like someone following a recipe he had read but never cooked. And he brought it to Corin, holding it in both his thin blue hands, the right one with its raw red palm, and did not spill a drop.
Corin held it to Tam's lips.
Tam did not drink. His lips were blue-gray and stiff and would not open. The tea ran down his chin, hot, steaming, onto the gray coat.
Corin tried again. And again. Slowly. A drop at a time.
The fourth time, Tam's lips moved.
Just a little. Just enough. A drop of hot sweet tea went in between them. And Tam's throat moved. And swallowed.
And his eyes opened.
Not much. A slit. Just enough to show a line of brown, wet and dull, under the frosted lashes. They did not look at anything. They looked at the stove. At the red glow through its little iron door. They stayed there, on the red, the way a moth's eyes stay on a candle.
"Tam," said Corin. "It's me. Corin. The harvester. You're out. You're out of the ice."
The brown eyes did not move from the stove.
But his lips moved again. Very slightly. Without sound. Shaping a word.
Corin bent close.
It came out of him so faint that it was hardly a breath. Hardly a word. A shape, pushed out into the warm air between frozen lips.
"—gravy—"
Corin's eyes stung.
"It didn't catch," he said. "She said to tell you. Mrs. Pratt. The gravy didn't catch. She's kept your supper. In the warming oven."
Tam's eyes closed again.
But the corner of his frozen mouth moved. Just a little. Up.
Corin left him there. With Gideon kneeling beside him, holding the cup, giving him tea a drop at a time, very carefully, the way Corin had shown him. And with Wat in the doorway, filling it, watching the passage. And with Mister Gant behind his desk, tied to his chair, watching all of them.
He went back along the passage to the counting room. Without his coat.
The cold hit him at once. In his shirt and his waistcoat, in the wet brick passage, he was shaking within ten steps. His teeth chattered. He did not try to stop them. He went past the frosted hatch, still open, still breathing out its white mist. Past Dace, sitting against the wall, tied now, with his own leather belt round his ankles and Wat's rope round his wrists, staring at the floor. Round the corner. Past the barrels and the cat.
Old Lom was still asleep.
He had not moved. All that noise. The crash of the hatch. Dace's roar, and his scream. The fight. And the old gray man was still sitting on his stool beside the barred door, with his back against the wall and his chin on his chest and his hands folded on his stomach, snoring his little whistling snore.
Corin looked at him closer.
The old man's ears. There was something in them. Something brown and soft and round, pushed deep into each one. Wax. Candle wax, rolled into balls.
Deaf, thought Corin. On purpose.
He could not hear the crying. That was why. He had stopped his own ears so that he could sit here, every night, beside the bars, and sleep.
Corin knelt beside the stool. He took the ring of keys off the old man's wrist. The string was tied in a knot, and his fingers were too cold and stiff to undo it, so he took Harl's long knife from his belt and cut it. The keys came away into his hand. Big black iron ones. Six of them, on a ring. Heavy. Cold.
Old Lom snored on.
Corin stood up, and turned, and looked through the bars.
It was dark in there.
He had not brought the lantern. He had left it with Gideon. The only light came from the lamp round the corner, far down the passage, and from the yellow glow at the bottom of the ice steps, and none of it reached far through the bars. He could see a floor of stone flags, covered in old straw, black and rotten. A wall, curving away, wet. A bucket.
And shapes. In the dark. Against the wall. On the straw.
They did not move.
That was what made his stomach turn. Eleven people, Gideon had said. Seven men. Three women. One girl. And not one of them had moved. Not when the hatch crashed open. Not when Dace roared. Not when Wat hit him like a falling wall and the whole cellar shook. They had stayed where they were, on the straw, in the dark, against the wall, perfectly still.
Because noise, down here, meant something was coming. And when something was coming, you kept still. You kept very still. And hoped it was coming for someone else.
Only the crying had not stopped. Small and thin and hopeless, from somewhere at the back. Going on and on.
Corin found the right key on the fourth try. It was the biggest, and the most rusted, and the lock fought him for a long time. Then it turned, with a sound like a bone breaking, and the barred door swung inward a few inches, on its own weight, and stopped.
Nobody moved.
"It's all right," said Corin.
His voice came out wrong. Shaking, with the cold. Too loud in the silence. He made it quieter.
"It's all right," he said again. "My name is Corin Vane. I'm a harvester. The men are gone. Most of them. The ones who are left are tied up. Mister Gant is tied to his chair." He swallowed. "The door's open. You can come out."
Nothing.
The crying stopped.
All at once. In the middle of a breath. The way a bird stops singing when a cat comes into the garden.
The silence after it was worse than the crying had been.
Corin stood in the open door with the ring of keys in his hand and his teeth chattering and looked into the dark. And slowly, very slowly, as his eyes got used to it, he began to see faces.
They were turned toward him. All of them. Pale smudges in the black. Eleven of them. Lying on the straw, sitting against the wall, crouched in the corner. Looking at him. Not moving. Not speaking. Not believing.
He understood then.
They had heard it before. Or something like it. It's all right. You can come out. And they had come out, and it had not been all right. It had been Mister Gant, smiling his small dry smile, with his black book open and his pen in his hand. Or Big Harl, laughing, holding a hand down on a glass.
They did not believe him. Why would they? He was a voice in the dark.
Corin did the only thing he could think of.
He knelt down.
There, in the open doorway. On the wet stone flags, in his shirtsleeves, shaking with cold. He knelt down so that his face was at the same height as theirs. And he held out his hands, empty, palms up, so that they could see there was nothing in them.
And he waited.
It was a long time.
Then one of the shapes moved.
A small one. At the back, in the corner, where the crying had been. It moved slowly. Not standing up. Crawling. On its hands and knees through the black rotten straw, toward the door, toward the faint yellow light from the passage. A little at a time. Stopping. Coming on.
It came into the light.
A girl. About twelve. Very thin, much too thin, in a dress that had been blue once and was now no color at all, and a shawl, and no shoes. Her hair was long and dark and tangled and matted with straw. Her face was a small white wedge, all eyes. Great dark eyes, red-rimmed and swollen, ringed with gray shadows like bruises. Her cheeks were raw. Raw and chapped and cracked, from crying. From months of crying, Corin thought. From crying every day, and every night, until the salt of it had worn the skin away.
She stopped an arm's length from him. On her hands and knees in the straw. And looked at him.
Tilly. No other name. Aged about twelve. Fear, very strong, constant. Homesickness, strong. Loneliness, strong. Note: girl's yield remains high. Keep.
"Tilly," said Corin.
The girl flinched. As if he had hit her.
"How do you know my name?" Her voice was a whisper. Cracked and rough and small. "Only he says my name. Only Mister Gant."
Corin did not know what to say. So he told her the truth.
"I read it," he said. "In his book. I took his book. It's upstairs. I'm going to take it away and show it to people, so that everybody knows what he did." He held her red, swollen eyes. "I'm sorry I read your name in it. I won't say it again if you don't want me to."
Tilly stared at him.
Then her face crumpled. All at once. Like a paper bag. And she did not cry. She had cried so much that there was nothing left to cry with. She just knelt there in the straw with her face crumpled up and her thin shoulders shaking, and no sound coming out at all.
And then she came the last arm's length, on her hands and knees, and put her face against Corin's chest, against his wet cold shirt, and held on.
He put his arms round her.
She was as light as Tam. Lighter. She smelled of straw and fear and old sweat. She was shaking so hard that her teeth clattered. He held her, kneeling in the doorway of the counting room, and he did not say anything, because there was nothing to say.
Behind her, in the dark, the other shapes began to move.
Slowly. One by one. Out of the straw. Off the wall. Out of the corner. Seven men and three women, thin and gray and filthy, in rags, blinking, shuffling, holding on to each other. Coming toward the open door. Toward the light.
The first of them, a big gaunt man with a docker's hook still hanging from his belt and a face like an empty sack, stopped in the doorway and looked down at Corin, kneeling there with the girl in his arms.
"Is it true?" he said. His voice was hoarse, as if he had not used it in a long time. "Gant's tied?"
"It's true."
The big gaunt man looked past him. Along the passage. At old Lom on his stool, snoring, with wax in his ears.
His face did not change. But something came into his eyes that Corin had seen once before, two hours ago, in the face of a sergeant of the Watch, standing in a frozen yard with the two halves of a broken pipe in his hand.
"Rennick," said the big gaunt man. "John Rennick. Docker. Pike Dock." He held out a hand. It was shaking. "I owe four gold, eleven silver and two coppers. Or I did, this afternoon. I don't know what I owe now. He'll have put it up."
Corin took the hand.
"You don't owe anything," he said. "Not anymore. Not to him."
John Rennick looked at him for a long time.
Then he looked down at his own hand, in Corin's. And he began, very quietly, to laugh. Or to cry. Corin could not tell which. Neither, he thought, could John Rennick.
Beer and Onions
They brought the eleven up the passage in a line.
Slowly. Holding on to each other. Holding on to the wet brick walls. Some of them could hardly walk. They had been sitting in the straw in the dark for so long, Corin thought, that their legs had forgotten what legs were for. A thin old woman with white hair down to her waist had to be carried, by John Rennick and another man, one on each side, with her arms round their necks and her bare feet dragging on the flagstones. A young man with a black eye and a bandaged hand walked with his eyes shut, holding on to the back of the coat of the man in front, like a blind man.
Tilly walked beside Corin. She would not let go of his sleeve. She held it in both her thin hands, a fistful of his wet shirt, and walked when he walked and stopped when he stopped, and did not look at anything but the floor.
They did not look at Dace as they passed him.
Not one of them. He sat against the wall, tied, with his great red-haired arms behind him and his boots strapped together with his own belt, and his small pale eyes on the floor, and they went past him in their line, close enough to touch, and not one of them turned their head. As if he were a sack of grain. As if he were not there.
Corin thought that was the most frightening thing he had seen all night.
They put them in Mister Gant's room. All of them. It was the only warm place. They sat on the red rug, and on the floor round it, and against the walls, in front of the glowing stove, packed close, the way sheep pack close in a storm. Gideon gave them tea. He made it the way he had made it for Tam, slowly and carefully and exactly, one cup at a time, and carried each one round in both his hands, and gave it to someone, and went back for the next.
They stared at him. At his bare thin arms and his torn shirt and his huge stuffed boots. At his white face.
One of the women started to cry when he gave her the cup. Not loudly. Just tears, running down her gray face, while she held the cup in both hands and looked at him.
"You," she said. "You were here. Before. I remember you. You were the little one. On the glass."
"Yes," said Gideon.
"You used to scream," said the woman. "Every morning. You used to scream for your mam."
Gideon looked at her for a long moment.
"I don't remember," he said politely. And went back for the next cup.
Behind his desk, tied to his chair, with the muffler round his mouth, Mister Gant watched his crop drink his tea.
Corin went back out into the passage.
He had his coat again. Tam was out of it now, sitting up by the stove, wrapped in a blanket off Gant's own narrow cot in the corner, with his eyes half open and the frost all melted off his hair and the color slowly, slowly coming back into his face, gray to white to the faintest pink. He had not spoken again. But he was breathing. Deep and slow and even. And when Corin had knelt beside him to take the coat back, Tam's hand had come out of the blanket and closed for a moment round his wrist, very weak, and let go.
Corin had put the coat on. It was warm from Tam. It smelled of frost and ice and brass and oil and summer rain. He had felt the Ledger, safe against his heart. And the jar in the deep pocket on the right, still humming its one low note. And the rod beside it.
He had taken the rod out again.
He held it now in his left hand, the good one, as he walked along the short wet passage toward the big man sitting against the wall. Past the hatch, still open, still breathing out its white mist. He stopped in front of Dace.
Dace did not look up.
He sat with his back against the brick and his chin on his chest and his small pale eyes on the flagstones between his strapped-together boots. His face was still the color of cheese. Sweat stood on it, in the cold, in big shining drops, on his forehead and his upper lip and the sides of his thick red nose. His breath came short and fast.
And he was looking, Corin saw, not at the floor. At his wrist. His right wrist, tied behind him, twisted round so that he could just see it past his own hip. At the place where the red mark was. Where, for one moment, there had been a thin bright silver spiral.
Corin crouched down in front of him.
The smell came off the big man like heat off a stove. Beer and onions. Sweat. Fear. And something else, under it all, that Corin did not know.
Smoke. Not coal smoke. Not wood smoke. Something sweeter and sharper and stranger. Like burnt sugar, and a spice he had no name for, and old dry bones.
"Dace," he said.
The big man's eyes came up. Slowly. From his wrist to Corin's face.
They were the palest eyes Corin had ever seen. Paler than Gideon's. Paler than Kross's, in the yard. Almost white. The blue in them was so faint that it was like the blue at the very edge of a candle flame, the part you can hardly see. And they were frightened. Not of Corin. Of something else. Of something behind Corin. Or inside him.
"What did you do to me?" said Dace. His deep slow voice, with its rolling r, had gone hoarse. "With that. That silver. What did you do?"
"I don't know," said Corin. Which was true. "What did it feel like?"
Dace stared at him.
"Like him," he said.
Corin waited.
"Like the boy," said Dace. "The little clerk. In the lane." His big red face worked. "When it touched me. When your silver touched me. I felt—" He stopped. Swallowed. The sweat ran down his face. "I felt his foot. Cold. In my hands. Like I was pulling his boot off again, after. With the laces still tied. And he was lying there on the heap, looking up at me, with his eyes open, and there was nothing in them. Nothing." He shut his pale eyes. "Like it was now. Like it was happening now. And I could feel it in my bones. I could feel it in my bones."
He opened his eyes.
"What are you?" he said. "What are you?"
Corin did not answer.
He was thinking of a lane behind a printing house. Of a young man lying on a heap of rubbish with his arms spread out and his eyes open, looking up at a gray strip of sky. Brown hair, curly, too long for a clerk. A round, freckled face. A wide mouth that looked as though it was used to laughing. One boot off. A red sock in the toe of it, darned at the heel with blue thread.
He did not remember any of it. It was in the Ledger.
But his body remembered. His hand on the rod remembered. And something deeper than that, something under the breastbone, behind the place where he kept the things he had written down, remembered too. A huge hand. Beer and onions. A cup with a spiral in it, cold against the lips.
Pell's dying, he thought again. Some of it stayed. It always stays.
And the rod remembers too.
"I'm the harvester," he said. "The one who came to the lane. The one who took Pell Dorran's last minutes out of him." He held the pale frightened eyes. "I've got them in me, Dace. Some of them. Still. Under everything. A big man's hand over his mouth. Beer and onions. A lantern swinging. And afterward, when he was lying on the heap, somebody pulling at his boot." A pause. "That was you. Wasn't it. Not the hand. The boot."
Dace made a sound. A low, thick, frightened sound, deep in his great chest, like a cow in a storm.
"I didn't kill him," he said. "I didn't. I swear it. I swear it by the fire. I never touched him while he was alive. Not once. Gorse held him. Gorse always does the holding. I just held the lamp. At the mouth of the lane. And after, when it was done, she said, Find the book, Dace, he'll have hidden it on him, and I pulled off his boot." His voice cracked. "And then the old man trod on a bottle, round the corner, and she said leave it, and we went. That's all. That's all I did. I just held the lamp while she—"
"While she what?"
Dace shook his head. Slowly. From side to side. His big shaved head rolling against the wet brick.
"I can't," he said. "I can't. She'll know. She knows everything. She'll know I said."
"Who will?"
"Her." The word came out of him in a whisper. "The white one. With the cup. With the flowers."
Not Vivienne, thought Corin. The white one.
"You work for Gant," he said. "Gant works for the Lady of the Bourse. For Vivienne. Doesn't he?"
"Gant works for her ladyship. Yes. The Lady in the dome." Dace's pale eyes went, very quickly, toward the green door. Toward the little warm room, where Mister Gant sat tied to his chair. And back. "But she lends me. Her ladyship. When the other one asks. The white one. They do business, the two of them. They help each other. One hand washes the other, Gant says." He licked his dry lips. "The white one asks for a big man, for a night's work, and her ladyship says, Take Dace, he's very strong and very quiet, and I go. And the white one pays. In gold." His mouth twisted. "Not our gold. Foreign gold. Little rings of it, with a hole in the middle. With a mask on."
Ring-pieces of Marrowmere gold, thought Corin. Five masks. Five different hands. Niles, the pawnbroker, in his back room on Gutter Street, laying them out on the counter one by one.
"How many times?" he said. "How many nights' work?"
Dace did not answer.
"How many, Dace?"
"Six," said Dace. Hardly aloud. "Seven. Over the years. I don't know. I don't count." He shut his eyes again. "The scribe in the lane was the last. Before that, a man on a bridge. Two winters ago. Under a bridge. At the landing. In the dark."
Corin went very still.
"A doctor," he said.
Dace's eyes came open.
They looked at Corin. Huge and pale and frightened. And in them, Corin saw the answer before the big man said a word.
"That was Gorse's," said Dace. "Not mine. I swear it. I wasn't on the landing. I was at the top of the steps, on the river road, with the lamp shut, keeping watch." His voice cracked. "I didn't see it. I heard it. I heard a man say please. And I heard her laugh. And then she said, In the river with him, Gorse. And I heard the water." He shut his eyes. "I didn't know he was a doctor. I didn't know. I've heard that water every night for two years."
