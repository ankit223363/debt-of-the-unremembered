Chapter 73: The Girl Who Talks to Nobody, Part 1 of 5: Into the Folds
Into the Folds
The Fold Hills took them in like a mouth.
That was what Lyra thought, walking into the first of them, in the last of the light. A mouth. Not a door. A door is flat, and you go through it, and you are in a room. This was not like that. The first great wave rose up on their left as they came to it, black and steep and folded, higher than the tallest house on Crown Walk, and its crest curled over above them, far up, like a lip. And then another rose on their right. And they were walking between them. Into the gap. And the gap went on, and narrowed, and turned, and the waves closed in behind them, and the open Glass was gone.
She looked back once. Only once. There was a slice of sky behind them, between two black curves, purple going to gray. And through it, far off, very small, the flat floor of the Glass, and the long dark line of the Trench. And on the swell beyond it, three dark specks, standing.
Then the gap turned, and the slice of sky was gone too.
It was darker in here. Much darker. The sun had gone, but the sky above was still holding a little light, the deep blue-violet of the last hour before full night. Up there, along the very tops of the waves, the crests still caught it. Every crest had a thin line of fire along it, red going to rose going to purple, curving away ahead of them and behind them and above them, so that the whole sky was crossed and recrossed with curving lines of colored light. Like the ribs of a great church roof, lit by candles from below.
But down here, in the troughs, it was night already.
The walls went up on either side of them. Not smooth, like the Trench. Folded. That was the word for it, now that Lyra was close enough to see. The black glass was creased in long ridges, one above another, going up and up, the way heavy cloth creases when you push it together on a table. Some of the folds were small, no bigger than her finger. Some were as big as her whole body. They caught the last light along their edges, faint red lines, and under each one was a sliver of perfect black.
And in the curve of every wave, the sky.
She had not expected that. But the glass was so dark and so smooth on the inside of the folds that it held the sky the way the open Glass held the stars. Every curving wall had a curving strip of sky in it. Red, and rose, and violet. Bent round, the way a reflection bends in the back of a spoon. So that wherever she looked, the walls were full of color. Sunset, bent and folded and folded again, a hundred times over, going away into the dark.
It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.
It was also the most frightening.
Because there was no straight line anywhere. The troughs went every way. They split. They joined. They went up, steep, over a low place between two waves, and then down again into another trough that went off at a different angle entirely. There was no north. There was no south. There was only the next turn, and the black walls, and the strip of sky overhead between the crests, getting darker.
If you got lost in here, Lyra thought, you would stay lost.
But there was the chalk.
The first arrow had been on the stub of the Trench wall. The second was on the left-hand wall of the first trough, a hundred steps in, where it split into two. Fresh white chalk on the black. Big and crooked, the point too large for the shaft. Pointing right.
Kestrel went right.
The third was at the next turning. The fourth at the one after. One at every place where the way divided, or turned, or went up over a ridge into another trough. Always the same white chalk. Always the same crooked hand. Always pointing on. Sometimes on the wall at the height of a child's shoulder. Sometimes low down, almost on the floor, as if the one who drew it had been sitting. Once, very high, at the full stretch of a small arm, as if they had climbed up on something to make sure it would be seen.
They followed the arrows.
Nobody said anything. Kestrel walked in front with her spear. Then Marrit, with her pole. Then the rest, in a line, close together, closer than they had walked on the open Glass. Corin on his crutch in the middle, with Oren behind him and Arran at his side. Lyra just behind Corin. She could hear the pat of the crutch on the floor of the trough, and the scuff of all their felt, and their breathing, and nothing else.
The ticking had stopped behind them, at the Trench, when the sun went down.
The silence in the Fold Hills was not like the silence of the open Glass. Out there, the silence had been big. Wide. Going out to the edge of the world. In here it was close. Folded in on itself, like the walls. Every sound they made went into the creases and did not come out. When Oda's spear knocked once against the wall, by accident, the sound went tok and then stopped, flat, as if someone had put a hand over it.
The last of the light went off the crests.
The lines of fire along the tops of the waves went from red to rose, and from rose to violet, and from violet to gray, and then out. One by one. Like candles being snuffed along a church, from the far end, coming toward them.
And then the stars came.
Not many. Only the strips of them between the crests overhead. And their reflections, faint and bent, in the curving black walls.
In the starlight, the chalk arrows glowed.
Lyra had not known chalk could do that. But it did, a little, in the dark, on the black glass. A faint gray-white shine. Just enough to see. Just enough to find the next one, and the next, at every turning, pointing on into the dark.
She found that she was thinking of a story her father used to tell. About two children lost in a forest, who dropped white pebbles on the path so they could find their way home by moonlight.
But these arrows were not pointing home.
They were pointing in.
Signs
The first sign that was not an arrow came about an hour in.
They had climbed. The trough they were in ran up between two waves, steep, so steep that the Ashborn went on hands and feet and Oren had to put his shoulder under Corin's arm and half carry him. At the top was a saddle, a low place, where the two waves leaned together and their crests almost touched overhead, so that it was like going under an arch. And on the far side the way went down again. Two ways. One to the left, one to the right.
On the wall between them, at the height of Lyra's chest, there was writing.
Kestrel stopped. They all crowded up behind her on the narrow saddle, in the starlight, and looked.
NOT THIS WAY
And under it, smaller, squeezed in as if it had been an afterthought:
(SLIPPY)
And an arrow, pointing firmly to the right. With a little line drawn across the left-hand way, on the floor of it, in chalk. A line like a fence. Or a bar across a door.
Nobody said anything for a moment.
Then Arran laughed.
Out loud. The bark. It went off the folds of the walls and was swallowed at once, but it was a laugh, a real one, the first he had laughed since the humming lump in the morning. He put his hand over his mouth. It came out round his fingers anyway.
"Slippy," he said. "Slippy."
"It's spelled wrong," said Vespera. "Or it isn't a word. Or both."
"It's a word," said Lyra. "My nurse used to say it. When the steps were wet." She looked at the chalk. "Mind, now, Miss Lyra. They're slippy."
Arran laughed again.
Kestrel did not.
She was looking down the left-hand way. The way that was barred. Lyra looked too. It went down steeply, the way the right one did. But not smoothly. The floor of it was ridged with long thin folds running downhill, sharp at the edges, shining. Like the teeth of a comb. Like knives laid side by side. And at the bottom, where it turned out of sight, there was nothing. Just black.
No. Not nothing. As Lyra's eyes got used to it, she saw that at the bottom of the slope the floor did not turn. It stopped. It went to an edge. And beyond the edge there was a drop.
She could not see how far.
"Slippy," said Kestrel quietly. Not laughing.
She went right.
There were more signs after that. Not at every turning. The arrows were at every turning. But every so often, among the arrows, something else.
A face. Drawn on the wall at the bottom of a long trough, where it widened out into a sort of round room between the waves. A big round face, as wide as Lyra's two hands, with two dots for eyes and a big curved line for a smile. Smiling out at them from the black glass. No nose. A few short lines sticking out from the top of the head, which might have been hair, or might have been the rays of a sun. Lyra could not tell.
"Well," said Oren. "At least somebody's happy."
A little further on, a drawing of a bird. Very simple. Two curved lines for wings, a dot for a head. Flying.
Then another arrow. And another. And then, at a place where the trough split three ways, a careful drawing of a hand. A small hand, traced round in chalk, with the fingers spread. Pressed flat to the wall and drawn round, the way children draw round their own hands. And beside it, an arrow, pointing left.
Lyra put her own hand up beside it. Not touching. Just beside. Her hand was much bigger. The chalk hand came up to the second knuckle of her fingers.
A child's hand. Vespera had been right.
"Like the handprints at Last Thorn," said Corin, beside her. Very low. "On the tents."
"No," said Kestrel, from ahead. She had heard. "Those are for the dead. This is not for the dead." She looked at the small chalk hand for a moment. "This is somebody saying, I was here. I am still here."
Then, at the next turning, the cup.
It was the strangest of all. Drawn very carefully, larger than the others, on a flat place on the wall where the folds were smooth. A cup. A plain tin cup, the kind soldiers drink from, with a handle on one side. Drawn in outline, with a few short wavy lines over the top, rising, the way you draw steam. And next to it, very large, much larger than the cup, a mark.
A hook. With a dot under it.
"What's that?" said Brin, in the southern tongue. He was frowning at it.
"It's a question," said Lyra. "A question mark. It means—when you ask something. You put that at the end."
Brin looked at the cup. Then at the mark. Then at her.
"Cup?" he said. "Asking?"
"Yes. I think so." Lyra looked at it. "I think it's asking if we'd like some tea."
There was a silence.
Then Brin began to laugh. Slow and low. Like a big dog grumbling. And Arran with him. And Sef, who had not laughed all day, put his linen-wrapped hand over his mouth and his shoulders shook. And even Oda's sharp brown face cracked, a little, at one corner.
Oren did not laugh. But he said, "Saints, I would," with such longing that Vespera snorted.
Lyra laughed too. She could not help it. A cup of tea. Out here. In the middle of the Glass, in the middle of the night, in a maze of black frozen waves, with three iron men behind them and no water to spare. Somebody wanted to know if they would like a cup of tea.
Kestrel did not laugh.
She stood in front of the drawing of the cup, very still, with her spear in her hand. And Lyra, laughing, looked at her face in the starlight and stopped.
Kestrel was not afraid. That was not it. But she was looking at the chalk the way she had looked at the light under Corin's hand that morning. Like someone looking at a thing that should not be there. A thing that was not in any story.
"Nobody lives in the Fold Hills," said Kestrel. Very low. To herself, Lyra thought, as much as to them. "Nobody. Not the Ashborn. Not the gray men. Not anyone. Not in Old Hask's time. Not in his grandmother's." She touched the gray line on her face. "There is no water. There is no food. There is nothing to burn, and nothing to grow. The Glass is too cold by night and too hot by day. Nobody can live here."
She looked at the cup with its little lines of steam.
"Somebody does," she said.
The Folds by Night
The cold came down into the troughs like water into a ditch.
It was worse here than on the open Glass. Lyra had not thought anything could be worse than the night before, in the heap, with the dry knife of the cold coming in through the gap by her face. But this was. Out there, the cold had come down from the sky, and the wind had moved it about, and there had been, at least, the warmth of the Glass deep under it, holding on. In here there was no wind. The cold came down off the crests and slid down the folded walls and pooled in the bottoms of the troughs, where they walked, and lay there. Still. Thick. Getting colder.
She could feel it climbing her legs. Through the felt. Through her boots. Into her knees.
The Glass under her feet was as cold as the stone floor of a church in winter. Colder.
And it was not flat.
That was the other thing. On the open Glass, the floor had been smooth, rolling in long slow swells, so that you hardly knew you were going up or down. In the Fold Hills, nothing was flat. The floors of the troughs were folded too. Ridged and creased and buckled, the same as the walls, only smaller. Long low ridges running across the way, so that you were always stepping up and down, up and down. Hollows that your foot went into without warning. Little sharp creases that caught the felt. And everywhere the slope. Always a slope. Down into a trough, up out of it, along the side of a wave with the floor tipping under you toward the wall.
The first time Lyra's foot went into a hollow, she went over.
She did not even feel it coming. One moment she was walking behind Corin, and the next her right foot had gone down into nothing, a hand's depth, and her knee had buckled, and she was on her side on the cold black glass with her hip shouting at her and the breath knocked out of her.
Oren picked her up. He did not say anything. He just reached down from behind, and took her under the arms, and stood her up, and let go.
She stood there for a moment, shaking. Not hurt. Not much. But shaking.
This was what she had given. This. Her feet had known how to go over ground like this. She was sure of it. Before. When she had the dancing. A dancer's feet know where they are without being told. They feel the floor before they touch it. They see in the dark. If there is a hollow, a dancer's foot has already found it and stepped round it and gone on, before the dancer has even thought the word.
She had had feet like that. She had had them her whole life. And she had given them away, two nights ago, in the burning camp, to stop an iron hand.
She did not regret it. She would do it again. Three children were alive in the long dark line of the families going east along the Edge, who would not have been.
But she stood in the dark at the bottom of a trough in the Fold Hills and shook, and could not make herself take the next step.
"Flat," said Oren behind her. Quietly. "Remember. Flat. Whole foot. Knees bent. Slide."
"There's no flat here."
"Then make it flat. Your knees are the flat. Let them take it." A pause. "You held my blade three times out of five tonight with feet you'd had two days. You can walk a ditch."
She bent her knees.
She slid her foot forward. Flat. Whole foot. Feeling for the floor with it, the way you would feel for the bottom of a stream with your foot when you cannot see the bottom.
It found a ridge. A small one. She felt the edge of it through the felt. And her knee, bent, took it. Without her telling it to. The knee went a little more bent, and the foot went up over the ridge and down the other side, and her weight went with it, and she did not fall.
She slid the other foot.
It found a hollow. The same kind that had put her down. But this time her knee was already bent, and her weight was already low, and the foot went down into the hollow and found the bottom of it and came up again. And she did not fall.
"There," said Oren. "There. Like that."
She did not answer. She was concentrating.
Slide. Feel. Bend. Slide.
It was slow. It was ugly. It was nothing like the way she used to walk, quick and light, without thinking. It was like the way an old woman walks on ice, with every bit of her paying attention.
But she did not fall again.
And after a while, she found that she was not thinking about it so hard. Her feet were doing it. Not the way they used to. Not knowing, the way a dancer knows. But learning. Like a child learning its letters. Slow and clumsy, and getting it wrong, and getting it right, and then getting it right again.
She thought of the L in the grit. Wandering. Wiped away.
The Glass did not give back what you had lost.
But perhaps you could learn something new, she thought. On top of the hole. Something that was not the same, and would never be the same, but that was yours. The way Oren said soldiers did, when they lost a hand.
She slid. Felt. Bent.
Ahead of her, Corin's crutch went down on the folded floor. Pat. He was finding it hard too. She could hear it in his breathing. The crutch was not made for ground like this. Twice she saw it slip on a crease, and Arran's hand shoot out and catch his elbow.
She went up beside him. On his bad side.
And after that, without either of them saying so, she watched the floor for both of them. When she felt a hollow with her foot, she said, "Hollow," very low, and he put the crutch down past it. When she felt a ridge, she said, "Ridge." When the floor tipped toward the wall, she said, "Left," or "Right," and he leaned the other way.
She did not know if it helped him. But it helped her. Having something to watch for that was not only her own feet.
"Hollow," she said.
Pat. Step. Step.
"Ridge."
The chalk arrows glowed faint in the starlight at every turning, pointing on, and they followed them, deeper and deeper into the frozen storm.
