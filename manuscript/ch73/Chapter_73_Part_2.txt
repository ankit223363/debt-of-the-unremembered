Chapter 73: The Girl Who Talks to Nobody, Part 2 of 5: The Shrine
The Shrine
They found the shrine a little after that, at a place where three troughs met.
Lyra almost walked past it. It was so small. A low heap of stones in the angle where two waves leaned together, no higher than her knee. Round gray river stones, like the cairns on the open Glass. But not a cairn. A cairn was tall and pointed, with a finger on top. This was flat. A ring of stones, set carefully in a circle on the folded floor, the size of a cartwheel. And in the middle of the ring, a flat stone, like a table. Like a plate.
Kestrel stopped beside it.
She did not say anything. She crouched down on her heels and looked at the flat stone in the middle. And Lyra, coming up behind her, looked too.
There was nothing on it.
"What is it?" said Lyra. Very low.
"A place we leave things," said Kestrel. She did not look up. "There are a few of them, in the Fold Hills. On the old way through. Old Hask's grandmother's people built them." She touched the flat stone with two fingers, lightly, the way she touched the gray line on her face. "When the Ashborn walk through here, going to the Trench, or coming back, we leave something on the stone. Bread. A little cheese. A skin of water, if we can spare it."
"For who?"
Kestrel was silent a moment.
"For whoever needs it," she said. "For the walkers who come after us, if they run short. For the lost ones, the old women say, though the lost ones do not eat." She took her hand away from the stone. "For the Silent Man."
Lyra did not know what that meant. She did not ask. There was something in Kestrel's voice that did not want to be asked.
"It's empty," said Oren, behind them.
"Yes."
"Is it usually?"
Kestrel did not answer at once. She bent lower over the flat stone, close, in the starlight, the way Corin had bent over the doll in the Trench. Looking. Then she put out one finger again and drew it across the surface of the stone. And held it up.
There was something on the tip of it. Lyra could not see what. Kestrel rubbed it between her finger and thumb. Then she smelled it.
"Crumbs," she said. "Bread. Ours. Our bread, from Last Thorn. The dark kind." She looked at the empty stone. "Arran's cousins came through here at the end of summer. Going to sing in the Trench. They left bread. A whole round of it. And a skin of water."
She stood up.
"It is gone," she said. "The bread, and the skin. And the crumbs are not old. They are dry, but they are not old. Days. Not a season."
"Birds," said Vespera.
"There are no birds in the Fold Hills," said Kestrel. "There is nothing for them."
"The wind, then."
"The wind does not take water skins."
Nobody said anything.
Lyra looked round. At the three dark troughs going off, each one into its own blackness, between the folded walls. At the strip of stars overhead. At the white chalk arrow on the wall of the left-hand trough, faint in the starlight, pointing on.
Somebody had been here. Somebody had taken the bread, and the water. Not long ago. And somebody had drawn the arrow.
The same somebody, she thought. It had to be.
Kestrel had seen the arrow too. Lyra saw her look at it. And then back at the empty shrine. And then she did a thing Lyra did not expect.
She took off her pack. And opened it. And took out a round of the flat dark bread, one of the last whole ones, and a strip of dried goat. And laid them on the flat stone in the middle of the ring. Carefully. The way you would lay a cloth on a table.
"Kestrel," said Oren. "We need that."
"We have enough," said Kestrel. "For ten days, if nobody is greedy." She tied her pack. "Someone out here is hungry. And they drew us arrows." She shouldered the pack. "It is giving. The Glass does not mind."
Oren did not argue.
Arran came and put something on the stone too. A strip of goat, from his own share. Then Sef. Then, after a moment, Brin, with his good hand. A little heap of food on the flat stone, in the ring of river stones, in the starlight, in the middle of nowhere.
Lyra had nothing in her hands. Her food was in her pack. She looked at the little heap.
Then she reached into her coat pocket. Not for the belt stone. Not for the heron. For the other thing. The thing she had picked up in the morning, after the blue fire.
Brin's thorn twig. With its end turned to smooth gray stone.
She laid it on the edge of the flat stone, beside the bread.
She did not know why. It was not food. It was not useful. It was only a twig. But it was the strangest and most beautiful thing she had been given since they came onto the Glass. A thing that had been burned and had not burned, and had turned to stone instead.
Whoever came here, hungry, in the dark, she thought, might like to have something strange and beautiful too.
Brin, beside her, saw what she had put down. He looked at it. Then at her.
And nodded, slowly, as if she had done exactly the right thing.
They went on. Left, the way the arrow pointed. Lyra looked back once, at the turning. The little heap on the flat stone, dark in the ring of pale river stones. And beside it, very small, the twig.
Then the trough turned, and it was gone.
The Voice
They heard it before they saw anything.
It was perhaps three hours into the hills. Lyra had lost count of the turnings. The stars had wheeled a long way overhead, in the narrow strips of sky between the crests. Her legs ached from the knees down with the sliding and bending, and her hands were so cold inside her gloves that she could not feel the ends of her fingers. Corin beside her was gray in the face and breathing in short hard pulls. Even Kestrel was walking more slowly.
And then Arran, at the front beside his sister, stopped.
He held up one hand. Flat. The Ashborn sign for still. And everybody stopped, all down the line, one by one, with a soft shuffle of felt.
And in the silence, Lyra heard it.
A voice.
Faint. Far off. Coming to them round the folds of the walls from somewhere ahead and a little above. Not words. Not at first. Just the sound of a voice. Rising and falling. Going on and on. A high, quick, light voice, like a bird's.
Talking.
Not singing. Not calling. Talking. The way a person talks in a kitchen while they are doing something with their hands. On and on. Without stopping. Without seeming to wait for an answer.
Oda's spear came up. Brin's good hand went to his knife. Oren's sword came out of its sheath with a long soft hiss, and he stepped past Corin and Lyra to the front, beside Kestrel, without a word.
Kestrel did not raise her spear. She stood with her head on one side, listening. Like Oren listened to the ticking.
The voice went on.
They went toward it. Slowly. Kestrel first, then Oren, then the rest close behind. The trough they were in curved to the right and climbed, gently, along the flank of a huge wave whose crest hung over them so far that it was like walking under the eaves of a house. And the voice got louder. And clearer. Until Lyra could hear that it was a girl's voice. Young. And that there were words in it.
The trough came out into a wider place. A sort of hollow, between three waves, with a floor that was almost flat for once, and the crests leaning in overhead from three sides so that the sky was only a small ragged patch of stars, high up. And on the far side of the hollow, in the side of the biggest wave, low down, a hole.
A round hole. Like the mouth of an oven. As high as Lyra's waist, perhaps a little higher. Black inside. Perfectly round, as if someone had pressed a great thumb into the glass when it was soft. And round its edge, on the folded black wall, chalk. A ring of it. Little white marks all round the rim of the hole, like the rays of a sun, or the petals of a daisy.
And from inside the hole, the voice.
It was quite clear now. They stood at the edge of the hollow, the ten of them, very still in the starlight, and listened to it.
"—and then you said nothing," said the voice, "and I said, well, that's very rude, isn't it, when somebody's asked you a question, and you said nothing again, which is ruder, I said, that's ruder than the first time, because now you know it's rude and you're still doing it. And you just sat there. Like a stone. Like a big hairy stone. And I said, I'm not giving you the end of the bread if you're going to be like that. And you still said nothing. So I gave you it anyway. Because you looked so sad. You always look sad when you're being rude. I think you don't mean it. I think it's just your face."
A pause. The tiniest pause. Barely long enough for a breath.
"I've decided," said the voice, "that tomorrow I'm going to teach you a word. One word. Just one. And you're going to say it. And it's going to be hello. Because that's the first word anybody should learn, I think, and you don't have it, so you should have it, and then you can say it to people. If any people come. Which they won't. But if they did. You could say hello, and they'd say hello back, and then you'd know how it works. It's very easy. You just open your mouth and—"
The voice stopped.
Not slowly. All at once. In the middle of a word.
The silence after it was enormous.
Lyra realized, a moment too late, that she had moved. She had stepped forward. A little. Without meaning to. And her felt had scuffed on the glass floor of the hollow.
In the round black mouth of the hole, nothing moved.
Then the voice said, quite differently, very quiet and very sharp:
"Who's that?"
Nobody answered.
"I can hear you," said the voice. "I can hear you breathing. Lots of you. Out there." A pause. "If you're the iron men, I'm not coming out. And neither's he. And you can't come in, because you're too big, so there."
Kestrel looked at Oren. Oren looked at Kestrel.
And it was Arran, of all of them, who answered. In the southern tongue. Softly. In the voice Lyra had heard him use to the children at the Edge.
"We're not iron," he said. "We're people. We followed your arrows."
Silence.
Then, from inside the hole, very small: "All of them?"
"All of them," said Arran. "Every one. Even the slippy one."
A longer silence.
And then a face came out of the hole.
Low down. At the bottom edge of the round black mouth. A small face, pale in the starlight, peering out sideways round the rim, the way a child peers round a door when it is not sure whether it is in trouble. Two eyes, very wide and very bright. A sharp nose. A mouth, open a little.
It looked at them. At all ten of them, standing at the edge of the hollow with their spears and their swords and their eye hides pushed up on their foreheads.
It looked for a long time.
And then it said, with great seriousness, as if it were the most important question in the world:
"Are you nice?"
Tansy
Nobody knew what to say to that.
Lyra opened her mouth. Shut it again. Beside her, Oren had lowered his sword a little, the point dropping toward the floor, the way a man lowers a sword when he finds he has drawn it on a kitten.
It was Arran again who answered.
"Yes," he said gravely. "We think so. Mostly."
The face looked at him. Then it looked along the line of them, one by one, slowly, the bright eyes going from face to face in the starlight. Lyra felt them go over her. Quick and sharp, like a bird looking at a crumb.
"You look nice," said the face to Arran. "You've got no eyebrows. Why've you got no eyebrows?"
"They got burned."
"Oh." The face thought about this. "Does it hurt?"
"Not anymore."
"Good." The eyes moved on. To Kestrel. Stopped. Went very wide. "Oh, you're tall." And on. To Vespera. "You're old." To Brin. "You're big." To Lyra. A long look. "You're pretty. You look sad, though." And on. To Oren.
The eyes stopped on Oren for a long time.
Oren stood there with his notched sword in his hand and his scarred face and his short gray beard white with frost, and his eye hide pushed up on his forehead, and looked back. He did not smile. Oren did not smile at strangers. He had a face, Lyra knew, that frightened most people who did not know him. The scar from the temple to the mouth pulled one side of it down. And in the starlight, with the frost in his beard and the sword in his hand, he looked like something out of a story you tell children to make them stay in bed.
"He looks cross," said the face.
Vespera made a sound like a crow choking.
"That's all right," said the face, kindly, to Oren. "Cross is all right. Lots of people look cross. He looks cross all the time." It jerked its head back toward the inside of the hole. "But he isn't. Not really. He's just got that sort of face. I expect you've got that sort of face too."
Oren opened his mouth. Then he shut it. Then, very slowly, he put his sword back in its sheath.
"Aye," he said. "I expect I have."
"Good," said the face. And then, all at once, the rest of it came out of the hole.
It came out the way a fox comes out of an earth. Low and quick and all in one movement. Head, shoulders, arms, and then the rest, scrambling, and then it was standing up on the floor of the hollow in front of the round black mouth, and it was a girl.
She was small. Wiry. Thin as a rail, but not weak-looking; thin the way a cat is thin, all string and quickness. She might have been twelve. It was hard to tell. Her face was older than twelve, in some ways, sharp and pinched and brown from the sun. And younger, in others. The way she stood. The way she looked at them, with her chin up and her eyes going everywhere.
She was wearing three coats.
Lyra counted them. She could not help it. The outside one was a man's coat, a long dark one, very old, much too big, with the sleeves turned back four or five times at the wrists so that her hands could come out of them, and the hem dragging on the glass behind her. Under it, showing at the collar and at the front where it hung open, another coat, brown, shorter, with horn buttons, also too big. And under that, a third, red. A child's coat, faded to a dull rose, that might once have fitted her and was now too small, so that it pulled tight across her shoulders and her wrists stuck out of the sleeves.
On her feet were boots. A man's boots. Huge. Old black boots with cracked uppers, that came halfway up her shins, and that she had stuffed with rag; Lyra could see the rags sticking out at the tops, gray and brown and blue, wound round and round her legs to keep the boots on. She stood in them the way a child stands in its father's boots in a game, with her feet planted wide.
And her hair.
It was brown, Lyra thought. Or dark fair. It was hard to say in the starlight. And it had been cut with a knife. Not by anyone who knew how. It stuck out in tufts and hanks, short in some places, almost to the skin, and long in others, down past her ears, as if she had grabbed handfuls of it and sawn them off whenever they got in her way. Which, Lyra thought, was probably exactly what she had done.
She had a stick of chalk in one hand. White. Worn down to a stub.
And she was not afraid.
That was the thing that struck Lyra most. Ten strangers, out of the dark, in the middle of the Glass, where nobody came. With spears and swords and knives. And she stood there in her three coats and her father's boots and looked at them as if they were a little bit interesting and a little bit disappointing, like a parade that had turned out smaller than she had hoped.
"I'm Tansy," she said.
Nobody said anything.
"That's my name," said Tansy. "Tansy. It's a flower. A yellow one. It grows by walls. It smells like a sore throat." She looked at them expectantly. "You're supposed to say your names now. That's how it works. I say mine and then you say yours."
"They call this one Kestrel," said Kestrel. After a moment. In a voice Lyra had never heard her use. A little uncertain.
"That's a bird," said Tansy, pleased. "I like birds."
And one by one, because there did not seem to be anything else to do, they told her. Arran. Brin. Oda. Sef. Marrit. Vespera. Oren. Lyra. And Corin, last, leaning on his crutch, very quietly.
Tansy said each one over after them, under her breath, the way you say a thing you mean to remember. And when Corin said his, she looked at him. Longer than at the others. At his gray face, and his crutch, and his gray hands on it.
"You're hurt," she said.
"A bit."
"He's hurt too," said Tansy. "Sort of. Not on the outside." She turned round, and her too-long coat swirled out behind her, and she bent down to the round black hole. "Come in," she said, over her shoulder, to all of them. "It's cold out here. It's warmer in. Not much. But a bit."
She went in, head first, like a rabbit.
And then her head came back out again.
"Wipe your feet," she said sternly.
They all looked down at their feet.
"That's a joke," said Tansy. "There's nothing to wipe. It's glass. Everything's glass." She looked at their faces. "You're supposed to laugh."
Arran laughed.
"Thank you," said Tansy, and went in again.
The Cave
It was a bubble.
That was the first thing Lyra understood, crawling in through the round black mouth on her hands and knees after Arran, with her sword scraping on the rim and the cold glass under her palms. It was not a cave, the way caves were in stories, dug out of rock, with a floor and a roof and walls. It was a bubble. A great round bubble in the glass of the wave, the way there are bubbles in the glass of a cheap window, or a bottle. Only this one was big enough to stand up in.
She stood up. Slowly. Her head did not touch.
It was round all over. No floor. No walls. No roof. Just one curving shape, like the inside of an egg, going up from under her feet and round and over and down again. Perhaps five or six of Oren's long strides across, at the widest. Higher than Kestrel in the middle. Lower at the sides, where it curved down to meet the floor, so that you had to bend there, and then crouch, and then lie down.
And the glass of it was green.
Not black, like the outside of the wave. The bubble had been made, Lyra thought, when the glass was soft and hot, sixty years ago. Some breath of air, caught inside the molten wave as it rose and curled and froze. And the glass round the air had cooled differently. Clouded. Gone milky and pale, the way the glass soldiers in the Trench had gone. So that the inside of the bubble was a soft clouded green, like the inside of a sea-shell. Like being inside a great green pearl.
It was lit.
Not by a fire. There could be no fire, here. By the hole.
The round hole they had crawled in through was on the side of the wave that faced up, a little, toward the patch of sky over the hollow. And the starlight came in through it. And the green glass took the starlight, and held it, and spread it all round the inside of the bubble, faint and soft and even. Not bright. Not like a lamp. But enough to see by. Enough that the whole round room glowed, very faintly, green and gray, like the inside of a wave seen from underneath.
And it was warmer.
Not warm. But warmer. Out of the dead still pool of cold at the bottom of the trough. The glass all round them had soaked up the heat of the day, and was giving it back, slowly. And there were ten of them coming in now, one after another, crowding in through the hole, filling the bubble with their breath.
Lyra moved aside to let the others in, and looked round.
Somebody lived here.
Against the far side of the bubble, where it curved down low, there was a nest. That was the only word for it. A great tumbled heap of blankets and cloth and skins. Lyra saw an Ashborn fur in it, shaggy and gray. A striped wool blanket, red and brown, like the ones the carters used on the north road. A piece of sacking. A man's shirt, very large. Another coat. A bundle of something that might have been sheep's wool, unspun. All of it heaped up and pushed together into a round hollow nest, the way a bird pushes together twigs and moss, with a dent in the middle where somebody slept.
Above the nest, where the curve of the bubble made a sort of shelf, there was a ledge. A natural one. A fold in the glass, running round one side of the bubble at the height of Lyra's chest, flat on top, a hand wide.
And on the ledge, a row of shiny things.
They were laid out with great care. In a line. Each one a little way from the next, so that they did not touch. The way a child lays out its treasures on a windowsill. Lyra went closer to look.
A spoon. Pewter, bent, the bowl dented.
A button. Brass. Big, an army button, with a crown on it, worn almost smooth.
A buckle. Brass too, square, from a belt.
A small round mirror, the glass cracked across, in a tin frame.
A thimble.
A key. Iron, rusty, very old, with a big round bow.
A bit of colored glass, blue, worn smooth at the edges, like the bits you find on a beach.
A ring. Copper, gone green.
A single silver earring, with a little drop on it.
And at the very end of the row, set a little apart from the others, as if it were the best one, a small brass bell. With a loop at the top, and a dent in its side. The kind of bell the Ashborn tied to their goats at Last Thorn, so that you could hear them in the dark.
She looked at it for a moment, and thought of the goats bleating in their knot at the Edge, going east.
Then she turned away and looked at the rest.
By the nest, on the curved floor, a tin cup. A plain soldier's cup, like the one in the drawing. Dented. With a crust of something dark in the bottom.
And all round the walls, everywhere, the pictures.
Chalk. Hundreds of them. Lyra had not seen them at first, in the faint green light, because they were so many that they did not look like pictures. They looked like the walls. But once she saw one, she saw them all. White chalk on the clouded green, from as low as a child could reach sitting down to as high as she could reach standing on tiptoe. Covering the inside of the bubble, layer on layer, the newer ones drawn over the older, faded ones underneath.
Faces. Smiling faces, like the one in the trough. Dozens. Birds, flying. Arrows. Cups. Suns. A house, with a door and two windows and smoke coming out of the chimney in a curly line. A row of stick figures holding hands. A big figure and a small one, side by side. A tree. A dog, or a sheep, or a goat; it was hard to tell. Another house. More faces. Letters, here and there, big crooked capitals. A whole line of them along the bottom of one wall, going round: TANSY TANSY TANSY TANSY, over and over, as if she had been practicing.
And one word, by itself, big, over the nest, drawn very carefully and gone over again and again until the chalk was thick:
HOME
Lyra stood in the middle of the green bubble with the ten of them crowded round her, and her breath going up in a little cloud, and looked at it.
And then she saw the man.
