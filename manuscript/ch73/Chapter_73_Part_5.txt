Chapter 73: The Girl Who Talks to Nobody, Part 5 of 5: Blue Hands
Blue Hands
Vespera got up while they were all still thinking about the door.
She did it without a word, the way she did most things. She got up off her place by the far wall, small and stiff and bent under the low curve of the bubble, and came across to the nest, stepping over Brin's legs and round Oda's spear. With her needle roll in one hand and her little clay pots clinking on her rope belt.
Tansy watched her come with wide eyes.
Vespera knelt down at the edge of the nest. In front of the girl. And held out her hand, palm up. The blue one. Stained to the wrist with a lifetime of indigo, so that in the green light of the bubble it looked almost black.
"Feet," said Vespera.
Tansy looked at the hand. Then at Vespera's face.
"Why's your hand blue?" she said.
"Because I put it in things that are blue. For a living. Feet."
"What things?"
"Vats. Big ones. Full of dye. For cloth." Vespera did not move her hand. "And ink. Small pots of it. For skin. Feet, girl. I'm not asking twice."
Tansy looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she stuck one of the huge black boots out of the nest.
Vespera took hold of it, and pulled.
It came off with a sucking sound. And then the rags. Yards of them, it seemed. Gray and brown and blue and red, wound round and round. Vespera unwound them, steadily, without comment, and laid them in a pile. And under the rags, at last, a foot.
Lyra, watching, drew in her breath.
It was a small foot. A child's foot. And it was a bad one. The heel was cracked open in two places, deep cracks, red and raw at the bottom. The toes were rubbed. There was a hard thick ridge of yellow skin all along the outside of it, where the big boot had rubbed and rubbed for two winters. And on the top of it, across the bones, the skin had gone dark and shiny in a long patch, the way skin goes when it has been burned and healed and burned again.
The Glass, Lyra thought. In the day. The first time. Once of hot, Tansy had said, as if it were nothing.
Vespera looked at the foot for a long time.
She did not say anything sharp. Lyra waited for it. You stupid child. Look at the state of this. It did not come.
Instead Vespera reached for the little clay pot of salve on her belt. The burnt-honey-and-pine one. And opened it. And dipped two blue fingers in it. And began, very gently, to work it into the cracks in the heel.
Tansy flinched.
"Stings," said Vespera. "I know. It's meant to."
"It's all right," said Tansy. Through her teeth. "I don't mind."
"Yes, you do."
"I don't."
"You can mind," said Vespera. "Out loud. Nobody here will think less of you." She did the second crack. "I minded, when I was your age. When I burned my hands the first time, in the vats. I minded so much the whole street heard."
Tansy looked at her.
"Did you cry?"
"Like a gull."
Tansy almost smiled.
Vespera did the other foot. It was as bad as the first. Worse, a little, on the heel. She cleaned both of them with a pad of gauze soaked in the sharp green wash she had used on Oda's blisters, and salved them, and then bound them. Over and under. Round the heel, round the ankle, back under the arch. The way she had bound Oda's. Every turn of the linen exactly where it should be.
And while she worked, she talked. Low. Not sharp at all. Lyra had never heard Vespera talk like that. About nothing. About the vats in the city. About a dyer's cat she had once, a gray one, that fell into the indigo and came out blue and stayed blue for a whole year, and how the street boys called it the Duchess. About the right way to wind a bandage, and the wrong way.
Tansy listened with her mouth a little open. She did not say anything. For once in her life, Lyra thought, she was not talking. She was listening. The way a cat listens to rain.
When Vespera had finished, she wound some of the rags back round the bandages, the cleanest ones, loosely. And put the boots by the nest, but did not put them on.
"Not tonight," she said. "Let the air get to them tonight. In the morning you can put them back on. And we'll find you some felt, so you're not walking on rags." She stood up, with a creak. "And tomorrow night I'll look at them again."
Tansy looked down at her bandaged feet. Then up at the old woman.
"Why?" she said.
Vespera was tying the strings of her needle roll. She did not look up.
"Because they need it," she said.
"But you don't even know me."
Vespera's hands stopped on the strings. Just for a moment.
"No," she said. "I don't." She tied the last knot, and tucked the roll into her belt. "That's never stopped me yet."
She went back across the bubble to her place, stepping over Brin, and lay down with her back to them all. And did not say anything else.
Tansy sat in the nest looking at her bandaged feet for a long time.
Then she leaned over to the silent man, and whispered, quite loudly, the way children whisper: "The old blue one's nice too. I thought she was cross. She isn't. She just has that sort of face."
On the far side of the bubble, Lyra saw Vespera's shoulders move. Once. As if she were laughing. Or the other thing.
The Bargain
The wind turned a little after that.
Lyra did not feel it. There was no wind in the bubble. But she heard it. A soft hollow moan from the round mouth of the hole, like someone blowing across the top of a bottle. Rising. Falling. The night air moving at last, out in the hollow between the three waves, coming from somewhere new.
And on it, very faint, so faint she thought at first it was her own blood in her ears, she heard the ticking.
She sat up.
So did Oren. So did Kestrel, by the hole, her head turning sharp. So did Arran, who had been almost asleep with his long legs folded under him.
Tick.
Tick.
Tick.
It was a long way off. Very far. Coming to them round the folds and the troughs and the crests, carried on the turning wind, broken up by every wave it passed, so that it came and went. A few ticks. Then nothing. Then a few more.
"They're frozen," said Lyra. "They stopped at sunset. We saw them stop."
"They did," said Oren. He had his head on one side, listening, the way he did. "That's them. Stopped. Not going." He listened again. "Listen to it. One tick. Then nothing for a long while. Then one more." He let out a slow breath through his nose. "That's not walking. That's settling. Like a house at night, creaking as it goes cold. Like a clock somebody's put their hand on, and it still wants to go, and every so often a little bit of it tries."
"Can they hear us?" said Tansy, from the nest. In a small voice.
"No," said Oren. "They don't hear. They just go where he is." He did not look at Corin. "And in the morning, when the sun gets into them, they'll go again. Straight for here. Over the top of everything."
No one said anything.
The wind turned again. The ticking went.
The bubble was very quiet.
Then Tansy said, "I know a short way."
Everybody looked at her.
She was sitting up in the nest, wide awake now, with her arms round her knees and her chin on them. Her bright eyes went from face to face.
"Through the hills," she said. "To the Ridges. And through the Ridges, to the door. There's a long way, that the Ashborn know, I've seen their marks. Round and round, following the troughs. Takes days. And there's a short way." She jerked her head at the man beside her. "His way. He found it. I'd never have found it. It goes under things and through things and over things, and it's horrible, and it's quick." She looked at Kestrel. "I can take you. If you want."
Kestrel looked at her for a long moment.
"Why would you?" she said.
Tansy shrugged.
"Because you're nice," she said. "You followed the arrows. Even the slippy one. And he gave me honey." She nodded at Oren. "And because those iron things are horrible. I heard them today, ticking, when I was out drawing arrows. I didn't know what they were. Now I do." She looked at Corin. "They want him, don't they? The hurt one."
No one answered.
"I thought so," said Tansy. "I can tell. He's got that look. Like a rabbit when the dogs are out." She turned back to Kestrel. "So I'll take you the short way. And they'll have to go the long way, because they don't know the short way, and they're too big for some of it anyway. And you'll get to the door before them."
"And then?" said Vespera.
"And then I don't know," said Tansy. "That's your business."
She looked down at the silent man beside her. And put her hand on his arm.
"But he comes too," she said. "Or I don't."
There was a silence.
Lyra saw them all look at the man. At the thin still shape in the nest, with the bread still in his hand and his eyes on the dark. A man who did not speak. Who did not look at anyone. Who the Ashborn told stories about to frighten their children. Who Kestrel would not turn her back on.
She saw Oren open his mouth.
She saw Vespera's eyes narrow.
She saw Oda's hand tighten on her spear.
And before any of them could say anything, Kestrel said:
"Yes."
Everybody looked at her.
She was still standing by the hole, with her spear upright and her back to the green wall. She had not moved. Her face, in the faint light, was very still.
"Yes," she said again. "He comes."
"Kestrel—" said Arran.
"He comes," said Kestrel. "We will not leave him here alone, with iron walking in the hills that goes through anything standing in its way. And we will not leave her here alone, with him." She looked at Tansy. "And she will not come without him. And we need her. So he comes."
She looked at the man. For a long moment.
"The old ones say," said Kestrel quietly, "that the Silent Man walks where the dead are thickest. And that is where we are going." She touched the gray line on her face. "Perhaps he knows the way better than any of us."
No one argued.
Tansy let out a long breath, as if she had been holding it. And then grinned, all at once, enormously.
"Good," she said. "That's settled, then." She lay down in the nest, against the man's side, and pulled a blanket up to her chin. "We go before it's light. He wakes up early. He always wakes up early. Earlier than anybody." She shut her eyes. "Goodnight, nice people."
And she was asleep, almost before she had finished saying it. The way children fall asleep. All at once, like a stone dropped into a well.
Tansy and Lyra
She was not asleep.
Lyra found that out a little later, when the others had settled, and the bubble had gone quiet except for breathing and Oren's first slow snores. She had lain down at last along the curve of the floor, between Corin and Vespera, with her coat pulled round her and her head on her pack. And she was looking at the chalk on the walls in the faint green light. At the faces. At the houses with their curly smoke. At TANSY TANSY TANSY going round.
And a voice said, very quietly, from the nest, "Are you awake?"
Lyra turned her head.
Tansy was lying on her side against the silent man, with the blanket up to her nose. Only her eyes showed over the top of it. They were open.
"Yes," whispered Lyra.
"I can't sleep," whispered Tansy. "I said goodnight and then I couldn't. It's too many people. I'm not used to people." A pause. "It's nice, though. It's nice having people."
Lyra did not know what to say. So she said, "Yes."
Tansy was quiet for a moment. Then: "Is he your husband? The hurt one?"
Lyra felt her face go hot in the dark.
"No."
"Your brother?"
"No."
"Your friend?"
Lyra thought about it.
"He doesn't remember me," she said. She did not know why she said it. To a child. In the dark. "He used to. He knew me very well. And then he had to give it away. All of it. Everything he knew about me. To save a lot of people." She swallowed. "So now he doesn't. He only knows my voice. And what I tell him."
Tansy's eyes, over the blanket, were very wide.
"That's like him," she whispered. She meant the man. Lyra saw her look up at his face. "He doesn't remember anything. Not even his own name. I tell him things. Every day. So he'll know."
"I tell Corin things," said Lyra. "One a day."
"Does it work?"
"I don't know." Lyra was quiet. "He writes them down. In a book. So he won't lose them again."
"I should get a book," said Tansy. "For him. Only I can't write very well. Only big letters. And my name. And HOME." She was quiet for a moment. "And NICE. I learned NICE for the arrows. I had to keep looking at the soap."
"The soap?"
"There was a bit of soap at a shrine once. Wrapped in paper. And the paper had writing on, and one of the words was NICE. NICE AND GENTLE, I think it said. For ladies' hands. So I kept the paper. And I copied it." Tansy's eyes crinkled over the blanket. "I don't know how to do the little mark, though. The little flying one, that goes between. YOU and RE. The paper didn't have that."
Lyra smiled into the dark.
"I'll show you," she said. Then she stopped.
She could not show her. She could not make the mark. She could not make any mark. She had tried in the grit, that morning, and her hand had wandered like a bird's foot.
"I mean," she said, "Corin will show you. He knows where it goes. He's very good at letters."
"Can't you?"
Lyra did not answer at once.
"I used to," she said. "I gave it away too."
Tansy looked at her for a long moment over the edge of the blanket.
"Everybody gives everything away," she said at last. Very quietly. Not like a child. "Everybody I ever met. Gives things or has things taken. That's why I came out here." She pulled the blanket a little higher. "But you're still nice. All of you. Even with things gone."
"Are we?"
"You followed the arrows," said Tansy. "You said your names. He gave me honey. You didn't laugh at my coats." She yawned behind the blanket. "That's nice. That's all nice is."
Lyra lay with her cheek on her pack and looked at the small face over the blanket, and the thin silent man beside it, with his hand on his knee and his eyes on the dark.
"Tansy," she said. "The bell. On your ledge. The little brass one."
"That's my best one," said Tansy at once. Sleepily. "I found it at the Edge. A long way along. In the snow. There was a goat, frozen, and that was on its neck. I took it off. I didn't think the goat would mind." A pause. "I ring it sometimes. When I'm sad. It sounds like a goat that's still alive."
Lyra did not say anything.
"Goodnight, Lyra," whispered Tansy. "The pretty sad one. Who isn't married to the hurt one."
"Goodnight, Tansy."
The eyes over the blanket closed.
And this time, Lyra thought, watching the small face go slack and the breathing slow, she really was asleep.
Lyra lay awake a little longer. Thinking about a girl alone on the Glass for two winters, with a silent man and a row of shiny things and a word copied off a soap wrapper. Saying names to the dark so she would not forget how.
Then she slept too.
And she did not dream.
On Guard
She woke in the small hours, and did not know why.
Not cold. She was warm, for once. Warmer than she had been since the tent of hands at Last Thorn. The bubble had filled up with their breath and their bodies, and the green glass all round was giving back the last of the day's heat, and someone, in the night, had pulled a blanket over her. Vespera, she thought. It smelled of ink and burnt honey.
Not a sound, either. There was no sound. Only breathing. Ten people breathing, and a child, in a green glass egg in the middle of a frozen storm. Oren's slow rasp. Brin's deep slow sighs. Somebody, Arran probably, muttering in his sleep in the round speech. The small quick breaths of Tansy in the nest.
No ticking.
She lay and listened for it a long time, to be sure. Nothing. The wind had turned back, or dropped. There was only the soft hollow breath of the night air across the round mouth of the hole, very faint, like someone breathing on the top of a bottle in another room.
She lifted her head.
The others had made a heap, in the night. Not on purpose. The way people do, when they are cold and tired and close together. They had drifted toward each other in their sleep across the curved floor, and now they lay in one great tangle in the bottom of the bubble. Backs against backs. Heads on arms. Feet tucked under other people's coats. Marrit curled small at the edge like a cat. Brin's long body across the middle like a fallen tree, with Oda and Sef against it. Arran with his head on his own pack and one arm flung out over Kestrel's shoulder. Kestrel had lain down at last. Lyra saw her, near the hole, on her side, with her spear along her body and one hand still on its shaft. Asleep.
Corin was beside Lyra. Close. On his back, with his arm over his eyes and his crutch lying along his side. His breathing slow and even. Asleep too.
Tansy was asleep in the nest. Curled up in a tight ball in the dent in the middle of the blankets, with only the top of her knife-cut head showing.
All of them asleep.
All but one.
The nest was empty, except for Tansy.
Lyra's heart gave one hard thump.
Then she saw him.
He had moved. In the night, while they slept, without waking anyone. He had got up out of the nest and gone across the bubble, over or round or between all of them, so quietly that not even Kestrel had stirred, and he was sitting by the hole.
Not in the hole. Not outside. Just inside it. On the curved green floor, between the heap of sleepers and the round black mouth of the cave. With his back to them all.
He was sitting cross-legged, the way he had sat in the nest. Very upright. Quite still. His hands were on his knees. The blanket he had worn was gone from his shoulders; it lay over Tansy now, Lyra saw, tucked round her, with its edge pulled up over the top of her head against the cold. And he sat in his thin dark stiff coat, with the night air coming in through the hole onto his face, and did not seem to feel it.
He was facing out.
Through the hole. Out into the hollow between the three waves, gray with starlight. And beyond it, into the dark trough they had come in by. And beyond that, Lyra knew, though she could not see it, the long way back through the folds and the troughs and the crests. Back over the saddle with NOT THIS WAY (SLIPPY). Back past the cup with its question mark, and the small chalk hand. Back to the stub of the Trench wall and the first crooked arrow. And beyond that, across the open Glass, to a low swell south of the Trench, where three tall shapes stood frozen in a row in the starlight, ticking now and then in their sleep, waiting for the sun.
He was facing the way they had come.
He was facing the ticking.
Lyra lay very still and watched him.
He did not move. He did not turn his head. He did not look back at the sleepers, to see if they were safe. He did not need to. She understood that, somehow, watching him. He knew they were there. The way you know the wall is at your back without looking.
He was between them and it.
That was all. That was the whole of it. He had got up in the night, while they slept, and put his blanket on the child, and gone and sat down between the sleepers and the dark they had come out of. Facing it. With his empty hands on his knees.
The way a soldier sits at the mouth of a camp. The way a dog lies across the door of a house. Without being told. Without being asked. Without anyone even knowing he was there.
As if he had done it before.
As if he had always known that something followed. That something always followed. And that someone had to sit, at night, between the sleeping and the coming dark, and wait for it.
Lyra thought of the Trench. Of the glass soldiers, all standing, all still, all waiting for someone to tell them they could put their hands down. Waiting sixty years.
This man was not glass. He breathed. She could see his back rise and fall, slow and even, in the starlight from the hole.
But he was waiting too.
She did not know for what. She did not think he knew. She thought, perhaps, that he had forgotten what he was waiting for a long time ago, with everything else. His name. His voice. Whoever had taught him to knock on a door in the middle of the Glass.
But he had not forgotten how to wait. Or where to sit. Or which way to face.
That had stayed.
She lay with her cheek on her pack and watched his still back against the gray hole of the night, and the stars beyond it, and something in her that had been tight since Last Thorn, since the tent of hands burned, since the iron hand came down, let go. Just a little.
She closed her eyes.
She knew she should get up. Go and sit with him. Take a turn. Tell him to go and sleep. She was a soldier's charge. She knew about watches. You did not let one man sit all night.
She did not get up.
She thought that if she went and sat by him and told him to go and lie down, he would not hear her. Would not look at her. Would only go on sitting there, facing the dark, with his hands on his knees.
Because that was where he belonged. She could see it. In the set of his shoulders. In the stillness of him. Some part of him, under all the nothing, under the beard and the silence and the eyes that went through everything, knew exactly where it was supposed to be.
Between them and what was coming.
She slept.
And when she woke again, a little before dawn, he was still there. Not moved. Not a hair. Sitting in the round mouth of the cave in the gray first light, facing back the way they had come. Toward the Trench. Toward the swell. Toward the ticking that would start again when the sun came up.
As if he had always known what follows.
