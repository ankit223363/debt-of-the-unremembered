Chapter 73: The Girl Who Talks to Nobody, Part 4 of 5: The Sun
The Sun
The man began to draw while they were still eating.
Lyra did not see him start. She was eating her own share, slowly, making it last. The bread was so hard she had to soften each bite in her mouth before she could chew it. The cheese was sharp and salty and wonderful. The salt. She could taste the salt. She thought of the night before, on the open Glass, holding the taste of salt out to nothing, and nothing taking it.
And when she looked up, the man's hand was moving.
His left hand. The one he had waved with. It had come off his knee and gone down, past the edge of the nest, to the curved floor of the bubble beside him. Where there was dust.
Lyra had not noticed the dust before. But there was a little of it there, on the floor beside the nest. A thin gray skin of it, fine as flour, that had drifted in through the hole over two winters and settled in the bottom of the bubble. Glass dust, Lyra thought. Or ash. Or both. And in it, the man's long thin finger was moving.
Drawing.
Not fast. Not slowly. Steadily. The way a man writes his own name, without thinking. His eyes were not on it. His eyes were still on the hole, on the dark, on nothing. But his finger moved in the dust as if it knew exactly where to go.
A circle.
Round, and closed, and very neat. Much neater than anything Tansy had drawn on the walls. The size of a saucer.
Then lines. Short ones. Going out from the circle all the way round. Even. Evenly spaced. Like the spokes of a wheel. Like the rays on the brass badges of the glass soldiers in the Trench.
A sun.
Lyra stopped chewing.
The finger went into the middle of the circle. And drew a line. A short curved line, like a little smile turned upside down, on the left side of the middle. And then another, just the same, on the right.
Two curves. Side by side. Curving down.
Like eyelids. Like the eyes of someone sleeping.
The finger stopped. Lifted. Went back to the man's knee, and lay there, and was still.
And in the dust on the floor of the bubble, there was a sun. A round sun with its rays going out all round it, and in the middle of it, two closed eyes.
Lyra heard someone say:
"The dark is the day with its eyes shut."
It was her own voice.
She had not meant to say it. She had not decided to. It had come out of her mouth on its own, the way her feet used to find the floor on their own, before she gave them away. Out of somewhere very low and very old. And as she heard it, she knew whose words they were, and her whole body went cold.
Her mother's.
Her mother, sitting on the edge of her bed in the nursery on Crown Walk, a long time ago. With the lamp turned down. And Lyra, very small, saying I don't like the dark, I don't like it. And her mother's cool hand on her forehead, and her mother's voice, low, with the faint foreign edge to it that it always had when she was tired.
The dark is only the day with its eyes shut, my heart. It's still there. It's only sleeping. And in the morning it opens its eyes.
She had not thought of it in years.
She had not thought of it. But somebody else had. She knew that too, with a cold sick jolt. She had heard those words since. Not from her mother. In the city, this winter, in the middle of everything. Used by a stranger as a password, in a story about her family that had no end to it. And she had known them then, and said so, that's my mother's, and nobody had been able to tell her why a stranger should have them.
She was staring at the drawing.
The bubble had gone very quiet.
Then Tansy, in the nest, said cheerfully, "Oh, he draws that all the time."
Lyra looked at her.
"That one," said Tansy. She nodded at the dust. "The sun with its eyes shut. He does it every day. Sometimes lots of times. On the floor, in the dust. On the walls, with his finger, where there's frost. Once he did it on my arm, with a bit of charcoal from a shrine fire." She pushed up the sleeve of her red coat, as if to show them, though there was nothing there now. "I asked him what it was. He didn't say, of course. So I decided it was a sun having a nap." She looked at the drawing fondly. "Because it's tired. From shining all day. So it shuts its eyes. That's what I think."
No one laughed.
Lyra could not take her eyes off it. The neat round circle. The even rays. The two closed eyes. Drawn in the dust in the middle of the Glass by a man who did not speak, and did not look at anyone, and did not know his own name.
The dark is the day with its eyes shut.
"Lyra," said Corin.
She did not answer.
"Lyra. What did you say?"
She made herself look at him. Across the bubble. He was sitting up very straight, by the hole, with his crutch across his knees. His face was white. He was looking at her the way he had looked at the man, when the man waved.
"It's my mother's," she said. Her voice came out thin and strange. "It's something my mother used to say. When I was little. When I was afraid of the dark."
She looked at the silent man.
He sat in the nest with his hands on his knees and a piece of bread in one of them, and his eyes on the dark beyond the hole. As if he had not drawn anything at all. As if he did not know there was a sun in the dust beside him, with its eyes shut, or what it meant, or who had ever said it.
Perhaps he did not.
Perhaps he did.
Whose Words
Later, when the others had begun to settle, Corin came across the bubble to her.
It took him a long time. There was no room. He had to go round on his good knee and his hands, dragging the crutch and the stiff leg, past Brin's long body and Marrit's pole and Oda's spear and Vespera's small curled shape, saying "Sorry, sorry" under his breath to each of them. When he got to her he lowered himself down onto the curved floor beside her with a grunt, and leaned his back against the green glass, and sat there for a while getting his breath.
She did not say anything. She was looking at the sun in the dust.
It was still there. The man had not rubbed it out. Tansy had not. No one had walked on it. It lay on the floor beside the nest in the faint green light, round and neat, with its rays and its closed eyes.
"Tell me," said Corin.
She did not ask what. She knew.
"My mother said it," said Lyra. "When I was small. When I was afraid. She'd sit on my bed and put her hand on my head and say it. The dark is only the day with its eyes shut." She was quiet. "I thought she made it up. For me. I thought it was mine. Mine and hers."
"And it isn't."
"I don't know what it is." She pulled her knees up and put her arms round them. "I thought, after she went, that it was the one thing I had of her that nobody else had. The way she said it. The words. I never told anyone. Not Rowan. Not my father. I kept it." She pressed her lips together. "And then this winter, in the city, a stranger used it. As a password. And now a man who can't talk draws it in the dust. In the middle of the Glass."
Corin did not say anything for a while.
"Where was she from?" he said at last. "Your mother."
"The east. Over the water. Her people. Fold it small, that's all my father ever said about them. That was their saying. That was what they did." She shook her head. "She never talked about them. Not to us. If you asked, she'd laugh and change the subject. Or she'd tell you a story instead, a made-up one, about a princess in a tower, so you'd forget you'd asked."
"Did it work?"
"Every time." Lyra almost smiled. "I was very easy to distract."
She looked at the man in the nest.
He was sitting as he always sat, it seemed. Hands on his knees. Eyes on the dark. Tansy was curled up against his side now, her head on his arm, still talking, but more slowly, the words running into each other the way they do when someone is falling asleep.
"Could he have known her?" said Lyra. Very low. "Is that possible? Could he be—" She stopped. She did not know what she had been going to say. From her people. Someone she knew. Someone she loved.
She did not finish it.
"I don't know," said Corin.
He was looking at the man too. And his hand, his right hand, had gone up again to his chest. To the place over his breastbone. Not pressing, now. Just resting there.
"When he waved," said Lyra. "At you. What was it like?"
Corin was quiet a long time.
"Like being called," he said at last. "From a long way off. By someone who knew my name. And I turned round, and there was nobody there. Only I knew there had been." He shook his head. "I don't know him, Lyra. I've never seen him before. I'd know. Even me, I'd know." A pause. "But something in me knows him. The way something in you knew those words. Before you'd thought about it. It just came out."
They sat together against the green wall.
"The old ones say he's looking for his name," said Lyra. "Kestrel said."
"Yes."
"So am I," said Lyra. "In a way. Looking for hers. My mother's. What she really was. Where she went." She put her chin on her knees. "And so are you. Looking for yours."
Corin looked at her.
"We're a fine lot," said Lyra. "Aren't we. Three people looking for names, in the middle of a place where nothing can be given back."
"Giving works," said Corin. "Vespera said."
"Then somebody had better give us some."
He laughed. Very quietly, so as not to wake anyone. And she found that she was smiling, a little, against her knees.
Then he took out the Ledger. And the pencil. And wrote, in the faint green light, without asking her, square and black:
HE DREW A SUN WITH ITS EYES SHUT. LYRA SAID: THE DARK IS THE DAY WITH ITS EYES SHUT. HER MOTHER'S WORDS.
And under it:
HE WAVED AT ME. I DON'T KNOW HIM. SOMETHING IN ME DOES.
He closed the book. And they sat there, side by side, and looked at the sun in the dust, until Tansy's voice in the nest ran down at last into silence, and stopped.
Corin and the Man
Corin did not lie down after that. Lyra thought he would. He was so tired that his face had gone the color of old paper, and his hands, when he put the Ledger away, were not quite steady. But he did not lie down.
He got up.
Slowly. With the crutch, and his hand on the green wall. And went across the bubble again. Not back to his place by the hole. To the nest.
Tansy was curled up against the man's side with her eyes shut. She did not stir. Lyra watched Corin lower himself down onto the curved floor at the edge of the nest, just beyond the heap of blankets, with his bad leg out in front of him and his back half turned to the rest of them. Facing the man.
Not close. An arm's length away. The way you would sit near a strange dog. Near enough to be seen. Not near enough to frighten.
The man did not look at him. His eyes were on the hole.
Corin did not say anything for a long time. He only sat there, with his crutch across his lap and his gray hands folded on it, and looked at the man's face. At the high forehead under the matted hair. At the long nose. At the sunken cheeks above the beard. At the pale eyes.
Lyra, across the bubble, could see Corin's face in profile. It was very quiet. Not frightened. Not curious, either, exactly. The look of a man trying to remember the words of a song, when he has only the tune.
Then Corin said, very low, "My name is Corin."
The man did not move.
"Corin Vane," said Corin. "That's what it says in my book. I was a clerk. In the city. In the Harvesting Section. I put my hand on jars of dead people's memories and walked around inside them." He was quiet a moment. "I'm not very good at remembering my own. They keep getting taken."
The man's eyes stayed on the hole.
"I don't know your name," said Corin. "Tansy calls you Nobody. The Ashborn call you the Silent Man." He paused. "I don't think either of those is your name. I think you had one. I think you had a lot of things."
Nothing.
Corin looked down at his own hands. At the gray, and the black fingertips.
"You waved at me," he said. "When I came in. You haven't waved at anyone else. Tansy says you've never waved at anyone." He turned the crutch a little in his hands. "I don't know why you did it. I don't know you. I'd remember. I think I'd remember." A pause. "But when you did it, something in me hurt. Here." He touched his chest. "Like when you see a house you lived in as a child, and somebody else has painted the door."
The man did not answer.
But his hands moved.
Lyra saw it. She did not think Corin did, at first. The man's long thin hands, lying on his knees, palm down. The right one moved. Slowly. It turned over. Palm up. Open. Empty. And lay there on his knee like that, the fingers a little curled, the way a hand lies when it has just let something go.
Lyra had seen a hand do that before. That morning, in the dark, at the edge of the heap. Corin's hand, lifting off the Glass, with the light rising to follow it. Turning over. Palm up. Empty. And the light sinking away.
Corin had seen it now. She watched him see it. Watched him look at the open empty hand on the man's knee, and then down at his own gray one, on the crutch. And then up again.
He did not say anything.
The man's hand lay there, open, for a long moment. Then it turned back over, slowly, and was still.
Corin sat looking at it for a long time.
Then he said, so quietly that Lyra almost did not hear it, "Goodnight." And then, after a moment, as if he were trying out a word in a language he did not speak: "Whoever you are."
He got up. Slowly. And came back across the bubble on his crutch. And lay down beside Lyra, on his back, with his arm over his eyes.
She did not ask him anything.
After a while she felt his hand, the gray one, come across the floor between them in the dark. And find her sleeve. And hold on to it. Not her hand. Just the cloth of her sleeve, at the wrist, between his finger and thumb. The way a child holds the edge of a blanket.
She let him.
The Door in the Middle
"I'm not asleep," said Tansy.
She said it without opening her eyes, from the nest, with her head on the silent man's arm.
"I was resting my mouth," she said. "It gets tired. Even mine." She opened one eye and looked across the bubble at Lyra and Corin. "You were whispering. About him. I heard."
"Sorry," said Lyra.
"It's all right. People do. At Haverstock everyone whispered about me." She opened the other eye. "You want to know where he goes."
Lyra had not known she wanted to know that. But she found she did.
"Where does he go?" she said.
Tansy sat up. Slowly, for her. Pulling her three coats round her. The man did not move. He went on looking through the hole at the dark.
"Every month," said Tansy, "when the moon's round, he goes off. On his own. Toward the middle." She pointed. Not at the hole. At the curved green wall to the left of it, as if she could see through it. North, Lyra thought. Or a little east of north. "He just gets up, in the evening, when the moon comes up big, and walks out. Doesn't look at me. Doesn't wait. Just goes. Like he's been called."
"And you follow him," said Corin.
"Course I do." Tansy looked at him as if he were stupid. "Somebody's got to. He'd walk off the edge of something. He'd walk into the Eye and never come out."
"The Eye," said Corin.
"If that's what it's called." Tansy shrugged. "I don't know what it's called. Nobody told me. I just call it the door."
"The door?"
"The door in the middle." She said it as if it were obvious. "That's where he goes. Every full moon. We walk all night, through the Ridges, and then there's a sort of—" She stopped. Her hands moved, trying to make the shape. "A sort of dip. A big one. Round. Like a bowl. And the glass goes different, there. Smoother. And there's stones round the edge of it, round ones, gray, with rings on."
Lyra's hand went to her coat pocket. To Davin's stone. The gray one, with the white ring round it like a belt.
"And in the middle of the bowl," said Tansy, "there's a hump. A sort of hill. Not big. Round, like an upside-down bowl. Iron and stone, all rusty. And there's a door in it." Her voice had gone quieter. "A real door. Iron. Like a door in a house. Only there's no house. Just the hump, and the door, in the middle of the bowl, in the middle of the Glass."
No one said anything.
"And he walks right up to it," said Tansy. "Every time. Right across the bowl. And I wait at the edge, because I don't like it in there, it makes my head feel funny. Loud. Like everything's too loud. And he walks right up to the door."
She stopped.
"And he knocks," she said.
The bubble was very quiet. Lyra could hear Oren breathing, slow, by the wall. Could hear the small creak of Kestrel's spear shaft in her hand.
"Knocks how?" said Corin. Very low.
Tansy looked at him.
"I can't do it like him," she said. "I've tried. He does it slow, with the side of his fist, like this." She closed her small hand and lifted it, and let it fall against the green glass beside her, soft. Once. "Lots of knocks. With stops in between. Some long stops, some short. And then the same again. And again. I always lose count. He never does." She let her hand drop into her lap. "I asked him to teach me. He didn't, of course. I don't think he knows he's doing it. I think it's just what his hand does, at that door. The way my hands take shiny things."
"And then?" said Corin.
"Then he waits," said Tansy. "With his hand flat on the door. For a long time. Till the moon's gone right over." She pulled her knees up. "Nobody answers. Nobody ever answers. Then he turns round and comes home. And I come home with him. And he's quiet for a day or two after. Quieter, I mean. Quieter than usual. He doesn't eat, even when I put it in his hand."
No one said anything for a while.
Lyra looked at the silent man. At his long thin hands, lying on his knees. She tried to picture them on an iron door in the middle of the Glass, in the moonlight. Knocking. Waiting. Knocking again. Month after month. For two winters that Tansy knew of. And how long before that, that nobody knew?
Knocking on a door that nobody opened.
"Who's he knocking for?" she said.
Tansy shrugged inside her coats.
"I don't know," she said. "I used to think it was his house. That he lived there, before, and he'd lost the key, and he was knocking to be let in." She looked up at his face. "But now I think it's somebody else's. I think he's knocking for somebody who was there once. And he wants to know if they still are."
She yawned. Hugely. A real one, this time.
"Anyway," she said, "the moon's not round for a few days yet. So he won't go. Not till then. Not unless something makes him."
Corin was looking at the man too.
"The Eye," he said. Very low. To himself. "A door in the middle of the Eye."
Lyra knew what he was thinking. Because she was thinking it too. Malakor's journal. In an iron box. Under a map table. In Sola's bunker, at the Glass. Take a child of Oakhaven with you.
A round hump of iron and stone. In the middle of a bowl. With a door in it.
They had been walking toward it for two nights.
And the silent man had been walking to it, and knocking on it, every full moon, for longer than anyone knew.
