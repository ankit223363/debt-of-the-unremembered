Chapter 73: The Girl Who Talks to Nobody, Part 3 of 5: Nobody
Nobody
He was sitting in the nest.
She had not seen him because he was so still. Because he was the same colors as the nest, gray and brown and dark, and the green light lay over him the same as it lay over the blankets. Because he did not move when ten strangers came crawling into his home in the middle of the night. Not a hand. Not a head. Not an eye.
He was sitting in the middle of the heap of blankets, cross-legged, with his back against the curve of the bubble and his hands resting on his knees. Quite upright. Quite still. The way the glass soldiers in the Trench had been still.
But he was not glass.
Lyra saw him breathe. A slow rise of the chest under the blankets, and a slow fall. That was all.
She went very still herself.
The others had seen him now too. She felt them see him. Behind her, a quick indrawn breath, and the soft scrape of a spear point coming up. Oren's hand, she knew without looking, was on his sword hilt again.
The man did not move.
He was thin. Very thin. Thinner than Tansy, even. Long thin wrists coming out of the cuffs of a coat that was dark and stiff with dirt. Long thin fingers on his knees. A long thin neck. You could see, under the edge of the blanket round his shoulders, the shape of his collarbones, sharp as the edges of a knife.
And he had a beard. A great wild tangled beard, gray and brown and black all mixed, that came down over his chest almost to his folded hands. And hair to match. Long, matted, hanging down round his face in ropes and hanks. So that his face was only a pale shape in the middle of it all. A high forehead. Sunken cheeks. A long nose.
And eyes.
They were open. Wide open. Pale, Lyra thought, gray or blue; it was hard to tell in the green light. And they were looking straight ahead. At the far side of the bubble. At the round black hole they had come in by.
At them.
No. Not at them.
Through them.
That was what it was like. Lyra stood in front of him, a few steps off, in the middle of the floor, and his eyes were on her, and they were not looking at her. They went into her and out the other side and on, to the curved wall behind her, and through that, and out, into the dark. As if she were not there. As if she were made of glass, like everything else.
She had never been looked at like that. It was worse than being stared at. It was like being nothing.
"That's Nobody," said Tansy.
She had climbed into the nest beside him. She was sitting close against his side, with her too-big boots stuck out in front of her and her shoulder against his arm, and she was looking up at the ten of them with a kind of pride. As if he were the best of the shiny things on her ledge.
"That's what I call him," she said. "Nobody. Because he doesn't say. He doesn't say anything. Not ever. Not his name or anything. So I had to give him one, and that's the one I gave him, because when people at home said who are you talking to, Tansy, I used to say nobody. And now it's true." She looked up at his face. "Isn't it? You're Nobody."
The man did not answer. He did not look at her. His eyes went on through them all, out into the dark.
"He doesn't mind," said Tansy. "I asked him."
He looked through Lyra.
She stood there and let him. She did not know what else to do.
And then the eyes moved. Very slowly. The way a lamp moves when someone carries it along a wall. Off Lyra. To Oren, beside her. And through Oren, the same, as if Oren were not there either. Oren, who stood like a wall. Who frightened grown men just by standing. The eyes went through him like light through a window.
To Kestrel. Through her.
To Vespera. Through her. Vespera's breath went out in a hiss.
To Arran. Brin. Oda. Sef. Marrit. One by one. Through them all.
And then to Corin.
Corin was at the back. He had come in last, as he always did, slowest, crawling in through the round hole with Arran pulling his crutch through after him. He was standing just inside the hole now, leaning on the crutch, with his gray face and his gray hands and his bad leg stiff. In the shadow, a little, where the green light was faintest.
The eyes came to him.
And stopped.
Lyra saw it happen. She was watching the man's face, and she saw it. The eyes came to Corin, and they did not go through him. They stopped on him. On his face. The way your eyes stop on a face you know, in a crowd, when you were not expecting to see it.
They looked at him.
Not through. At.
The whole bubble went quiet. Even Tansy.
Corin did not move. Lyra saw his hand tighten on the crutch. Saw his throat move. He was looking back at the man. At the wild beard and the pale face and the eyes. And his own face had gone very strange. Very still. Like a man listening for something a long way off.
Then the man's hand moved.
The left one. It came up off his knee. Slowly. Not far. Only as high as his chest. And the long thin fingers opened. Spread. And the hand moved, once, from side to side. A small, short, quick movement. Just once.
A wave.
Like a boy waving. Like a small boy waving from the back of a cart, as it goes away down a road, to someone standing at a gate. Not knowing if they will wave back. Waving anyway.
Then the hand came down again, and lay on his knee, and was still.
And the eyes went off Corin, and on, through the wall, into the dark. As if nothing had happened.
No one said anything.
Lyra looked at Corin.
He was standing quite still by the hole, with both hands on the top of his crutch. His face was white under the gray. His mouth was a little open. And his hand, his right hand, the gray one, had come off the crutch and was pressed flat against his chest. Over the breastbone. Pressing hard. The way you press your hand on a place that aches.
"Corin?" she said.
He did not seem to hear her.
"Corin."
He looked at her. His eyes were wet.
"I don't know," he said. Very low. As if she had asked him something. "I don't know why."
He did not take his hand off his chest.
In the nest, Tansy was looking from the man to Corin and back, with her mouth open.
"He's never done that," she said. In a small voice, for her. "Not ever. Not to me. Not to anyone." She looked up at the man's face. "Why did you do that? You've never done that."
The man did not answer. He sat in the nest with his hands on his knees and looked through all of them, out into the dark, like a stone.
Tansy's Story
Tansy told them while they unpacked. She told it the way she seemed to do everything: all at once, without stopping, with her hands going.
They had settled round the edges of the bubble, where it curved down, with their packs off and their backs against the green glass. There was not much room. Ten of them, and Tansy, and the man in the nest. Oren had to sit with his knees up under his chin. Brin had to lie down. But it was warmer with all of them in, and out of the dead cold of the trough, and nobody wanted to go back out.
The man did not move from the nest. Tansy sat beside him, swinging her too-big boots, and talked.
"I'm from Haverstock," she said. "Do you know Haverstock? On the hill. With the cross. And the smithy that was the General's house. And the Sun and Pike. Do you know it?"
"We were there," said Lyra. "Five days ago."
Tansy stopped swinging her boots.
"Were you?" she said. And for a moment her face did something quick and complicated, and then it was gone. "Was the widow there? Widow Brennock? With the broom?"
"Yes."
"She used to chase me with it," said Tansy. "For stealing. I used to steal things. Little things. Shiny things." She glanced up at the ledge, at the row of treasures, without seeming to know she was doing it. "I don't mean to. My hands just do it. I see a shiny thing and then it's in my pocket. The widow said I had magpie fingers." She swung her boots again. "Everybody said that. Magpie fingers. Bad blood. They said my mother had magpie fingers too, before she died. I don't remember my mother."
She said it quite cheerfully.
"I lived with my uncle," she said. "He wasn't very nice. He took things too. Not shiny things. Other things. My shoes, when he was cross. My supper. The money I got for watching Mother Garrow's geese. He'd take it and drink it at the Sun and Pike, and then he'd come home, and if I said anything, he'd take more." Her boots swung. "And then one winter, two winters ago, I was sitting on the wall at the top of the town, by the cross. And I was thinking. And I heard old Tom by the fire at the inn, he was telling somebody about the Glass. About how nobody can take anything on the Glass. Nobody can take anything from you, out there. Not ever. That's what he said."
She looked round at them all.
"And I thought," said Tansy, "well. That's where I'm going, then. Because I'd had enough of people taking things."
No one laughed.
"So I went," said Tansy. "That night. I took my uncle's boots, because mine were too small and he'd taken them anyway. And his coat. And Mother Garrow's coat off the peg, which was wrong, I know it was wrong, but it was snowing. And my old one, because it was mine." She plucked at the three coats, one after another. "And a loaf, and a cheese, and a bottle of water. And I walked down the hill, and over the moor, and down the Lip, all in the snow. And I didn't go to Last Thorn, because they'd have sent me back. I went round. Along the Edge. And I walked out onto the Glass. On my own."
"At night?" said Kestrel. Very quietly.
"In the day," said Tansy. "I didn't know about the day. It was winter, so it wasn't too hot, not at first. But I nearly died anyway. Twice. Once of hot and once of thirsty." She said it as if she were talking about a cold. "And then I got lost. Really lost. And I found the Ridges, and I was walking in them, and I'd drunk all my water, and I was crying. Not loud. Just a bit."
She stopped swinging her boots.
"And he was there," she said.
She looked up at the man beside her.
"Standing," she said. "Just standing. In the middle of the Ridges, all on his own. Not doing anything. Looking at nothing. Like one of the glass men. I thought he was one of the glass men, at first. Only he wasn't green. And when I got close, he was breathing." She leaned her head against his arm. "I said, hello. He didn't say anything. I said, I'm lost. He didn't say anything. I said, I'm thirsty, I'm going to die. And he didn't say anything. But he turned round. And walked. And I followed him, because what else was I going to do. And he walked and walked, all night. And in the morning we came to a place where the Ashborn leave things."
"The shrines," said Kestrel.
"Is that what they are? The little piles of stones, with the bread on? And the water skins?" Tansy nodded. "He took me to one of those. And there was water. And bread. And he sat down and watched me eat it. And then he walked again, and I followed him again, and he brought me here."
She spread her hands, taking in the bubble. The nest. The ledge. The chalk.
"And I've been here since," she said. "Two winters. With him. I feed him. From the shrines. He'd forget to eat if I didn't. He forgets everything. He'd sit there till he died, I think, if nobody put bread in his hand." She patted his arm. "And I talk to him. All the time. Every day. So he won't forget what talking is. Because I think he knew, once. I think he used to talk. Before. And I don't want him to forget it all the way." She looked up at his face, at his pale eyes going through all of them. "Even if he never does it again. I want him to know what it sounds like."
No one said anything.
"And nobody takes anything from me," said Tansy. "Out here. Old Tom was right. Nobody can." She looked round at them all, and her chin came up. "So I'm not going back. If that's what you're thinking. I'm not going back to Haverstock. Ever."
"Nobody's going to make you," said Oren.
It was the first thing he had said since she called him cross. His voice was very rough.
Tansy looked at him for a long moment.
"Good," she said. "Because I'd bite."
The Silent Man
Kestrel had not sat down.
Lyra noticed it while Tansy was talking. Everyone else had found a place round the curve of the bubble and sunk into it, the way tired people sink. But Kestrel had stayed standing. Just inside the round mouth of the hole, with her spear upright beside her and her back to the green wall. Where she could see the whole bubble. Where she could see the man in the nest.
She had not taken her eyes off him once.
When Tansy finished, Kestrel spoke. Not to Tansy. To the rest of them. In the southern tongue. Low.
"We know him," she said.
Tansy stopped swinging her boots.
"Not him," said Kestrel. "We have never seen him. Not this close. But we know of him. Every Ashborn child knows of him." She touched the gray line on her face. Slowly. "The Silent Man of the Glass."
No one said anything.
"Old Hask told it," said Kestrel. "And his mother before him. There is a man who walks on the Glass. Not one of us. Not one of the gray men. Nobody knows where he came from. He walks where the dead are thickest. Along the Trench, at night. Through the Ridges. Round the shore of the Eye. Walkers see him sometimes, a long way off, standing. Always standing. Always alone. Looking at nothing." She paused. "And he never speaks. If you call to him, he does not turn. If you go near him, he walks away. If you follow him, you lose him."
"I didn't lose him," said Tansy.
Kestrel looked at her. Just for a moment.
"No," she said. "You did not."
She looked back at the man.
"We leave bread for him," she said. "At the shrines. Bread, and water, and sometimes a little cheese, when we have it. We have done it since before this one was born. We did not know if he took it. Sometimes the bread was gone. We thought it was birds. Or the wind." Her eyes went to Tansy. "It was you."
"It was for him," said Tansy. "I didn't eat it. Well. I ate some. But mostly it was for him."
"We know," said Kestrel.
She did not say anything for a while.
"The old ones say," she said at last, "that he is a soldier. From the war. One who did not turn into glass when the sun came. One who was standing in the wrong place, or the right place. And the sun went through him and did not stop him. And took everything out of him, everything inside, and left him walking." She touched the line again. "The old ones say he is looking for his name. That he walks among the dead because he thinks one of them has it. And that one day he will find it, and speak, and then he will lie down."
"That's sad," said Tansy.
"It is a story," said Kestrel.
"Is it true?"
Kestrel looked at the man in the nest. At the wild beard and the pale face and the eyes going through everything.
"The war was sixty years ago," said Vespera, from the far side of the bubble. Her voice was dry and sharp. "Look at him. He's no more than forty. Fifty at the very outside. If he was a soldier at the war, he'd be eighty now. Or dead."
"Yes," said Kestrel.
"So it isn't true."
"So it is a story," said Kestrel.
She still had not sat down. Lyra watched her. Watched the way she stood, with her back to the wall by the hole, and her spear upright, and her weight a little forward on her feet. The way you stand when you are ready to move.
"Sit down," said Tansy. "You're making me tired, standing there. There's room. You can sit by me." She patted the blanket beside her, on the side away from the man. "Come on. He won't hurt you. He's never hurt anybody."
Kestrel did not move.
"We will stand," she said.
"Why?"
Kestrel did not answer.
But Lyra saw her glance, quick, at the man. And then at the wall behind her own back. And she understood.
Kestrel would not sit down with her back to him.
Kestrel, who had walked onto the Glass at midnight with iron men behind her. Who had sung to the dead in the Trench every year since she was a girl. Who carried a whole crowded bed of the dead inside her, and got up every morning anyway. Kestrel was afraid. Not much. Not so that anyone who did not know her would see. But enough that she would not turn her back on a thin silent man sitting in a heap of blankets with a child leaning on his arm.
Lyra looked at the man again.
He had not moved. His hands were on his knees. His eyes were on the hole, on the dark beyond it. He was breathing, slow and even.
She tried to see what Kestrel saw. She could not. He looked like a beggar. Like one of the men who sat on the steps of Saint Bride's in the winter, with their hands out, who had drunk or lost or burned everything they had and sat there with nothing behind their eyes.
Except that those men looked at you. Even with nothing behind their eyes. They looked at you because you might give them a coin.
This man did not look at anyone.
Except Corin.
She turned her head. Corin was sitting just inside the hole, on the other side from Kestrel, with his bad leg out straight and his crutch across his lap. He was not looking at the man now. He was looking at his own hands. At the gray right one, which he had taken off his chest at last, and which was lying on his knee, palm up, the fingers a little curled.
He was very pale.
Lyra wanted to go to him. She did not. There was no room to get across the bubble without stepping on someone.
She sat where she was, with her back against the cold green glass, and watched Kestrel watch the man, and the man watch nothing, and Corin watch his own empty hand.
Supper
It was Oren who brought out the food.
He did not ask Kestrel. He reached into his pack, and then into Brin's, which was nearest, and took out what was there. And laid it on a fold of felt on the curved floor of the bubble, in the middle, where everyone could reach.
The flat dark bread of Last Thorn. Hard now, almost like bark, but bread. A strip of dried goat for each of them. A lump of the hard white cheese from Thistle Ford, wrapped in a cloth, that Granny Abbe had pressed into Corin's hands at the ford a week ago. The last of Granny Abbe's dried apples, in their little cloth bag, gone brown and leathery. And then, from deep at the bottom of his own pack, wrapped in three layers of oilcloth and tied with string, a small clay jar with a wax seal.
He held it up in the green light.
"Honey," he said. "Doctor Silas. For the leg. Also for eating." He looked at Corin. "I've been carrying it since the city. Didn't tell you. You'd have wanted to save it."
Corin looked at the jar. Something moved in his face.
"I would have," he said.
"Aye. Well." Oren broke the seal with his thumbnail. "No more saving."
And Tansy, in the nest, made a sound.
It was not a word. It was a sound like a small animal makes. Lyra looked round, and saw her. She was sitting bolt upright beside the silent man with her hands clenched on her knees, staring at the food on the felt. At the bread. At the cheese. At the apples. At the honey, golden in its little jar in Oren's big fist.
Her mouth was open. And she was not talking.
It was the first time since they had found her that she was not talking.
"Come on," said Oren. Roughly. "Come and have some. Don't sit there gawping."
Tansy came.
She came out of the nest the way she had come out of the hole. All at once. Scrambling. And she knelt by the felt, and she took a piece of bread, and she did not eat it. She looked at it. She held it in both her hands and looked at it as if it were a thing in a shop window that she could not afford.
Then she ate it.
She ate it like a starving dog. Lyra had seen starving dogs, in the alleys behind the docks, when the fish carts came. The way they ate. Not tasting. Not chewing. Tearing and swallowing, tearing and swallowing, with their eyes going everywhere, in case someone took it away. Tansy ate like that. The bread went in three bites. Then a piece of cheese, whole, so that her cheeks bulged. Then the dried goat, torn with her teeth like a fox. She did not say anything. She did not look at anyone. She ate.
No one stopped her. No one said anything. Lyra saw Kestrel watching from her place by the hole, with her face very still. Saw Vespera, sharp-eyed, looking at the thin wrists sticking out of the too-small red coat, and at the hollows in the girl's cheeks. Saw Marrit, very old, nod slowly, as if this were a thing she had seen before, in some other winter.
Oren cut a slice of apple with his knife and dipped it in the honey and held it out.
Tansy took it. Put it in her mouth.
And stopped.
She sat there, on her knees on the felt, with her cheeks still full of cheese, and the apple and the honey in her mouth. And her face changed. Lyra watched it change. All the sharpness went out of it. All the quickness. Her eyes went wide, and then they went wet, and then they shut.
She chewed. Very slowly.
She swallowed.
"Oh," she said. Very small. "Oh."
Then she opened her eyes, and looked at Oren, and said, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "That's the nicest thing I've ever had in my whole life."
"It's only honey," said Oren.
"It's not only," said Tansy.
She did not take another piece. Lyra saw her look at the honey. Want it. And not take it. Instead she picked up a second piece of bread, a big one, and a strip of goat, and a slice of apple. And dipped the apple in the honey herself, carefully, so that it was thickly covered. And then she got up off her knees, and went back to the nest.
She did not eat them.
She knelt beside the silent man. And took his right hand, very gently, off his knee. And opened his fingers, one by one, the way you open the hand of a sleeping baby. And put the piece of bread in it. And closed his fingers round it.
Then she held the apple slice up to his mouth.
"Here," she said. "Here. It's honey. Taste. Go on."
The man did not look at her. His eyes went on through the wall.
But his mouth opened.
Tansy put the slice of apple in it. And he closed his mouth. And chewed. Slowly. Without any change in his face at all.
"There," said Tansy. "Isn't that nice? That's honey. Say honey."
He did not say anything.
"That's all right," said Tansy. "Tomorrow, then." And she tucked the strip of goat and the other half of her share into a pocket of the outside coat. Saving it for him. Lyra saw her do it. Saw her hand go to the pocket and push it in deep and pat it flat, the way you would hide a thing from someone who might take it. Even here. Even out here, where nobody could take anything.
Then she sat down beside him in the nest again, close against his arm, and began, at last, to talk.
"That's Oren," she told him. "The cross one. He gave us honey. So he's not really cross. I told you he wasn't. And that's Lyra, the pretty sad one, and that's Corin, the hurt one, and that's the tall one, she's called Kestrel, that's a bird—"
She went round them all. Every name. Getting every one right.
The man sat with the bread in his hand and did not eat it, and did not look at any of them, and did not say anything. But Lyra, watching, thought that his head had tipped, a very little, toward the sound of the girl's voice. The way a plant leans toward a window.
