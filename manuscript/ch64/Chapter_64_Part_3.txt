Chapter 64: The Ashborn, Part 3 of 5: Old Hask
Old Hask
Someone was humming.
Corin had been hearing it for a while without knowing it, the way you hear the wind. A low cracked old voice, somewhere behind the crowd on the far mats, humming a tune with no shape to it. Not a song. Only a sound that went up a little and down a little and round and round, like a stream going over the same stones.
Now the humming came closer.
The crowd on the far mats moved. People shifted aside, quietly, without being asked, to make a path, and down the path there came a very old man, with one hand on the shoulder of a small boy who walked in front of him and led him.
He was blind. Corin saw that at once. His eyes were open, but they were white. Not gray. White, all over, milky and clouded, like the eyes of a fish that has been a long time out of the water. They did not move. They looked straight ahead at nothing as he came, and his face above them was turned a little upward, as if he were listening for rain.
He was small and bent and very thin, so thin that the hide coat he wore hung off him like a coat on a peg. And he was the oldest person Corin had ever seen. Older than Granny Abbe. Older than Silas. His skin was brown and creased and folded on itself, everywhere, like the skin of an apple left all winter in a loft. And it was gray. All of it. Not a line. Not patches. His whole face was gray, from the edge of the white hair on his head down to his chin, and his neck, and his hands. The gray of old ash. The gray of Kestrel's line, all over him.
He had a beard. It was long and white and thin, and it came down to the middle of his chest, and it had been plaited. Not into one plait. Into many. Seven or eight thin little plaits, each one tied at the end with a scrap of colored thread, red and blue and yellow and green, so that they swung and clicked against each other as he walked. Some of the plaits had bone beads in them. One had a small brass bell, the size of a pea, that made a tiny sound.
He was still humming.
The boy led him to the edge of the mats, beside Kestrel, and stopped, and the old man stopped too. Kestrel got up at once. She took the old man's other arm, very gently, and she and the boy let him down between them onto a mat, slowly, a little at a time, the way you might let down a bucket into a well. It took a long while. His knees cracked. He breathed out hard. But he did not stop humming the whole time, and when he was down at last, sitting cross-legged on the mat with his hands on his knees, he went on humming, round and round, for a little while longer, and then he stopped.
He turned his face toward the fire. Toward the warmth of it.
"Well now," he said.
His voice was thin and cracked and high, like an old reed pipe, and it had a laugh in it somewhere.
"Well now. Kestrel, little one. Who's this you've let in?"
"Three from over the Lip, Grandfather," said Kestrel. She had sat down again, close beside him. "From the valleys. And further than the valleys."
"Further," said the old man. "Yes. I can smell the further on them. The city, is it? Coal smoke and river mud." He sniffed. "And lamp oil. No. Not oil." He sniffed again, longer, and his face changed a little. "Ah. The other lamps. The new ones. I know that smell. It came up here once, a long time ago, on the men in gray."
Nobody said anything.
"This is Old Hask," said Kestrel to the three of them. "He is the oldest of us. He carries more than anyone at Last Thorn."
"More than you, little one?" said Old Hask, and laughed. A dry, wheezing laugh, like the wind in a chimney.
"More than any of us," said Kestrel.
"That's because I've been here longer. You'll catch me up." He turned his white blind eyes, very slowly, along the row of them. Oren first. Then Lyra. He stopped at Lyra for a moment and tilted his head, the way a bird does. "Ah," he said softly. "Little one."
Then he came to Corin. And stopped.
For a long moment the old man did not speak. He sat with his blind face turned toward Corin, and his head a little on one side, and his lips moving, slightly, as if he were talking to himself without any sound. The little plaits in his beard swung and were still.
Then he put out his hand.
"Come here, little one," he said. "Come here, so I can see you."
Corin looked at Kestrel. She nodded.
He got up and went round her, and knelt down on the gravel in front of the old man.
Old Hask's hand came up and found his face. It was dry and light and very cold, like a dead leaf, and it trembled a little. It touched his forehead first. Then his eyes, very gently, so that Corin closed them. Then his nose, and his cheeks, and his mouth, and his chin. Then his ears. The old fingers went behind his left ear and stayed there, on the small bare place where the hair did not grow, and pressed it a little, and Corin felt a shiver go all down his back.
"Hm," said Old Hask.
The hand went down. To Corin's neck. To his shoulder. And down his left arm, over the coat, slowly, until it came to the place. The place a hand's width below the shoulder, less a finger, where the gray stopped under the sleeve. The old fingers stopped there. As if they could feel the line through the cloth.
They could. Corin knew they could.
"Hm," said Old Hask again.
Then his hand came away from the arm, and went back up, and lay flat on the middle of Corin's chest, over his heart. And stayed there.
The old man was quiet for a long time.
The fire hissed. Corin knelt on the gravel with the old dry hand on his chest and his eyes still closed, and waited. He could hear his own heart, under the hand. He could hear the hum of the Glass. He could hear, very faintly, the little brass bell in the old man's beard, ringing a tiny ring each time the old man breathed.
"There's a room in you," said Old Hask at last.
His voice had changed. It was not laughing now. It was quiet and slow and careful, the way a man talks when he is feeling his way across a dark room and does not want to knock anything over.
"There's a room in you," he said, "with the door off its hinges."
Corin opened his eyes.
The old man's white eyes were looking past him, at nothing. But his face was very close.
"I can feel the draft from it," said Old Hask. "Cold. Coming out. All the time. You can't shut it, can you? You've tried. Many times. But the door won't sit in its frame anymore. Something broke it." His hand pressed a little on Corin's chest. "Long ago. When you were very small."
Corin could not speak. His mouth had gone dry.
"And it wasn't kicked in," said Old Hask, very softly. "No. It was broken from the inside. Somebody went out of it in a hurry. Pushed it so hard it came off its pins, and never stopped to put it back." The old head tilted. "Do you know who it was, little one?"
"No," said Corin. It came out in a whisper.
"No," said Old Hask. "No. I didn't think so." He was quiet for a moment more. Then he patted Corin's chest, twice, lightly, the way you might pat a horse to say you are done with it, and took his hand away and laid it back on his knee. "Well. You'll find out. Everybody does, in the end. Up here."
He turned his face back toward the fire and began, after a while, to hum again.
Corin knelt where he was. He could not get up. His legs would not do it. He knelt on the gravel and stared at the old man, and behind his left ear the bare place prickled and burned as if the cold old fingers were still on it.
Then a hand took his arm.
It was Lyra. She had come round Kestrel and knelt down beside him and taken hold of his sleeve, and she was pulling, a little.
"Come and sit down," she said. "Come on. You're shaking."
He let her pull him up, and lead him back to his mat, and sit him down. She sat down beside him, close, closer than before, so that her shoulder was against his arm. She did not let go of his sleeve.
Old Hask hummed. Round and round. The plaits in his beard clicked.
"He says that to everyone," said Arran, from across the fire. But he said it quietly, and he did not laugh, and Corin saw him look at Kestrel, and Kestrel did not look back.
A Map in the Ash
"You are going over," said Kestrel.
It was not a question either. Nothing Kestrel said seemed to be a question. Corin was beginning to understand that it was just the way the Ashborn talked. They said what they saw, and waited to see if you would say it was wrong.
"Through," said Corin. "Not over. To the far side."
"To the far side." Kestrel was quiet for a moment. "Why?"
He did not know how to answer that. There were too many answers, and none of them were the whole of it. Because the rod in his pocket pointed north, always north, whichever way he held it. Because Malakor had told him the way was right. Because there was a war in him he could not remember and a place on the far side of the Glass where it had started. Because the men in gray were coming behind them. Because a small hand in a big dry hand, in a memory with no voice in it, had been walking this way.
Because his feet went that way.
"There's something there," he said at last. "On the other side. Something I need to see."
Kestrel looked at him for a long moment.
Then she leaned forward and reached out to the edge of the fire, where the gray ash lay soft and cold in a drift against the black stones, and smoothed it flat with the side of her hand. She smoothed it carefully, the way a baker smooths flour on a board, until it was a small square of clean gray powder about the size of a book.
Then she drew in it with one finger.
She drew a long wavy line along the bottom of the square. "The Lip," she said. "Where you came over." A row of small marks above it, like little crosses. "The thorns. Last Thorn." A dot, among them. "Here. Where we sit." Then a straight line, above the thorns, from one side of the square to the other. "The ridge." And above the ridge, she drew nothing at all. She only smoothed it again, with her fingertips, so that it was very flat and very clean and very empty.
"The Glass," she said.
She let them look at the empty space for a while.
Then she put one finger in the very middle of the empty space, and pressed, and made a little round hollow there, like the hole a raindrop makes in dust.
"The Eye," she said. "In the middle. A mile across, they say. Maybe less. Maybe more. Nobody has measured it."
"What's in it?" said Lyra.
"Nobody knows," said Kestrel. "Or the ones who know have not come back to tell us." She did not lift her finger from the hollow. "But we know this. Out on the Glass, nothing works. No taking. No burning. You cannot pull a thing out of someone, and you cannot put a thing of your own into the fire, not out there. It will not light. The Glass will not let it. You can give. A word. A story. Bread. Those you can give. But you cannot take, and you cannot burn." Her finger pressed the hollow a little deeper. "Except in the Eye. In the Eye it all comes back. Everything. They say it comes back stronger."
"Who says?"
"The old ones. The ones who walked it in the war."
Old Hask, beside her, stopped humming for a moment, and then started again.
Kestrel drew a line, then. From the dot of Last Thorn, up across the ridge, and out onto the clean gray space of the Glass. It went straight for a little way. Then it bent. Then it went straight again, and bent again. A zigzag, crooked, like the path of a man walking in the dark who keeps losing his way and finding it.
"The old track," she said. "The way they came, in the war. You will not see it. There is nothing to see. But there are stones. Here. And here. And here." She touched each bend in the line with her fingertip. "Cairns. Very old. Some of them have fallen. You go from one to the next. If you lose the next one, you stop. You sit down. You wait for light. You do not go on without it. If you go on without it, you walk in circles until you die."
Oren was leaning forward, frowning at the ash. "How far between them?"
"It changes. A mile. Two. Sometimes more."
"And how far across, all of it?"
Kestrel was quiet. Then she said, "Ten days, walking. If the walking is good. If you walk by night."
"By night," said Oren.
"By night." She looked up at him. "By day the Glass is warm. It hums. It is like walking on the top of a stove. And the light comes up out of it, and you cannot see where you are going, and you get no shade from it anywhere. By night it is cold. Very cold. Colder than you have ever been. But the light goes down, and the stars come out, and you can see the cairns against them. The old ones walked by night. Always. And slept by day, under their coats." She looked back at the ash map. "It is the only way."
There was a little silence.
And it was then, while she was bent over the ash, with her left hand flat on the gravel beside the map to hold herself steady, that Corin saw the mark.
It was on the back of her hand. Her left hand. Between the knuckles and the wrist, on the flat of it, where the skin was thin and brown and lined with veins. A small dark shape, about the size of a coin. Not gray. Blue. A faded blue-black, the color of very old ink, sunk deep into the skin.
A flower.
A little wild rose, with five round petals and a dot in the middle. The kind that grows in the hedges along the roads in summer, pale pink, with yellow at the heart. Very simple. The kind of thing a child might draw. The lines of it had blurred and spread with the years, the way ink does in old skin, so that the edges of the petals were soft and gray. It must have been there a long time. Since she was young, perhaps. Since she was a girl.
Corin looked at it, in the firelight, and thought: a rose.
He thought: the Ashborn have marks for things. The line on their faces. The hands on their tents. That must be one of them. A mark for something.
And then Kestrel lifted her hand off the gravel and sat back, and the firelight went off it, and the rose was gone into the dark of her sleeve.
And he did not think of it again.
"Ten days," said Oren slowly. He was still frowning at the ash. "And whatever's behind us is a day behind us. Maybe less."
Kestrel's eyes came up quick. "Behind you?"
Corin told her. Not all of it. He told her that there were men coming from the south. Men from the city. That they had been sent after him by people who did not want him to cross. That they would be at Thistle Ford by now, perhaps, or near it. That they did not tire, and did not sleep, and did not stop.
He did not tell her what they were. He was not sure he could have said it so that she would believe him.
Kestrel listened to all of it without moving.
Then she reached out with her right hand and passed it, flat, over the ash map, once. And the map was gone. The Lip, the thorns, the ridge, the crooked line, the hollow in the middle. All gone. Only a smooth gray square of ash again, clean and empty.
"Then they will come here," she said. "In a day. In three. And when they come, you must be ready to walk." She stood up, tall, against the milky light over the ridge, and looked down at Corin. "But you will not walk the Glass with a drowned woman on your back. Not with the fish girl and the woman who sold children shouting at each other inside you. Out there, there is no one to take her off you. Out there, she will be yours until the end." She touched the gray line at the tip of her nose. "So first we will lighten you. Here. Where it can still be done."
Not Tonight
The crowd on the far mats had begun to go. Not all at once. In ones and twos, the way people leave a hall after a wedding, slowly, stopping to talk. Mothers lifted sleeping children off the mats and carried them away into the dark between the tents. Old men got up stiffly and went off together, leaning on each other. The young man and the young woman who had been holding hands went last, still holding hands, and the girl looked back once at Lyra over her shoulder, curious, and then was gone.
The children with the spears had been sent to bed long ago. Corin had watched them go, protesting, in a line, with Arran walking behind them like a shepherd behind sheep.
Now there were only a few of them left round the fire. Kestrel. Old Hask, humming, with his blind face to the ridge. Arran, who had come back and sat down again with his flute in his lap. The three of them. And the goat, asleep now, a warm heap against the back of Oren's coat.
The fire had burned down. Kestrel put more on it from a pile beside her. Not wood. Thorn. Long dry twisted branches of it, gray and spiny, that she broke over her knee with her bare hands without seeming to notice the spines. And a handful of something else, from a hide bag at her belt. Small dry brown curls, like wood shavings, or like the dried petals of some flower. She threw them on, and the burnt-honey smell came up again, thick and sweet, and the flames went up yellow.
Lyra had been quiet a long time.
Corin had felt her being quiet. It was a particular kind of quiet, with Lyra. Not the quiet of someone who has nothing to say. The quiet of someone holding a thing in her mouth and turning it over and over, deciding whether to let it out.
Now she let it out.
"There was a doll," she said. "In the fens."
Kestrel did not move. But her eyes, which had been on the fire, came up.
"Robin had it," said Lyra. "A little boy. Wat's boy, on the punt that brought us north. In his basket. A rag doll, an old one, with a cloth face and a little blue dress. And the stitches on it were strange. They all went backward. Every one. Leaning the wrong way, like..." She stopped, and turned her gloved hand over in the air, trying to show it. "Like a person sewing with the wrong hand. On purpose."
Kestrel said nothing.
"I told Granny Abbe about it, at Thistle Ford. She said it was the old valley kind," said Lyra. "She said they make them for the children who go over the Glass. And she said if I wanted to know any more, I had to ask at Last Thorn. She said the Ashborn don't lie about the dead." Lyra's voice had gone a little tight. "So I'm asking."
The fire cracked.
Arran had stopped turning the flute in his fingers. He was looking at Kestrel. Old Hask had stopped humming.
For a long moment Kestrel did not answer at all. She sat with her long arms round her knees and her face to the ridge, and the firelight moved on the gray line on her skull, and she did not speak, and Corin thought she was not going to. He thought she was going to let the question go by, like a stranger on a road, without looking at it.
Then she said, "What children?"
Lyra blinked. "What?"
"Which children did she mean. Granny Abbe. When she said the ones who go over the Glass." Kestrel's voice was very even. "Did she say?"
"No. She just said children."
"Then she did not lie either."
Lyra waited. Kestrel did not go on.
"That's not an answer," said Lyra.
"No," said Kestrel. "It is not."
She turned her head, slowly, and looked at Lyra. And her face was not unkind. But it was shut. Shut like a door, or like a hand closing over something small.
"Not tonight," said Kestrel.
Lyra opened her mouth.
"Not tonight," said Kestrel again, more gently. "You have come over the Lip today, and down the thorns in the dark, and you have eaten our bread, and you have sat with your faces to the Glass for the first time. And you have heard about the dead. That is enough for one night. That is more than enough." She looked at the fire again. "The dolls are another kind of dead. They need a day of their own. And there are things about them that are not ours to tell. Not only ours."
"Whose, then?"
Kestrel did not answer that either.
Lyra sat very still. Corin could feel how much she wanted to push. He could feel it in the way her shoulder had gone hard against his arm, in the way her hands had come together in her lap and knotted. She had always pushed. That was who she was. She had pushed him under the Bourse, and in the roof garden, and in the snow on the dyke road. She did not let go of a thing once she had her teeth in it.
But she did not push now.
She looked at Kestrel's face, at the shut door of it, for a long time. And then she let her breath go, slowly, and her hands came apart in her lap.
"All right," she said. "Not tonight."
"Thank you," said Kestrel.
"But I'll ask again."
"Yes," said Kestrel. "We know." And something moved at the corner of her mouth. It was not quite a smile. But it was the nearest Corin had seen her come to one.
Old Hask began to hum again, very low.
Arran turned the flute in his fingers, and then put it to his lips, and then took it away again without playing.
"She'll tell you," he said to Lyra, across the fire. Quietly, the way you might tell a child a thing that is true but that the grown-ups do not like to say out loud. "When it's time. She always does. She told me." He looked at the flute. "It took her four winters."
"Arran," said Kestrel.
He shut his mouth.
And Corin, sitting between them with Lyra's shoulder against his arm and the warm heavy smell of burnt honey in his throat, found that he was thinking of the rose. He did not know why. The little faded blue rose on the back of Kestrel's hand, with its five round petals. He had not thought of it since he saw it. He did not think of it now, exactly. It was only there, at the edge of his mind, the way a word is there sometimes when you are trying to think of a different one.
Then Old Hask's hum went up a little, and down, and round, and the rose went out of his head again, and he did not miss it.
