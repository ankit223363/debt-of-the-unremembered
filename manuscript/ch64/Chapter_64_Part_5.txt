Chapter 64: The Ashborn, Part 5 of 5: Three Notes
Three Notes
They did not sleep.
They lay down. All three of them, side by side under the goat skins along the north wall of the tent, with their feet to the door and their faces to the ridge, the way Arran had laid out the furs. Oren on the left, by the door, because Oren always lay by the door. Lyra in the middle. Corin on the right, against the curve of the hide wall, with his crutch laid beside him and his coat rolled under his head.
Oren blew out the lamp. The dark came down. Only the red of the coals in the fire ring, and the three stars in the smoke hole, and the faint milky shine through the hide from the light above the ridge.
And the hum.
It was louder in the dark. Or it seemed louder. Corin lay with his eyes open and felt it come up through the gravel and the grass mats and the goat skins, into his back, into his ribs. A low steady note, so low it was more like a feeling than a sound. Like lying on the deck of a ship at anchor and feeling the sea move under the boards.
Beside him, Lyra lay very still. He could tell she was not asleep. Her breathing was too careful. Too even. The breathing of someone who is counting their breaths, in and out, to make themselves sleep, and not sleeping.
On her other side, Oren's breathing went slow and deep almost at once. Then, after a while, very softly, he began to snore.
Lyra let out a breath that was almost a laugh.
Corin lay and looked at the stars in the smoke hole.
Then, outside, someone began to play.
It was a flute. A thin high sound, clear and a little breathy, like wind across the mouth of a bottle. It came from somewhere close. Just outside the tent, Corin thought, on the north side, between the tent and the ridge. Someone sitting out there in the cold, in the dark, playing to themselves.
Arran. It had to be. The little bone flute on the cord round his neck.
He was not playing a tune. He was only playing three notes. The same three, over and over. A low one, and a high one, and one a little lower again, in between, that stopped short, as if it had been about to go somewhere and had changed its mind. Then a pause. Then the same three again. Low, high, a little lower. Then a pause.
Over and over. Lazily. The way a man drums his fingers on a table while he waits for something. Not thinking about it.
Beside Corin, Lyra had gone still.
Not the careful stillness of before. A different kind. All at once, the way an animal goes still in a field when it hears a sound it knows. He felt it through the goat skin between them. Her whole body had gone hard, from her shoulders to her feet. She had stopped breathing.
The flute played the three notes again.
Low. High. A little lower. And the stop.
"Corin," she whispered.
"I hear it."
"That's..." She stopped. He heard her swallow in the dark. "That's the song. That's the start of it. The moon song. Mother's song. Those are the first three notes."
He knew. He had known as soon as he heard them. He had heard Lyra hum that song on the dyke road in the snow, flat, the way she sang everything, and in the loft at Thistle Ford when she thought he was asleep. He did not know the words. He was not sure he had ever known them. But the three notes went into him and fitted somewhere, the way a key fits a keyhole you did not know was there.
The flute played them again.
Lyra sat up.
She did it so fast that the goat skin slid off her and the cold rushed in. Then she was up on her knees, and pushing past Corin's feet, and crawling to the door, and putting her hand on the flap.
"Lyra," said Corin.
She did not answer. She pushed the flap aside and went out on her hands and knees into the dark.
He went after her. It took him longer. His leg was stiff and the crutch caught on the door frame and he had to pull it through. When he came out, the cold hit him like a slap. It was much colder than it had been by the fire. The air was dry and sharp and it hurt to breathe, and the gravel under his hands was so cold it burned.
Arran was sitting on a log, a few steps from the tent, between it and the ridge. He had a fur round his shoulders and his knees drawn up and the flute at his lips. He was facing the ridge. The milky light from beyond it lay on his face and on the gray line down his nose. He did not look round when they came out. He went on playing. The three notes, and the pause. The three notes, and the pause.
Lyra was standing a step behind him. She had got to her feet. She stood there in the cold, in her stocking feet on the frozen gravel, without her coat, with her arms wrapped round herself, staring at the back of his head.
"Where did you learn that?" she said.
Arran stopped playing.
He took the flute away from his mouth and looked round at her, surprised. Then he grinned his wide white grin.
"Learn what?"
"That." Her voice shook. "What you were playing. Those three notes. Where did you learn them?"
Arran looked at the flute in his hand, as if he had forgotten it was there. Then back at her. He shrugged.
"Nowhere," he said. "Everybody knows that."
"Everybody?"
"Everybody here. The little ones play it on reeds before they can talk." He turned the flute in his long fingers. "It's nothing. It's what you play when you're bored." He looked at her more closely, and the grin went thinner. "Why? Do you know it?"
Lyra did not answer.
She stood there with her arms round herself and looked at him. And Corin, behind her, leaning on his crutch in the bitter cold, could not see her face. But he saw her shoulders. And he saw them go down, slowly, a little at a time, the way shoulders go down when a person has been holding something up for a long time and has just found out it was never as heavy as they thought. Or that it was much heavier.
"Is there more?" she said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "After the three. Is there a song that goes with it?"
Arran thought about it. Really thought, with his head on one side.
"No," he said. "I don't think so. It's just the three. It's always just the three." He frowned a little. "Old Hask says there used to be more. A long time ago. He says the rest of it went over the Glass with somebody and didn't come back." Then he laughed his bark of a laugh. "But Old Hask says a lot of things."
Lyra stood very still.
Then, without a word, she turned round and walked past Corin, back to the tent, and got down on her hands and knees, and went in.
Arran looked at Corin. Corin looked at Arran.
"Did I say something?" said Arran.
"No," said Corin. "Not you."
Arran looked at the tent for a moment longer. Then he looked down at the flute in his hand, and turned it over, and did not put it back to his mouth.
After a while he got up, and pulled the fur round his shoulders, and went away along the edge of the camp in the dark, toward the line of thorn trees at the foot of the ridge.
Corin stood alone in the cold, by the tent, listening.
Inside, very faintly, so faintly he was not sure he heard it, Lyra was humming. Flat. Three notes. And then a fourth, that went up and hung, and stopped.
The Last Tree
He did not go back into the tent.
He stood in the cold for a long while, listening to Lyra hum, until the humming stopped. Then he listened to the quiet. Then, because he could not make his feet go in, and because the ridge was there, low and dark, a stone's throw away, with the milky light lying along the top of it like frost, he turned and went up it.
It was not high. Four or five times his own height, perhaps. A long bank of loose gray gravel and small stones, with a few thin thorn bushes growing out of it, bent low, all leaning south. The crutch sank into the gravel at every step and he had to pull it out. The stones rolled under his boots and rattled down behind him. It took him a long time. Twice he had to stop and lean on the crutch and breathe, with the cold air burning in his chest.
At the top there was a tree.
One tree. A thorn tree, like all the others, but bigger. Much bigger. It stood on the very crest of the ridge, alone, with its trunk as thick as a man and its branches spread out wide and black and twisted against the sky. All of them leaning south. All of them reaching away from what was on the other side. As if the whole tree had spent a hundred years trying to walk away and had never been able to pull its roots out of the ground.
There was someone sitting under it.
Kestrel. She sat with her back against the trunk on the south side of it, on the camp side, with her knees drawn up and her fur cape round her, facing north. Facing out past the tree, across the top of the ridge, at what lay beyond.
She did not turn her head when he came up. But she said, "You did not sleep."
"No."
"No." She moved over a little, on the gravel, to make room beside her against the trunk. "Nobody sleeps the first night. Sit."
He sat. It took a while, with the leg and the crutch. She did not help him. She waited, and when he was down, with his back against the rough black trunk beside her, she did not say anything more.
He looked out.
And there it was.
He had seen it from the Lip, at noon, under the winter sun. Green-black and shining, flat to the edge of the sky. He had thought, then, that he knew what it looked like.
He had not known.
At night the Glass was not green. It was black. A black so deep and so smooth that it did not look like ground at all. It looked like water. Like the sea on a still night, with no wind, so still that there was not one ripple anywhere on it from here to the end of the world. It began beyond the foot of the ridge, past a last long slope of scrub and gravel, perhaps a mile off, though in that light it looked much closer, where the land stopped in a clean straight line. And it went out from there. On and on and on. Flat. Until it met the sky.
And in it there were stars.
The sky above was full of them. More than he had ever seen in the city, where the lamps put them out. More of them than anybody could count, big and small, white and blue and faintly gold, so many and so close that the sky looked dusty with them. And every one of them was in the Glass too. Down below. Upside down. A second sky, under the first, as clear and as full, so that the line where the land met the sky was not there anymore, and he seemed to be sitting on the edge of a great black bowl with stars all round it, above and below, and nothing in the middle.
He could not breathe for a moment.
It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.
And it was the most frightening.
Then he saw the lights.
They were under the stars. Under the surface of the Glass. Small pale lights, soft and cloudy, like candle flames seen through a frosted window. Not many. More than his fingers, perhaps. Not much more. Scattered far apart across the black. And they were moving. Very slowly. Drifting, the way fish drift in a deep pool, rising a little and sinking a little, going nowhere.
"What are they?" he said. His voice came out in a whisper.
"We do not know," said Kestrel. "We call them the lost ones. They are always there, at night. They are never there by day." She watched them. "The children think they are the ones who walked out and did not come back. Looking for the way home."
"Are they?"
"We do not know," she said again.
They sat and watched the lights drift. The cold came up out of the ground into Corin's legs and his back and his hands. It was a cold like nothing he had felt before. Not wet, like the city cold. Not windy, like the hills. Dry and still and deep, so that it seemed to go in through his skin without stopping and settle in his bones. His breath smoked and hung in front of his face and did not drift away.
And the hum. Up here, at the top of the ridge, with nothing between him and the Glass, it was everywhere.
Kestrel spoke after a long time.
"There is a story," she said. "We tell it to the children. When they are frightened of the Glass. When they are small, and they wake up in the night, and they cry because they can feel it there, looking."
Corin waited.
"Once," said Kestrel, "a long time ago, a child walked the Glass twice."
She said it in a different voice. A storytelling voice. Lower and slower and softer, the way a mother talks to a child in the dark.
"Once out. And once back. A small child. Smaller than the ones who stopped you on the path. He went out over the Glass with an old man, in the first frost, when nobody goes out. And everybody said, that is the last of them. And all the winter they were gone, and nobody looked for them, because there was no use in looking. And then in the spring..." She stopped. "In the spring, when the thorns came into flower, the child came back. Walking. Over the ridge. Just there." She lifted one hand from her knees and pointed, very slightly, at the crest of the ridge a little way along from the tree. "With the old man holding his hand."
The lights drifted under the stars.
"And he was not afraid," said Kestrel. "That is how the story ends. He came down the ridge into the camp, and he was not afraid of anything. Not the Glass. Not the dark. Not the lost ones. Because he had walked it, out and back. And nothing out there had been able to keep him."
She put her hand back on her knee.
"That is the story," she said. "We tell it so the children will be brave."
Corin sat very still.
He was thinking of the old men by the fire at the Sun and Pike. Tom with his one blue eye, and Huw with the stones. Talk, years back. Of an old man and a lad going out on the Glass at first frost. And coming back in spring.
He was thinking of a small hand in a big dry hand.
"Is it true?" he said.
Kestrel did not answer at once. She sat with her face to the Glass and the double sky, and the starlight lay on the gray line on her skull.
Then she said, quite simply, the way she said everything:
"No. It isn't true."
She turned her head and looked at him.
"It is told to make children brave," she said. "That is all it is for. Nobody walks the Glass twice. Not in the first frost. Not a child. The ones who go out do not come back. Not ever." Her eyes in the dark were steady, and he could not read them. "Remember that, when you are out there. Do not walk it because of a story. Walk it because you must."
Then she looked back at the Glass.
And Corin looked at it too. At the black, and the stars in it, and the small lost lights drifting under the stars, and the place on the crest of the ridge, a little way along from the tree, where she had pointed.
There was nothing there. Only gravel, and starlight, and a thin bent thorn bush leaning south.
He looked at it for a long time anyway.
The Shape of It
Kestrel went down first. She got up without a word, and touched the trunk of the tree once with the flat of her hand, the way you might touch the shoulder of a friend as you leave, and went away down the south side of the ridge into the dark of the camp. She did not look back at the Glass. She did not need to. She walked down the slope sideways, Corin saw, with her face turned to the north the whole way, the way a person backs out of a room where someone is sleeping.
He stayed a while longer. Until the cold was so deep in his hands that he could not feel the crutch. Then he went down too.
He did it sideways, like her. He did not decide to. His body did it.
The tent was dark and warm, after the cold. The coals in the fire ring had gone to a dull red glow. Oren was snoring, softly and steadily, like a big cat. Lyra was asleep at last. She lay on her side with her back to Oren and her face toward Corin's empty place, curled up small under the goat skins, with one gloved hand out on the fur in front of her face, open, as if she had been reaching for something when sleep took her.
He got down beside her, very slowly, so as not to wake her, and pulled the skins over himself, and lay on his back.
The three stars were still in the smoke hole. They had moved a little. One of them was nearly gone off the edge.
The hum came up through the ground into his back.
He lay and listened to it, and to the two of them breathing, and to the goat bell somewhere out in the dark. And he took out the green book, in the dark, by feel, from inside his coat, and found the pencil, and wrote. Not looking. He could not see the page. He wrote by feel, slowly, the way he had taught himself to write in the dark of his room on Tallow Street, feeling each letter with his fingers as he made it, so that it would come out the right way round.
LAST THORN. THE ASHBORN. KESTREL.
THEY CARRY THEIR DEAD TOGETHER SO NOBODY DROWNS.
I WROTE THE PAGE FOR GIDEON. SHE FOUND MY WRONG LETTERS.
He put the book away again, inside his coat, against his chest. Then he lay still and looked at the stars.
And after a while he shut his eyes and went down, into himself, the way he went down when he was harvesting. Past the shelves. Past the rows and rows of them in the dark. Past the front room, full to the walls with Vivienne. The fish girl. The jars. The orange trees. The basket on a doorstep.
Down to the place where Malakor was.
Or where Malakor had been. It was very dark there now. Very quiet. Like a room where the fire has gone out and the people have gone to bed.
Malakor.
Nothing.
Malakor. I know you're there. You said when you stopped talking, it wouldn't mean you'd gone.
Nothing. A long, long silence. So long that Corin began to think the old man was gone after all. That the slope and the page had taken the last of him. That he had used himself up, writing a page for a boy he had never met, and was not there anymore.
Then, so faint it might have been the hum of the Glass, so faint it might have been nothing at all:
Boy.
I need to ask you something.
You always need to ask me something. Barely there. A thread of a voice. Ask, then. Quick. I'm tired.
Corin lay in the dark with his eyes shut and the hum in his back and Lyra's breathing beside him. He thought of the old blind man's hand on his chest. Of the room with the door off its hinges. Broken from the inside. Somebody went out of it in a hurry. He thought of Kestrel's story, and the child who walked the Glass twice, and the old man holding his hand. He thought of the small hand in the big dry hand.
He thought of the crate.
He had seen it, in pieces, these last weeks. In the dreams that were not dreams. A box. Wooden. Dark inside, and close, and smelling of straw and fear. Two of them in there. Two small bodies, pressed together in the dark. And then the lid. And light. And one of them going out.
And one of them staying.
Who got out of the crate? he said.
The silence was the longest yet.
Corin waited. He did not move. He did not breathe. He lay in the warm dark of the tent with the goat skins over him and the stars in the smoke hole and the Glass humming on the other side of the ridge, and he waited for the old man to answer. And it seemed to him that the whole camp had gone quiet to listen. The goats. The bone charms. The thorns. The lost lights under the Glass.
Then Malakor said:
I don't know, boy.
Very slowly. Very tired. Every word coming from a long way off, like a man calling across a valley in a wind.
I don't know. I've looked. I can't see it. A pause. But I can feel the shape of it. Like a word on the tip of your tongue. Like a name you knew once and can't say. It's there. It's right there. I just can't get hold of it.
Try, said Corin. Please. Try.
Nothing.
Malakor. Please.
Nothing.
He lay there and listened, as hard as he had ever listened to anything. To the dark place inside himself where the old man had been.
And there was nothing there at all. No voice. No breath. No dry far-off laugh. Not even the feeling of someone sitting quiet in a dark room. Only the dark, and the cold, and the hum of the Glass coming up through the ground, steady and patient, filling all the empty places in him, one by one.
Beside him, in her sleep, Lyra's open hand closed on the fur.
