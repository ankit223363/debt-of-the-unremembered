Chapter 64: The Ashborn, Part 1 of 5: The Thorn Slope
The Thorn Slope
The thorns on the last slope above the Glass had all grown leaning away from it, as if even the bushes had spent sixty years trying to leave.
Corin went down among them in the last of the light.
There was no path. Or there was a path, but it was not a path for people. It was a path for goats, narrow and twisting, a thread of bare gray gravel winding down between the bushes, never going straight for more than a few steps. The thorns grew so close on either side that they touched across it in places, and he had to push through them with his shoulder. They caught at his coat. They caught at his sleeves and his collar and the cuffs of his breeches, small hooked spines on black leafless twigs, and every one of them held on for a moment before it let go, so that walking through them was like walking through a crowd of people all plucking at him at once, wanting something.
The crutch was worse than useless here. The gravel was loose, and it slid away under the crutch's foot in little gray rivers, and twice the crutch went out from under him altogether and he sat down hard on the slope with a jolt that went up through his bad leg into his teeth. The second time he did not get up for a while. He sat on the gravel among the thorns, breathing, with his leg out in front of him, and looked down.
From here he could see the trees.
They were at the bottom of the slope, a long way down still, a line of them, taller than the thorns and darker, standing in a row along the very edge of the Glass. He could not tell what kind of trees they were. They had no leaves. They were black against the shining. And among them, low down, he could see small orange lights. Fires. Several. And the gray threads of smoke going up from them, leaning back on the wind toward him, sweet, like burnt honey.
And beyond the trees, the Glass.
It was close now. Very close. He could see that it was not quite flat, the way it had looked from the Lip. It had a texture, very faint, like the surface of a pond when the wind breathes on it. And it was changing color as he watched. The sun had gone down behind the hills in the west, and the sky was the color of a bruise, purple and yellow and gray, and the Glass took all of those colors and threw them back up into the sky, darker, so that the whole world below him had gone the color of strong tea, of old brandy, of the inside of a bottle held up to a dying fire.
The hum was very loud.
It was not a sound anymore. Not really. It was more like being inside something. Like being inside a bell after it has been struck, when the sound has stopped but the bronze is still shaking. He felt it in the gravel under his hands. He felt it in his bones. He felt it in his teeth, and in the soles of his feet, and in the back of his skull, and behind his left ear, where the skin was burned.
Malakor.
He reached inward, and found the place where the old man lived, the room where Lyra used to be.
The room was there. He could feel the shape of it. But the old man's voice, when it came, was like a candle in a drafty passage. Now there. Now gone. Now there again, small and blue and leaning, and then gone.
...boy...
I'm here.
...the Glass... close... can't...
Then nothing. Then, a long moment later, so faint that Corin had to hold his breath:
...still here. Just... quiet.
Then nothing at all.
Corin sat on the slope with his hands flat on the humming gravel and found that he was frightened.
Not of the Glass. Not of the thorns or the dark coming on or the fires at the bottom of the slope. He was frightened of the quiet. Of the place in his head where a voice had been for so long, and was now going away, like a lamp going down a long street at night, getting smaller and smaller.
He had not known, until now, how much he leaned on that voice. How often, in a day, he turned to it without thinking. Malakor, what's this? Malakor, is that right? Malakor, what do I do? He had not known that it was the thing that made him not alone.
"Corin?"
Lyra had come back up the path for him. She was standing a few steps below him among the thorns, in the black coat with the red scarf wound round her neck, looking up at him. Her face was pale in the strange brown light off the Glass. There were scratches on her cheek where the thorns had caught her.
"Are you hurt?"
"No."
"Then why are you sitting down?"
He thought about it. Rule one.
"He's going," he said. "Malakor. The closer we get, the quieter he goes. I can hardly hear him now." He looked down at his gray-gloved hands on the gravel. "I didn't know I'd mind."
She did not say anything for a moment. Then she came up the path to him and held out her hand.
He looked at it. A small hand in a black glove, held out to him in the dusk among the thorns.
He took it. She pulled, and he pushed with his good leg, and he came up off the gravel, and found his crutch, and stood.
She did not let go of his hand at once.
"You've still got me," she said. Not looking at him. Looking down the slope at the fires. "I'm not quiet. Ask anyone."
Then she let go, and turned, and went on down the goat path through the leaning thorns, and he followed her.
Stop
They were nearly at the bottom of the slope when the thorns in front of them spoke.
"Stop."
Oren stopped. His hand went to his sword. Corin stopped behind him, and Lyra behind Corin, and for a moment the three of them stood quite still on the narrow goat path in the dusk, with the thorns pressing in on both sides and the hum coming up through their boots, and nothing moved anywhere.
Then the thorns on the left of the path rustled, and three small figures stepped out of them.
Children.
They were very small. The biggest of them did not come up to Corin's chest. They were dressed in tunics and leggings of gray-brown hide, patched and laced with leather thongs, and over the tunics, short capes of the same hide with the fur turned in. Their feet were wrapped in felt. Their heads were bare, and their hair was cut short, close to the skull, the way you might cut a sheep's wool, so that their heads looked round and small and very naked in the cold.
And each of them had a line painted down the middle of the face.
It was gray. A pale ash-gray, the gray of cold fire, painted in a stripe as wide as a finger from the top of the forehead straight down the nose to the tip of it. It made their faces look strange in the dusk, split in two, like the faces of carvings on an old church.
Each of them held a spear.
The spears were too big for them. They were as long as a man, made of some pale smooth wood, and their points were not iron. They were bone. Long sharp yellowish points of bone, carved and polished and bound to the shafts with sinew. The three children held them in both hands, pointed forward, very steady, at Oren's belt buckle.
"Stop," said the biggest one again. A girl, Corin thought, though it was hard to tell. Her voice was high and very serious. "You don't come in. Not till we say."
Oren looked down at the three bone points, and then at the three small serious striped faces above them.
His hand came off his sword.
"All right," he said gravely. "We'll not come in."
The girl looked at him. Then at Corin. Her eyes went to his hands, the way everyone's did, and stayed there, and something changed in her face. Not fear. Something more like recognition, or the start of it, the way a person's face changes when they see a word they have learned but not yet read in a book.
"Who are you?" she said.
"Travelers," said Lyra, behind Corin. "From the valleys. Granny Abbe sent us. At Thistle Ford."
The girl did not look at her. She was still looking at Corin's hands.
"You're one of the gray ones," she said.
Corin did not answer. He did not know what she meant. He thought he could guess.
The other two children had not moved at all. They stood on either side of the girl, holding their spears very steady, very straight, like soldiers on guard at a gate. They were boys, he thought. One of them had a cold, and his nose was running, and he was trying very hard not to sniff, because sniffing was not something guards did.
For a long moment nobody said anything.
Then there was a sound overhead.
A crack of a branch. A rustle. And something big and dark dropped out of the black tree above the path and landed on the gravel in front of the three children, light as a cat, and straightened up.
It was a young man.
He was tall and thin and quick, all elbows and knees, in the same patched gray-brown hide as the children. His head was shaved close too, but he had let a long tail of dark hair grow at the back, and it was plaited and tied with a bit of red thread. The gray line ran down his face from his hairline to the tip of his nose, the same as theirs. Round his neck, on a cord, there hung a little pipe of yellowed bone, with holes in it. He was grinning. He had a wide mouth and very white teeth and he was grinning at them all as if they were the best joke he had heard all winter.
Then he laughed.
It was the strangest laugh Corin had ever heard. Short and loud and sharp, like a dog barking once, and then stopping, as if it had surprised itself.
"Don't mind them," he said. "They've been practicing that all winter." He turned to the children and put on a very solemn face. "Very good. Very fierce. You can let them in now."
"We weren't going to," said the girl. "Not till we said."
"And now I've said." He put his hand on top of her round shaved head, very gently, and turned her round toward the trees. "Go and tell Kestrel. Three. From over the Lip. One of them gray." He looked over his shoulder at Corin, and the grin went a little thinner. "Very gray," he said. "Tell her that."
The girl looked at him, and at Corin, and then the three of them were gone, down the path through the thorns, running, with their long bone spears held up straight over their heads so as not to catch.
The young man watched them go. Then he turned back.
"Arran," he said, and touched the gray line on his own face with two fingers, from his forehead down to the tip of his nose, in a quick neat movement, like a man touching his hat. "Welcome to Last Thorn." He looked at Oren, up and down, at the sword and the beard and the shoulders. Then at Lyra, in her black coat and her red scarf. Then, for the longest, at Corin. "You've come a long way to get to the end of the world."
"Yes," said Corin.
Arran looked at him for a moment longer.
Then he laughed his bark of a laugh again, and turned, and went down the path ahead of them, and they followed him out of the thorns.
Last Thorn
The path came out of the thorns at the bottom of the slope, and there was the camp.
Corin had expected something small. A few tents. A fire. The kind of place hunters might put up for a week and then leave.
It was not small.
It filled a long shallow bowl of ground between the last of the thorn slope and a low ridge of gravel to the north. And beyond the ridge, very close now, close enough that Corin could feel it on his face like the warmth from an oven door, was the Glass. He could not see it from down here. The ridge hid it. But he could see the light of it, a faint pale shine in the sky above the ridge, the color of milk, as if the moon were rising there though the moon was not up yet. And he could hear it. The hum was louder here than it had been on the slope. It was in the ground and in the air both, deep and steady, the way the sea is in the air of a harbor town, so that after a while you stop hearing it and only feel it.
There were more tents in the bowl than Corin could count on his fingers. Many more. They were not like any tents Corin had seen in the valleys or in the city. They were low and round, like upturned bowls, made of hides stretched over frames of bent wood, and the hides were the same gray-brown as the children's tunics, stitched together in great patched sheets. Each tent had a hole in the top, and from most of the holes a thin line of smoke rose straight up into the still evening air and then bent, all together, toward the south, away from the Glass, as if the smoke did not want to go there either.
And on every tent there were handprints.
Gray handprints. More of them than he could feel. Pressed onto the hides in ash, the same ash-gray as the lines on the children's faces. Big hands and small hands. Some fresh and sharp, some old and faded almost to nothing, so that you could see where new ones had been pressed over old ones, again and again, year after year, like the rings in a tree. On some tents they were scattered at random. On others they were in rows, neat and close, from the ground right up to the smoke hole. One tent near the middle of the camp was so covered in them that there was hardly any brown hide left showing at all. It was simply gray, all over, a tent made of hands.
"What are they?" said Lyra quietly.
Arran looked back at her. He did not laugh this time.
"The ones we carry," he said. "One hand for each."
He did not say anything more, and Lyra did not ask.
Between the tents there were paths of beaten earth, and along the paths there were racks. Wooden racks, waist-high, made of poles lashed together, and on the racks there was bread. Flat round loaves, dark brown, laid out in rows to cool or to dry, Corin could not tell which. There were more loaves than he could feel. The smell of them was everywhere, warm and a little sour, and under it there was another smell, sweeter and heavier and stranger. A smell like honey burning in a pan.
It came from the smoke. The fires in the tents were not burning wood, or not only wood. Something else was in them. Some kind of root, or resin, or dried flower. It made the smoke smell of burnt honey, thick and sweet and dark, so that the whole bowl was full of it, and Corin felt it settle on his tongue like a taste.
There were goats.
They were everywhere. Small, shaggy, long-haired goats, black and white and brown, with yellow eyes and long curved horns. They wandered loose along the paths between the tents, nosing at the bread racks and being slapped away, standing on the tops of rocks, lying in heaps in the lee of the tents with their legs folded under them. Some of them wore little bells on leather collars, and the bells made a faint dull clunking as they moved. One of them came straight up to Oren and put its nose against his knee and looked up at him with its strange flat yellow eyes, and Oren looked down at it and said, "No," very firmly, and it went on looking at him.
And there were people.
They were coming out of the tents now. The children had run ahead and told them, and the word was going round the camp, and people were coming out to look. Men and women and more children. Old ones, bent over sticks. Young ones with babies on their hips in slings of hide. They all wore the same patched gray-brown hide. They all had their heads shaved, or cut very short. And they all had the gray line, from the forehead to the tip of the nose, on every face, young and old.
Some of them had more than the line.
Corin saw an old man by a bread rack whose whole forehead was gray, and his cheeks too, as if the line had spread out on either side of his nose like ink soaking into paper. He saw a woman with a baby on her hip whose line ran down past her nose and over her lips and her chin and down her throat into the neck of her tunic. He saw a boy, not yet grown, whose line had a second line crossing it, from one ear to the other, so that his face had a gray cross on it.
And they had bone.
Bone everywhere. Little carvings of bone hung on cords round their necks and their wrists. Bone pins in their hide capes. Bone beads plaited into the hair of the ones who still had hair. Bone charms hung from the poles of the bread racks and from the frames of the tents, clicking softly together in the still air. Small carved shapes. Birds. Fish. Hands. A great many hands.
They did not come close. They stood in the doors of their tents and along the paths, and they watched the three strangers walk through their camp behind Arran, and they did not speak. Their faces were not angry. They were not afraid, either. They were only watchful, the way the faces of people in a village are watchful when a stranger walks down the main street on market day.
But Corin saw their eyes go to his hands. Every one of them. And then to his face. And then to his hands again.
And he saw some of them touch the gray lines on their own faces, as Arran had done. Quick. Two fingers, forehead to nose. As if they were greeting him.
Or as if they were warding something off.
"Why do they keep doing that?" said Lyra under her breath.
"I don't know," said Corin.
Oren said nothing. He walked between them with his hand not quite on his sword, the goat still trotting behind him, and his eyes going everywhere, at every tent and every path and every face, the way a soldier's eyes go everywhere in a town he does not know.
They came to the middle of the camp.
There was a big fire there, in a ring of black stones, burning low and red, with that same burnt-honey smoke rising from it. Around it there was a wide space of beaten earth, and on the earth there were mats of woven grass laid in a circle. And beyond the fire, on the far side, the side toward the Glass, there was the tent Corin had seen from the edge of the camp. The gray one. The tent of hands.
It was bigger than the others. Twice the size at least. Its door flap was tied back, and inside it there was the red glow of another fire, and the shadow of someone moving.
Arran stopped at the edge of the mats.
"Wait here," he said. "She'll come out."
He went round the fire, light and quick on his long legs, and ducked in at the door of the gray tent, and was gone.
The three of them stood at the edge of the circle of mats, in the red light of the fire, with the whole camp watching them from the shadows between the tents, and the goat nibbling at the hem of Oren's coat, and the hum of the Glass coming up through the ground under their feet, and the burnt-honey smoke in their mouths.
Nobody said anything.
Lyra's hand found Corin's arm, in the dark, at her side. She did not hold it. She only touched it. The way you might touch a wall in the dark to know where it is.
Then the flap of the gray tent moved, and a woman came out.
