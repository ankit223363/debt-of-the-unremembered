Chapter 80: The Eye, Part 3 of 5: Loose
Loose
He was not the only one who felt it.
Lyra stepped down off the last of the stones onto the black glass behind him. And stopped. And drew in her breath, sharp, the way you do when you step into cold water. He saw her hands come up. Both of them. The good one and the bandaged one. Up in front of her, a little way from her body, with the fingers spread. As if she was feeling for something in the air.
"Oh," she said.
Just that. Very quiet.
He knew what she was feeling. He could see it on her face. The same thing that was in his fingertips. The flood. The prickle. Something that had been held still for days, out on the Glass, and was now loose again. Moving. Waking up.
She had burned before. Many times. More than he knew, probably. Small burns and big ones. In the city. At Last Thorn. And out on the Glass she had not been able to, not once, and he had seen her, once or twice, at night, in the heap, with her hand pressed flat on her own chest as if she were checking something was still there. Now it was there. All of it. Every word she had not yet spent. Every face. Every song. Loose, inside her, the way his were loose inside him.
She put her hands down. Slowly. Into her coat pockets. And kept them there.
Then Oren stepped down.
He was a big man, and he came off the stones heavily, with his sword hand out for balance. His boot hit the black glass. And he stopped dead.
He did not say anything for a long moment. He stood very still, with his scarred face gone blank, and his eyes looking at nothing. Listening. Not with his ears. With the rest of him.
"Saints," he said at last. Very low.
Corin looked at him.
"I can feel them," said Oren. "My words. The ones I've still got." He swallowed. "Out there, they sat still. I could count them. I sat on that horse's hoof and counted them and knew they'd all be there in the morning. And now—" He stopped. Shook his head. "Now they're moving. Like fish in a net. Like they want to get out."
Nobody said anything.
"I'll lose more," said Oren. "In here. Won't I? If I fight. If I have to."
Kestrel had come back along the line. She stood a little way off and looked at him. She did not lie to him. She never lied to anybody.
"If you burn," she said. "Yes. In here, you can burn. And if you burn, you lose."
Oren looked at her a long moment.
Then he nodded. Slowly. And straightened his back. The bad back, the bruised one. And put his sword hand on the hilt at his hip.
"Then I'll try not to," he said. "And if I have to, I'll choose what."
He walked on. Out onto the black glass. After the silent man and Tansy. Heavily. Carefully. The way a man walks across a floor he knows is rotten.
Corin watched him go.
And thought of the six holes. The shut. The hard cold. The thing you sit on. The little one for soup. The white flowers. The round things the boys beat. And of how many more holes there might be, by the end of this. In Oren. In Lyra. In him.
He did not want to think about it.
He thought about it anyway. And then he took a breath of the silent, still, normal air of the Eye, and went after them.
Boy. You Came.
And then, in the silence, somebody spoke.
Boy.
Corin stopped walking.
He had not known he was walking. He had taken a step, two, out onto the black glass, after the others, with the fire in his fingertips. And now he stopped. Dead. With the crutch planted and the stiff leg swung half forward and his breath caught in his chest.
Boy. You came.
It was not outside him. It was not in his ears. The others had not heard it. He knew that without looking. It was inside. In the place behind his eyes. In the place where the voice had always been, all through the city, all through the fens and the hills and the long cold road north. Close and clear. Not thin. Not faint. Not far off, through a wall, the way it had been at the end, before the Edge. Close. As if the speaker had only stepped out of the room for a moment, and come back in, and sat down in his old chair by the fire, and was looking at Corin over the top of his spectacles.
Dry. Old. A little rough. A voice that had been talking for a very long time, and was tired of it, and was going to go on anyway.
Malakor.
Corin opened his mouth.
Nothing came out.
He tried again. He tried to say the name. Or you. Or where. Or anything at all. His mouth opened and his throat worked and nothing came out of it but breath. As if the words had all run back down into him and hidden, the way children run and hide when a stranger comes to the door.
He had thought he would never hear it again. He had not known he had thought that. He had not let himself think it. Since the first step at the Edge, when the voice had said Count, boy, and then nothing. Nothing. Not one word. For days. Through the first night, and the trench, and the iron horse, and the storm, and the Ridges. Nothing. And he had carried the nothing the way you carry a stone in your pocket, without looking at it, without taking it out. And told himself the old man was only sleeping. Only waiting. Only folded up small somewhere deep, the way Kestrel said the dead fold up small when they are tired.
He had not believed it.
And now.
Don't make a fuss, said Malakor. Dry. Very dry. There isn't much of me left that isn't folded.
Corin made a sound. He did not know what kind. Something between a laugh and a sob, that came out of his chest all at once and went up into the silence.
Lyra turned. A few paces ahead. She looked back at him.
"Corin?"
He could not answer her. He stood on the black glass with his hand over his mouth and his eyes stinging.
The Glass, said Malakor. Inside. Slowly. It holds everything still. Everything. Every voice in you. The dead ones. It holds them like a hand holds a bird, so they can't sing. You didn't know that. You thought I'd gone. A pause. Something that might have been a dry laugh. I thought I'd gone too, for a while. In the middle of the long white. I couldn't find the edges of myself.
Corin took his hand away from his mouth.
Where are you? he thought. He could not say it. He thought it, as hard as he could.
Here. Very close. Where I always am. Behind your left ear, like a bad habit. A pause. But the Eye doesn't hold. The Eye lets go. That's what it is, boy. That's what it always was. The one place in all this glass where things move again. Where you can reach. Where you can take. The voice went quieter. Where you can be taken from.
Corin swallowed.
I know, he thought. I can feel it. In my hands.
Good. Then you'll be careful.
A silence. Then, in a different voice. Lower. Not dry at all.
You came, boy. I didn't think you'd come. Not all the way. Not with that leg.
Corin found that his face was wet.
He wiped it with the back of his gray hand. And looked up. At Lyra, standing on the black glass a few paces ahead, watching him with her face white and frightened.
"He's back," said Corin. His voice came out cracked and hoarse. But it came. "Malakor. He's back. He's talking."
Lyra's face changed.
She did not say anything. She only came back to him, the few paces, across the black glass, and took his arm. And held it.
Tell her, said Malakor, that I said she has very cold hands. And that it's nearly noon. And that we haven't got all day.
Corin laughed. Properly. Out loud. In the silence. The first laugh that had been all his own since the Ridges.
And went on walking.
Writing It Down
He stopped, a little way on, and wrote it.
He had to. He was afraid, if he did not, that it would go. That he would take another step on the black glass and the fire in his fingertips would jump, and something would burn, and it would be this. The voice. Boy. You came. He could not let it be this.
So he stopped. And leaned on his crutch. And took the book out of his coat, out of its oilcloth, and opened it to the last page. Under his own shaky capitals from the chapel. I THINK SHE SAID IT TO ME FIRST.
He found the stub of the pencil in the spine.
His hand was shaking. Not from the cold. From the fire in his fingertips, which made the pencil feel strange in his grip, as if it were alive. He held it hard and pressed it to the paper.
MONDAY.
He stopped. Was it Monday? He thought it was. Lyra would know.
"Is it Monday?" he said.
Lyra looked at him. "Yes."
MONDAY. THE EYE.
He wrote it big. Bigger than the chapel lines. Then underneath:
MALAKOR CAME BACK.
He looked at that for a long time. Then he wrote the last line. Slowly. Pressing so hard that the pencil nearly went through the page.
HE SAID BOY, YOU CAME.
You've left out the bit about her cold hands, said Malakor.
Corin almost laughed again. He did not write it down. Some things were not for the book. Some things you kept the other way. In you. Where they could burn. And you just had to be careful.
Looking Down
He had told himself he would not look down.
He looked down.
He could not help it. Nobody could. Out on the clear black glass of the Eye, a little way from the beach, with the silence round them and the noon sun straight overhead, they all stopped, one after another, and looked down between their feet. Even Kestrel. Even the silent man, who had been walking straight toward the low dark shape in the middle as if nothing else in the world mattered, stopped, and bent his head, and looked.
And under the glass, there was a village.
It was a long way down. Corin could not tell how far. Further than the tallest tower in the city was tall. Further than the deepest well. The glass was so clear that it did not seem to be there at all, so that standing on it was like standing on air over a great drop, and his stomach turned over and his good knee went weak. But the sun was straight overhead, and its light went straight down through the clear glass, down and down, and at the bottom of the light, very small, very far, very still, there were houses.
Roofs. He saw the roofs first. Slate, some of them, gray and square. Thatch on others, gone dark. A cluster of them, crowded together, the way the roofs of a village crowd together round a crossing of roads. Chimneys. A little square, in the middle, with something round in it that might have been a well. Lanes going away from the square between the houses. A wall, with a gate in it, gate open.
And a church.
A tower. Square. Taller than the houses, standing up at one side of the square, with a pointed roof and a little shape on top that might have been a cross or a weathervane. He could see the dark slits of its windows. He could see the shadow it threw, short, at noon, on the roofs beside it.
Everything was perfectly still.
That was the worst of it. Not the drop. Not the depth. The stillness. Nothing moved down there. Nothing at all. It was like a village in a painting. Like a village in one of those glass balls they sold at the winter fair in the city, that you shook to make the snow fly. Except that nobody had ever shaken this one. It sat there at the bottom of the clear dark, perfect, untouched, frozen at noon on some day long ago, with the sun on its roofs.
And there were things in the lanes.
He saw them as his eyes got used to the depth. Small dark shapes. A cart. He could see the cart, in the lane that ran from the square to the gate. A farm cart, with two big wheels and a load of something heaped on it, and a horse between the shafts. Standing. Its head up. One foot lifted. Caught in the middle of a step.
Another horse, further along. Lying down. Or fallen.
And figures.
He did not want to see the figures. He saw them. Too small to make out. Only shapes. Dark, upright, here and there along the lanes and in the square. Standing. Some of them with their arms up. Some of them with their faces turned up. Toward the sky. Toward him. Toward the light that had come down on them on that day, at noon, and stopped them where they stood, and poured itself over them, and set.
The whole street. The whole village. Every roof and every lane and every cart and every person. All perfectly still. Under the glass. Like a village under a frozen lake.
Corin could not breathe.
Beside him, Lyra had turned her face away. She was looking at the sky. At anything but down. Her hand on his arm was shaking.
Ahead, Oren was standing very still with his head bent, looking down between his boots, and his lips were moving. Corin could not hear what he said. He did not need to. He knew the shape of it. Saints. Saints. Saints.
And behind Corin's left ear, very quietly, Malakor said:
Applegarth. That was its name. There was a fair here every autumn. I bought a knife at it, once, when I was young. A good knife. With a horn handle.
A long pause.
I've never been able to remember what I did with it.
Corin did not say anything. He could not.
He made himself look up. Away. At the low dark shape in the middle of the Eye. At the silent man's back, walking toward it again.
And he walked after him. Over the drowned village. Over the roofs and the square and the cart and the horse with its foot lifted. Over the people with their faces turned up.
He did not look down again.
But he felt them. Every step. Looking up at him through the glass.
Nobody Walks Here
Kestrel was walking slower than usual.
Corin saw it. He saw it because he was slow too, and for once he was keeping up with her. She walked a little way ahead of him on the black glass with her spear in her hand, and every few steps her eyes went down. And came up again. Quickly. As if she had touched something hot.
"You've never been here," he said.
He did not mean it as a question. It came out as one anyway.
Kestrel did not look at him.
"Nobody walks here," she said. "Nobody. Not the Ashborn. Not ever. We come to the beach. Some of us. Once, when we are grown. To see. To pick up a stone. And then we go home." She looked down again. At the drowned roofs far below her feet. And up. "We do not walk on the Eye. The old ones say you should not walk over the dead's roofs. They say it is like walking over their faces."
"But we're walking here."
"Yes." Her mouth went thin. "We are."
She walked on a few steps.
"The little one walks here," she said. "Tansy. Every month. With him. She told this one, in the cave. She thought it was nothing. She thought it was a walk." Kestrel shook her head slowly. "A child from the south, who knows nothing. Walking over the dead's roofs every month like it was the lane behind her house. Nobody told her not to. There was nobody to tell her."
"And him?"
Kestrel looked ahead. At the silent man, walking at the front with Tansy holding his coat. Straight across the black glass. Not looking down. Not once.
"He does not look down," she said. "Have you seen? Never. Not since the beach. He walks as if there is nothing under him at all." A pause. "Or as if he has already seen it. And does not need to look again."
Corin looked at the silent man's back.
The long thin shape. The stiff dark coat. The wild hair. The bound hands, held out from his sides. Walking.
"Who is he?" said Corin. Quietly. "Do you know? Does anybody?"
Kestrel was quiet for a long time.
"No," she said at last. "Nobody knows. The little one found him in the Ridges. Standing. Two winters ago. That is all anybody knows." She looked at Corin sideways. "But he knew the short way. He knew the knock. He did your captain's stroke, at the horse. Better than your captain." She looked ahead again. "Somebody who walks over the dead without looking down. Somebody who has been here before. Long before. When there was something to see."
She did not say any more.
And Corin did not ask. He walked on over the drowned valley, behind the silent man, and did not look down either. He found that he could not take his eyes off the long thin back in front of him.
Behind his left ear, Malakor said nothing at all.
Which was, Corin thought, its own kind of answer. Though he could not have said to what.
Noon on the Eye
The heat on the Eye was different.
Out on the open Glass, the heat had come up from below. Out of the ground. The dark glass soaked up the sun all morning and gave it back all afternoon, so that the air over it shook and your feet cooked through the felt. Here it did not. Here the glass was clear, and the sun went straight down through it, down and down into the dark, to the drowned roofs at the bottom. It did not stop. It did not come back up. The glass under their feet was only warm. Like a stone in a garden in spring.
But the sun overhead was the same sun. And there was no shade. Not anywhere. Not a pole, not a hide, not a wall, not a swell. Only the flat black shining glass going away on every side, and the sky, white with heat, and the sun in the middle of it like a coin held too close to the eye.
It pressed down on them. On their heads, through their hoods. On their shoulders. On the backs of their necks.
And there was no water.
Corin's tongue had stuck to the roof of his mouth. He had to peel it off, every few steps, and it came away with a small dry sound, and stuck again. His lips had split in three places. He could taste the blood. When he swallowed, there was nothing to swallow, and his throat clicked.
Brin went down first.
He did not fall. He knelt. All at once, in the middle of a step, on the black glass, with one hand pressed to his cracked ribs and the other flat on the glass in front of him, holding himself up. His big head hanging. His breath coming in short hard gasps through his teeth.
Sef was beside him before anybody else. Then Kestrel.
"Up," said Kestrel. Not unkindly. "Up. It is not far. You can see it."
Brin lifted his head and looked at the dome. Still a long way. Still small. Shaking in the heat.
He said something in the round speech. Two words. Kestrel's face did not change.
"Yes," she said. "Water there. Maybe. We go and see."
And she and Sef got their shoulders under his arms, one each side, careful of his ribs, and lifted him. And he came up, gray-faced, and walked again, between them. Slowly.
They all walked slowly after that.
Corin counted his steps. He did not mean to. He did it the way Lyra did it, to have something to hold. One. Two. Three. Up to nine. And then start again. One. Two. Three. Over and over. Nine and nine and nine. He did not know how many nines it was to the dome. He did not try to know. He just counted to nine, and started again, and the dome got a little bigger every time.
Beside him, not touching, Lyra was counting too. He could see her lips. Her numbers went past nine. His stopped. But they walked in the same rhythm.
Ahead, the silent man did not slow down.
He walked at his long even pace, straight toward the dome, with Tansy holding his coat. And Corin saw that he was not sweating. Everybody else was. Corin could feel it running down his own back under his shirt, drying almost before it ran. He could see it on Oren's neck, on Kestrel's face, darkening the back of Lyra's collar. But the silent man walked through the noon heat as if it were a cool morning. His face was dry. His wild hair hung still.
Only his hands, held out from his sides, dripped. Slowly. Dark. Onto the black glass behind him. A line of dark spots, a pace apart, all the way back across the Eye to the beach. Like the trail of crumbs Arran and Sef had left on the false trail. Like something saying: This way. This way. I came this way.
Corin followed the spots.
Nine. And nine. And nine.
And the dome grew.
