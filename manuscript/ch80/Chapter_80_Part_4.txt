Chapter 80: The Eye, Part 4 of 5: A Warning
A Warning
Kestrel stopped them a little way further on.
She turned round on the black glass, in the silence, and held up her spear. Not high. Just enough. And they all stopped, and stood round her, with the drowned village under their feet and the low dark shape of the middle still a long way off.
She looked at them. At each one. The way she looked at the water skins, weighing them.
"Listen," she said. "All of you. Listen to this one."
They listened.
"Out there," said Kestrel. She pointed back with her spear. At the beach, and the slope going up beyond it, and the whole wide Glass beyond that. "Out there, nothing can be taken. You know that. You have lived it. Six days. Seven. Out there you could not take. You could not burn. Nobody could take from you." She paused. "In here, that is gone."
Nobody said anything.
"In here," said Kestrel, "things can be taken again. You can lose things. You can burn things. Somebody can reach into you and take what they want." She looked at Corin. Then at Lyra. Then at Oren. "Everything that was still out there is loose in here. Every word. Every face. Every thing you love. Loose. Like coins in a pocket with a hole in it."
She lowered her spear.
"Be careful what you want," she said. "In here. Be careful what you reach for. Be careful what you are afraid of. Because in here, wanting is the same as reaching. And reaching is the same as burning." She looked at them all again. "The old ones say so. The old ones say this is where the light came out of her. All of it. Everything she had. Because she wanted, very badly, for one thing to stop. And she reached for it. With everything."
She turned. And walked on.
They followed her.
Corin felt it as he walked. What she meant. He had felt it the moment he stepped off the beach. The fire in his fingertips. The reach, come back. It was there all the time now, waiting. Like a dog at his heel. Like a hand half-open in his pocket. He could feel the shine of the others round him. Of Lyra, beside him. Of Oren, ahead. Of all of them. The way he used to feel it in the city, in the Long Office, in the streets. The memories in people, glowing faintly, like lamps behind curtains. He could reach out and touch any one of them. He did not. He held his hands still on his crutch. But he could.
And he could feel his own.
The things in him. The things that were his. Not many, now. Not as many as there had been. But there. His name, on his chest. Lyra, on his chest. The heron. The kettle. A few others. He could feel them sitting in him, loose. Waiting. The way coins sit in a pocket. And he knew that if he was frightened enough, or angry enough, or wanted something badly enough, his hand would go into that pocket on its own. And take one out. And spend it.
He walked very carefully after that.
Lyra was walking very carefully too. He could see it. Her shoulders were stiff. Her hands were held close to her body, the good one and the bandaged one, as if she was afraid of what they might do.
"I can feel them," she said. Low. Not looking at him. "My burns. The ones I haven't spent yet. Out there, I couldn't. I forgot they were there. And now I can feel them all again. Sitting in me." She swallowed. "Like coins in a pocket. Like she said."
"Don't," said Corin.
"I won't."
"Not in here. Whatever happens."
"I know." She looked at him then. "You too."
He did not answer that.
He could not promise it. Not now. Not with the fire in his fingertips and the drowned village under his feet and the low dark shape in the middle of the Eye getting closer with every step. He had made a promise once, to Silas, about burning himself. He had kept it, mostly. He did not know if he could keep it in here.
So he did not answer.
And Lyra did not ask again. But she put her good hand on his arm. And kept it there.
The Fire in His Fingers
He nearly took something from Lyra.
He did not mean to. That was what frightened him most, afterward. He did not mean to at all.
They were walking. She was beside him, on his right, with her good hand on his arm. And the fire was in his fingertips, under the black. Burning. Waiting. The reach, come back after all those days, like a dog that has been shut out in the cold and is wild to be let in. And he was holding it still. Holding it hard. With everything he had.
And she squeezed his arm.
Just a small squeeze. Her fingers tightening on his sleeve for a moment, the way they did when she wanted him to know she was there. And his arm felt it. And the fire felt it. And without him telling it to, without him even knowing, the reach went out of his fingertips and up his arm and across, into her hand, into her.
And found something.
He felt it. Bright. Warm. Close. Very close, right at the front of her, the way a thing is at the front of you when you have just been thinking about it. A memory. Glowing like a lamp behind a curtain.
A long hall. Black and white floor, like a chessboard. Gold evening light coming in through tall windows. A woman's hands, holding a small girl's hands. A voice counting, in a language he did not know. One, two, three, lift, turn. And the small girl's feet, in soft slippers, trying to follow. And laughing. Both of them laughing.
It was so bright. It was so close. It would have been the easiest thing in the world. Like picking an apple off a low branch. Like breathing in. He felt the reach close round it, the way a hand closes, and begin to draw—
He stopped.
He did not know how. He wrenched it back. All of it. The reach. Pulled it back out of her and down his arm and into his fingertips and held it there, shaking, burning, and pulled his arm away from her hand so hard that she stumbled.
"Corin—"
He was standing on the black glass with his gray hand held out away from him, away from her, as if it were something on fire. His whole body shaking. His breath coming in gasps.
"Don't," he said. "Don't touch me. Not my hand. Not in here."
She looked at him. White.
"What happened?"
"I nearly—" He could not say it. He made himself say it. "I nearly took something. From you. I didn't mean to. You touched my arm and it just—went. On its own. And it found—"
He stopped.
"What?" said Lyra. Very quiet. "What did it find?"
He looked at her.
Rule one. No lies. Not even the kind ones.
"A hall," he said. "Black and white. Evening. A woman teaching a little girl to dance. Counting. In a language I don't know." His voice cracked. "Your mother."
Lyra's face did not move.
Then it did. Something went across it. Fast. Like the shadow of a bird going over a field. And was gone.
"I burned the dancing," she said. Very quietly. "At Last Thorn. To stop the iron. My feet don't know it anymore. I thought all of it was gone."
"Your feet don't," said Corin. "But you've still got the hall. Her hands. The counting. That wasn't the dancing. That was her." He swallowed. "It was right at the front. It was so bright."
She did not say anything for a long time.
Then she put her hands in her coat pockets. Both of them. The good one and the bandaged one. Deep in. Where he could not touch them, and they could not touch him.
"Then I'll keep my hands to myself," she said. "In here." She looked at him. "And you keep yours."
"Yes."
They walked on. Side by side. Not touching. For the first time since the Ridges.
It was the hardest thing he had ever done.
Behind his left ear, very quietly, Malakor said:
Good, boy. That's what it's like in here. That's what it was always like. That's why she sealed the door.
What the Old Man Remembers
Malakor talked while they walked.
Corin had not expected that. In the city, the old voice had come and gone. A word here. A sentence there. Long silences between, when Corin did not know if he was there at all. Now it talked. Steadily. Quietly. Behind his left ear. As if it had been saving up words for all the days of silence on the Glass and now had to spend them.
It was green, said Malakor. All this. Did you know that? Green. A valley. A long one, running north to south, with a river in the bottom of it, and hills on either side, and orchards all up the hills. Apples. Pears. Plums, on the south slopes. In spring the whole valley went white with blossom. You could smell it from the Edge. From further. The carters coming up from the south used to say they knew they were near when they could smell the pears.
Corin walked. Crutch, swing, step. Over the black glass.
There were villages, said Malakor. Six of them. Seven. Strung along the river like beads. Applegarth was the biggest. Under us now. The one with the church. The others were smaller. Farms, mostly. Mills. A ford. A bridge. A pause. All under us now. All of it. Under this.
Why were you here? thought Corin. You were Ministry. You taught them. The harvesters. Why did you come here?
A long silence.
Because I was ashamed, said Malakor at last.
Corin did not say anything.
I was forty-four, said Malakor. Old enough to know better. I'd been teaching in the Ministry for nearly twenty years. Teaching young men how to reach into people and take. How to find the bright places in a mind. How to draw them out. I was very good at it. I was the best they had. A dry sound. And I watched what my students did with it. Year after year. I watched them go out into the city and the valleys with their jars and their stamps and their silver rods, and come back with the insides of people in boxes. And I told myself it was not my fault. I only taught. What they did with it was theirs.
Crutch, swing, step.
And then one winter, said Malakor, I couldn't tell myself that anymore. I don't remember why. Something small, I think. Something I saw. A child, maybe. A jar. I don't remember. A pause. I think I burned it, later. Whatever it was. So I wouldn't have to remember why I left.
And you came here.
I came here. I walked north. On foot, in the snow, like you. And I found her people. Sola's. And I told them who I was, and what I'd done, and that I was sorry, and that I would carry stretchers. The voice went softer. I'd carried stretchers when I was young. In an earlier war. Before I learned to reach. It was the only other thing I knew how to do.
And they let you?
She let me. A long pause. She looked at me. Up on that iron horse of hers, in the orchard, with the blossom coming down. And she said, "You taught them to take. Can you teach my people not to be taken from?" And I said I didn't know. And she said, "Then carry stretchers until you do."
Corin walked.
She talked like a sergeant, said Malakor. Short. Hard. Every word like a nail. And then at night, round the fires, she sang like a girl. Old songs. Valley songs. With the soldiers. A pause. I'd never heard anything like it. I've never heard anything like it since.
Corin felt something stir in him. Under the ink. Deep down, where the silence was. Sola's silence. Wide and calm and still.
And for a moment, just for a moment, he thought he heard, very far down in it, somebody singing.
Then it was gone.
Was I there? he thought. He did not know why he thought it. Was I there? Before? Is that why—
No, said Malakor. Quickly. No, boy. You weren't born. Not for years and years after. A pause. But you were born in the north. Not so very far from all this, as the crow flies. Oakhaven.
Corin stopped walking.
Oakhaven, he thought. It's on my chest. You were born in Oakhaven. I don't remember it.
No, said Malakor. You wouldn't. And did not say any more.
And Corin walked on, over the drowned valley, with the old man silent again behind his ear, and the name going round in his head with Hask's three notes. Oakhaven. Oakhaven. Like a stone turning over and over in a stream.
The Dome
It grew as they came to it. The way the Ridges had grown. The way the chapel had grown out of the white.
A low dark shape on the black glass. Then a hump. Then a dome.
It was not big. That surprised him. He had thought, from far off, from the beach, that it must be huge, to be seen from so far. It was not. It was the size of a small house. A cottage. Round. Low. Sitting on the clear black glass in the very middle of the Eye like a turtle on a frozen pond.
It was made of stone. And iron. Both. Melted together.
He saw that as he came close. It had been stone, once. Gray blocks of it, the same kind as the chapel, set in rings, one on top of another, rising to a round top. And over the stone, or through it, iron. Bands of it. Great curved straps of dark iron, running up the sides of the dome from the bottom to the top, like the ribs of a basket, holding the stone. Riveted. He could see the heads of the rivets, as big as his fist.
And all of it had melted.
Not like the chapel. The chapel had slumped and run. This had fused. The stone and the iron had run into each other, at the joins, and set, so that you could not tell anymore where one stopped and the other began. The stone had gone dark and glassy, like the glass of the ground. The iron had gone soft and lumpy, like candle wax, and hardened again in folds.
And half of it was sunk.
The dome sat in the glass of the Eye. Not on it. In it. As if the glass had been liquid once, a lake of fire, and the dome had sunk into it a little way, like a stone dropped in mud, before the fire cooled and set and held it. Its lower rings of stone were under the glass. Corin could see them, down through the clearness, going on below, the bottom half of the dome under the surface like the bottom half of a bubble.
And down the sides of it, from top to bottom, there were long dark streaks.
Rust. Red-brown. Running down from every iron band, down the dark glassy stone, all the way to the place where it met the black glass of the Eye. In long wavering lines. Like tears. Like a face that has been crying for a very long time and has never once wiped its eyes.
There was one door.
On the side facing them. The south side. Iron. Not melted. Like the door frame of the chapel. Whole. Square-edged. Set into the fused stone between two of the iron bands. As tall as a man. Taller. A plain flat iron door, dark, with rust round its edges. No handle. No ring. No keyhole. No hinges that he could see.
The silent man walked straight to it.
He did not wait for anybody. He did not look back. He had been walking at the front all morning, with Tansy holding his coat, at his long even pace, straight down the slope and over the beach and out across the Eye, toward this. And now he walked straight to the door. Ahead of everyone. As if the rest of them were not there. And stopped in front of it.
And lifted his right hand.
The bound one. Wrapped in Tansy's torn gray shirt, dark with blood. He made it into a fist, as well as he could. And turned it. So that the side of it, the soft side, the little finger side, faced the door.
And knocked.
Tansy had let go of his coat. She was standing a few paces back, on the glass, beside Kestrel. And she turned to the rest of them, with her face lit up, the first time it had lit up since the Ridges. Proud. So proud. The way a child is proud when the grown-ups finally see the thing she has been telling them about for days.
"See?" said Tansy. "Every month. I told you. Every month, he comes, and he knocks."
The Knock
Corin heard Lyra stop breathing.
He was beside her. He felt it. Her shoulder, a hand's width from his, went stiff, and her whole body beside him went still, and the breath stopped in her. All at once. As if somebody had put a hand over her mouth.
He looked at her.
She had gone white. Whiter than he had ever seen her. Whiter than in the trench. Her lips were the same color as her face. She was staring at the silent man's hand on the iron door, and her eyes were very wide.
The silent man was knocking.
Slowly. With the side of his bound fist. On the flat iron. Each knock a dull deep sound, tonk, that did not ring the way iron should ring, but went into the door and stopped, as if the door were very thick and very old and full of something heavy.
And there were stops between the knocks.
Short ones and long ones. Corin heard them. And without wanting to, without meaning to, he began to count.
Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk.
Four.
A stop. Long.
Tonk. Tonk.
Two.
A stop.
Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk.
Nine. He could hold nine. Nine was the edge of where he could still hold numbers, the last one before they went soft and strange. He held it.
A stop.
Tonk. Tonk. Tonk.
Three.
Then a long stop. Much longer.
And then it began again. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk. Four.
Corin's hand moved.
His right hand. On its own. Down at his side. He felt it move and looked down at it, and it was making the shapes. The way it had in the shade by the iron horse. Four fingers, thumb in, a short push. Two fingers, laid flat. The whole hand, turning, folding from the little finger to the thumb. Three fingers, drawn back to his chest. Laid over his heart.
Witness. Hold. Fold. Home.
Four. Two. Nine. Three.
"The calls," said Oren.
He said it from behind Corin. Hoarse. Corin turned his head. Oren was standing a few paces back, on the black glass, staring at the door with his scarred face gone gray and his mouth open. "Those are the calls. The old ones. The end-of-day ones. Witness, hold, fold, home." He looked at Corin's hand. "Same as yours. The same as you made at the horse."
"My father's taps," said Lyra.
It came out of her in a whisper. Hardly a sound. She was still staring at the door.
"My father," she said. "At the end. After he burned. When he didn't know me anymore. When he didn't know anybody. He sat in his room on the Hill and tapped on the wall. With one finger. All day. Every day." She swallowed. "Four. And then two. And then nine. And then three. Over and over. And nobody knew what it meant. Nobody ever knew. The doctors said it meant nothing. They said it was only his hands remembering something his head had lost."
The silent man knocked. Tonk. Tonk. Two.
"That," said Lyra. "That's it. That's what he tapped. Exactly that. With the same stops."
Corin looked at the door.
And saw, for the first time, that it was not plain.
He had thought it was plain. From further off. A flat dark iron door with rust round its edges. But close to, in the noon light, standing at the silent man's shoulder, he saw that the iron was carved. All over. Shallow. Worn. Rusted in the lines. So faint that you could only see it when the light came at it from the side.
Shapes. Hands. Carved into the iron in a ring, round the middle of the door. A hand with four fingers up and the thumb folded in. A hand with two fingers laid flat. A hand turning and folding. A hand with three fingers over a carved heart.
Witness. Hold. Fold. Home.
Over and over. All round the ring. Four hands, and then the same four again, and again, round and round, the way the knocks went round.
And in the middle of the ring, in the center of the door, a sun.
A round sun, carved deep. With lines coming out of it on every side, like rays. Bigger than a man's hand spread out. Its center was smooth. Worn smooth. As if many hands had pressed on it, a long time ago.
The silent man finished knocking.
Three. Tonk. Tonk. Tonk.
And stopped. And stood there, with his bound fist still raised. Waiting.
Nothing happened.
The door did not open. The iron did not move. Nothing answered. Nothing at all.
And after a long moment, the silent man lowered his hand. And stood in front of the door with his head a little bowed. The way, Corin thought, a man stands at a door he has knocked on every month for years, and that has never once been opened.
