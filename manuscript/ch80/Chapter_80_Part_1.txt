Chapter 80: The Eye, Part 1 of 5: Leaving Arran
Leaving Arran
They could not take him through the short way.
Corin understood that before anybody said it. He understood it the moment the silent man stopped, a little after midnight, in a narrow passage between two lines of walls, and turned, and looked at the litter. And then looked at a place low down at the foot of the wall in front of him. A dark place. A crack.
A wall had fallen there. Long ago. One whole section of a Ridge, many paces long, had come down across the passage, slanting, the way a tree comes down across a path in a storm. Its top end lay on the ground. Its bottom end was still caught against the next wall, a little way up. And under it, between the fallen wall and the glass floor, there was a gap. A long low black slit, running away into the dark under the fallen glass.
It was not high. It came to Corin's knee. Less.
You could not carry a litter through that. You could not carry anything through that. You would have to go on your belly.
Kestrel looked at it for a long time.
Then she looked at the litter. At her brother on it, under his hide, with his face to the stars.
Nobody said anything. Nobody needed to.
"Here," said Kestrel.
They did it quickly. There was no time to do it slowly. There was no fire, and no thorn heap, and nothing to dig with, and you could not dig in glass anyway. So they did what the Ashborn did, Corin learned, when somebody died far out on the Glass and could not be brought home.
They built him a cairn.
Not of stones. There were no stones. Of glass. The fallen wall had broken when it fell, long ago, and there were pieces of it lying everywhere along the passage. Big pieces and small. Dark green, nearly black, rippled on one side and sharp on the other. The Ashborn went along the passage picking them up, carefully, with their hands wrapped in the ends of their sleeves so the edges would not cut. Oda with her one good arm. Brin, very slowly, with his face gray. Sef. Marrit.
Oren, too. And Lyra.
Corin could not carry glass and a crutch at the same time. So he stood by the litter. With Kestrel. And watched.
They laid Arran down on the floor of the passage, against the foot of the fallen wall, where it met the ground. On his hide. With his hands folded on his chest. And Kestrel knelt and straightened his plait, the long one at the back with the red thread, and laid it over his shoulder, on his chest, so it could be seen.
Then the glass went over him.
Piece by piece. The big flat pieces first, leaning together over him like the roof of a tent, so that none of them touched his face. Then the smaller ones, piled round and over those, to hold them. Each person bringing a piece, and kneeling, and setting it, and going back for another. Without speaking. The wind singing in the Ridges round them.
Tansy brought the smallest piece of all. A little sliver of green, no longer than her finger. She set it at the very top, very carefully, balanced, so it would not fall.
When it was done, it was a low dark mound of broken glass at the foot of the wall, with the starlight caught in it. Like a heap of frozen water. Like a little hill.
Kestrel stood over it.
She did not say any words. She did not touch her gray line. She stood with her spear in her hand and her brother's flute buttoned over her heart, and looked down at the mound for a long time.
Then she turned to Corin.
He did not know why her. Of all of them. She turned and looked at him, and he saw in the starlight that her face had not changed at all. Not once. Not since the yard. And that it was costing her everything she had to keep it that way.
"You think we leave him," she said.
Corin did not say anything.
"We do not leave him." She touched her own chest. Over the flute. Then she reached out and touched his. Two fingers. Over the black words he could not read, below his collarbone. "He comes. He is in six of us. In you. In me. He comes all the way." She took her hand back. "That," she nodded at the mound, "is only what he wore."
And she turned, and went to the crack under the fallen wall, and got down on her belly, and went in.
Corin stood and looked at the mound a moment longer.
Somewhere in his chest, under the ink, something laughed. Short. Like a fox. He did not let it out this time. He held it. Gently. Like a bird in his hands.
Then he went to the crack.
The Short Way
The silent man's short way was a crack under a fallen wall of glass so low that they crawled through it on their bellies like children under a fence.
He went first. The long thin shape going into the dark with his bound hands held up off the ground. He went in on his elbows. Corin saw him do it. He lay down on the glass at the mouth of the crack, flat, and put his elbows forward, and drew himself in, like a man swimming. Keeping his hands off the ground. Not once did they touch the glass. Then Tansy, after him, small and quick, wriggling like an eel, pushing her lamp ahead of her. Then Kestrel.
Then the others. One at a time.
And then it was Corin's turn.
He lay down. It took a long time. He had to get down onto his good knee, and then onto his hands, and then onto his side, and then onto his front, with the stiff leg out behind him like a log. The glass was cold. Colder than it had been all night. It came up through his coat and his shirt and into the new ink on his chest, and made the black words burn.
He looked into the crack.
It was dark. Very dark. Tansy's lamp had gone on ahead, round some bend, and there was only a faint blue glow, far in, on the underside of the fallen wall. The underside was rippled, like the walls. And it was very close. A hand above his head. Less.
He pushed his crutch in ahead of him. Then he went after it.
He could not crawl the way the others crawled. He could not use his knees. The stiff one did not bend, and the good one could not do all the work alone. So he went on his elbows and his good knee, dragging the stiff leg, the way he had dragged it across the floor of the bubble in the Fold Hills. Pushing the crutch ahead. Pulling himself after it. Elbow. Elbow. Knee. Drag. Elbow. Elbow. Knee. Drag.
The fallen wall pressed down above him. He felt it on his back. Not touching. Nearly. He felt the cold of it on his neck. He felt the whole weight of it, hanging there over him in the dark, leaning on nothing but the wall it had fallen against, waiting.
He did not think about that.
He thought about Hask's three notes instead. He hummed them. Under his breath. Elbow, elbow, knee, drag. Up, down, round.
Behind him, Lyra came. He could hear her. Her breath. The scrape of her sword scabbard on the glass, with the bent end of the blade sticking out of it. And behind her, Oren. Swearing. Very quietly. Steadily. In a low voice. A big man in a small place.
"Saints and all their candles," said Oren, somewhere behind in the dark. "Saints and all their little candles."
The crack went on. Longer than he would have thought. It bent, a little, to the left, and then to the right, and once it went down, a little, so that he had to slide on his chest into a hollow and then drag himself up out of it on the far side. And once it got lower still, so low that his crutch would not go through standing up, and he had to turn it on its side and push it flat ahead of him. And for a moment he could not go forward at all. The glass above was pressing on his shoulders. He was stuck.
He lay there. In the dark. With the weight of the fallen wall on his back.
And something in him went very still. And calm. And far off.
And his body turned. On its side. A little. Without him telling it to. The way a woman turns sideways to go through a narrow gate in a hedge. And his shoulders came free, one and then the other. And he was through.
He lay on the other side of the low place, breathing hard.
Sola. Again. Her body knew things his did not. It knew how to get through narrow places. He did not know where she had learned it. In the war, perhaps. In the valleys, before. Crawling under fences to get into orchards.
He said, very quietly, into the dark, Thank you.
Nothing answered. He had not expected it to. Her silence lay in him, calm and wide, like still water.
He went on.
And then there was light ahead. Not blue. Gray. And air. Cold, moving air on his face. And the glass above him went up, and up, and he was out. Out from under the fallen wall, into a passage, onto his knees on the glass, with his crutch in his hand and the stars over him.
Tansy was sitting there, waiting, with her lamp in her lap.
"He found this," she said. To Corin. To nobody. Proudly. Looking at the silent man, standing at the end of the passage with his back to them. "He found this all by himself. I'd never have found this. I'd have walked round, like a normal person."
Out of the Ridges
They came out of the last of the Ridges an hour before dawn.
Corin did not know it was the last, at first. It was only another wall. Another dark rippled line of glass across the stars, with another gap in it. The silent man went through the gap, and Tansy, and Kestrel, and the rest, and Corin came after them on his crutch, the way he had come through more of them than he could count since dusk. Crutch, swing, step. Into the gap. Through.
And there was nothing on the other side.
No wall. No passage. No yard. Nothing. Just the Glass. Open. Flat. Going away in front of him under the stars, dark and faintly shining, as far as he could see.
He stopped. In the mouth of the gap. And stood there, with the last wall on either side of him and the open glass in front.
The wind had stopped singing.
He had not noticed it stop. It had been singing all night, in every gap, low and high, the great soft choir with its mouths shut. He had stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing rain. And now it was gone. He had come out of it. Behind him, very faint, he could still hear it, going on in the walls. In front of him, nothing. Only the hum. Thin and high and steady, the way it had been since the Edge.
No. Not quite the way it had been.
Higher.
He listened. He was not sure, at first. Then he was. The hum was higher. A little. Not much. The way a note goes up when you tighten a string. And it was not only in his ears. It was in his teeth. A faint thin ache, in the roots of them, the way your teeth ache when you bite on something very cold.
The others had heard it too. He saw them stop, one by one, on the open glass in front of him. Turn their heads. Listen.
Kestrel did not stop. She walked on a few paces, out onto the open glass, and then turned round and looked back at them all. At the line of the Ridges behind them, black against the stars, running away east and west.
"That is the last," she said. "There are no more walls now. Not until the middle."
Nobody said anything.
Corin looked back too. At the long dark line of walls they had come through. All night. Gap after gap after gap. Somewhere back in there, in a narrow passage at the foot of a fallen wall, there was a low mound of broken glass with the starlight caught in it.
And further back, beyond that, in a yard between three walls, two iron men lay on their faces in the dark with their springs torn out.
And further back than that, somewhere out on the open glass beyond the Ridges, the last three. And the four lights. Coming.
He turned his back on all of it.
Ahead of him, low down on the far edge of the world, the sky was beginning to go gray.
Tansy came and stood beside him. With her lamp. She looked out at the open glass and the gray coming up along the edge of the sky.
"By noon," she said. Quietly. "We'll be there by noon. If nobody's slow."
She looked at his crutch. Then up at him.
"You're slow," she said. Not unkindly. "But that's all right. He'll wait. He always waits."
And she went after the silent man, out onto the open glass, with her lamp swinging.
The Bowl
The dawn came up behind them, on their right, and showed them where they were.
Corin had thought the Glass was flat. It had always been flat, or nearly. Long low swells, like the backs of sleeping animals, and the hollows between them, but flat, mostly. Going away level on every side to the edge of the world.
This was not flat.
He saw it as the light came. Saw it first in the long shadows. His own shadow, and the others', reaching out ahead of them across the glass to the west. They did not lie flat. They sloped. Downhill. Very gently. So gently you could not have felt it in your legs. But you could see it in the shadows.
The ground was going down.
And not only in front of them. On every side. He turned, on his crutch, and looked round. East. West. Back the way they had come, to the dark line of the Ridges, which stood now a little higher than they did. The glass sloped down from all of it. From the Ridges behind and from the far edges of the world on either side. Down. Toward the middle. Toward a place somewhere ahead, still hidden in the gray, where all the slopes met.
Like the inside of a bowl.
That was what it was. He saw it whole, all at once, the way you suddenly see the picture in a puzzle. A great shallow bowl, miles across. So wide and so shallow that you could walk into it all night without knowing you were in it. And they were walking down the inside of it, from the rim toward the bottom. The way a marble rolls.
And the hum climbed.
With every step down, it climbed. He could hear it now without listening. It was not a hum anymore. It was a whine. A thin, high, steady whine, like a mosquito in a dark room, but a mosquito the size of the world. It went into his teeth and stayed there. It went into the bones behind his ears. It went into the black tips of his fingers, so that they ached, faintly, the way a bad tooth aches before it starts to hurt properly.
Nobody liked it. He could see that. He could see it in the way people walked. Their shoulders up. Their heads a little down. Oda kept shaking her head, quickly, like a dog with water in its ear. Marrit had her hands over her ears inside her hood. Brin walked with his teeth set, holding his side, gray in the face.
Only the silent man did not seem to mind.
He walked at the front with Tansy, at his long even pace, with his bound hands held out from his body. His head up. Straight down the slope, toward the bottom of the bowl. As if the whine was a sound he knew. As if he had been hearing it all his life and had stopped noticing, the way a man who lives beside a mill stops hearing the wheel.
Corin walked behind him.
Crutch, swing, step. Downhill was easier. That was one good thing. The slope helped. It pulled him forward, gently, a little at every step, so that the crutch went further and the stiff leg swung further and the good foot came down easier. He was not slow, going down. Not as slow. He kept up.
Lyra walked at his right side.
She had not said much since the yard. Neither had he. There was nothing to say that was not already there between them. She walked beside him with her bandaged hand held against her chest and her bent sword sticking up out of its sheath at her hip, and every now and then, without looking at him, she put her good hand on his arm. Just for a step. And took it away.
He did not know if she knew she was doing it.
He did not tell her.
Oren on the Slope
Later in the morning, Oren dropped back to walk beside him.
He did not say why. He did not need to. He came back down the line, slowly, past the others, until he was walking on Corin's left, the bad side, where the silent man usually walked. The silent man was at the front now, with Tansy. Every one of them could see why he could not help Corin anymore. His hands were held out from his sides in their gray bundles, and they had not stopped bleeding.
So Oren walked there instead.
He did not put his hand under Corin's elbow. He would not have. It was not his way. He just walked there, close, a little behind, with his long sword in its sheath at his hip and the two packs on his back, his and Corin's. Corin had tried, at the start of the night, to take his own pack back. Oren had looked at him. Just looked. And Corin had stopped trying.
For a long time they walked without talking.
The slope went down. The whine climbed. The sun came up behind them, on their right, and their shadows went out long on the glass ahead, going downhill.
"He went in front of the little one," said Oren at last.
Corin did not answer.
"I saw it," said Oren. "I was running. I was too far. I'd never have got there. Nobody would. Except him. On his knees. With that foot." He shook his head slowly. "Thirty years I've been watching men fight. Men die. I've seen brave men. I've seen a lot of them. Most of them were brave because they had to be. Because there was no other way out."
He was quiet for a few steps.
"That boy had a way out," he said. "He was on the litter. Against the wall. Nobody would have blamed him. He had one foot. He was hurt. He could have lain there." A pause. "He chose."
"I know," said Corin.
"I want you to remember it." Oren did not look at him. "You've got him in you now. A bit. The old woman says so. So you remember it. Not the laugh. Anybody can have the laugh. Remember that he chose."
Corin walked.
Crutch, swing, step. Down the slope.
"I'll write it down," he said.
"Write it down," said Oren. "And remember it anyway."
They walked on.
After a while Corin said, "Oren."
"Mm."
"In the yard. When the second one came. I stood up." He swallowed. "No. She stood up. In me. Sola. I couldn't. I was too frightened. She did it."
Oren did not say anything for a long moment.
"I know," he said. "I saw. You came up off that glass the way I come up. The way she must have."
"Did she—" Corin stopped. Started again. "You said you'd tell me. Every time. If she spoke. Did she say anything? In the yard?"
Oren was quiet.
"No," he said. "She didn't say anything. She just stood there." A pause. "She stood there like a wall, lad. With your face. And looked at that iron coming. And didn't move."
He looked at Corin then. Sideways. With his gray eyes.
"I've never seen anything like it," he said quietly. "Not in thirty years. Not on any field."
Corin did not know what to say to that.
So he did not say anything. He walked on down the slope, with Oren on his bad side and the two packs on Oren's back creaking with every step. And somewhere deep in him, under the ink, under the silence, he thought he felt something. Very small. Very still. Like a hand set for a moment on his shoulder, and then lifted.
He did not tell Oren. He did not know how.
Clearer
By the middle of the morning, he could see into the ground.
It came on slowly. So slowly that he did not notice it start. The Glass had always been dark. Dark green, nearly black, smoky, like the glass of an old bottle. You could see the stars in it at night, faint and blurred. You could see the Ash-lights drifting under it, deep down. But you could not see into it. Not really. It was like looking into a muddy pond. Only the shine on the top, and darkness under.
Now the darkness was thinning.
He noticed it first at a rest. Kestrel had stopped them, in the full glare of the morning, and they had not put up the shades, because there were not enough poles left, and because Kestrel said it was not far now. So they stood about on the glass with their eye-strips over their faces and their hoods up, and drank their mouthful of water each, and Corin looked down.
And saw his own feet. And under his own feet, a little way down, the glass.
Not the top of it. Into it. A finger's depth. Two. The dark smoky green had gone a little paler, here. A little clearer. Like a bottle that has been washed. And he could see, a little way down into it, small things. Bubbles. Long thin streaks, like hairs, running through it, gold and rust and pale blue. A tiny cloud of something gray, hanging in the glass like a puff of smoke caught and frozen.
He walked on, and looked down as he walked.
And every mile, it got clearer.
The green went pale. Then paler. Then it was not green at all, but a sort of gray, the gray of rainwater in a glass. And he could see further down. A hand's depth. Then an arm's. Then further than he could tell. The bubbles went down and down in the clearing glass like bubbles in deep water, smaller and smaller, until they were only pinpricks of light. The streaks of color went down too, twisting, like weeds.
And under them, a long way under, he began to see shapes.
Dark shapes. Blurred. Too far down to make out. Lumps. Lines. Something that might have been a wall, or a hedge, or the edge of a field. Something long and thin that might have been a road. Something round.
He stopped looking down after that. He made himself. He looked at Tansy's back instead, and the silent man's, at the front of the line, and kept walking.
But he could feel them. Under him. The shapes. The way you can feel somebody standing behind you in a room before you turn round.
Lyra had seen them too. He knew because she stopped putting her hand on his arm. She walked with both her hands at her sides, and her eyes straight ahead, and her face very white under the eye-strip.
"Don't," she said. Quietly. Not looking at him. "Don't look down. I can't. If you do I'll have to."
He did not look down.
And the whine went on climbing, in his teeth and his fingertips and the bones behind his ears, until it was not a sound anymore at all but a feeling. A pressure. Like the air before a storm. Like the moment just before something breaks.
