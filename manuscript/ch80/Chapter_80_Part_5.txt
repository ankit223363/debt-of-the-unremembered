Chapter 80: The Eye, Part 5 of 5: Vance Blood
Vance Blood
It won't open for that, said Malakor.
Corin started. He had forgotten, for a moment, that the old man was there. That he could be there. Behind his left ear. Watching through his eyes.
Knocking, said Malakor. Dry. Knocking won't do it. He's been knocking for years, by the sound of it, poor devil. Knocking's only the greeting. It tells her who's come. It doesn't let them in.
Her?
Sola. A pause. She built this. Did you think somebody else built it? Out here? In the middle of her own valley? This was her place, at the end. Her room under the ground. Where she kept her maps, and her plans, and her people. And when she knew what she was going to do, at the end, she sealed it. So that nobody could get in after. Not the Ministry. Not the Bank. Not anybody. Another pause. Nobody but her own.
Her own?
Her blood, said Malakor. She sealed it so only her own could open it.
Corin turned his head.
Oren was standing a few paces back on the black glass. Still gray in the face. Still staring at the carved door. With his sword in his hand.
"Oren," said Corin.
Oren looked at him.
"He says—" Corin stopped. Started again. "Malakor says it won't open for knocking. He says she sealed it. Sola. So that only her own could open it." He swallowed. "Her blood."
Nobody said anything.
Oren looked at the door. At the carved sun in the middle of it. At the smooth worn place in the center of the sun, where many hands had pressed.
Then he looked down at the sword in his own hand.
His father's sword. And his father's father's. Plain. Long. A little heavy at the point. Sharpened for nothing in a forge at Haverstock, in the house where Sola Vance was born, by a smith who said Not in her house. Not to a Vance.
He did not say anything. He did not ask anybody. He walked forward. Past Corin. Past Lyra. To the door. And the silent man, standing there with his bound hands lowered and his head bowed, stepped aside for him. Without being asked. As if he had known all along that someone would come one day and he would have to step aside.
Oren stood in front of the iron door.
He held up his left hand. The big scarred palm. And he put the edge of his sword against it, near the heel. And drew the blade across. Once. Quickly. Without flinching.
The blood came at once. Dark. Welling up along the line and running down into his wrist, into his sleeve.
He did not look at it. He looked at the door.
And he put his hand on the sun.
Flat. His whole palm. Pressed against the carved center of it, on the smooth worn place, with the blood between his skin and the iron. He pressed hard. Leaning his weight into it. The way you would lean on a door that had stuck.
And he spoke.
Corin did not think he meant to. He thought it came out of Oren the way the throw had come out of Corin, or the hand-signs. From somewhere underneath. Low and rough, in the silence of the Eye, with his forehead nearly touching the iron.
"I'm here," said Oren.
And then, even lower:
"Aunt. I'm here."
For a long moment, nothing happened.
Then, deep in the door, something groaned.
Corin felt it more than heard it. Through the glass. Through his feet. A long low grinding groan, deep down, as if something very large and very heavy that had not moved for a very long time had been woken up and did not want to be. Iron on stone. Stone on iron. A shudder ran through the whole dome. Rust fell from the iron bands in a fine red rain. Down the long tears on the sides of it. Onto the black glass.
And the door moved.
Inward. Slowly. So slowly. A finger's width. A hand's. Grinding. Scraping. With a sound like a millstone turning for the first time in years. Opening inward into darkness. Not swinging, the way a door swings on hinges. Sliding. Back and down, into the wall, the way a stone slides back in a tomb.
Oren took his hand away from the sun.
There was a print on it. His whole palm. Dark red on the rusted iron, in the very center, on the smooth place. Every line of his hand.
The door ground on. Inward. Until there was a gap. Wide enough for a man. And beyond the gap, darkness. And a breath of air came out of the darkness, cool, dry, smelling of old iron and dust and something else. Something like the inside of a church on a winter evening, when all the candles have been put out.
And then it stopped.
Open.
Oren stood in front of it with his cut hand hanging at his side, dripping on the black glass, and looked into the dark. His face had gone very still.
Corin did not say anything. Nobody did.
Behind his left ear, very softly, Malakor said:
Well done, Vance.
Her Father's Hands
Lyra had not moved.
She was standing where she had stood when the knocking began. A few paces back from the door. On the black glass. With her hands deep in her coat pockets and her face white. Staring. Not at the open dark. At the iron door itself, slid back now into the wall. At the carved hands on it. The ring of them. Four fingers, two, the folding, three over the heart. Round and round.
Corin went to her.
He did not touch her. He could not. Not in here. He stood beside her, close, with his crutch under his arm and his hands held tight on the handle of it, and waited.
"Every day," said Lyra.
She did not look at him. She was looking at the hands.
"Every day," she said. "For two years. After he burned. I went to him, wherever he was, wherever they were keeping him. And I sat with him. And he didn't know me. He smiled at me, the way he smiled at everybody. Very polite. And asked if I'd come about the drains." Her voice did not shake. "And all the time I sat there, he tapped. On the wall. On the arm of his chair. On the back of my hand, if I let him hold it. With one finger. Four. Two. Nine. Three."
Corin did not say anything.
"I used to count them," said Lyra. "To have something to do. To not cry. I'd sit there and count his taps. Four. Two. Nine. Three. Over and over. Hundreds of times. Thousands. I counted them so many times I'd hear them in my sleep." She swallowed. "I thought it was nothing. Everybody said it was nothing. Just his hands. Doing something on their own, while the rest of him was gone. Like a clock that keeps ticking in an empty house."
She took one hand out of her pocket. The good one. And reached out, slowly, toward the door. Toward the ring of carved hands. And stopped, a finger's width from the iron. And did not touch it.
"It wasn't nothing," she said. "Was it?"
"No," said Corin.
"It was the calls. Oren's calls. Sola's calls. The ones they made at the end of the day. Witness. Hold. Fold. Home." She was quiet a moment. "And the knock. On this door. The one he's been knocking. Every month. For years."
She looked round. At the silent man, sitting with his back to the dome beside the open door. Looking south. Not at her.
"How did my father know it?" said Lyra. Very low. "How did my father know the knock on Sola's door? He was never here. He never came north in his life. He was a lord on the Hill who read books by the fire and laughed at Oren saluting him. How did he know?"
Corin did not know.
He looked at the carved hands. At the sun with Oren's bloody palm print on it.
And behind his left ear, Malakor was silent.
Not asleep. Corin could feel him there. Awake. Listening. Very still. The way a man goes still when a question is asked in a room, and he knows the answer, and is not going to say it.
Malakor, Corin thought. Do you know?
Nothing.
Malakor.
Not now, boy, said the old voice at last. Very quiet. Not here. Not yet. One door at a time.
And would not say anything else.
Lyra put her hand back in her pocket.
"Oren," she said.
Oren turned from the dark doorway.
"Did you ever teach him?" said Lyra. "My father. The calls. Did you ever show him?"
Oren looked at her for a long time. Then he shook his head. Slowly.
"Never," he said. "Not once. Not in twenty-two years. He never asked. I never thought to." He looked at the carved door. "I didn't know he knew them. I used to hear him tapping, at the end. In his room. I thought it was nothing too."
Nobody said anything.
The sun stood straight overhead. The Eye lay round them, black and clear and silent, with the drowned village far below. And the dark doorway breathed its cold old breath past them, smelling of iron and dust and candles put out.
"One door at a time," said Corin. Very quietly. To Lyra. Because it was all he had.
She looked at him.
Then she nodded. Once. And her face closed up again, white and hard, the way it did. And she walked past him to the doorway, and stood beside Oren, and looked down into the dark.
Oren's Hand
Vespera bound Oren's hand before anyone went down.
She would not let them go until she had. She took his left wrist in her cold old fingers and turned the palm up, there in the doorway, in the noon light, and looked at the cut. It ran right across, from the base of the first finger to the heel of the hand. Deep. Clean. Still bleeding.
"You great fool," she said. "You could have just pricked your thumb."
Oren looked down at her.
"Didn't seem enough," he said.
She did not argue with that. She put salve on the cut, from the tin, and a pad of linen over it, and wound a strip round and round to hold it, and tied it off. Her hands did not shake. His did. Corin saw it. A small, steady tremor, in the big scarred fingers, that would not stop.
Oren saw him see it.
"It's nothing," he said. Low. "It's just—" He stopped. Looked at the door. At the print of his own hand on the carved sun. "I said something. To it. When I put my hand there. I didn't mean to."
"I heard," said Corin.
Oren's face went a dull red under the gray of his beard.
"Like a fool," he said. "Talking to a door."
"It opened."
Oren did not answer that.
Corin hesitated. Then he said it. Because he had promised, in the chapel. Every time. And because it was true.
"Malakor heard too," he said. "When it opened. He said something. To you."
Oren looked at him sharply.
"What?"
"He said, Well done, Vance."
Oren stood very still in the doorway with his bound hand held in Vespera's.
For a long time he did not say anything at all. Then he took his hand back from her, slowly. And looked at it. At the white linen, with the red coming through it already, in a line across the middle.
"Sixty years," he said. Very quietly. Not to Corin. Not to anybody. "Sixty years she waited. And the first one of hers to come knocking can't even say a whole sentence to her." He shook his head. "Aunt. I'm here. That's all I had."
"It was enough," said Vespera. Dry. "Wasn't it? The door's open."
Oren looked at her.
Then, very slowly, the corner of his mouth went up. On the side the scar did not pull. Just a little.
"Saints and all their candles," he said. "I suppose it was."
And he turned, and drew his sword with his good hand, and went to the top of the steps.
The Man Waits
The silent man did not go in.
Corin had thought he would. He had thought, of all of them, the silent man would go first. He had walked across half the Glass for this. He had knocked on this door every month for longer than anybody knew. He had walked through the storm and the Ridges and torn the springs out of two iron men with his bare hands, all to come here, to this. And now it was open.
And he did not go in.
He stood beside the open door for a moment. Looking into the dark. With his bound hands held out from his sides and his long face very still and his pale eyes on the gap.
Then he sat down.
Right there. On the black glass. Beside the door. With his back against the fused stone of the dome, just to the left of the opening, so that the cool dark breath from inside came past his shoulder. He folded himself down the way he always did, long legs and long arms, until he was sitting cross-legged. And laid his bound hands on his knees. Palm up.
And looked out. Not into the door. Away from it. Out across the Eye. South. Back the way they had come. Over the clear black glass and the drowned village and the beach of ringed stones and the long slope going up beyond it.
Like a dog, Corin thought. Like a dog that has walked a very long way to bring its master home. And now the master is home, and through the door, and the dog does not go in. It lies down on the step. Outside. With its nose on its paws. And watches the road.
Tansy looked at him.
She looked at him for a long time. And then she looked at the open door. At the dark. And Corin saw her want to go in. He saw it in her face, plain as print. She wanted to see. She had wanted to see for two winters. Every month, for two winters, she had stood back and watched him knock on this door and nobody answer. And now it was open.
And she looked at him again, sitting there with his back to it.
And she went and sat down beside him.
On his right side. Close. With her knees drawn up and her uncle's coat pulled round her and her empty lamp in her lap. Leaning against his arm. And looked out where he was looking. South. Over the Eye.
"We'll keep the door," said Tansy.
She said it to all of them. Firmly. The way a grown-up says a thing that has been decided.
"You go down. Do what you've got to do. We'll stay up here and keep the door." She looked up at Kestrel. "Somebody's got to. In case."
She did not say in case of what. She did not need to. Everybody knew what was out there. Coming. Over the slope. Through the Ridges. Three iron men, and four lights.
Kestrel looked at her. Then at the silent man. Then at the door.
She nodded.
"Sef," she said. "Marrit. Stay with them. Keep your spears." She looked at Brin, gray with pain, holding his ribs. At Oda, with her arm bound to her side. "You two also. Rest. Watch the south."
Nobody argued.
The Ashborn sat down on the glass round the door. Sef on one side. Marrit beside him, leaning on her spear. Brin lowering himself slowly, with a hiss of breath. Oda with her back to the dome. All of them facing south. A little ring of people round an open door in the middle of the Eye, with the noon sun on them and the drowned village under them.
And the silent man in the middle. Sitting very still. Looking at the road.
Corin looked at him a moment longer.
Then he turned to the door.
Kestrel was there. And Oren, with his cut hand wrapped now in a strip of something Vespera had given him. And Vespera, with her bag. And Lyra.
And the dark.
A Stone for Sef
Before he went down, he went to Sef.
It was only a few steps. Crutch, swing, step, along the curve of the dome, to where Sef sat on the black glass with his spear across his knees and his narrow brown face turned south. He looked up when Corin's shadow fell across him. He did not say anything. He never did.
Corin put his hand in his right pocket. Not the left. The right. And took out the stone.
Small. Gray. Smooth. With its thin white ring.
He held it out.
Sef looked at it. Then at him. Then at it again. And Corin saw him not understand, and then understand. Saw it go across his face. Slowly. The way the light goes across a field when a cloud moves.
"You walked with him," said Corin. "All day. On the false trail. You held him up." He did not know how to say the rest. He said it anyway. "He's in me too. A bit. I can feel him laughing. I don't know what at. But he's laughing."
Sef did not say anything.
"I can't give you that," said Corin. "The laugh. I don't know how. It's not mine to give." He held the stone out a little further. "So this. Instead. It's from the shore. From the middle. Where we came." He swallowed. "For him. To keep."
Sef looked at the stone for a long time.
Then he put out his hand. His brown hand, with the bone spear's calluses across the palm. Palm up. And Corin put the stone in it, without touching him. Carefully. Letting it drop the last little way. Because in here, he did not dare to touch anybody.
Sef closed his fingers round it.
He did not say thank you. He did not say anything at all. He held the stone in his fist, and he put his fist against his chest, over his heart. The way the old call went. Three fingers. Home.
And then he looked up at Corin. And the corner of his mouth moved. The place where a smile would be.
Corin nodded. Once.
Then he turned, and went back along the curve of the dome, to the door. To Lyra, and Oren, and Kestrel, and Vespera.
And to the steps going down.
The Steps
He went to the door.
Crutch. Swing. Step. On the black glass. With the silence round him and the fire in his fingertips and the dead village under his feet. Lyra on his right. Oren on his left, with his cut hand wrapped. Kestrel and Vespera behind.
He stood in the doorway and looked down.
Steps. Iron ones. Going down into the dark. Steep. Narrow. Each one a plate of iron set into the stone, worn smooth in the middle by feet that had gone up and down them a long time ago. And on one side, against the wall, an iron rail. Rusted. Running down beside the steps into the black.
The light from the doorway went down a little way. A few steps. He could see the first, and the second, and the third, in the gray noon light that came in past his shoulder. Then the fourth, dimmer. The fifth, dimmer still. And then the dark took them, and he could only see the shape of the rail going down, a faint line, into nothing.
He counted them. He could not help it.
One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.
And then there were more. He could see that there were more. Going down past nine into the dark. But the numbers stopped working. They went soft in his head. Strange. Like trying to hold water in his hand. He knew there were more steps. He could not have said how many.
It did not matter.
The smell came up out of the dark. Old iron. Dust. Cold stone. And that other thing, under it all, the thing like the inside of a church on a winter evening when all the candles are out. Very faint. Very old. Something that had been shut in down there for a very long time, and was only now, slowly, beginning to come out into the light.
Corin stood in the doorway with his crutch under his arm and the dark in front of him.
And he was frightened.
Not the way he had been in the yard, with the iron coming. Not the way he had been in the trench. A quieter fear. Deeper. The fear of a boy at the top of the cellar stairs in a strange house, at night, with a candle, who has been told to go down and fetch something, and does not want to, and knows that he will.
You used to fear the dark, said the black words on his chest. Not anymore.
He had told Lyra, in the chapel, that he thought that was true now.
He was not so sure.
Lyra came and stood close beside him.
She did not say anything. She did not need to. She stood beside him in the doorway and looked down into the dark with him, with her hands deep in her coat pockets where they could not touch him, and her shoulder a finger's width from his. Not touching. Close enough that he could feel she was there. Steady.
Oren stepped past them both.
He went first. Of course he did. Down onto the first iron step, with his sword in his right hand and his cut left hand on the rail. And then the second. And then he stopped, and turned, and looked back up at Corin. His scarred face was gray in the light from the doorway.
"Mind your feet," said Oren. "They're steep. And slippy, I'd guess. Take the rail."
Corin took the rail.
It was cold. Rough with rust under his gray fingers. And as he touched it, the fire in his fingertips jumped. Just for a moment. As if there was something in the iron. Something old. Something that knew him, or knew what he was.
He did not let go.
Behind him, Kestrel had lit Tansy's lamp. Tansy had given it to her. Corin had heard them at the door, low. You'll want it more than us. It's dark down there. Bring it back. And now the small cold blue flame came over his shoulder, held out in Kestrel's thin brown hand, and went down past him into the stairwell, and lit the iron.
The steps went on below the light. He could see more of them now. The rail. The wall beside the rail, stone, blocks of it, dry and gray and close. Old marks on the stone. Scorched, in long black streaks, as if fire had come down here once, a long time ago, and licked the walls, and gone back up.
And at the bottom of the light, far down, the last step he could see. And beyond it, flat. A floor. And the dark going away sideways. A passage.
He looked back once. Up. Out through the doorway. At the square of noon light, white and blinding. At the silent man sitting beside it with his back to the dome. At Tansy beside him, small, with her knees up, looking in at them, at the lamp going down, with her face very serious.
She lifted one hand. Not a wave. Just lifted it.
He lifted his back.
Then he turned, and put his weight on the rail, and took the first step down.
And behind his left ear, close and clear and dry as dust, the old voice spoke.
There, said Malakor. That's her rail. She went up and down this every day, at the end. With her maps under her arm. A pause. I came down it once. Carrying a stretcher. Sixty years ago.
Corin stood on the top step with his hand on the rail.
Where? he thought. Where is it? The thing we came for. Where?
A long silence.
Then Malakor laughed. A dry rustle. Like old paper.
Under her table, he said. Where she kept everything that mattered. Go on, boy. Down you go. I'll know it when I see it.
