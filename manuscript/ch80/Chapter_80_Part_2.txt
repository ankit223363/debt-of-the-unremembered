Chapter 80: The Eye, Part 2 of 5: Empty Skins
Empty Skins
The water ran out a little before noon.
Kestrel did not say so. She did not need to. Corin saw it. At the last rest, she went down the line with the last skin, the way she always did, and stopped in front of each person, and held it out, and they took one mouthful and gave it back. And she weighed it in her hand after each one. He saw her do it. Bouncing it a little. Listening to the slosh.
And then, after Marrit, she stopped bouncing it.
She held it out to Sef. He took it. Lifted it to his mouth. And Corin saw his throat move once, a little, and then not again, and saw him lower the skin and look at it. And then hand it back to Kestrel without a word.
Kestrel held it up. Upside down. Over her own open mouth.
Nothing came out.
She rolled it up tight, the way she rolled up all the empty ones, and tied it, and put it in the bottom of her pack. Because on the Glass you never threw away a thing that could hold water. Not even when it was empty. Not even when there was nothing left to put in it.
Then she stood up and looked at all of them. Seven of them still waiting, who had not had their mouthful.
Corin was one.
She did not say she was sorry. She did not say anything at all. She looked at them, and then she turned and looked down the slope, toward the bottom of the bowl, toward wherever they were going. And then she started walking again.
And they followed her.
Corin's mouth was dry. It had been dry since dawn. Now, knowing there was nothing, it got drier. His tongue felt too big for it. His lips were cracked, the old cracks from the iron horse opening again, and when he licked them they tasted of salt and blood. The sun was on the back of his neck, through his collar, like a hand pressing. The glass under his feet was hot through the felt and the boot.
He walked.
He thought about water. He could not help it. He thought about the pump on the landing on Saint Bride's Lane, that he did not remember and knew was there. He thought about the river in the city, brown and wide and stinking, and how he would have lain down in it and drunk it now. He thought about rain. Rain on a slate roof, very loud, very close, as if he were lying right under it in a narrow bed.
That was not his. That was Tam's. A boy he had carried, once. Long ago. He did not remember Tam. He remembered Tam's rain.
He walked.
Ahead of him, Tansy was not talking. She had not talked all morning. She walked beside the silent man with her empty lamp hanging from her hand, and her head down.
Once, he heard her say something to the man. Very small. He did not catch it. But he saw the man stop walking. And turn. And look down at her. And then, slowly, lift one of his bound hands, and rest the bundled gray cloth of it on top of her head. Just for a moment. The way you would rest your hand on the head of a tired dog.
And then go on walking.
And Tansy went with him. Holding on to his coat.
Corin found he could not look at that either.
He looked at the slope instead. At the glass going down ahead of them, clearer and clearer, toward the bottom of the bowl. And at the line he could see now, far ahead, low down, where the slope seemed to end.
A pale line. Gray. Flat. Running across the bottom of the bowl from one side to the other.
Like a beach.
The Shore
It was a beach.
Corin did not believe it until he was standing on the edge of it. Even then he did not quite believe it. A beach. In the middle of the Glass. In the bottom of the bowl, at the end of the long gentle slope, with the whine in his teeth and the sun straight overhead.
The slope ran down to it and stopped. Just stopped. The glass under his feet, clear as rainwater now, so clear that he had stopped looking down because of what he could see in it, came to an edge. A soft edge. Not a line. And on the far side of the edge, there were stones.
Small ones. Gray ones. Lying on the glass in a long low bank, the way pebbles lie on a beach where the sea has thrown them up. Heaped a little. Sloping a little. Running away to the left and the right in a great wide curve, as far as he could see in both directions. Curving round. Like the shore of a lake.
He stood at the edge of them on his crutch and looked.
There were so many. He could not have counted them. Nobody could have. More than all the stars on all the nights since the Edge. More than all the grains of grit in the storm. Small gray stones, each about the size of the end of a thumb. Smooth. Rounded. As if water had rolled them for a very long time, though there was no water here, and had not been for longer than anyone could remember.
And every one of them had a ring.
He bent down, to see. Carefully. On his crutch, with the stiff leg out. And looked at the nearest ones. At the stones lying at the very edge of the bank, by his boot.
A white ring. Round each stone. A thin clean line of white, going all the way round, like a belt. Not painted. In the stone. Part of it. As if the stone had been made in two pieces, gray and gray, and joined with a thread of white in between. Every one he looked at. Every single one. A small gray stone with a white ring round it.
He straightened up. Slowly.
The others had come up beside him. Along the edge of the beach. Standing, the way people stand at the edge of the sea for the first time, not quite ready to step onto the sand.
"What are they?" said Oren. Low.
Nobody answered.
Kestrel crouched and picked one up. She held it in her palm and looked at it. Turned it over. Held it up to the sun, so that the light came through the white ring, and it glowed.
"Nobody knows," she said. "Nobody has ever known. They are just here. They have always been here, since the light. Round the Eye. All the way round." She closed her hand on it. "The old ones say they are what is left of the valley when the light came. The stones of the river. The stones of the fields. Burned. Made new." She opened her hand again. "The old ones say a lot of things."
She did not put the stone back. She put it in her coat. With the flute.
Lyra had gone still beside him.
Corin looked at her. She had her hand inside her coat. On the left. Feeling for something. And then she drew it out, and opened it, and there in her palm, on the bandage, was a stone.
Small. Gray. With a white ring.
"Davin's," she said. Very quietly. "The boy at Haverstock. With the dog. He gave it to me on the hill. For luck. He said his granda found it on the top. He said they come from the middle." She looked out at the beach. At the great curving bank of stones, all the same, all ringed, going away out of sight on either side. "He said they come from the middle."
Nobody said anything.
And Corin put his own hand in his own pocket.
His left coat pocket. Where he kept the things he did not want to lose. He had not put his hand in it for days. Not since the trench. He did not know, really, what was in it. Only that there were things in it, and that his hand knew them, and went to them sometimes, without him, when he was frightened or thinking hard.
His fingers found a scrap of thread. Faded. With a knot in it.
And under the thread, something small, and hard, and smooth, and round.
Wren's Stone
He took it out.
He held it in his palm. His gray palm, with the cuts healing across the back of the hand and the black tips of the fingers curled round it. And he looked at it in the noon light.
A small gray stone. Smooth. Rounded. About the size of the end of his thumb.
With a thin clean white ring going all the way round it.
He did not remember where he had got it. He stood there with it in his hand and reached for it, down, into himself, and there was nothing there. Smoke. A gap. He knew it was his. His hand knew it. His hand had been holding it in the bottom of his pocket for weeks, through the city and the fens and the hills and the fire and the Glass. But he did not remember who had given it to him, or when, or why.
The book would know.
He did not take out the book. He did not need to. Lyra was looking at the stone in his palm, and her face had changed.
"Wren," she said.
He looked at her.
"Wren gave you that," said Lyra. "The cadet. Kross's girl. With the twelve silver buttons. In the city. She came to you in Kross's gray carriage, in a servant's cloak. She'd lied for you. To the Ministry. And she gave you that, and said it came from the beach below the Fish Stairs." Lyra swallowed. "She said she thought it came from the Glass. She didn't know why she thought so. She just did."
Corin looked at the stone.
Then he looked at the beach.
At the great curving bank of them. More than anyone could ever count. Every one the same. Every one gray, and smooth, and the size of the end of his thumb, with a thin white ring.
The same.
Not like. The same. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting on a page you do not remember writing. It was not a stone that looked like these stones. It was one of these stones. It had come from here. From this beach. From the shore of the Eye, in the bottom of the bowl, in the middle of the Glass.
And somehow, somewhere, long ago, it had gone all the way to the city. To a beach below the Fish Stairs, by the river, where the mud was. And a girl in a white coat had picked it up. And given it to him. And said, I think it came from the Glass.
And now he had brought it back.
He did not decide what to do. His hand decided. Or something in him did. Some piece of somebody he carried. He did not know whose. He crouched, slowly, on his crutch, with the stiff leg out. And held the stone out over the edge of the beach. Over the bank of its brothers.
And held it up. Against them. So that he could see it and them at the same time.
And it was one of them. He could not tell it from the others. If he had opened his hand and let it fall among them, he would never have found it again. Not in all the years of the world.
He did not open his hand.
He looked at it a long moment. Then he looked at it against the beach again. And then, while he was looking, a strange thing happened. His eyes lost it. Just for a breath. He was holding it up in front of the bank of stones, and for one breath his eyes could not find it. It was gone. Disappeared. Swallowed up in all the others. As if it had gone home and the beach had closed over it.
Then he blinked, and it was back. In his fingers. Small and gray and ringed and his.
His heart was going very hard.
He put it back in his pocket. With the thread. And closed his hand over the pocket, outside his coat, and held it there.
"You could leave it," said Lyra. Very quietly. "It's where it came from."
"No," said Corin.
He did not know why. He only knew it was the wrong thing to do. That it was not his to leave. That somebody had given it to him, a girl in a white coat who had lied for him, and that it was a gift, and that you did not give back a gift by throwing it on a heap.
"She gave it to me," he said. "It's not mine to give back. Not here." He looked at the beach. "Maybe to her."
Lyra looked at him for a long moment.
Then she put Davin's stone back in her own coat. Inside. On the left. And did up the button over it.
Tansy's Pockets
Tansy was filling her pockets.
Corin saw her when he looked up from his own. She had gone a little way along the beach, by herself, while the others stood at the edge of it. And she was crouched down on the bank of stones with her uncle's coat spread round her like a tent, picking them up. One at a time. Looking at each one. Holding it up to the light, the way Kestrel had. And then either putting it back, very carefully, exactly where it had been, or putting it in her pocket.
She was very serious about it.
Corin went over to her. Slowly. On the stones, with the crutch sinking in. She did not look up.
"What are you doing?"
"Choosing," said Tansy.
She held one up. Turned it over. Looked at the white ring. And put it back.
"Not that one," she said. "That one's got a chip."
She picked up another. Looked at it. Put it in her pocket.
"I take one every time," she said. "Every time we come. Every month. One for every time." She patted her pocket. It clicked. "I've got lots. In the cave. In a row, on the shelf. By my bed. One for every time we came, and he knocked, and nobody answered."
She picked up another stone. Looked at it. Put it back.
"This time I'm taking extra," she said. "Because there's lots of us. One for everybody." She looked up at him at last. "One for you. One for the Captain. One for the lady with the sword. One for Kestrel. One for the old ones. One for Brin, because his ribs are broken. One for Oda, because of her arm." She stopped. Her face changed. "One for Arran."
Corin did not say anything.
"He'd have liked one," said Tansy. Very fiercely. "He'd have liked them. He liked everything. He'd have picked up a whole lot and juggled them and dropped them all and laughed." She sniffed. Hard. "So I'm taking one for him. And I'm going to put it on the shelf. With the others. At the end of the row."
She picked up a stone. A good one. Smooth and round and gray, with a clean white ring. She looked at it for a long time.
Then she put it in her other pocket. By itself.
"That's his," she said.
And she got up, and brushed her knees, and went back along the beach to the silent man, with her pockets clicking.
Corin stood on the stones and watched her go.
And then he did a thing he had not planned. He bent down. Slowly, on the crutch, with the stiff leg out. And he picked up a stone. Any stone. The nearest one. Small and gray and ringed. And he held it in his palm, and closed his gray fingers round it.
Not for him. He had his. Wren's.
For Sef. Who had walked the false trail all day beside Arran, and held him up the last mile, and had cried all night in the heap without a sound.
He put it in his right pocket. Not the left. The left was for the things he did not want to lose. This one was for giving away.
Vespera at the Edge
Vespera had not stepped onto the beach.
She was standing at the top of it, where the slope of clear glass ran down and met the first of the stones. Very still. With her bag held against her chest in both arms and her pale eyes on the black glass beyond the beach. On the Eye.
Corin went back up the stones to her. It was hard going, uphill on the shifting pebbles, with the crutch sinking in. She did not look round when he came. But when he stopped beside her, she spoke.
"Hask brought me here," she said. "Once."
Corin looked at her.
"Thirty years ago," said Vespera. "More. When I was young and he was already old. He walked me all the way from the Edge, the way you've come. The trench. The horse. The Ridges. I was sick the whole way. I cried at the trench. I wouldn't walk over the dead in the Ridges until he carried me." Her mouth moved. Not quite a smile. "He was very patient. He was always very patient. It was the worst thing about him."
"Why did he bring you?"
Vespera was quiet for a long time.
"To see," she said. "He said anyone who was going to work with the Glass should see the middle of it once. Where it came from. Where she stood." She looked out at the Eye. At the low dark dome in the middle of it. "He stood me right here. On this spot. And he said, Look. That's what one woman can do, if she wants a thing badly enough. That's what wanting can do. Never forget it."
She looked down at the bag in her arms.
"And then he gave me my pot," she said. "The gold-rimmed one. With the ink in it. The ground glass, from under the Edge. He'd been carrying it the whole way. He gave it to me here. On the beach." Her old hands tightened on the bag. "He said, Write on them. The ones who come back from the middle. The ones who lose themselves. Write them back. And I said I didn't know how. And he said, You'll learn."
She was quiet again.
"I learned," she said. "Thirty years. I learned. And I never came back here. Not once. I said I never would." She looked at Corin. At his chest, under his coat, where her black words were. "And here I am."
Corin did not know what to say.
"He's in you," said Vespera. "Hask. A bit of him. You hum his tune."
"Yes."
"Is he—" She stopped. Her face did something he had never seen it do. It went soft. Just for a moment. Just at the edges. "Is he glad? To be back here?"
Corin thought about it. He listened, inside, to the three notes that were always there now, under everything. Up and down and round.
They had changed, a little, since the beach. He had not noticed until she asked. They were slower. Lower. Like a man humming to himself while he looks at something he has not seen for a long time.
"I think so," said Corin. "I think he's looking."
Vespera closed her eyes.
Then she opened them. And her face was hard and old and dry again, the way it always was. And she stepped down onto the beach. Onto the stones. With her bag in her arms.
"Well," she said. "Then let him look. Come on. We haven't got all day."
And she went down the bank of ringed stones toward the Eye, without looking back, the way she had told him once that Hask used to walk. As if she knew exactly where she was going, and had been there before, and did not much like it, and was going anyway.
Into the Eye
They crossed the beach.
It was not wide. A short walk, for anybody with two good legs. Corin did not try to count it. The stones moved under his boot and his crutch, clicking, sliding, the way pebbles move on a real beach. The end of the crutch sank in. The stiff leg dragged furrows. Behind him he heard the others coming, the click and slide of stones under all their feet, loud in the whine.
And on the far side of the beach, the Eye.
He had heard it called that. The Ashborn called it that. Tansy called it that. He had not known why. He knew now.
It was glass. Like all the rest. But not like any glass he had seen since the Edge. Not dark. Not smoky. Not green. Clear. Perfectly, utterly clear. Clear as the air. Clear as nothing. Like the cleanest window in the world, laid flat on the ground. And under it, darkness. Deep, deep darkness, going down and down and down further than the eye could follow, the way darkness goes down in very deep water.
Black ice. That was what it looked like. A frozen lake. A great round frozen lake, with the ice gone black and clear because the water under it was so deep that no light came back.
It filled the whole bottom of the bowl. The beach ran round it in its great curve, and the Eye lay inside the curve, round and black and shining, as wide as a city. Wider. Its far edge was lost in the haze of the noon heat.
And in the middle of it, far out, there was something. A shape. Low. Dark. Sitting on the clear black glass like a stone on a frozen pond.
Corin looked at it. And looked away.
He came to the last of the stones. The edge of the beach, where the gray pebbles stopped and the clear black glass began. A clean line. Like a step down. Like the edge of a pool.
The silent man had already stepped over it. He was standing a few paces out on the black glass, with Tansy holding his coat, waiting. Kestrel had stepped over. Oren had stepped over, and stopped, and looked down between his boots, and gone white.
Corin stepped over.
And the hum stopped.
Not faded. Not went quiet. Stopped. All at once. As if a hand had closed over it. One moment it was there, the whine in his teeth and his bones and his fingertips, the high thin endless sound he had heard every waking moment and every sleeping one since the first step at the Edge. The next it was gone.
And there was silence.
He had forgotten what silence was. He stood on the clear black glass with his crutch under his arm and heard nothing. Nothing at all. And then, slowly, as his ears understood, things. Small things. Normal things. The click of a stone behind him, as Lyra stepped off the beach. Her breath. His own breath. Loud. So loud. Rasping in and out of his dry mouth like a saw. The creak of the felt round his boot. His heart.
Sound was normal here. Sound was just sound.
And then he felt it.
It came up through the soles of his feet. Into his good leg and his stiff one. Into his hip. Into his chest, under the ink. Into his arms. His hands. A rush. A flood. Warm, and then hot, and then a prickling, fierce, like needles, more than he could count. The way blood floods back into a foot that has been asleep. The way feeling comes back into frozen hands held to a fire, so that you want to cry out with it.
The tips of his fingers burned.
He looked down at them. At his gray hands, on the crutch handle. At the black tips. They were prickling. Stinging. As if something was running into them from the inside. Filling them up. The way a dry riverbed fills when the rain comes.
He knew what it was.
It had been gone so long that he had stopped feeling its absence. Since the Edge. Since the first step onto the Glass, where nothing could be taken and nothing could be burned. Gone. And he had carried on without it, the way you carry on without a sound you have stopped hearing.
It was back.
Magic. The thing in him that reached. That took. That burned. The Harvester's hand that was not a hand. He felt it come back into him like a tide. Into his fingertips. Into the gray. Into the place behind his eyes where it had always lived, until the Glass.
He could reach again.
He could take again.
He could burn.
He stood on the black glass, with the silence round him and the fire in his fingertips, and was more frightened than he had been since the trench.
