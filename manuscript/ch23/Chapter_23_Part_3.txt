Chapter 23: Needle, Part 3 of 5: Needle
Needle
Corin wiped his hands on the cloth.
He did it slowly, because his hands were shaking and he did not want the man at the table to see. The cloth was soft linen, clean and dry, and it smelled faintly of lavender. When he lifted it away there were black streaks of tar on it, and a few gray threads of hemp. He folded it again, the way he had found it, and laid it back on the boards. Then he pulled himself the rest of the way up through the trap door and stood.
The roof was low. If he stood straight, his hair brushed against the beams. He bent his head a little and stepped to one side, to make room for Vespera.
She came up the ladder without a sound. She did not wipe her hands on the cloth. She stepped over it, and stood beside Corin, and looked at the man at the table with no expression on her face at all.
Now that Corin could see the whole room, he saw that it was long and narrow, like the rope-walk below it. It ran almost the full length of the building. The roof came down steeply on both sides, so that the only place a person could stand up straight was along the middle, under the ridge. The beams of the roof were old and dark, and someone had hammered nails into them all the way along, and from every nail there hung something. Lamps. Bunches of dried herbs. Small bags of cloth tied with string. A pair of old boots. A birdcage with no bird in it, its little door standing open.
The floor was bare boards, swept clean. There were no letters on it. Corin checked that before he took another step, and he saw Vespera check it too. Her eyes moved over the boards quickly, the way a cat's eyes move over a room when it first comes in.
The shelves ran down both sides of the room, under the slope of the roof. They were plain wooden shelves, built by hand, and they were not quite straight. Some sagged in the middle under the weight of what they held. All of them were full of jars.
Up close, the jars were even stranger than they had looked from the trap door. Corin stepped closer to the nearest shelf and bent to look. The jar in front of his face was a little smaller than his fist. The glass was thick and green and full of tiny bubbles. Inside it, a pale gold mist turned slowly round and round, like a cat turning before it lies down. Every few moments a brighter thread of light ran through the mist and faded again. The label tied to the neck was a small square of brown paper. On it, in neat black ink, someone had written:
Lie: that the river man owed nothing. Cost: my mother's hands in bread dough. Kept.
Corin read it twice. Then he looked at the jar next to it.
Lie: that the clerk at Barrow Street saw a fire. Cost: the smell of the sea at Gullwick, the first time. Kept.
And the next.
Lie: that the Hessel boy fell. Cost: a song about a gray horse. Kept.
And the next, and the next, and the next. All the way along the shelf. All the way up to the roof. Hundreds of small brown labels, in the same neat hand. Each one with a lie at the top, and a cost below it, and the same last word.
Kept.
"Don't touch them," said the man at the table. "Please."
Corin straightened up.
The man had put down his pen. He turned on his stool, slowly, and looked at them both.
He was very thin. That was the first thing Corin saw. He was thinner than anyone Corin had ever seen who was still standing up and walking about. His face was long and narrow, and the skin of it was stretched tight over the bones, so that you could see the shape of his skull underneath, the sharp ridge of his brow and the hollows of his cheeks. His neck was long and thin. His wrists, where they came out of his sleeves, were no thicker than broom handles. He wore a dark gray coat that had once been good, with a high collar and a row of small black buttons, but it hung on him the way a coat hangs on a hook. Under it he wore a white shirt, very clean, buttoned to the throat.
His hair was black, going gray at the sides, and he wore it cut short and combed flat. His eyes were dark brown, and very quick. They moved over Corin's face, and then down to his hands, and then to the silver rod in his coat pocket, and then to his boots, and then back to his face, all in less time than it took to blink. Corin had the strange feeling that the man had just read him, the way you would read a page.
His hands were the last thing Corin noticed, and the thing he remembered longest afterwards. They were long and thin like the rest of him, with long thin fingers. And they were perfectly clean. There was not a single stain on them. Not a drop of ink. Not a smudge of color. Next to Vespera's hands, which were spotted and soaked with every color an ink-binder could use, his hands looked like they had been carved out of white soap.
"You must be Corin Vane," the man said. "The one who eats Ash."
Corin said nothing.
"I'm Hollis," the man said. "Though I suppose you knew that. People call me Needle, in the places where people talk about me at all. I don't mind it. It's a good name for the work." He looked past Corin, at Vespera. "You never called me that."
"No," said Vespera.
"You called me boy. For twelve years. Even when I was taller than you."
"You were a boy."
"I was twenty-four when I left."
"You were a boy," Vespera said again, in exactly the same voice.
Something moved in Hollis's thin face. It was too small and too quick to be a smile. Then it was gone.
"Sit down," he said. "Both of you. There are stools. I won't hurt you. I couldn't, really, even if I wanted to. You've already climbed my ladder, Mr. Vane, and you held on to it until the truth won. That means you know how this works now. And she—" he nodded at Vespera "—she taught me everything I know. Anything I could write, she could unwrite. It would be a waste of ink."
There were two stools by the table. They were plain three-legged stools, the kind a milkmaid sits on. Corin looked at Vespera. She did not move. After a moment, she shook her head very slightly.
"We'll stand," Corin said.
"As you like." Hollis turned back to the table and picked up his pen again. He did not write anything with it. He only held it, turning it slowly between his long clean fingers. It was a fine pen, made of a long white feather, with a steel nib. "You've come about the Fallowfield matter, I expect. Lord Thorne. The old man with the bad heart."
"We've come because someone tried to kill him," said Corin. "Twice now."
"Not me," said Hollis. "I want to be clear about that. I don't kill people. I never have. It's one of my rules."
"You write lies into them."
"Yes."
"And that's better?"
Hollis looked at him. There was no anger in the look. There was something else, something that Corin could not name at first. Then he understood. It was the look of a man who has had this argument with himself so many times that he is tired of it.
"I don't know if it's better," Hollis said. "I know it's different. A dead man stays dead. A man with a lie in him can still have breakfast. He can still hold his wife's hand. He can still walk to church and sing. He only believes one thing that isn't true. Most people believe a great many things that aren't true, Mr. Vane, and nobody had to write them in. They get along well enough."
He set the pen down. He laid it very carefully on the table, so that it lined up with the edge of the paper in front of him. Corin noticed that everything on the table was lined up like that. The pens lay side by side in a row, all pointing the same way. The ink bottles stood in a straight line, with their labels facing out. The papers were stacked with their corners exactly together.
And on the top sheet of paper, there was writing.
Corin did not mean to look at it. He had not meant to look at the floor downstairs, either. But the lamplight was on the page, and his eyes went to it before he could stop them. He saw only a few words before he tore his gaze away. They were not words of false ink. He knew that at once, because nothing happened inside his head when he read them. They were only words. Ordinary black ink, on ordinary paper.
But he saw the hand they were written in.
It was a small, neat, upright hand. The letters were close together and very even. And every time there was a letter g, the tail of it did not curl round into a loop the way most people wrote it. It went straight down and then turned sharp to the left at the bottom, like a fishhook.
Corin had seen that hook before.
He had seen it on the arms of a frightened girl in Vespera's leather chair on Hook Lane, the girl who called herself Mary Coyle and was not Mary Coyle. He had seen it moving under her skin like ants, in a hundred fine black lines, every g with the same sharp little hook at the bottom. He had written it down in his Ledger that same night, with his hand shaking: The hook on the g.
Now he was looking at the hand that had made it.
"You've noticed my g," said Hollis. He did not sound surprised. "Everyone does, in the end. It's a bad habit. She tried to cure me of it." He tipped his head toward Vespera. "She used to make me write out whole pages of nothing but gs. Good. Gate. Grace. Garden. Over and over. Loop, loop, loop. But my hand always went back to the hook as soon as she stopped watching. Hands are like that. They remember what they want to remember."
"That's how we found you," said Corin.
"I know. I thought it might be." Hollis sighed. It was a thin sound, like air going out of a small bag. "Ten years I've been careful about everything else. The paper. The ink. The places I go. Who sees me. And in the end it's my own hand that gives me away."
Corin looked back at the shelves. At the jars. At the hundreds of small labels, all written in that same hooked hand. Lie. Cost. Kept.
"What are they?" he asked. "The jars."
Hollis followed his look. For a long moment he said nothing. Then he got up off his stool and walked to the nearest shelf. He moved carefully, bending his long thin body under the slope of the roof. He stopped in front of the jars and looked at them the way a man looks at a row of old letters from people he has lost.
"You know the price of the work," he said. "Every spell has a price. You pay it in memory. You know that better than most. You've paid more than most."
Corin said nothing. His left hand had gone to his coat pocket without his meaning it to, where the Ledger sat.
"False ink is no different," Hollis said. "To write a lie into someone, you have to pay with a truth. One of your own. That's the rule. The ink is made from it. You take a true memory, a real one, something that happened to you, and you burn it down into the ink. Then you write with it. And the lie goes into the other person, and the truth goes out of you, and it's gone. Forever. That's how Vespera taught me. That's how it's always been done."
He reached out and touched the shelf beside one of the jars. He did not touch the jar itself. Just the wood beside it.
"But I found a way," he said quietly, "not to burn them all the way down."
Vespera made a small sound. It was the first sound she had made since they came into the room. Corin turned and looked at her. Her face had gone pale under the dust.
"You didn't," she said.
"I did." Hollis did not look at her. He kept looking at the jars. "It took me years to learn to do it on purpose. There's a moment, when the memory is going into the ink, when it's half in and half out. Like a dream when you're waking up. If you catch it then, if you're very quick, you can pull it back. Not all of it. Most of it goes into the ink. But a little part stays. The heart of it. The part that's still yours. And if you have a jar ready, and you seal it fast enough, you can keep that part."
He turned around at last, and looked at them both. His thin face was calm. But his eyes were wet.
"So I can't remember these things," he said. "Not anymore. Not inside my own head. They're gone from there, the same as any spell-price is gone. I don't know what my mother's hands looked like in the bread dough. I don't know what the sea smelled like at Gullwick. I don't know the song about the gray horse. But I know they were. I know they happened. And I know they're here. On these shelves. In these jars. Safe." He swallowed. "Every lie I have ever told cost me something true. And every true thing it cost me, I kept."
He spread his long clean hands, to take in the whole room.
"This," he said, "is everything I've ever lost."
Teacher and Student
For a long time nobody said anything.
The lamps burned. The mists turned in their jars. Somewhere in the room the small ticking sound went on and on, and Corin saw now where it came from. It was a clock on the wall above Hollis's table, a cheap round clock with a tin face and black hands. The hands said twenty minutes to three.
Vespera walked past Corin.
She did it slowly, the way she did everything. Her boots made no sound on the clean boards. She went down the long room, under the low roof, between the shelves of jars, and she did not look at any of them. She looked only at Hollis. She stopped when she was close enough to touch him, and she stood there, and she looked up at his face.
She was so much smaller than he was. Even bent under the roof, he stood a head and a half above her. But Corin thought, watching them, that it did not look that way. It looked like a tall thin boy standing in front of a woman who had caught him doing something wrong.
"You kept them," Vespera said.
"Yes."
"All of them."
"Every one."
"That is against the oldest law of the Binders. Older than write only what is true. Older than Haddon, or his master, or his master's master. You pay the price, and you let it go. You do not hold on. You do not cheat."
"I know."
"Do you know why?"
Hollis said nothing.
"Because if you can keep what you pay," Vespera said, "then it is not a price. And if it is not a price, then there is nothing to stop you. Nothing at all. You can write a thousand lies, and lose nothing, and never have to stop and ask yourself if it was worth it. The price is what keeps us honest, boy. It is the only thing that ever has."
"It wasn't for that," Hollis said. His thin voice was very quiet now. "I didn't keep them so I could write more. I didn't keep them to cheat."
"Then why?"
He looked down at her. For a moment Corin thought he would not answer. Then he said:
"Because I couldn't stand it."
He turned away from her and looked at the shelves again. His long hands hung at his sides.
"Do you remember," he said, "the first week I came to you? When I was twelve? You gave me a pot of the cheap black ink, and a needle, and a pig's skin from the butcher's. And you told me to write my own name on it. Over and over. Until my hand stopped shaking."
"I remember."
"I don't," said Hollis.
Vespera went very still.
"I know it happened," Hollis went on. "I know because I wrote it down, long ago, before I lost it. I have a book. Like his." He nodded at Corin without looking at him. "I keep a list of everything I've paid. So I know there was a pig's skin. I know there was a needle. I know you stood behind me with your hands folded and said Again, Hollis. Properly, this time. But I don't remember it. I can't see your face. I can't smell the ink. I can't feel the skin under my hand. It's just words on a page. Like a story about somebody else."
He was quiet for a moment.
"That one was the price of a lie I wrote on a dock clerk, seven years ago," he said. "I didn't know how to keep them yet, then. So it's gone. All the way gone. It's not in any jar." He touched his chest, just once, over his heart. "And there is a hole here where it used to be. A small one. I can feel the edges of it, sometimes, in the night. I lie awake and I put my hand on it and I think, there was something here. There was something good. And I sold it for eight silver to a man who wanted his partner to believe he'd been paid."
Vespera said nothing. Corin could not see her face from where he stood. He could only see her back, small and straight in the black wool coat.
"After that one," said Hollis, "I started trying to catch them. To pull them back, at the last moment. I spoiled a lot of ink. I lost a lot of memories that way too, more than I can count, because I'd catch them wrong and they'd tear, and half would go into the ink and half would just… go. Into the air. Like smoke out of a window. But in the end I learned it." He lifted his hand and moved it slowly along the edge of the shelf. "And every one I've caught since then is here. I can't remember them. I'll never remember them. But I can come up here at night, and light the lamps, and sit with them. And I know they're not lost. I know they're somewhere. That's all. That's all it's for."
Corin found that his throat had gone tight.
He thought of his Ledger, in his coat pocket. Of the long lists in it, in his own small hand, page after page. Burned: the taste of my mother's soup. Burned: my first friend's name. Burned: the way the light came through the window of the schoolroom. He thought of the way he read it sometimes, late, by the light of a stub of candle, and felt nothing at all, because the words were only words, and the things they named were gone. He thought of the hole in his own chest, the one he could feel the edges of in the night.
He understood Hollis Marsh. He did not want to. But he did.
"You could have come back," Vespera said at last. Her voice was rougher than Corin had ever heard it. "At any time. In ten years. You could have walked down Hook Lane and knocked at my door."
"And said what?"
"I'm sorry would have been a start."
Hollis laughed. It was not a happy sound. It was thin and dry and short, like a stick breaking.
"I'm not sorry," he said. "That's the trouble. I'm not sorry I left. I'm not sorry I took the gold. I've never been hungry since. I've never been cold. I've got a roof that doesn't leak and lamps that don't smoke and white shirts, Vespera. White shirts. When I was twelve I didn't own a shirt at all. I had a coat made out of a sack. You remember that. You must remember that."
"I remember."
"And you were going to keep me at that table for the rest of my life, writing true things for people who couldn't pay. Writing Mother on a widow's wrist for two coppers. Writing a dead child's name on a sailor's arm for nothing, because he cried. You'd have kept me poor forever and called it honest." He shook his head. "I'm not sorry I left. I'm only sorry it cost what it cost."
Vespera looked at him for a long moment.
Then she did something that Corin did not expect.
She reached up, slowly, and took hold of the front of his coat. Not hard. She did not pull him. She only held the cloth between her stained fingers, the way you might hold the coat of a child to stop him running into the road. And she looked up at his thin face.
"You are a fool," she said quietly. "You were always a fool. A clever one. The cleverest I ever had. But a fool."
"I know."
"You don't know anything. If you knew anything, you would not be sitting up here in the dark with a hundred jars of your own life and a clock and a pen, waiting for me to come and find you. You would have run. You knew I was coming. You knew it the moment I drew your ink out of that poor girl in my chair. You've had days. You could have been on a boat to Ostmark by now."
Hollis said nothing.
"So why are you still here?" said Vespera.
He looked down at her hand on his coat. Then he looked at her face. And Corin saw something change in his eyes, something that made him look, for a moment, much younger. Almost like the boy in the sack coat.
"Because I've been told to do something," he said. "Tomorrow. Today, I mean. This morning. And I don't want to do it. And I didn't know who else to tell."
Vespera let go of his coat.
"Tell me," she said.
Hollis glanced past her at Corin. His quick dark eyes went over him again, the way they had before, reading him like a page.
"Him too?" he said.
"Him too," said Vespera. "He climbed your ladder. He's earned it."
Hollis was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, slowly, and turned back to his long table. He sat down on his stool. He picked up the white feather pen again and held it between his fingers, turning it, the way a man fiddles with something when he is nervous and does not want to show it.
"Before I tell you," he said, "there's something I want to show you. Both of you. But mostly her." He did not look up. "It's on the top shelf. At the end. On the left. Behind the lamp with the green glass."
Corin looked where he said. At the far end of the room, where the roof came down lowest, there was one last shelf, high up, close to the beams. On it stood a lamp with a chimney of green glass, so that its light came out cool and pale, like light under the sea. And behind the lamp, half hidden, there was a jar.
It was not like the others. It was older. The glass was cloudy and scratched, as if it had been carried about in a pocket for a long time. The cork had been sealed and sealed again, many times, so that there were rings and rings of red wax around it, one on top of the other, thick as the bark of a tree. And the label tied around its neck was not the same brown paper as the others. It was a scrap of something else. Pale, and soft, and old.
"Bring it here," said Hollis. "Please. You're closer than I am. Carefully."
Corin went to the end of the room. He bent under the roof. He reached up past the green lamp, and he felt the cold glass under his fingers, and he lifted the jar down.
It was heavier than it looked. The mist inside it was not gold or white or blue. It was a soft deep gray, like the sky on a winter evening just before it snows. And it was not turning slowly, the way the others turned. It was very still. It sat at the bottom of the jar like a sleeping animal, curled up small. Only, now and then, a faint silver light went through it, like a slow breath.
He turned the jar so he could see the label.
The paper was not paper at all. It was a piece of skin. Pig's skin, very old, soft and yellow as a glove. And on it, in black ink, in a child's large shaking letters, someone had written the word HOLLIS, over and over and over, until there was no room left. And across the bottom of it, in the same hooked hand as all the other labels, but smaller, and older, and much less steady, there were four words.
V. — first lie.
Kept.
