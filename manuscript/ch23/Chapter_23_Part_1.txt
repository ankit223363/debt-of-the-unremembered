Chapter 23: Needle, Part 1 of 5: The Ink-Binder in the Dark
The Ink-Binder in the Dark
Vespera came across Fuller's Yard at half past one, walking as if the frozen mud were the floor of her own shop.
Corin saw her from a long way off. He had been standing in the shadow of the last wooden shed for nearly an hour, with his back against the boards and his hands in his armpits and his breath going up in clouds, watching the faint yellow light in the window under the gable of the old rope-walk. It had not moved. It had not gone out. Once, a little after one, a shadow had passed across it, thin and quick, and gone. That was all.
Moll had gone. He had stood by the little bridge with his towel-wrapped hand, and listened to Corin, and shaken his head, and said, A rope-walk. In the middle of the night. With an ink-witch. Saints help us. And then he had said, I'll be at the Watch house, harvester. At the back gate. If you need me. Knock fast, and limped off into the dark toward Needle Street, whistling.
Wat had gone, with the old woman in his arms, and Gideon's lantern bobbing ahead, and the eleven shuffling after it, and Tam carried on a chair of hands. Toward the dye stream, and the tunnel, and the arches. Toward Mother Grue and her poker.
And Corin had stood alone in the shadow of the shed and watched the light, and waited, and thought about a big man sitting on a stair in a stable, with a sword across his knees, patting the step beside him and frowning, trying to remember what it was called.
Then Vespera came.
She was smaller than he had expected.
He had read about her so many times. In the Ledger, page after page, since the very first week. Vespera. Hook Lane. The ink lady. With the needles. He had read about her shop, with its hundreds of needles hanging from the beams on threads, catching the light. About her long dark table, with the pale hollow worn into the corner of it by a boy's elbow. About her arms, covered from wrist to shoulder in a thousand fine lines of writing, names and dates and true things, so small you needed a lens to read them. Write only what is true.
And he had sat in her chair, he knew, and she had sat beside him while he wrote, and read over his shoulder. Two days ago. Three. He did not remember it.
In his head, she had grown tall. The way people do, in books. She was not tall. She was a small, thin, straight-backed woman in a long black wool coat that came down to her boots, buttoned to the throat, with a black shawl over her head and shoulders against the cold. She walked very fast and very straight, with short quick steps, like someone who had a great deal to do and not much time to do it in. And she carried a bag. A long flat bag of old black leather, with a brass clasp, held tight under one arm, against her side, the way a doctor carries his bag.
Juno was beside her. Walking, for once, not climbing. Close at Vespera's elbow, with her hands in her pockets and her sharp face turned up, talking, low and fast. Vespera was not answering. She was looking straight ahead. At the old rope-walk. At the light under the gable.
She came up to the shed and stopped.
Up close, in the faint starlight, her face was the face of the Ledger. Gray, and old, and very tired. Thin, with deep lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth, and dark eyes, sunk deep, that did not look tired at all. That looked, Corin thought, like the eyes of someone who had not slept for ten years, and had stopped needing to. Under the edge of the shawl, her hair was gray, pulled back hard. And on her hands, which were bare, in this cold, without gloves, there were stains. Purple, and black, and a faint silver. Worked into the skin so deep that they would never come out.
She looked at Corin.
"You found him," she said.
Her voice was low and dry and a little hoarse. Like a pen scratching.
"Dace found him," said Corin. "The big man from the Bell. He carried the lady up the ladder. Twice."
"I know. Juno told me." Vespera's dark eyes went past him, to the old rope-walk, to the light. "Ten years." She said it to the light, not to him. "Ten years I've looked. In every den on the Lower Docks. In every back room in the Cut. In every place on the Hill where they don't call it a den. Ten years." Her mouth moved. "And he's been sitting in a rope-walk four streets from my door."
She was quiet for a moment.
Then she looked down at the long flat leather bag under her arm. And at her own stained bare hands, holding it.
"I brought them," she said. "My needles. The way you asked."
"Why did I ask?" said Corin.
Vespera looked at him.
And something happened at the corner of her thin mouth. Not a smile. Vespera did not smile, the Ledger said. She doesn't smile. Don't wait for it. But something. A crack in the stone.
"Because you know me," she said. "Even when you don't."
They went across the yard together.
The three of them. Vespera in front, small and quick and straight. Corin behind her, with the rod in his left hand and the dark lantern shuttered in his right. Juno at the back, very quiet now, with her hand round the brass key at her throat.
The old rope-walk got bigger as they came close. And longer. Much longer. Corin had known it was long. Dace had said, You need a long room to make rope. As long as the rope. He had not understood until now how long that was. It ran along the whole of the far side of Fuller's Yard and on, past the end of the yard, past the backs of the houses beyond, behind a wall, out of sight. A low, narrow, endless building, like a barn that someone had stretched out and stretched out until it was as thin as a fence. Old brick at the bottom, black with soot. Old timber at the top, gray and split and sagging. And a long sagging roof of slate, with gaps in it, where the slates had slid off and smashed in the yard below and nobody had put them back.
All along the top of the wall, under the eaves, there was the row of tiny dark windows. Small and square, a few paces apart, going away into the dark like the portholes of a ship.
Only the last one, at the near end, at the top of the gable, had the light in it.
At the foot of the gable, there was a door.
Wide and low and wooden, with great iron hinges and a bar across it. Old. Older than anything in the yard. It had been painted once, dark red. Now it was mostly bare gray wood, split and silvered with age, with a few flakes of red still clinging in the cracks, like dried blood.
The bar was not across it. It was leaning against the wall beside it.
And the door was open. A crack. A hand's width.
Vespera stopped in front of it.
She did not touch it. She stood and looked at it. At the crack. At the dark inside. And Corin saw her stained bare hand tighten on the leather bag under her arm until the knuckles went white.
"He knows," she said. Very low.
"Knows what?"
"That I'm here. That I've found him." She did not take her eyes off the crack in the door. "He always left a door open. When he was a boy. When he'd done something. Broken something, or spoiled a page, or taken a copper from the tin. He never hid it. He never lied about it. Not once, in twelve years." Her voice went lower. "He'd leave the door of his room at the top of the ladder open, a hand's width, so I'd know he was waiting. So I'd come up and find him. Sitting on his bed, with his hands in his lap. Waiting to be told."
The wind went across Fuller's Yard. It moved the black shawl on her head. It moved the door, very slightly, on its great iron hinges, with a long low creak.
"He used to say, I'd rather you found me, than had to look," said Vespera.
She stood there a moment longer.
Then she pushed the door open, and went in.
Corin went after her.
And behind him, Juno said, very quietly, in a voice he had not heard her use before. Small, and young, and frightened.
"Corin."
He turned.
Juno was standing on the step, outside, in the starlight. She had not come in. She was looking up. Not at the door. Not at the gable. Above it. At the sky.
At the long sagging roof of the rope-walk, with its gaps where the slates had gone. At the far end of it. A long way off. Where it ran past the end of the yard and on behind the wall, out of sight.
"There's somebody on the roof," she said. "At the far end. Sitting. With their feet hanging down."
Corin looked.
He could not see anything. A long gray ridge of slate against the stars. A chimney. Darkness.
"I can't see them," he said.
"I know," said Juno. "You never can." She swallowed. "It's not her. Not Wisp. It's someone else. Bigger." She was holding her brass key so tight he could see the string cutting into her neck. "They were there when we came across the yard. They haven't moved."
Hollow, thought Corin. One of hers. And then, colder: Or one of his.
I want to see who he runs to.
He looked at the roof for a long moment. At the place where Juno was looking. At nothing.
Then he looked at Juno.
"Stay outside," he said. "Here. By the door. Watch them. If they move, if anybody comes, anybody at all, you whistle. Like a bird. And then you run."
"I'm not leaving you—"
"You're not leaving me. You're watching the door." He held her eyes. "That's the most important thing anybody can do tonight. Watching the door. Will you?"
Juno looked at him for a long moment. Her sharp chin was trembling.
Then she nodded. Once. And sat down on the step, in the cold, with her back against the old gray doorpost and her knees drawn up and her hand on her key, and turned her face toward the far end of the roof.
Corin went in.
The Long Room
It smelled of tar.
That was the first thing. Before Corin's eyes had got used to the dark, before he could see anything at all, the smell came at him out of the black. Thick and dark and sticky and sweet, like burnt sugar and pine and old ships. Tar. A hundred years of it. Soaked into the floor and the walls and the beams, so deep that the cold could not kill it.
And under the tar, rope. Old hemp, dry and dusty and a little sour, like hay that has been kept too long.
And under the rope, very faint, so faint that he almost did not catch it, something else. Sharp. Metallic. Like the taste of a coin held in the mouth.
He had smelled that before. In the Ledger. The sour coin-smell of the false ink. In a shop on Hook Lane, with a girl in a leather chair, and words crawling on her arms.
He opened the shutter of the lantern. Just a crack.
The yellow light went out in front of him, thin and wavering, and found nothing. It went out and out and did not stop. It touched the floor, a little way ahead. Wide old boards, black with tar and dust, with deep grooves worn into them along their length, as if something heavy had been dragged up and down over them for years and years. It touched the walls on either side. Close. Very close. The room was so narrow that Corin could almost have touched both walls at once with his arms out. Old brick at the bottom, crumbling. Old timber above, gray and split.
And it touched the roof. Low. A long low sagging roof of old beams, with the slates above them, and gaps between the slates where the stars showed through, small and sharp and cold.
But it did not touch the end.
The room went away in front of him into the dark. On and on. Straight as a ruler. Getting narrower and narrower, the way a road gets narrower in the distance, until it was a point, and then nothing. There was no end to it that the lantern could find. There was only the long low tunnel of tar-black boards and gray timber, going away forever.
As long as the rope, Dace had said.
Corin had never thought about how long a ship's rope was.
Along both walls, every few paces, there were things. Old wooden frames, taller than a man, standing against the brick. Like the frames of looms, or ladders, or the skeletons of something. They had hooks on them, iron ones, rusted, at different heights, and here and there an old piece of rope still hung from a hook, gray and rotten, swinging very slightly in the draft from the door. Between the frames, on the floor, there were wooden things like great spinning wheels, as tall as Corin's chest, with their spokes broken. And barrels, split. And coils of old rope, black with tar and dust, lying in heaps like sleeping snakes.
Pigeons. He could hear them. Up in the beams, in the dark, somewhere above. A soft shifting. A murmur. A flutter of wings, and then still.
Vespera was standing in the middle of the floor, a few paces ahead, with her back to him.
She was not moving. She was looking down.
"Shut the lantern," she said. Without turning round. Very low. "Shut it. Now."
Corin did not understand. But he shut it.
The dark came back, thick and black, and the stars showed through the gaps in the roof.
"Why?"
"Because of the floor," said Vespera.
She let him open it again. A crack. After a while. After she had knelt down in the dark, and put her stained bare hand flat on the tarry boards in front of her, and held it there, for a long time, without speaking.
"Now," she said. "Look. But only at the edge. At the light, not the floor. Look at the floor out of the side of your eye. The way you'd look at the sun."
Corin did not know how to look at a floor out of the side of his eye. He tried.
And he saw it.
There was writing on the floor.
All over it. Everywhere. On every board, as far as the thin yellow crack of lantern light could reach. Not carved. Not painted. Written. In ink. Very small, very fine, very neat, in lines that ran along the grain of the wood, from the door toward the far dark end of the room. Hundreds of lines. Thousands. So close together that at first Corin had thought they were the grain itself, the dark streaks in the old boards, the marks of a hundred years of rope being dragged.
They were not the grain.
They were words.
And they moved.
Just a little. Just at the edges. The way the writing on the girl's arms had moved, in Vespera's shop, under the lamp. A letter would move. The M loosened, and the a slid. Very slowly. Very faintly. Like grass in a slow wind. Like weed under water. The letters stirred on the boards, and settled, and stirred again.
And the coin-smell came up off them, stronger, sour and sharp.
"False ink," said Corin.
His voice came out very small.
"False ink," said Vespera. She was still kneeling, with her hand on the boards. "On wood." She said it as if she could not believe it. As if she were saying a fish on a mountain or snow in a fire. "On wood. I didn't know it could be done. I didn't know anyone could make it hold. Not on a dead thing. Not on a thing that has no blood." She took her hand away from the boards and looked at it. At her stained fingers. "It needs blood. To hold. It needs a heart under it, pushing the ink round. That's the first thing I ever taught him. The ink is nothing without the blood."
She looked down the long dark room.
"He's found a way," she said. "Without me."
"What does it say?" said Corin.
"Don't," said Vespera.
She said it so sharply that he jumped.
"Don't ask me that. And don't read it. Whatever you do. Don't look at it straight. Not one word." She turned her head, still kneeling, and looked up at him over her shoulder, and her dark sunk eyes were fierce in the thin lantern light. "False ink on skin, you have to be written with. It has to go in. Under. With the needle. It's a slow thing, a hard thing, it takes hours, and it hurts." Her mouth tightened. "But this. This is on the outside. On a floor. On a thing you walk on. It can't go into you through the skin." A pause. "So it goes in through the eyes."
Corin understood.
The cold went down his back like water.
"If I read it," he said, "I'll believe it."
"If you read it, you'll believe it." Vespera got up. Slowly. With one hand on her knee. "Whatever it says. Exactly as if it were true. As if you'd always known it." She looked down the long room, into the dark, at the thousands of lines of fine stirring ink running away along the grain of the boards. "And you'll keep walking. Reading. Line after line. Believing. All the way down the room. And by the time you got to the end, harvester, if there is an end, there'd be nothing left in you that was yours."
She looked at him.
"That's how he guards his door," she said. "Not with locks. Not with men. With lies. Lies laid on the floor like a carpet. And anyone who comes to find him has to walk on them."
Corin stood in the doorway of the long room with the lantern in his hand and the cold in his back.
He thought of Juno, outside on the step. If he had let her come in. If she had run ahead, the way she always did. Quick and light and sure, on her toes, with her sharp black eyes looking down at the floor to see where to put her feet.
Reading.
Saints, he thought. Saints.
"How do we get across?" he said.
Vespera did not answer at once.
She stood looking down the room. Then she bent, and unclasped the long flat leather bag under her arm, and opened it. Corin saw, in the thin lantern light, rows and rows of needles. Hundreds. Fine and long and shining, every one in its own little pocket of black velvet, sorted by size, the way a jeweler sorts his stones. And small pots of ink, stoppered with wax, in a row. Black. Purple. Silver. And a lens, with a brass handle. And a thin brush.
She did not take out a needle. She took out a pot.
It was the silver one. Small. No bigger than a thimble. She broke the wax with her thumbnail and the smell came out of it, and it was not the sour coin-smell of the false ink. It was clean. Sharp and clean, like cold water, like the air after rain.
"The truth," said Vespera, "is heavier than a lie."
She said it like a lesson. Like something she had said a thousand times, to a boy at a table, with his elbow wearing a hollow into the corner of the wood.
"Always. A lie is light. It floats. It moves. You saw it moving. It has to move, because it has nothing to stand on. But the truth sits still. It doesn't need to move. It's already where it is." She dipped the thin brush into the silver ink. "So if you put a little truth on a lie—just a little, just a drop—the lie can't hold. It can't bear the weight. It slides off."
She knelt down again.
And on the first board, at the very edge of the long room, by the doorway, with the thin brush, very quickly, very small, she wrote.
Corin did not read it. He looked at it out of the side of his eye. A word. One word. Silver. Very short.
And under it, on the board, the stirring black lines of the false ink went still.
Not slowly. All at once. The way a crowd goes still when somebody shouts. They stopped moving. They lay on the wood, quite still, and the edges of them went soft and gray. And the sour coin-smell coming up off that one board died away, and was gone.
Vespera moved forward a step. Onto the board. She knelt again.
And wrote the word again, on the next.
"What does it say?" said Corin. "The silver."
Vespera did not look up. Her brush moved. Quick and small and sure.
"It says floor," she said.
Corin stared at her.
"That's all?"
"That's all." She moved forward again. Another board. Another word. "This is a floor. That's the truest thing there is, about a floor. It's a floor. It isn't a road, or a river, or a door, or a lie. It's a floor, and you walk on it, and it holds you up." Her brush moved. "Tell a lie it's a floor, and it remembers what it really is. And it lets go."
She looked back at him, over her shoulder, with the thin silver brush in her stained fingers.
"Walk where I've written," she said. "Only where I've written. And keep your eyes on my back. Not on the floor. On my back." A pause. "And if you feel yourself starting to read, harvester, if your eyes slide, if a word comes up at you and you feel it going in—you say so. You say it out loud. And I'll stop."
"How long will it take?"
Vespera looked down the long dark room.
At the thousands of lines of stirring false ink, going away along the grain of the boards, forever, toward the far end where the light was.
"As long as the rope," she said.
