Chapter 23: Needle, Part 2 of 5: Do Not Read
Do Not Read
It was slow.
Slower than anything Corin had ever done. Slower than the coal heap. Slower than the waiting behind the crates. Vespera knelt, and wrote, and moved forward one board. And knelt, and wrote, and moved forward one board. And he came after her, one board at a time, stepping only where the silver was, with the lantern held low and shuttered almost shut, so that only a thread of yellow fell on the floor in front of her brush, and his eyes fixed on the back of her black shawl.
On the back of her shawl. Not on the floor.
It was the hardest thing he had ever done.
Because the floor wanted to be read.
He had not known that a thing could want. But it did. He could feel it. All round him, on every side, on every board he did not step on, the lines of fine black ink lay stirring along the grain. And they pulled at him. Not like a hand. Like a voice. Like someone saying his name, softly, over and over, just at the edge of hearing, in a crowded room. Look. Look down. Just one word. Just one. It won't hurt. Look.
He did not look.
He looked at the back of Vespera's shawl. At the fringe of it, where it lay over her shoulders. Black wool, gone brown at the edges with age, with a darn in it, near the left shoulder, in gray thread. He looked at the darn. He counted the stitches in the darn. Eleven. He counted them again. Eleven.
Good, said Malakor, very quietly. Count, boy. Keep counting. It's a thing your mind can hold on to. While your mind is holding on to something, it cannot be reading.
They went on.
Twenty boards. Thirty. Fifty.
The door behind them got smaller. The square of starlight in it, gray and pale, shrank, and shrank, and was a long way back. The far end of the room did not get any closer. It was still dark. Still nothing. The long low tunnel of tarry boards and gray timber still went on and on in front of them, narrowing to a point.
Vespera's brush moved. Quick and small and sure. Floor. Floor. Floor. And under it, every time, the stirring lines went still and gray.
But she was getting slower.
Corin could see it. In the set of her thin shoulders, under the shawl. In the way she knelt, now, with one hand flat on the board to hold herself up. In the way her breath came, short and quick, white in the thin yellow light. She was not young. He did not know how old she was. The Ledger had a number, but he had not read it today. Old. Older than she looked. And she had been kneeling on a freezing floor for half an hour, writing.
And the silver pot in her other hand was getting low.
He saw it when she dipped the brush. The bottom of the pot, showing. A little silver left, in a ring round the edge, like the last of the milk in a jug.
"Vespera," he said.
"I know," she said. Without turning round.
"How much is left?"
"Enough." A pause. Her brush moved. Floor. "Perhaps."
They went on.
It happened at the broken board.
Corin did not see it coming. Neither did Vespera. She moved forward, onto the next board, the way she had moved forward a hundred times, and knelt, and the board gave way under her knee.
Not all at once. It cracked. Loud, in the silence, like a stick snapping. And the end of it went down, sharply, into a hole underneath, and Vespera went down with it, sideways, with a cry, her arm going out, her brush flying, the silver pot jumping out of her hand and rolling away across the boards into the dark.
Corin did not think. He reached for her. With his right hand, the bad one, the bandaged one. Dropping the lantern.
The lantern hit the boards and fell on its side and the shutter swung open.
And the light came out. All of it. Yellow and bright and sudden. It went out across the floor in a great wide fan, along the tarry boards, over the stirring lines of ink, for twenty paces, thirty, lighting everything.
And Corin, with his hand on Vespera's arm, pulling her up out of the hole, looked down.
He did not mean to. His eyes went where the light went. The way eyes do.
And he read.
It was one line.
That was all. One line. On the board beside his left boot, in the sudden yellow light, running along the grain. Fine black letters, very neat, very small, with a little hook on the tail of every g.
He read it before he knew he was reading. The way you read a word on a shop sign as you pass it in the street. All at once. Without meaning to.
There is no ladder.
And it went into him.
He felt it go. Through his eyes and into his head, cold and smooth and quick, like a sliver of ice sliding down the back of his throat. And it settled. Somewhere behind his eyes. And it was true.
There was no ladder.
Of course there was no ladder. There had never been a ladder. He knew that. He had always known it. There was a loft, at the top, under the gable, with a light in it. Dace had said so. But there was no ladder. You could not get up to it. You could only stand at the bottom and look up at the trap door in the ceiling, out of reach, forever.
He knew it the way he knew his own name. The way he knew the cold. As simply and as surely as that.
And he knew, at the same time, somewhere very far down, under the cold sliver of ice, that it was a lie.
That was the terrible thing. He knew both. At once. There is no ladder. And, under it, very faint, like a voice from the bottom of a well: Dace climbed it. Twice. Carrying her. And the two things sat side by side in him, and the first one was much louder, and much stronger, and much more sure.
His eyes were sliding. Along the board. To the next line.
Turn back. There is nothing here. Turn back. You have already—
"Corin!"
Something hit him across the face.
Not hard. A hand. Flat. Thin and bony and cold. Across his cheek, with a sharp crack. His head went round with it. Away from the floor. Up.
Vespera was standing in front of him. Out of the hole. On the silver board. With her black shawl fallen back off her gray hair and her face white and fierce in the yellow light, and her stained hand still up, where it had hit him.
"Look at me," she said. "At me. Not down. At me."
He looked at her.
At her face. At her dark sunk eyes. At the deep lines from her nose to the corners of her mouth.
"What did you read?" she said.
He could not answer at first. His mouth would not work.
"What did you read, harvester?"
"There's no ladder," said Corin.
Vespera's face did not change.
"Is that all?"
"I think so." He swallowed. "Turn back. There's nothing here. I started the next one. You stopped me."
She looked at him a long moment.
Then she bent, very quickly, and shut the shutter of the lantern on the floor. The light went out. The dark came back, black and thick, with the stars in the gaps of the roof.
And the floor stopped pulling.
All at once. Like a hand letting go. Corin felt it. The soft voice at the edge of his hearing, saying look, look, went quiet. Because there was nothing to see. Because in the dark, the ink was only ink. Lines on a floor that nobody could read.
He stood in the black, shaking.
"The dark," he said. When he could. "It only works in the light. If you can't see it, you can't read it."
"Yes." Vespera's voice came out of the black in front of him. Low and hoarse. "Yes. I should have seen it. He keeps it dark. He always kept his room dark. He liked to work by one candle." A pause. "The big man. Dace. You said he carried her across."
"Twice."
"In the dark. With the rats." Her breath went out, long and slow. "He must have known the way. Or been told it. A path. A wall to keep your hand on. And the lady in his arms with her eyes shut." She was quiet. "That's how she crosses it. She never looks down. She's carried. She never once looks at what he's written for other people."
Corin stood in the dark with his hand on his cheek, where she had hit him.
There is no ladder.
It was still there. Behind his eyes. Cold and smooth and sure. He could feel it, the way you feel a splinter under a fingernail. There is no ladder. There never was.
"Vespera," he said. "It's still in me. The lie. I know it's a lie. But it's still there. It still feels true."
"It will." Her voice was very quiet. "Until I take it out. Or until you see the ladder with your own eyes, and put your hand on it, and climb it. Then the truth will be heavier, and it will slide off." A pause. "Can you do that? Can you climb a ladder you know isn't there?"
"I don't know."
"Neither do I," said Vespera. "Nobody's ever had to."
They went on in the dark.
Vespera found the wall. The left one. She found it with her hand, and then she found something on it. About waist high. A rope. Old and rough and greasy with tar, stretched along the wall on iron hooks, from the door toward the far end. A rail. A guide.
"Here," she said. "His path. The big man's path. Put your hand on it. Shut your eyes. Don't open them. Not for anything."
Corin put his hand on the rope. He shut his eyes.
They walked.
It was easier, in the dark, with his eyes shut. And it was worse. Because now there was nothing. No light. No floor. No back of a black shawl with a darn in it. Only the rough greasy rope under his left hand, and the tarry boards under his boots, and the cold, and the soft shuffle of Vespera's steps in front of him, and the pigeons murmuring in the beams overhead.
And the voice in his head. Not Malakor's. His own. Saying, very calmly, very reasonably, over and over: There is no ladder. There is no ladder. You're walking toward nothing. When you get to the end, there'll be no way up. Turn back.
He kept walking.
He counted his steps, the way Malakor had told him. He got to three hundred and lost count, and started again.
And at last, at four hundred and something, Vespera stopped.
Corin opened his eyes.
Above him, in the dark, a long way up, there was a square of yellow light. Faint. Thin. Leaking out round the edges of something flat and wooden, set into the boards of a ceiling.
A trap door.
And under it, going up from the floor at his feet into the dark, there was nothing at all.
No ladder.
Just the dark. And the square of light, out of reach, forever.
The Trap Door
Corin stood at the end of the rope and looked up at the square of light.
It was not a large square. It was about as wide as a man's shoulders, and it was cut into a ceiling of old gray boards. The light that came through it was yellow and soft, the kind of light a lamp makes when the wick is turned low. It moved a little, the way lamplight moves, and it threw a pale shape onto the dark below. The shape fell on the floor near his feet. It fell on the dust, and on the curls of old hemp, and on the small black letters that ran across the boards like ants.
He did not look at the letters. He had learned that much.
He looked at the space under the trap door instead. It was empty. He could see the wall behind it, a wall of rough brick with white salt growing in the cracks. He could see a nail in the wall, bent and rusty, with a bit of string hanging from it. He could see the dust in the air, turning slowly in the light. He could see all of that very clearly.
He could not see a ladder.
"Vespera," he said. His voice came out small. "There's no ladder."
She was behind him somewhere in the dark. He heard her stop. He heard the soft sound of her coat brush against the rope.
"There is a ladder," she said.
"I'm looking at the place where it should be. I'm looking at it now. There is nothing there. There's a wall. There's a nail with a string on it. There's nothing else."
"Corin." Her voice was flat and calm. It was the voice of a woman who had stood in a great many bad rooms and had stopped being frightened of them a long time ago. "What did you read?"
He thought about it. It was hard to think about. When he tried to remember the words, they slid away from him, like a fish in cold water. But he made himself hold on.
"Before the rope," he said slowly. "When I opened my eyes. There was a line by my foot. Near the wall. I didn't mean to read it. It was just there, and the light was on it, and I read it before I knew I was reading."
"What did it say?"
"It said, There is no ladder."
She was quiet for a moment.
"Then that is why you cannot see it," she said. "He wrote that line for anyone who came this far. Most people would not come this far. Most people would read the first line on the floor, by the door, and walk into the wall and break their nose and go home. But if someone was careful, and came along the rope with their eyes shut, they would open their eyes here, at the end, because they would want to see the way up. And the first thing they would see is that line. Hollis is a careful man. He thinks about what careful people will do."
Corin swallowed. His mouth was dry. The air in the long room tasted of tar and old rope and something else, something sharp and sour, like vinegar that had gone bad.
"How long will it last?" he asked.
"A small line like that? A few hours. Maybe a day. False ink is not strong when it is used on many people at once. He spread it thin across the whole floor. A thin lie wears off like a thin coat of paint."
"We don't have a few hours."
"No," she agreed. "We don't."
He heard her moving closer. She came up to stand beside him, and he turned his head and saw her for the first time since they had shut their eyes at the door. She looked very small in the dark. Her gray hair was coming loose from its knot. There was a smear of dust on her cheek, and her black wool shawl had slipped down from one shoulder. Her eyes were open now too, but she was not looking at the floor. She was looking at him.
"Listen to me," she said. "A lie written in false ink is not a wall. It is a curtain. It hangs between you and the thing. The thing is still there. Your eyes cannot see it, because the curtain tells your eyes what to see. But your hands do not read. Your feet do not read. Your body does not care what a word says."
She reached out and took his right hand in hers. Her fingers were dry and rough, and stained in many colors at the tips: blue, black, a faded green, and a deep red that had soaked into the skin around one nail. She lifted his hand and held it out in front of him, into the empty space under the trap door.
"Shut your eyes," she said.
He shut them.
"Now reach."
He reached. His hand moved forward into the air. He felt nothing. He felt the cool damp of the room on his skin, and the faint warmth of the lamplight from above, and nothing else.
"There's nothing," he said.
"Further."
He leaned forward. His arm was straight now. He felt the sleeve of his coat pull tight at the shoulder. He felt the stitches of Lyra's mending on the elbow rub against his skin. And then the tips of his fingers touched something.
It was wood.
It was round, and smooth, and a little greasy, the way wood gets when a great many hands have held it over a great many years. It was about as thick as a broom handle. It was cold. When he pressed against it, it did not move.
He opened his eyes.
His hand was hanging in the empty air. His fingers were curled around nothing at all. He could see the wall behind his hand. He could see the bent nail, and the bit of string. He could see the dust, turning in the light. He could see his own fingers, closed into a loose fist, holding air.
But he could feel the wood.
The feeling made him sick. It was the strangest feeling he had ever had, and he had had a great many strange feelings since he first took up the rod. His eyes said one thing and his hand said another, and the two of them were shouting at each other inside his head. His stomach turned over. He felt the sour taste rise up in his throat. For a moment he thought he was going to fall.
"Shut your eyes," Vespera said again, very quietly. "Shut them, and keep them shut. Don't argue with the lie. You will lose. A lie is always louder than the truth when you look straight at it. Just shut your eyes and let your hands tell you what is real."
He shut his eyes.
At once it was easier. In the dark behind his eyelids there was no empty space, no wall, no nail. There was only his hand, and the cold round wood inside it. The shouting in his head went quiet. His stomach settled.
"Good," said Vespera. "Now find the next one."
He let go with his right hand and reached up. Slowly. Carefully. His fingers moved through the air like a blind man's fingers moving across a table, feeling for a cup. They went up a little way, a hand's width, then two. Then they found it. Another round piece of wood, just like the first. A rung.
He reached down with his left hand and found the side of the ladder. It was a long flat piece of wood, rough at the edges, with the rungs set into it in deep holes. Someone had driven a nail through the side into the end of each rung to hold it tight. He could feel the head of the nail under his thumb, a small cold circle of iron.
"It's here," he said. His voice shook. "It's really here."
"Of course it's here," said Vespera. "Hollis climbs it every day. He is not a bird."
In spite of everything, Corin almost laughed.
"I'm going up," he said.
"Go slowly. Keep your eyes shut the whole way. When you get to the top, don't open them until your head is through the hole. There may be more lines up there. If there are, you must not read them."
"And you?"
"I'll be behind you. I read nothing on the way in. My eyes are clean. If you fall, I'll tell you where you are."
He put his right foot on the bottom rung.
That was the worst part. His foot did not want to do it. His foot had been listening to his eyes for the whole of his life, and his eyes had said there was nothing here, and his foot believed them. When he lifted it and put it down, he felt every muscle in his leg go tight, waiting to fall through the air and hit the floor. It did not fall. The rung was there. It took his weight. It creaked a little, a dry old creak, like the sound of a door in a house where nobody lives.
He lifted his other foot and put it on the next rung.
He climbed.
It was not a long ladder. Afterwards, when he thought about it, he guessed it had been about twelve rungs, maybe fourteen. A man could climb it in the time it took to count to twenty. But it felt much longer than that. Each rung was a small war. Each time he reached up with a hand, some part of him was certain he would close his fingers on nothing. Each time he lifted a foot, some part of him was certain the foot would go down into empty space. He made himself do it anyway. He breathed through his nose, slowly. He counted the rungs under his breath.
One. Two. Three.
The wood was old and greasy. In some places it was worn smooth and thin in the middle, where hands and feet had rubbed it for years. In other places it was rough, and small splinters caught at his palms. Once his fingers found something soft and sticky on a rung, and he guessed it was tar, from the rope-walk. The smell of it was strong here. It came down through the trap door from above, thick and black and bitter. He could smell lamp oil too, and old paper, and the faint sweet smell of beeswax.
Six. Seven. Eight.
The light grew warmer on his face. Even with his eyes shut he could see it, a soft orange glow through the skin of his eyelids. He could hear things now from above. A small ticking sound, like a clock. A faint scratching sound. The creak of a board as something heavy settled on it.
He stopped.
"There's someone up there," he whispered.
"I know," Vespera whispered back. She was close below him. He could feel the ladder move a little as she climbed. "It's Hollis. He knows we're here. He has known since we opened the door."
"How?"
"Because he wrote the floor. Every time someone steps on a line, he feels it. Like a spider feels the web shake. That's how the old ink-binders built their houses. Hollis learned it from me."
Corin hung there on the ladder in the dark, with his eyes shut and his heart beating hard.
"So he's waiting for us," he said.
"Yes."
"And we're climbing up to him anyway."
"Yes."
"Is this a good idea?"
There was a short pause. When Vespera spoke again, there was something in her voice that he had not heard before. It was not quite fear. It was older than fear, and more tired.
"No," she said. "But it is the only one we have. If he meant to kill us, he would have written a different line on the floor. He would have written the floor is ten feet to your left, and let us walk off the end of the loft into the yard below. He didn't. He wrote There is no ladder. He wanted to slow us down. He did not want to stop us. I think he wants to talk."
"Or he wants to have time to get ready."
"Or that," she agreed. "Climb."
He climbed.
Nine. Ten. Eleven.
His right hand reached up for the twelfth rung and found, instead, the edge of the trap door. It was a hard square edge of wood, and above it there was a flat floor, smooth and dry and warm. He felt along it with his fingers. There was something lying on the boards near the edge. It was small and soft. A cloth, folded.
He pulled himself up another rung. His head was close to the hole now. He could feel the warm air on his face, and the light was very bright through his eyelids.
"Don't open them yet," Vespera said from below.
"I know."
He climbed one more rung. Then one more. He felt his head rise up through the square of the trap door, into the warm room above. He felt his shoulders come through. He put both arms flat on the boards of the upper floor and held himself there, half in and half out, with his legs still on the ladder.
And as he did, something strange happened.
He felt the lie let go of him.
It was a very small feeling. It was like a thread being pulled out of a seam. It was like the moment in the morning when you wake up and the dream you were having slides away and you cannot remember it anymore. One moment the words There is no ladder were sitting in the back of his mind like a cold stone. The next moment the stone was gone. He did not know where it went. He only knew that it was gone, and that the space where it had been felt clean and light and empty.
He understood, without being told, why it had happened. He had touched the truth too many times. He had held the ladder in his hands. He had put his weight on it, rung after rung. He had climbed it all the way to the top. His body had told his mind, over and over, there is a ladder, there is a ladder, there is a ladder, and at last his mind had listened. The lie had nothing left to hold on to. It had slid off him like water off an oiled coat.
He looked down past his own shoulder, with his eyes still shut, and then, very carefully, he opened them a crack and looked at the ladder.
It was there.
It was an old wooden ladder, gray with age and black with tar, leaning against the edge of the trap door. Its rungs were worn pale in the middle. A small rag was tied around the top of one side, to stop it rubbing the wood of the floor. Far below, at the bottom, he could see Vespera's gray head and her pale stained hands gripping the sides. She was looking up at him.
"It's gone," he said. "The lie. I can see it now."
She nodded once.
"That is what the truth does," she said. "If you hold it long enough. Remember that, Corin. Remember it when you are in the next bad room. A lie can tell your eyes anything it likes. It cannot hold your hands forever."
He looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned his head and looked into the room above.
He had expected a workshop. He had expected a dark dirty place, full of tar and rope and danger. What he saw instead made him stop breathing for a moment.
The room was full of light.
There were lamps everywhere. There were lamps on the floor, and lamps on shelves, and lamps hanging from hooks in the low sloping roof. Some of them were good brass lamps with glass chimneys. Some of them were cheap tin lamps with cracked glass. Some of them were just saucers of oil with a wick floating in the middle. Their light was soft and yellow and very warm, and it filled every corner of the room, so that there were no shadows anywhere. And between the lamps, on every shelf, along every wall, stacked in rows that went right up to the roof, there were jars.
Hundreds of jars.
They were small glass jars, the kind that people keep jam in, or pickles, or honey. Each one had a cork in the top, sealed with a ring of red wax. Each one had a paper label tied around its neck with string. And inside each jar, something moved. Something soft and slow, like smoke, or like mist on a river in the early morning. Some of the mists were white. Some were gold. Some were a pale clear blue, like the sky in winter. Some were a deep warm red. They turned and drifted and curled inside the glass, and the lamplight shone through them, and threw soft colors onto the walls.
At the far end of the room, at a long table covered in papers and bottles and pens, a man sat with his back to the trap door. He was writing. Corin could hear the soft scratch of his pen.
He did not turn around.
"Mind the cloth," the man said, in a thin, dry, careful voice. "By your hand. I put it there for you. You'll want to wipe the tar off your palms before you touch anything in here. I don't want it on my jars."
Corin looked down. There was the folded cloth, lying on the boards near his right hand. It was clean and white.
Below him, on the ladder, Vespera had gone very still.
"Hello, Teacher," the man at the table said, still without turning. "You took your time."
