Chapter 23: Needle, Part 4 of 5: The First Lie
The First Lie
Corin carried the jar down the long room in both hands.
He walked slowly. He was afraid of dropping it. He was afraid of tripping on a board, or knocking his head on a beam, or catching his coat on the corner of a shelf. He had held a great many strange things in the last six years. He had held burnt-out Ash still warm from a dead mage's skull. He had held the cold glass vials in the Black Bank's vaults. He had held his own Ledger in the dark and read the names of things he would never get back. But he did not think he had ever held anything that felt as heavy as this small, cloudy, scratched old jar.
The gray mist inside did not move as he walked. It stayed curled up at the bottom of the glass. Only the faint silver light went through it, now and then, slow as breathing.
Vespera was standing where he had left her, beside Hollis's table. She watched him come. She did not hold out her hands. She did not say anything. Her face had gone very white under the dust, and her mouth was a thin hard line.
Corin stopped in front of her. He held the jar out.
She did not take it.
She looked at it. She looked at the label for a long time. At the old pig's skin, soft and yellow. At the child's large shaking letters, HOLLIS, HOLLIS, HOLLIS, crowded one on top of the other, the way a child writes when he is frightened and his hand will not stop. And at the four small words across the bottom.
"That's the skin," she said at last. Her voice was very low. "From the first week. The one I gave you to practice on."
"Yes," said Hollis.
"I threw it away. I remember throwing it away. In the bin by the back door."
"I took it out again. That night. After you went to bed." He was not looking at her. He was looking at his own clean hands, on the table. "I'd never written my name before. Not anywhere. Nobody had ever taught me. And I didn't want it to go in the bin with the potato peelings. So I took it out and dried it by the stove and kept it under my mattress. For twelve years."
Vespera closed her eyes for a moment.
"And the jar?" she said.
Hollis was quiet. When he spoke again, his voice had changed. It had gone careful and slow, the way a man's voice goes when he is walking across ice and does not know how thick it is.
"You remember the night I left," he said. "The man in the good coat. With the gold ring. And the purse."
"I remember."
"He told me what they wanted. One lie. On one man. He gave me the words on a piece of paper, and he gave me the purse, and he said a carriage would come for me at midnight at the end of Hook Lane, and I was to bring my needles and my ink and nothing else." Hollis turned the white pen between his fingers. "So I went upstairs to my room. Up the ladder. And I made the ink."
Corin understood then. He felt it go through him like cold water.
"You made it there," he said. "In her house."
"There was nowhere else." Hollis did not look up. "I sat on my mattress with the candle and the pot and I did it the way she'd taught me. The way she'd told me never to do. I reached into my own head and I took hold of a true memory, a real one, and I started to burn it down into the ink."
He stopped. His long fingers were very still on the pen.
"I didn't choose it," he said. "I want you to know that. I didn't sit there and think, I'll burn this one, this is the one I can spare. I just reached in, the way you reach into a bag in the dark, and took the first thing that came to my hand. That's how it's done. If you choose, you choose wrong. You pick the ones that don't matter, and the ink comes out thin and weak, and the lie won't hold. You have to let your hand pick. And your hand always picks the thing that matters most."
He looked up at Vespera at last.
"It was you," he said. "Something about you. I don't know what. I'll never know what. It was a memory of you, and it was a good one. That's all I know. I could feel it was good. It was warm, like a coal in my hand. And I started to burn it, and it wouldn't burn."
The lamps hissed softly. The clock ticked on the wall.
"It fought me," Hollis said. "I'd never felt anything like it. Not before, not since. Every other memory I've ever burned, it goes quiet when you start. Like a candle going out. But this one didn't. It held on. It pulled back. I could feel it trying to stay. And I sat there with my hand shaking and the ink pot in my lap and I thought, if I can't do this one, I can't do any of it. I'll have to give back the purse. I'll have to stay here, forever, at that table. And I pulled harder. And it tore."
He put the pen down on the table, very gently.
"Most of it went into the ink. Enough for one lie. That was the price, and I paid it. But a piece of it came loose. It tore off, the way a bit of cloth tears off on a nail. And it didn't go into the ink, and it didn't go back into me. It just… hung there. In the air. Over the candle. Like a bit of gray smoke. I could see it." He swallowed. "I'd never seen a memory before. Not outside a person. I didn't know you could."
"And you caught it," said Corin.
"There was an empty jar on the windowsill. She used to give me my jam in it. On Sundays." Hollis almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, thin and crooked and very sad. "I didn't think. I just grabbed it and held it up and the smoke went in. Like it wanted to. Like it was looking for somewhere to hide. And I jammed the cork in, and I sat there on the mattress holding it, and I shook so hard I thought I'd break the glass."
He nodded at the jar in Corin's hands.
"That's what's in there," he said. "The part of her I couldn't burn. That's how I learned it could be done, keeping them. By accident. That night. I spent the next seven years trying to learn to do it on purpose. But that one was the first, and it was luck, and it's the only one I didn't choose to keep. It chose."
He looked at the jar for a long moment. Then he looked away.
"I tied the skin round it before I went down the ladder," he said. "I don't know why. I suppose I wanted them to be together. My name, that she taught me to write. And the bit of her that wouldn't leave. Then I put it in my coat and went out to the end of Hook Lane, and the carriage came, and I never went back."
Vespera opened her eyes.
She reached out at last, and put her stained hands around the jar, over Corin's hands. Her fingers were cold. He let go, slowly, and she held it by herself. She lifted it a little, so that the light from the lamps came through the cloudy glass, and she looked at the gray mist sleeping at the bottom.
And Corin saw something.
It was very small. He nearly missed it. He had spent six years learning to see the small things that came off memory, the faint warmth that came off Ash, the colors that hung around a mage's head after a spell. His eyes were trained to it, the way a hunter's eyes are trained to the movement of a deer in long grass. That was why he saw it at all.
When Vespera lifted the jar, her left sleeve slid back an inch. And on the inside of her wrist, where Corin knew there were four old faded letters and a line, the letters she had written fifty years ago and could never finish, the silver light in the mist went bright.
Not very bright. Just a little. Just for a moment. Like a sleeping face that hears its name and almost wakes. Then it faded again, and the mist lay still.
Vespera did not see it. She was looking at the gray mist, not at her own wrist. Hollis did not see it either. He was looking at the floor.
Corin opened his mouth to speak. Then he shut it again. He did not know why. It was not his to say. Not yet. Not here, in this room, with the clock ticking and the lamps hissing and the old woman holding the jar as if it might break her heart.
He made a note in his head instead, the way he had learned to do when there was no time for the Ledger. The gray jar. Her wrist. The four letters. The light went bright. He would write it down later. He would write it down the moment he had a pencil and a minute alone.
"What is it?" Vespera said. "The memory. Do you know? Even a little?"
"No," said Hollis. "I told you. I can't remember it. That's the price. I only know it was you. And it was good."
"You've never opened it."
"No."
"Ten years, and you never opened it."
"No." He looked up at her. "Would you?"
Vespera said nothing.
"If I opened it," Hollis said, "I think it would go back into me. That's what I've always thought. It would go back where it came from, and I would remember. Whatever it is. Whatever we had. And then I'd know what I'd sold, Vespera. I'd know exactly what it was I gave away for a fat purse and a white shirt. And I don't think I could do the work after that. I don't think I could ever write another lie." He shook his head slowly. "So I kept it shut. And every night I came up here and sat with it, and I didn't open it. For ten years."
"And now you're giving it to me."
"Yes."
"Why?"
Hollis looked at her for a long time. In the warm light of the lamps, his thin face looked very tired, and very much older than thirty-four.
"Because after this morning," he said, "I don't think I'll be able to sit with it anymore. I think after this morning, I won't deserve to."
Vespera lowered the jar slowly. She held it against her chest, against the black wool of her coat, in both hands, the way a woman holds a small sleeping child.
"I won't open it either," she said. "Not now. Maybe not ever. It's not mine to open. It's half yours."
"Keep it anyway."
"I'll keep it." She looked at him over the top of it. Her small dark eyes were wet, but her voice was steady again. It was her shop voice. Her table voice. The voice she used on clients who had come in with a bad idea. "Now. You said you were told to do something this morning. Something you don't want to do. Tell me what it is. All of it. And don't leave anything out, boy, because I will know."
Hollis drew a long breath.
Then he leaned forward, and pulled a sheet of paper out from under the stack in front of him, and laid it flat on the table where they could both see it.
"This," he said, "is what I'm meant to write."
Monday's Work
The paper was cream-colored, thick and soft, the kind of paper that costs more for one sheet than a docker earns in a day. Corin knew it the moment he saw it. He had held a card made of the same paper in Madame Hesper's shop, not two weeks ago. A small stiff card with gold edges, and a name in the corner in tall thin writing, and a curled capital C with a hook at the top like a shepherd's crook.
There was no name on this sheet. There was no C. But there was a smell coming up off it, faint and sweet and heavy, and Corin knew that smell too.
Lilies.
He looked at Vespera. She had smelled it as well. Her mouth had gone thin.
The writing on the paper was in black ink. It was very plain writing, plainer than any Corin had ever seen. Every letter was exactly the same size as every other letter. Every line was exactly the same length. There were no loops, no curls, no little flourishes at the ends of words. It looked less like a person's writing than like a row of fence posts. It was the kind of hand a clerk uses when he has been told to copy something so that nobody could ever say whose hand it was.
"Read it," said Hollis. "It's only ordinary ink. It won't do anything to you."
Corin bent over the table and read it. Vespera read it beside him. He could hear her breathing, slow and careful.
I, Aurelian Thorne, being of sound mind, remember the following, and know it to be true.
I remember that on the first day of the Frost Month, at my house in Crown Walk, I gave to my dear sister Cressida, of my own free will, all my papers, all my lands, all my rights in the Thorne name, and the keeping of my children.
I remember that I signed. I remember the pen. I remember that I was glad.
I remember that I have two children only. A son and a daughter. I remember there were never any others.
I remember that I have nothing hidden. Nothing folded. Nothing kept. I remember that there is nothing more to find.
I remember that my sister has always been kind to me.
At the bottom, set a little apart from the rest, there was one more line.
Write it over the old word.
Corin read it all twice. Then he straightened up slowly. His back was cold. He could feel his heart beating in his ears.
"The old word," he said.
"On his wrist," said Vespera. Her voice was flat and quiet and very dangerous. "FOLD. I wrote it there myself. Eleven years ago. At his own asking. He came to me late at night, alone, in a plain coat, and sat in my chair and held out his arm and said, one word, Vespera, small, where it won't be seen. And I wrote it, and it's held ever since. Through the burning. Through everything." She looked at Hollis. "She wants you to write over it."
"Yes."
"With this."
"Yes."
"Do you know what it will do?"
Hollis did not answer at once. He reached out and touched the corner of the cream paper with one clean fingertip. He moved it a little, so that it lined up with the edge of the table.
"I know what it says," he said at last. "I know what it'll make him believe. That he gave everything away. That he's glad of it. That there's nothing hidden. That there were only ever two children." He paused. "And I know that whatever's under that word on his wrist, whatever he folded down there before they burned him, if I write this over it, it won't be folded anymore. It'll be gone. The lie will sit on top of it like a stone on a grave. He'll never find it again. Nobody will."
Corin thought of Lord Thorne.
He thought of the old man in the white room at Pruett's livery stable, propped up on his pillows, with his thin white hands lying on the blanket. He thought of the way those hands moved sometimes, tapping, tapping, on the wall and on the bed and on the back of Lyra's hand. Four. Two. Nine. Three. He thought of the way Lord Thorne's lips had moved when Corin tapped it back to him, all those days ago in Lyra's rooms. The way they had pressed together and pushed out, slowly, making a shape. W.
He thought of the height chart on the wall of the empty townhouse. The third set of marks, gouged out. The W. The little oak leaf.
I remember that I have two children only. I remember there were never any others.
"She's going to make him forget," Corin said. "The other child. The one he hid."
Hollis looked up at him quickly. His sharp dark eyes went narrow.
"You know about that," he said.
"Some of it."
"More than I do, then. I don't know anything about any child. I don't want to know. I'm not paid to know." Hollis picked up the white pen again and turned it. Faster now. "I'm paid to copy what's on the page. That's all I've ever done. Somebody writes the words, and they give them to me, and I put them in. Every comma. Every letter."
"Who writes the words?" said Vespera.
"I've never been told."
"But you know."
Hollis was quiet.
"You know," said Vespera again. "This paper. This smell. You know whose it is."
"I know whose servant brings it," Hollis said. "That's not the same thing."
"Tell me."
"A big man. Very big. He eats meat pies out of a paper bag, and he never wipes his fingers. He smells of beer and onions. He came on Saturday night, with this, and a purse, and a time." Hollis let out a thin breath. "Today. Noon. A carriage will come for the old man from a house in the country, a place called Fallowfield. It'll take him there. And I'm to go out to Fallowfield this afternoon, by the back road, in a cart, and wait in a room upstairs until they bring him to me. And then I'm to write it. All of it. On his arm. Over the old word."
"Gorse," said Corin softly.
Hollis looked at him.
"The big man," said Corin. "His name is Gorse. He works for Councillor Cressida Thorne. He's the one who held my friend Pell down by the river, nearly two weeks ago, while the lady in the white mask stood by. And he's the one who put Doctor Aske in the river after. I've seen him hold Pell. I've eaten it. It's in my dead friend's Ash, in my head, right now." He heard his own voice going hard and cold, and he did not stop it. "That's who's paying you, Hollis. That's whose gold bought your white shirts this time."
Hollis said nothing for a long moment.
The clock ticked. The lamps hissed. In their jars along the walls, the soft mists turned and drifted, gold and white and blue and red, all the true things Hollis Marsh had ever paid away.
"I said I don't kill people," Hollis said at last. Quietly. "That's my rule. I've never broken it."
"This is worse than killing him," said Vespera.
"I know." He put down the pen. "I know it is. That's why I'm still sitting here. That's why I didn't run." He looked at the paper. "When I first read this, on Saturday night, I thought, it's only one more. Only one more lie on one more man. I've done hundreds. What's one more? And I sat down to make the ink, the way I always do. And I reached in for a true thing to burn."
He stopped.
"And?" said Vespera.
"And my hand went to the top shelf," said Hollis. "Not in my head. Here. In this room. My real hand. I was sitting at this table and I looked up and my hand was on the shelf by the green lamp, on your jar, and I didn't remember getting up." He spread his long clean fingers on the table and looked at them as if they belonged to someone else. "Hands remember what they want to remember. That's what I told your boy, there, about my g. It's true. My hand knew. Before I did. It knew that if I wrote this, I would have to let go of the jar. That I couldn't sit with it anymore. That this was the one that would be too far."
He looked up at Vespera.
"So I didn't make the ink," he said. "Not on Saturday. Not on Sunday. I sat here with the paper and the clock, and I waited. I knew you'd come. Sooner or later. After the girl. After you drew my ink out of her. I knew you'd come up those stairs and stand there and call me a fool." Something moved in his thin face again, that small quick thing that was not quite a smile. "I wrote that line on the floor, there is no ladder, so I'd have time to put the kettle on. That's all. I was going to make you tea."
Vespera stared at him.
Then, to Corin's great surprise, she laughed.
It was a short, rough, startled sound, like a crow calling. She put one stained hand over her mouth to stop it, and it came out anyway, between her fingers. It was the first time Corin had ever heard her laugh. In six years. He had not known she could.
"Tea," she said.
"There's a pot on the stove," said Hollis, "behind you. I didn't get to light it. You were very quick on the rope."
Vespera shook her head slowly. She lowered her hand. Her eyes were wet again, but she was not crying. Her face looked different. Softer. Ten years younger.
"Then you won't do it," she said. "The old man. Fallowfield. You won't write it."
"No," said Hollis. "I won't."
"They'll kill you."
"Probably."
"Then come with us. Now. Tonight."
Hollis looked at her. Then he looked past her, at the shelves. At the hundreds of small jars, each with its brown label, each with its soft turning mist. All the true things he had lost. All the things he had kept.
"I can't carry them," he said. "Not all of them. Not quickly."
"Then leave them."
"I can't do that either."
"Hollis—"
Corin was not listening anymore.
He had turned his head. He was looking down the long room, toward the trap door, toward the square hole in the floor with the top of the old ladder sticking up out of it. Something had changed. He did not know what. Some small thing. A sound. A smell.
Then he knew.
The light coming up through the trap door was the wrong color. It had been dark down there, in the long room below, when they climbed up. Dark as the inside of a sack. Now there was a light. It was coming up through the square hole and throwing itself onto the beams of the roof above. It was orange, and it moved. It jumped and flickered and danced.
And there was a smell coming up with it. Thick and black and bitter.
The smell of burning tar.
