Chapter 23: Needle, Part 5 of 5: Smoke
Smoke
"Fire," said Corin.
He said it quite quietly. He did not shout. Afterwards he could not understand why. It was as if his voice had not yet caught up with what his eyes and nose were telling him. He said it the way a man might say rain, looking out of a window.
Then the smoke came up through the trap door, and his voice caught up.
"Fire!"
The smoke came up thick and black and rolling. It did not drift, the way smoke from a chimney drifts. It poured. It came up through the square hole in the floor like water coming up through a hole in the bottom of a boat. It boiled up against the low roof and spread out along the beams, black and greasy, and the lamps hanging there went dim and orange inside it.
Corin ran to the trap door.
He got there in four steps, with his head bent under the roof. He dropped to his knees beside the hole and looked down. The heat hit him in the face like an open oven. His eyes watered at once. Through the smoke, through the water in his eyes, he could see the long room below.
It was burning.
The rope-walk was a long, long building, and every bit of it was made of things that wanted to burn. The floor was old dry boards. The walls were old dry boards. And all along both sides, on the racks and pegs and wheels, there was rope. Coils of it. Heaps of it. Old tarred rope, black and sticky, that had been sitting in the dark for years soaking up tar. And somebody had poured oil on it. Corin could smell the oil under the tar smell, sharp and thin, like lamp oil. The fire had started at the far end, by the door, and it was running down both sides of the long room toward him, along the racks of rope, faster than a man could run. The flames were orange and yellow, and at their tips they were black with smoke.
And the floor was burning too. The floor, with all its small black lines of false ink. He saw the lines curl up and blister in the heat. He saw some of them glow for a moment, a strange cold silver, before they went black. He did not read them. He did not need to. He did not think anyone would ever read them again.
The ladder was gone.
It had been standing under the trap door when they climbed up. Now there was nothing there. He looked down through the smoke and saw it lying on the floor, a little way off, on its side. Somebody had pulled it away. It was already starting to burn at one end.
And beside it, at the bottom of the hole, looking up, there was a man.
He was very big. He was so big that for a moment, through the smoke, Corin thought he was two men standing close together. He had a thick neck and a round red face and small eyes. He wore a long brown coat with the collar turned up, and a flat cap pulled down low. In one hand he held a torch, a stick of wood with rags tied round the end, dipped in tar and blazing. In the other hand he held a paper bag. As Corin watched, he put his hand into the bag and took out the last bit of a meat pie, and put it in his mouth, and chewed. Then he crumpled the bag and dropped it into the fire.
"Evening, Needle," Gorse called up. His voice was deep and slow and almost friendly. "Lady says you talk too much."
He looked at Corin. His small eyes did not change at all.
"And she says the Ash-eater's to stay up there with you. Which is handy. Saves me a walk."
Corin's hand went to his coat. To the rod. And then to the other side, to Big Harl's long knife, the one he had taken at the Bell. He did not know what he meant to do with either of them. Throw it, maybe. He had never thrown a knife in his life.
But Gorse was already turning away. He walked off through the smoke, slow and heavy, not hurrying at all, toward the burning door at the far end. Corin saw his broad back go small and dark against the orange light. Then the smoke closed over him and he was gone.
"Shut it!" Vespera said behind him. "Corin! The trap! Shut it!"
There was a lid. He had not noticed it before. It was lying flat on the floor beside the hole, a square of thick boards with iron hinges on one side and an iron ring on the other. He grabbed the ring. It was already hot. He heaved the lid up and over and let it fall. It came down over the hole with a great flat bang, and the smoke that was coming up was cut off at once, the way water is cut off when you turn a tap.
But it was not enough. There was smoke in the room already. A great deal of it. It hung under the roof in a thick black cloud, and it was coming down, lower and lower, as more and more of it pressed in. And it was coming in through the cracks now. Between the boards of the floor. Up through the gaps around the edge of the trap door lid. Thin gray threads of it, curling up everywhere, like steam from a kettle.
And the floor was getting warm under his knees.
He stood up, coughing. He bent low, under the smoke, where the air was still a little clearer. His eyes were streaming. He could hardly see.
"Is there another way down?" he said.
"No," said Hollis.
Hollis was standing in the middle of the room. He had not moved at all. He stood under the low roof with his arms hanging at his sides, and he was looking at the shelves.
The jars were starting to crack.
Corin heard the first one go. It made a small sharp sound, like a twig snapping. It was one of the jars on the lowest shelf, near the floor, where the heat was coming up through the boards. The thick green glass split straight down one side, and a thread of gold mist came out of the crack. It came out slowly. It hung in the air for a moment, just above the shelf. And then it was caught by the moving air, and pulled toward the smoke, and it was gone.
As it went past him, Corin smelled bread.
Warm bread, with a crust on it, just out of an oven. And flour. And a kitchen with a stone floor. And for one moment, less than a moment, he saw two hands. A woman's hands, red and rough, with the sleeves pushed up to the elbow. They were deep in a big brown bowl of dough, pressing and folding and turning it, pressing and folding and turning. There was flour on her wrists. There was a thin gold ring on one finger, almost hidden in the dough.
Then it was gone.
He heard Hollis make a sound. It was not a word. It was the sound a man makes when he has been hit very hard in the stomach.
My mother's hands in bread dough.
Another jar cracked. And another. And then three at once, on the shelf on the other side, with a sound like small stones dropped on a stone floor. The mists came out of them, white and blue and red, and went drifting up toward the smoke, and as each one went past him Corin caught something. A smell. A sound. A flash of color.
Salt wind, and gulls, and a gray sea going on forever under a gray sky.
A man singing, in a cracked voice, a slow song about a gray horse that came home in the snow.
A blue door with a brass knocker. A dog barking somewhere behind it.
A small boy laughing. A spinning top, red and yellow, going round and round on a wooden floor.
They went past him like birds. Like birds let out of a cage. He could not hold any of them. He did not try. They were not his. They went up into the black smoke under the roof, and they were gone.
"Hollis," said Vespera. "Hollis. We have to go."
Hollis did not move.
He was watching them go. All of them. His long thin face was quite still, and in the orange light coming through the smoke it looked like a face carved out of wax. His eyes were open very wide. He was not blinking.
"I can't remember them," he said. His voice was very quiet. Corin could barely hear it over the roar of the fire below. "I can't remember any of them. But I knew they were here. I knew they were somewhere."
Another jar cracked. A pale green mist came out. It smelled of grass after rain.
"Now they're nowhere," Hollis said.
Vespera crossed the room to him in three quick steps. She still had the gray jar held against her chest in one arm. With her other hand she took hold of his coat again. The front of it, the way she had before. But this time she did pull. Hard. He was so thin that he came forward at once, almost falling.
"They are not nowhere," she said. Her face was very close to his. Her voice was low and hard and furious. "They are out. That is all. They are out of their jars, and they are in the air, and they will go where memory goes when it is free. Into the wind. Into the river. Into the dreams of people asleep in this city. Some child in a cellar on Cinder Lane will dream of a woman making bread tonight, and wake up happy, and never know why. That is not nowhere. That is better than a shelf."
Hollis looked down at her. His eyes were wet. His mouth was shaking.
"Now move, boy," said Vespera. "Or I will drag you."
He moved.
It was as if something had let go inside him. He turned, quickly, and went to his long table. He did not take the pens. He did not take the ink. He pulled open a drawer under the table, and took out a book, a small, thick, battered book bound in brown leather, with a strap around it, and pushed it into the front of his coat. Corin knew without being told what it was. It was the list. The list of everything Hollis Marsh had ever paid.
Then Hollis looked at the cream-colored paper still lying on the table. The paper that smelled of lilies.
Corin got to it first.
He did not think about it. His hand just went out and took it, and folded it in half, and in half again, and put it inside his own coat, in the pocket next to his Ledger. Proof. Real proof, in somebody's hand, of what the lady at No. 11 Crown Walk meant to do to her own brother. He did not know yet who he would show it to. But he was not going to let it burn.
Hollis watched him do it. He did not try to stop him.
"Good," he said. "Keep that. I'd rather they knew it was gone than thought it burned."
The smoke was very low now. It was down past the tops of the shelves. Corin had to bend almost double to get under it, and even down there the air was hot and thick and tasted of tar. He was coughing so hard he could hardly breathe. His eyes were burning. The floor under his boots was hot. He could feel it through the soles.
Somewhere below them, something gave way with a long, slow, tearing crash. The whole loft shook. The lamps on their hooks swung wildly, throwing their light about. Three more jars fell off a high shelf and smashed on the boards, and their mists went up, gold and gold and white.
"The window," Hollis gasped. "The gable. At the end. Behind the green lamp."
Corin turned.
At the far end of the room, where he had found the gray jar, where the roof came down lowest, there was a small square of darkness in the wall. He had not seen it before, because the green lamp had been in front of it, and the green lamp's light had made everything around it hard to see. It was a window. A small one, hardly bigger than a cupboard door, with four little panes of dirty glass. And through the glass, very faint, he could see the night sky. Not black anymore. Dark gray. The color of old pewter.
It was nearly dawn.
He ran for it, bent double, coughing, with the hot floor under his feet and the smoke pressing down on him and the roar of the fire getting louder and louder underneath. He knocked the green lamp off its shelf with his arm as he got there. It fell and broke on the boards, and the oil ran out of it, and caught.
He did not look back. He got both hands on the window frame. It was stuck. Of course it was stuck. It was an old window in an old wall, and nobody had opened it in years, and the wood had swollen tight.
He pulled out Big Harl's long knife, and turned it round, and smashed the glass with the heavy handle.
Cold air came in. Wonderful cold air, wet and sharp and smelling of the river. He put his face to the hole and breathed it in, great gulps of it, and for a moment that was all he could do.
Then, from just outside the window, very close, so close that he nearly fell over backward with fright, a voice said:
"Took you long enough."
Out by the Gable
It was Juno.
She was crouched on the roof just outside the window, on the slope of old gray slates, with one hand on the stone edge of the gable to hold herself steady. Her face was black with soot. Her short hair was full of ash. The brass key on its string had swung out from under her collar and was hanging in front of her, turning slowly, catching the orange light from inside. NIGHT DOOR III. She was breathing hard, as if she had been running, and her eyes were very wide and very bright.
"Juno," Corin said. It came out as a croak. "How did you—"
"Drainpipe," she said. "Round the back. Big one. Iron. It's got brackets every few feet, like a ladder, if you don't mind them being rusty." She coughed and spat over the edge of the roof. "I saw him coming, Corin. The big one. With two others. They had cans. I whistled the warning, like you said, but you were inside with the doors shut and I don't think you heard. And then they were at the door, and I couldn't get past them, so I went round the back and up." Her voice shook, just a little. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry I didn't stop them."
"You couldn't have stopped them," said Corin. "Nobody could. You did right."
"Is she in there? Vespera?"
"She's here. Hold the edge. We're coming out."
He turned back into the smoke. He could hardly see anything now. The room was full of it, black and thick, with the orange light of the burning green-lamp oil glowing through it near the floor. He could hear the fire below roaring, a deep, steady, terrible sound, like a great wind blowing through a narrow street. He could hear jars cracking, one after another after another, a sound like ice breaking on a pond. And he could feel the floor. It was hot, really hot now, so hot he could feel it burning through the soles of his boots.
"Vespera!"
"Here." Her voice was close. She came out of the smoke bent almost double, with the gray jar held tight against her chest in one arm and Hollis's sleeve in her other hand. Hollis came behind her, stooping, coughing, with one long arm over his face. "Is it open?"
"It's open. Juno's outside. Go."
The window was very small. Vespera was small, but even so it was hard. Corin took the jar from her, carefully, and held it while she put her head and one arm out through the broken frame. Juno caught her hand from outside. Corin pushed from inside. There was a sound of cloth tearing, and Vespera said a word that Corin had never heard her say before and would never hear her say again. And then she was through, out onto the roof, crouched beside Juno on the slates.
Corin handed the jar out to her through the window. She took it in both hands and held it to her chest.
"Now you," Corin said to Hollis.
Hollis looked at the window. Then he looked back over his shoulder, at the room. At the smoke. At the shelves he could no longer see, and the jars he could hear breaking, one by one, in the dark.
"Hollis," said Corin. "There's nothing left to save. Go."
Hollis looked at him. For a moment his thin face was very close to Corin's, so close that Corin could see the red rims of his eyes and the soot in the lines of his skin. His quick dark eyes moved over Corin's face, the way they had done when he first turned around on his stool. Reading him. And then something in them changed.
"You've got a lot of holes in you," Hollis said. "I can see them. Like a shirt that's been mended too many times. You've paid more than I have, I think. And you never kept any of it."
Corin said nothing.
"Be careful, Ash-eater," said Hollis softly. "One day there won't be enough cloth left to hold the patches together."
Then he turned, and folded his long thin body up like a carpenter's rule, and went out through the window as easily as a cat.
Corin went last.
It was the hardest thing he had done all night. Harder than the rope in the dark. Harder than the ladder he could not see. The window frame was full of broken glass and his coat caught on it and tore, and a piece of glass cut the back of his hand, and the smoke came pouring out of the hole all around him so that for a long moment he could not see or breathe at all. He felt hands grab his arm. Juno's small hard hands, and Vespera's cold stained ones. They pulled. He came out onto the roof in a rush of black smoke and fell on his knees on the wet slates, coughing so hard he thought he would bring up his heart.
When he could lift his head, he looked around.
They were high up. Much higher than he had thought. The rope-walk was a long building, and the loft was at the very end of it, and the gable end stood up over Fuller's Yard like the bow of a ship. Below them, a long way down, the yard was a square of frozen mud, gray in the gray light. On the far side of it he could see the row of old wooden sheds, and the dye stream with its little bridge, and the backs of the tall black buildings beyond.
And there were people coming. Lots of people. He could see them pouring out of doors and alleys into the yard. Men in their shirts and nightcaps. Women with shawls thrown over their heads. A boy with a bucket. Somewhere, a long way off, a bell had started to ring. It was the fire bell from the Watch house in Saint Bride's, clang, clang, clang, fast and frightened.
The smoke was going up from the rope-walk in a great black column. It went straight up into the still morning air, higher than the roofs, higher than the bell towers, and at the top it spread out flat like the head of a mushroom. Under it, the long roof of the rope-walk was glowing. The slates were cracking in the heat, with sharp popping sounds, one after another. Here and there flames had come up through the holes and were dancing along the ridge.
And the sky in the east was turning pale.
Not gold yet. Not pink. Just pale. A thin cold gray, like water, low down over the roofs beyond the river. It was nearly dawn. Corin looked at it and felt something tighten in his chest. First light. Gant's men were coming back to Pruett's livery stable at first light, with the whole downstairs.
"We can't go down there," said Juno. She was looking at the yard. "Too many people. Too many eyes. Somebody'll remember us."
"No," said Corin. "Across."
The rope-walk was not standing on its own. On its left side, close enough to touch, there was a warehouse, a big square ugly building with a flat lead roof. Its edge was only a short step from the gable where they stood, across a narrow gap of empty air. Beyond the warehouse there was a row of houses, tall and thin, with steep slate roofs and crowds of chimney pots. And beyond those, the roofs went on and on, roof after roof after roof, gray and wet and shining, all the way to the river.
"I know the way," said Juno. She was already standing up. She stood on the wet slope of the roof as easily as if it were a pavement. "I came this way on Thursday, when I was running your messages. You can get all the way to the coal wharf without touching the ground, if you know where the planks are."
"Then lead," said Vespera.
Juno led.
She went first, across the gap to the warehouse roof, in one long step, without even looking down. Vespera went next. She was slower, and more careful, with the jar held tight in the crook of one arm, but she did not hesitate. Corin went after her. He did look down. He wished he had not. The gap was only a stride wide, but at the bottom of it, a long way down, there was an alley full of broken barrels and rubbish, and a cat looking up at him with green eyes.
He stepped across.
The lead roof was flat and wet and very cold. It was the coldest thing he had ever felt after the heat of the loft. He stood on it, breathing hard, and turned round to help Hollis across.
Hollis was not there.
Corin stared. The gable stood empty behind him. The broken window was pouring out smoke. The slates were glowing. But there was no thin dark figure crouched on them.
"Hollis!"
"There," said Juno. She pointed.
He was on the other side of the rope-walk. The right side. Corin did not know how he had got there. He must have gone over the ridge of the roof while they were looking at the warehouse, quick and quiet in the smoke. Now he was crouched on the edge of a different roof, a low tiled shed on the far side, a long way off already. As they watched, he stood up. He looked back at them across the burning roof, through the smoke. He was too far away for Corin to see his face. Only his shape. Long and thin, and very dark against the pale sky.
He lifted one hand.
It was not quite a wave. It was more like the way a man lifts his hand to someone across a crowded street, to say I see you, or I'm going now, or don't follow. Then he turned, and dropped down off the edge of the shed roof into the alley below, and was gone.
Nobody said anything for a long moment.
"Will he be all right?" said Juno at last. Her voice was small.
"He'll be all right," said Vespera. She was looking at the place where he had gone. Her face was very still. "He was always all right. That was the trouble with him. He always landed on his feet." She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, more softly, "He'll go to ground. Somewhere far. He knows they'll be looking. He'll change his name and cut his hair and he won't write a word for a year. And then one day I'll walk into some shop in some town I've never heard of and see a g with a hook on it on a sign over the door, and I'll know."
She looked down at the jar in her arms. The gray mist lay still and curled at the bottom of it, sleeping.
"Are you going to open it?" said Corin.
"No."
"Not ever?"
"Not today," said Vespera. And she unwound the black wool shawl from her shoulders, and wrapped the jar up in it, slowly and carefully, the way you would wrap a baby against the cold. Round and round. Until there was nothing to see but a bundle of black wool. Then she held it against her chest and did not look at it again.
Corin looked at her wrist. The sleeve had fallen down over it. He could not see the four letters. He thought of the silver light going bright in the mist. He did not say anything.
Below them, in Fuller's Yard, somebody screamed. There was a long, slow, grinding roar, and the whole gable end of the rope-walk leaned outward, and then, very slowly, fell in on itself. A great cloud of sparks went up into the morning. The loft was gone. The table. The clock. The lamps. The shelves. All of it, falling down into the fire in the long room below.
Corin watched it go. He thought of the bread dough. The gray sea at Gullwick. The song about the gray horse. The blue door. The small boy and the red-and-yellow top. All of them out now, in the wind, going wherever memory goes when it is free.
He hoped Vespera was right. He hoped some child would dream of bread tonight.
Then he turned his back on the fire and looked east. At the pale gray line low over the roofs. At the coming day.
His hand went to his coat pocket. The Ledger was there. And the folded cream-colored paper beside it, smelling faintly of lilies. And Big Harl's long knife in his belt. And the rod.
Monday, he thought. Pruett's. First light. Gant's men. The whole downstairs. Nine o'clock, the Ministry carriage. Noon, the carriage from Fallowfield.
And Lord Thorne in his bed, with the word FOLD on his wrist, and nobody but Oren and Lyra and Mrs. Hobb to stand in front of him.
"Juno," he said. "How fast can you get us to Linden Walk?"
Juno looked at him. Then she looked at the sky. Then she grinned, a sudden white grin in her soot-black face.
"Over the roofs?" she said. "Faster than a horse."
"Then go."
They went. Across the lead roof of the warehouse, and along a plank that somebody had laid across the gap to the next roof, and up the steep wet slates of the first of the tall thin houses, with the chimney pots standing round them like a crowd of gray men in tall hats. Juno in front, light and quick as a bird. Vespera in the middle, with her bundle held tight. Corin at the back, with his cut hand bleeding and his lungs full of smoke and his heart going like a drum.
Behind them, the column of black smoke went up and up into the pale sky over Fuller's Yard, and the fire bell went on ringing, and ringing, and ringing.
Ahead of them, the sun was coming.
