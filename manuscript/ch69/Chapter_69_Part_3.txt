Chapter 69: Fire at the Edge, Part 3 of 5: They Walk In
They Walk In
Arran came down into the firelight at a run and did not stop. He went straight past the sentries, who had turned to face the slope with their spears lowered, and straight past the first tents, and he was shouting now, at last, one word over and over in the round Ashborn speech, and the camp came awake all at once like a kicked hive.
People came out of the tents. Men with spears. Women with babies. Children, half asleep, in their furs. Goats bleating in the pen. Somebody threw more thorn on the big fire and it went up roaring and yellow and lit the whole camp, and the faces, and the tents with their gray handprints, and the thorn trees, and the first long stretch of the slope above.
And Lyra saw them.
They came out of the thorns at the bottom of the slope, where the goat path came down. Not running. Not hurrying at all. Walking. At the pace of a man walking down a street. In a line, one beside the other, ten paces apart, as neat as soldiers on parade.
Four of them.
They were tall. As tall as Oren. Man-high, and broader than any man. Man-shaped, with two legs and two arms and a body and a head. But not men. Not anything like men. Lyra knew that from the first moment, before she could see them properly, from the way they moved. Too smooth. Too even. Every step exactly the same length as the last. No sway. No swing of the arms. Nothing. Just walking. Steadily. Down the slope. Through the thorns.
The thorns did not stop them.
They did not go round them. They went through. Straight through the thick bushes, with the long gray spines tearing at their sides and their arms and their shoulders, and the branches snapping off them with sharp dry cracks, one after another, crack, crack, crack, like twigs on a fire. They did not slow down. They did not seem to notice. They walked through the thorn bushes the way a man walks through long grass.
And they ticked.
Close now. Loud. A steady, hard, patient ticking, coming out of all four of them, all in time. Tick. Tick. Tick. And under the ticking, other sounds. A whirring. A grinding. A long soft click, every few steps, like a key turning in a lock. The sounds of something wound up, and going.
They came into the firelight.
They were made of iron. Dark iron, plates of it, overlapping, like the scales of a fish or the plates on a beetle's back, black in the firelight and dull, with rust in the joins. And at the joins, where the plates met, at the shoulders and the elbows and the knees and the neck, brass. Bright brass, polished, shining gold in the fire. Wheels and rods and pins. You could see them move, through the gaps between the plates, turning, sliding, as the legs went forward and back.
Their hands were not hands. They were three fingers each, long and flat and blunt, like the blades of a shovel, folded together.
And their heads.
Their heads were blank.
Smooth ovals of dark iron, the shape of an egg, with no face. No eyes. No nose. No mouth. Nothing. Except one thing. A slit. A single narrow slit across the front of each head, where the eyes would have been, as wide as a finger. And in the slit, as they came into the light of the fire, something glinted. Glass, perhaps. Or brass. A small hard shine, moving a little, from side to side, as the heads turned.
As they looked.
They stopped at the edge of the camp. All four at once. Between one tick and the next. In their line, ten paces apart, with the snapped thorn branches still hanging off their shoulders.
The ticking did not stop. It went on. Tick. Tick. Tick. Waiting.
Nobody in the camp moved.
Lyra had her sword out. She did not remember drawing it. It was in her hand, and her feet were set, and Corin was behind her, on his crutch, and Oren was in front of her and to one side, with his own sword up, and she could hear him breathing.
And from somewhere behind the four iron shapes, out of the dark, up the slope, among the thorns, a voice spoke.
It was soft. That was the first thing. Soft and polite and very clear, the voice of a well-brought-up man in a drawing room asking someone to pass the salt. It did not shout. It did not need to. It carried all across the silent camp.
It said one word.
Lyra did not understand it. It was not a word in any language she knew. It was a sound. A soft, worn, old sound, like a word that has been said so many times that it has lost its edges, like a coin rubbed smooth.
But the four iron shapes understood it.
Their heads turned. All together. All four of them, at the same moment, the blank iron ovals swinging round, slow and smooth, with a soft whirr of brass. Away from the crowd. Away from the fire. Away from the spears and the children and the screaming goats.
Toward the north side of the camp.
Toward the last tent before the ridge, with the six or seven old faded handprints on it and the one new small one.
Corin's tent.
And the ticking changed. Got faster. Louder. Tick-tick-tick-tick.
And they walked.
Fire
The second one took the lantern.
It was hanging on a pole outside the first tent they passed, at the south edge of the camp, an ordinary clay oil lamp in a little cage of bent thorn, the kind every tent had by its door at night. The Eraser did not stop walking. It did not turn its head. Its long iron arm came up and out, smooth, with a whirr of brass, and the three flat fingers closed on the cage and lifted the lamp off its pole, the way you might pick an apple off a branch as you walked past a tree.
And swung it.
Not hard. Not fast. A slow, easy, sideways swing, the whole length of the iron arm. Into the side of the tent.
The clay lamp broke against the hide. Lyra heard it. A small dull crack. And the oil came out in a splash, burning, and ran down the side of the tent in a sheet of yellow flame.
The hide caught at once.
It was old. It was dry. It had been stretched over that frame of bent thorn for years and years and smoked from inside by a thousand fires, and it went up like paper. One moment there was a splash of flame on the side of it. The next the whole side of the tent was burning, and the flames were going up over the curve of the roof to the smoke hole, and the gray handprints on it were curling and blackening and vanishing in the heat.
Someone screamed inside.
The flap burst open and a woman came out with two children, one under each arm, and fell on the gravel, and got up, and ran.
The Eraser walked on. It still had the lamp cage in its fingers. It dropped it, without looking, and walked on.
And the wind came.
Lyra had not felt any wind all evening. The night had been still, the smoke going straight up. But now, with the first tent burning, a wind came. From the south. Down the thorn slope from the Lip, cold and steady. As if the burning had called it. It caught the flames from the first tent and laid them over, sideways, toward the north, toward the next tent along. Long yellow tongues of fire, licking out flat across the gap.
The next tent caught.
And the next.
It went along the south edge of the camp like a fuse burning. Tent after tent. The flames leaping the gaps between them on the wind. The hides going up one after another with a soft heavy roar, like a great breath let out. The bent-thorn frames showing black through the flames like the ribs of an animal. The bone charms on their strings bursting with little cracks in the heat. The smoke going up, thick and black and choking, and the wind taking it and laying it over the camp, so that everything went dark and red at once.
And the smell. Burning hide and burning hair and burning fat. And under it, sweet and wrong, the burnt-honey herb in the tents, going up all at once.
People were running everywhere.
Out of the tents. Away from the fire. Toward the middle of the camp, toward the big fire, toward the north. Mothers with babies. Old men with sticks. Children screaming. In the pen at the east edge the goats had gone mad, screaming in their high terrible voices and hurling themselves at the woven fence, and the fence broke, and they came pouring out across the camp in a black and white flood, leaping over the mats and the bread racks and the people, bleating.
And through all of it, through the fire and the smoke and the running people and the screaming goats, the four iron shapes walked.
They did not hurry. They did not run. They walked. In their line, ten paces apart. Through the middle of the camp. North. Toward the last tent before the ridge. Ticking.
And they did not stop for anything.
That was what Lyra saw. That, more than the fire. People ran in front of them, and they did not stop. They did not strike. They did not grab. They were not cruel. They simply did not stop. A man ran into one of them, in the smoke, blind, and bounced off it as if he had run into a wall, and fell. And the Eraser walked on, and its iron foot came down beside his hand. Not on it. Beside it. It did not step on him. It did not step round him. It did not seem to know he was there.
An old woman was standing in front of another one. In its path. Frozen. Too frightened to move. Lyra saw her. And the Eraser walked up to her, and did not slow down, and its long iron arm came up and moved her. Pushed her aside. The way you push aside a branch on a path. Not hard. Just enough. She fell in the gravel, and it walked on.
As if people were thorns.
As if the whole camp, every person in it, every child and goat and tent and fire, were nothing but thorn bushes on a slope, to be walked through.
The Ashborn men came at them with spears.
Lyra saw Brin, the big slow man, run at the third Eraser from the side with his bone spear held low, and drive it at the iron with all his weight behind it. The bone point hit the plates on the thing's side. And broke. Snapped off, with a crack like a dry stick, and spun away into the dark. The shaft splintered in Brin's hands.
The Eraser did not stop. Did not turn. Its iron arm swung out, sideways, the way the other had swung the lamp, and caught Brin across the chest, and lifted him off his feet.
He went through the air. Lyra watched him go. A big heavy man, flying, like a child thrown by a giant. He went over the broken fence of the goat pen and came down inside it, among the trampled straw and the dung, with a sound she heard over everything.
And lay still.
Then moved. Rolled over. Got to his knees.
Alive.
Other spears came. Oda's. Sef's. Three of the men from the bread racks. Bone points on iron. Every one broke. Every one. The Ashborn bone spears, made for goats and wolves and the long dark of the edge, broke on the iron plates like icicles, and the Erasers walked on through the shower of broken bone as if it were rain.
Ticking.
The smoke was so thick now that Lyra could hardly see. Her eyes streamed. Her throat burned. The fire was everywhere on the south side of the camp, and spreading north on the wind, tent by tent, and the heat of it came across the open ground in waves.
And the four iron shapes walked on through it, north, toward the ridge.
What Steel Can Do
Oren met the first one by the big fire.
It was the one at the west end of the line, the nearest to them. It came round the edge of the great fire in the middle of the camp, through the smoke, ticking, with the firelight running red and gold over its iron plates and its brass joints, and its blank head turned north. And Oren stepped out in front of it.
He did not shout. He did not say anything. He just stepped into its path, in the open, between it and the north, with his sword up in both hands and his feet set wide on the gravel, the way he had stood against Arran, and against a hundred men before Arran, in a hundred yards and streets and fields. A big gray-bearded man in a shirt with his sleeves rolled up and the firelight on his face.
The Eraser did not stop.
It walked into him the way it had walked into the thorns. And Oren cut.
The first cut was at the shoulder. High and hard and fast, a downward stroke with all his weight in it, the kind of stroke that would have taken a man's arm off at the joint. Lyra saw it land. She saw the blade come down on the place where the iron plate of the shoulder met the brass of the joint.
And sparks.
A great spray of them, white and orange, bursting out from the blade like water from a stone thrown in a pond. The sound was terrible. A scream of steel on iron, high and grinding, that went into Lyra's teeth. The blade skidded. Slid off the curve of the shoulder plate. Went past. And left a mark. A bright silver scratch, a hand long, on the black iron.
That was all.
The Eraser did not stagger. Did not turn. Did not stop walking. It came on.
Oren gave a step. Brought the blade round, and up, and cut again. At the neck. At the joint between the blank iron head and the body, where there was a ring of brass, a collar of it, and a gap.
Sparks again. The scream of steel. The blade bit into the gap, a little way, and caught on something inside, and was thrown off. The Eraser's head did not even turn.
Oren gave another step. Back. Back. His face in the firelight was set hard, his teeth bared in his beard, and the sweat was running down it.
The Eraser's arm came up.
Not at him. It was not attacking him. Lyra could see that, even in the smoke and the fire. It was moving him. The way the other one had moved the old woman. The long iron arm came up and out, slow and smooth, to push him aside, like a branch.
And Oren cut at the arm.
At the wrist. Where the three flat iron fingers joined the arm, at a joint of brass. A short hard chopping stroke, down, with all the strength in his shoulders.
And this time the blade bit.
Lyra heard it. A different sound. Not the scream of steel skidding off iron. A dull thick chunk, like an axe going into green wood. The blade went into the joint of the wrist, between the brass pieces, a finger's depth.
And stuck.
Oren pulled. The sword did not come. It was wedged in the brass of the iron wrist, and the Eraser's arm was still moving, still coming up and out, slow and steady, and lifting the sword with it, and Oren with it, hanging on to the hilt with both hands, his feet coming off the gravel.
He set his boot against the iron of the thing's side and wrenched.
The blade tore free. With a shriek of metal and a spray of sparks and a little shower of brass, bright bits of it, falling on the gravel like coins. Oren fell back. Rolled. Came up on one knee, with the sword in his hand, and the edge of it notched now, a bite out of it near the point the size of a fingernail.
The Eraser's wrist was bent. Twisted, a little, at the joint. The three fingers hung at a wrong angle.
It did not seem to notice.
It walked on. Past Oren, kneeling on the gravel. Not looking at him. North. Toward the ridge. Ticking.
Oren knelt there and looked after it, breathing hard.
"Saints," he said. Very low. "Saints and all their mothers."
Lyra was beside him. She did not know how she had got there. She had her sword in her hand and she had not used it. She had not had time.
"The wrist," she said. "You got the wrist."
"I got a finger's worth of the wrist," said Oren. "And it didn't even slow down." He got up. He looked at the notch in his blade. "There's nothing to cut. It's all plate. And under the plate there's more plate. And under that there's wheels." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "You can't kill a clock, Lyra. It's not alive. You can only break it."
And behind them, by the big fire, someone said Lyra's name.
She turned.
Corin.
He was standing by the fire, on his crutch, in the smoke. His face was gray. Not with ash. With something else. His gray hands were at his sides, and they were shaking, and his eyes were on the four iron shapes walking north through the camp, and on the burning tents behind them, and on the people running, and on the children.
She saw it in his face. She knew what he was thinking. She knew it the way she knew her own name.
He could stop them.
He was a harvester. He had more in him than anyone. Shelves and shelves of it. Years and years of other people, and his own life, and a war he could not remember. If he reached in and took a handful of it, a great handful, and let it go into the fire, all at once, the fire that would come up into him then would be enough to stop four clocks. More than enough. She had seen what he could do when he burned. She had seen it under the Bourse, with the dome coming down. She had seen the pillar of white light go up into the sky.
And she had seen what was left of him afterward.
"No," she said.
Corin looked at her.
"You promised," said Lyra. "Silas. You promised him. Not a candle. Not one."
"I know." His voice was raw. "I know. But they're burning the..."
"And if you start," said Lyra, "you won't stop."
He did not answer.
Because it was true. They both knew it was true. She could see him know it. If he reached in, now, here, with the camp burning and the children screaming and the iron walking, he would take a handful, and it would not be enough, and he would take another. And another. And he would not stop until there was nothing left on the shelves. Nothing left of him. Only a gray man standing in the ashes of Last Thorn, breathing, with nobody inside.
That was how they drowned.
Corin stood there for one long moment, in the smoke, with his gray hands shaking at his sides.
Then he dropped his crutch.
And turned. Not toward the Erasers. Away from them. Toward the nearest burning tent. Where the flap had fallen shut and somebody inside was screaming, high and thin and young.
And went into the fire.
