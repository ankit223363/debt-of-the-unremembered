Chapter 69: Fire at the Edge, Part 1 of 5: Making Ready
Making Ready
The whole camp was sewing.
Lyra had never seen anything like it. She came out of the tent at first light, with the cold so sharp it made her eyes water, and every Ashborn in Last Thorn seemed to be sitting outside a tent with a needle. Old women and young ones, men and boys, children with their tongues between their teeth. All of them bent over something in their laps. And all of it the same thing.
Felt.
Thick gray-brown felt, made from the goats' winter wool, pounded and rolled until it was as stiff as leather and as soft as a blanket at the same time. It came in long strips, from great rolls of it that the men carried out of the gray tent on their shoulders. And the Ashborn were cutting the strips with bone knives and sewing them into shapes. Long ones, and wide ones, with holes, and thongs to tie them.
Boots, Lyra realized. Not boots. Covers for boots. Great clumsy felt bags that went over a boot from the toe to above the ankle and tied round the calf, so that your whole foot was wrapped in a fat soft gray parcel.
"For the Glass," said Arran, when she asked him. He was sitting on a log with a felt bag over his own foot, tying the thongs, very fast. His eyes were still red. But he was grinning again, a little, at one corner of his mouth. "The grit cuts leather. Fine grit. Glass dust. Like sand made of knives. You walk on it two days in leather and your boots fall off your feet in pieces. And then your feet do." He pulled the thong tight. "Felt doesn't cut. It just wears. And when it wears, you sew on more."
He held up the foot. It looked like a small gray sheep.
Lyra laughed. She could not help it.
"Laugh," said Arran. "You'll be wearing a pair by noon."
She was. Two Ashborn women came and sat her down on a mat and took her boots off her without asking, and measured her feet with a knotted string, and sewed her a pair while she watched, and put them on her, and tied them. They were warm. They were heavier than they looked. When she stood up and walked she felt like a child in her father's slippers, and she had to lift her feet higher than she was used to, so as not to trip.
"You'll learn," said one of the women, kindly, watching her stumble.
There was more. All morning, there was more.
The stones. Flat smooth gray river stones, each about the size of a loaf, that the children had been collecting from somewhere down the slope for days, and that lay now in a heap by the big fire. The men put them into the fire, right into the red heart of it, with long forked sticks, one after another, until the fire was full of stones and you could hardly see the flames. They would stay there all day, Kestrel said. Getting hot. Hotter than you could touch. And at the end of the day, the men would take them out with the sticks, and wrap them in felt, three layers, and tie them, and each walker would carry one.
"For the nights," said Kestrel. "Out there the fire will not warm you. Thorn burns blue on the Glass and gives no heat. These are all the warmth we will have." She looked at the stones in the fire. "Two nights, they keep. Perhaps three. After that we sleep in a heap, like puppies, and are glad of each other."
The water. Skins of it, goat skins, sewn up at the legs and the neck and filled from the spring at the bottom of the thorn slope, and tied off. Three each. Lyra lifted one and nearly dropped it. It was as heavy as a small child, and cold, and sloshed. She tried to imagine carrying three of them, on her back, for ten nights.
"A mouthful at each rest," said Kestrel. "A cup at night. No more. Not for anyone. Not for the strongest. Not for the youngest." She looked at Lyra. "There is no water on the Glass. Not one drop, from the Edge to the Eye. What you carry is what you drink. When it is gone, it is gone."
And the eye strips. The long narrow bands of smoked hide with the two slits, like the ones Arran had given them on the ridge. Everyone had one. Lyra tied hers round her neck, the way the Ashborn did, so that it would be ready.
And bread. Racks of it. More than Lyra had ever seen. Flat dark rounds, dried hard in the cold until they rang when you knocked them, stacked in piles and tied in bundles with thongs. And dried goat meat in strips, black and tough. And a bag of the sticky black berries for each walker. And, from somewhere, the last of Granny Abbe's dried apples, and Doctor Silas's jar of honey, which Lyra had thought was gone and which Oren had been carrying all this time, wrapped in his spare shirt, without telling anyone.
"HONEY," Lyra read on the label, in Silas's spidery hand, when he showed her. "FOR THE LEG. ALSO FOR EATING. DON'T TELL ME WHICH."
She laughed again. Then she found that her eyes were stinging, and stopped.
The camp went on sewing all morning. The fire roared with its belly full of stones. The sky over the ridge was clear and pale and the Glass under it blazed, and by the middle of the morning the warm breath of it was coming over the crest and down into the camp, and the snow on the tent roofs was dripping.
And underneath all of it, under the sewing and the stones and the water skins, Lyra could feel something else. Something the Ashborn did not say. In the way they looked up from their needles, every so often, all at once, toward the south. Toward the thorn slope and the Lip.
Listening.
She found she was doing it too.
The Five
Kestrel named them at noon.
She did it by the big fire, with the stones glowing in its heart and the whole camp gathered round on the mats and the bare earth, facing north. She did not make a speech. She stood up, tall and straight, in her long hide coat and her pale fur cape, with the gray line running down her shaved skull, and she waited until the camp went quiet. It did not take long.
Then she said a name.
"Arran."
Nobody was surprised. Lyra could see that. Arran got up from where he was sitting, a little way off, with his flute in his hand, and walked across the open ground and stood behind Kestrel, at her left shoulder. He did not grin. His face was very quiet. He touched the gray line on his face with two fingers, forehead to nose, and stood still.
"Brin."
A big slow man got up from among the men by the bread racks. Broad and heavy, with thick arms and a round shaved head, and a gray line that spread out over both his cheeks. Lyra had seen him at the stick fight, at the front of the crowd, shouting louder than anyone. He did not shout now. He came and stood beside Arran, and his big hands hung at his sides.
"Oda."
A woman. Middle-aged, short, wiry, with a sharp brown face and quick black eyes, and a bone spear in her hand that she seemed never to put down. Lyra had seen her teaching the children to throw, at the edge of the thorns. She came without a word.
"Sef."
A young man, not much older than Lyra, thin and quiet, with the gray only beginning on his face, a little past the tip of his nose. He stood up from beside a girl with a baby on her hip, and the girl caught his hand, once, hard, and let it go. He came and stood with the others and did not look back at her.
"Marrit."
The last was old. Not as old as Hask had been. But old, gray-faced, with white stubble on her shaved head and a back as straight as a spear, and a long walking pole of pale wood. Lyra had heard someone say that Marrit had walked further onto the Glass than anyone living, once, to the fourth cairn, looking for a son who did not come back. Marrit came slowly across the open ground, leaning on her pole, and took her place at the end of the line.
Five.
Kestrel looked at them. Then she turned back to the camp.
"And us," she said.
That was all.
The camp sat in silence for a long moment. Then someone began to hum. A low sound, wordless, from somewhere among the women. Then another. Then more, until the whole camp was humming, very low, all together, a long slow note that went up a little and down a little and did not stop. Lyra did not know what it meant. She thought it might be a kind of blessing. Or a kind of goodbye.
And then the children started.
They had been sitting together at the front, a dozen of them, the ones from the path and others, with their round shaved heads and their painted lines. Very quiet, all through the naming. And now that it was done, and none of their names had been said, they understood.
The girl from the path, the serious one, was the first. She stood up. She stood up very straight, with her bone spear in her hand, and she looked at Kestrel, and her face went red, and she said something in the round Ashborn speech, very loud and very fast. Lyra did not understand the words. She did not need to. It was the voice of a child who has been told she is too young, and knows she is not.
Kestrel looked down at her and said one word.
The girl said something back. Louder.
Kestrel said the word again.
And the girl threw her spear down on the ground, hard, so that the bone point stuck in the frozen earth and the shaft quivered. And burst into tears. Furious, ugly tears, with her fists clenched. And turned and ran away between the tents.
Then the others started. All of them. Shouting, protesting, crying. A small boy with a runny nose stamped his foot. Two others clung to Arran's legs. One sat down on the ground and howled. The mothers went among them, catching them, lifting them, hushing them.
And Arran, standing behind Kestrel with the boys round his knees, looked down at them.
And then, very slowly, very solemnly, he took the bone flute from round his neck, and put it to his lips, and played. Not the three notes. Something else. A silly, jumping, squeaking little tune, like a goat falling down a hill. Over and over. With his eyes crossed.
The small boy with the runny nose stopped stamping.
Arran played it faster. And crossed his eyes harder. And did a little jig on the spot, in his felt-wrapped feet, so that he looked like a sheep standing on its hind legs.
The boy on the ground stopped howling. He stared. Then he laughed. A wet, hiccuping, unwilling laugh.
And then they were all laughing. Through their tears. Clinging to Arran's legs and laughing, while he played his silly goat tune and danced his sheep dance, round and round, until even the mothers were smiling, and the girl who had thrown her spear came creeping back out from between the tents to watch, red-eyed, and pretending not to.
Lyra watched him.
She thought of him sitting alone by the cold ashes yesterday, with his face on his arms.
She thought he was the bravest person she had met in a long time.
Only What's in Your Arms
Oren found her in the early afternoon and handed her a stick.
It was one of the sticks from the fight with Arran. Thorn wood, stripped of its bark and its spines, as long as a sword and a half and as thick as her wrist. It was heavier than her sword. Much heavier. She took it in both hands and nearly dropped the end of it on her own foot.
"Come on," said Oren.
He took her out to the east edge of the camp, past the goat pen, to a little flat stretch of bare frozen ground between the last tents and the first thorn bushes. Nobody was there. The goats watched them over the woven fence with their flat yellow eyes. Oren had his own stick, the one he had beaten Arran with three times. He stood it on its end and leaned on it, and looked at her.
"Show me your guard," he said.
She showed him. She had been showing him her guard since she was eight years old. In the yard at Crown Walk, in the mornings, while the house guards drilled and her governess called her name from the schoolroom window. She set her feet. She lifted the stick. She held it the way she would hold her sword, the point up and forward, angled across her body.
Oren looked at her for a long time.
Then he swung his stick.
Not hard. Not fast. A plain, slow, overhand cut, the first cut anyone learns, coming down at her left shoulder. She saw it coming a long way off. She had all the time in the world.
And she reached for something to burn.
She did not mean to. It was not a choice. It was the way her body had learned to fight, all this winter. You saw the blow coming. You reached inside yourself, quick, for something small, a face, a word, a smell, and you let it go into the fire, and the fire came up into your arms and made them fast and hard and sure, and the blade went where you wanted it before you knew you had wanted it. She had done it so many times. In the sewer. On the roof garden. In the snow on the dyke road. It was like breathing.
She reached.
And caught herself. Just in time. Snatched her hand back from the fire inside, the way you snatch your hand back from a hot stove.
And Oren's stick came down on her shoulder.
Not hard. He had pulled it. But hard enough. It knocked her guard aside and thumped into her shoulder through her coat and she staggered and nearly fell.
She stood there with her shoulder throbbing and her face hot.
"There," said Oren.
She glared at him.
"You saw that," he said. "Didn't you. You felt it."
She did not answer.
"You reached," said Oren. "For something to burn. I saw it in your face. Your eyes went funny, the way they go. And then you stopped yourself, because you remembered. And by the time you'd stopped yourself, I'd hit you." He lifted his stick and leaned on it again. "That's what you do. That's how you fight now. You've done it all winter. You fight like somebody who expects to burn."
Lyra rubbed her shoulder.
"I always have," she said. "Since... since the sewer. It's how I won."
"It's how you won," said Oren. "Yes. In the city. Where there's always something to burn and a fire to burn it in." He jerked his head toward the north, toward the ridge and the milky shine beyond it. "Out there, there isn't."
The goats watched.
"Out there," said Oren, "you reach, and there's nothing. No fire. Nothing goes. Nothing comes back. You reach, and you get a fistful of air, and while you're standing there with your fistful of air, a thing made of iron hits you." He looked at her very steadily. "On the Glass you won't burn. You can't. You'll only have what's in your arms."
She looked at the stick in her hands.
"What's in my arms," she said.
"What I put there," said Oren. "When you were eight. And nine. And twelve. And sixteen. Before you ever burned anything. Before your mother went. Before the sewer." His voice went a little rough. "It's all still there, Lyra. Every lesson. Every parry. Every morning in the yard. You haven't burned that. I know you haven't. I'd have seen. You've just stopped using it, because the fire was quicker."
He lifted his stick.
"So we're going to find it again," he said. "Now. This afternoon. Before we go. Guard."
She set her feet. She lifted the stick.
He swung. The same cut. The plain slow overhand at her left shoulder.
And she reached.
She could not help it. It was in her too deep. Her eyes went, and her hand went inside for the fire, and she caught it, and the stick hit her.
"Again," said Oren.
She set her feet.
He swung.
She reached. Caught it. Hit.
"Again."
This time she gritted her teeth and did not reach. She made herself not reach. She held her hand away from the fire inside by force, the way you hold a door shut against a wind. And her arms, with nothing in them, were slow and stupid and stiff. The stick came down. Her guard came up late and wrong, and the blow skidded off it and caught her elbow.
She yelled.
"Good," said Oren. "You didn't reach. Now. Stop holding the door. Stop fighting the fire. Forget the fire. It isn't there. It's never been there. You're eight. You're in the yard. It's raining. Your governess is shouting. I'm swinging at your shoulder, slow, like this, the way I did a thousand times. And you know what to do. You've always known."
He swung.
Slow. Plain. Overhand. At her left shoulder.
And Lyra, standing on the frozen ground at the edge of the world with a stick in her hands and her elbow throbbing, did not reach.
She remembered.
Not a picture. Not a memory, the kind you can burn. Something lower. Something in the muscles of her wrists and the backs of her knees and the set of her shoulders. The yard at Crown Walk, wet stones, the smell of rain. Oren's stick, coming down. And her own arms, eight years old and thin as twigs, coming up to meet it. Not fast. Not strong. Just right. Angled. So that the stick slid off.
Her arms did it.
The stick came down, and her guard came up, angled, and Oren's stick slid off it with a dry knock and went past her shoulder into the air.
She stood there, breathing hard.
Oren looked at her over the end of his stick.
He did not say anything. He did not smile. But something in his face, under the gray beard, eased.
"There," he said quietly. "That's in your arms."
Lyra looked at her hands on the stick.
They were shaking. But they had done it.
Two Hundred
"Again," said Oren.
She did it again.
The same cut. The plain slow overhand at her left shoulder. The same parry. Her arms remembering, angled, so that his stick slid off hers with a dry knock.
"Again."
Again.
She started counting on the tenth. She did not decide to. She never decided to. The numbers just started, the way they always did, under her breath, like a clock that has been wound and set going. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Each parry a number. Each knock of wood on wood.
Oren did not count. He did not need to. He knew she was counting. He could see her lips move. He swung, and she parried, and he swung again, and his face under the gray beard did not change at all.
Twenty.
Thirty.
Her arms began to ache at forty. It was the stick. It was so much heavier than her sword, and she was holding it out from her body, angled, and every time his stick came down on it the shock went up through her wrists and into her elbows and her shoulders. By fifty the ache had become a burn. By sixty her hands were sweating inside her gloves, in the cold, and she could feel the stick beginning to slide.
She gripped harder.
"Don't grip," said Oren, swinging. "Gripping makes you slow. Hold it like a bird. Tight enough it can't fly. Loose enough it can breathe."
She loosened her grip. The stick slid. She nearly lost it. She caught it.
Seventy.
The goats had lost interest. They had gone back to the middle of their pen and were standing in a heap, chewing, with their backs to her.
Eighty. Ninety.
At a hundred she missed.
It was not much. Her arms were too slow, by a breath, and her guard came up a finger's width too low, and Oren's stick slid off the top of it instead of the side, and came down and caught her on the shoulder. Not hard. He had felt her go, and pulled it. But it caught her.
And she reached.
She felt herself do it. When she was tired, when her arms failed, the old way came back. Her hand went inside, quick, for the fire, the way it had all winter.
She caught it. Pulled back. Held it.
And stood there with her shoulder stinging and her chest heaving and her face burning with shame.
Oren lowered his stick.
"That's the danger," he said quietly. "Not now. Now you're fresh. Now you can think. It's when you're tired. When you've been fighting half the night and your arms are lead and the thing in front of you won't stop. That's when you'll reach. That's when your hand goes for the fire on its own, because it always has. And out there," he jerked his chin north, "there'll be nothing, and you'll be standing there with your hand open, and it'll kill you."
She did not answer.
"So we go till you're tired," said Oren. "And then we go some more. Until your hand stops reaching. Until it's so tired of reaching and finding nothing that it gives up." He lifted his stick. "Guard."
She set her feet. She lifted the stick. Her arms screamed.
She started again at one. She did not know why. It seemed only fair.
One.
The sun went down the sky behind the thorn trees. The shadows of the tents stretched out long and blue across the frozen ground. Somewhere in the camp the women were singing as they worked, and the smell of the stones in the fire came on the wind, hot and dry and mineral. Children came and watched for a while, and got bored, and went away. Arran came and watched, with his arms folded, and did not laugh, and went away.
Fifty.
Her arms were not hers anymore. They belonged to someone else. Someone very old and very tired. They came up, and up, and up, angled, and the stick slid off, and they came down, and came up again. She did not tell them to. They did it on their own.
A hundred.
She missed twice between a hundred and a hundred and fifty. Both times she reached. Both times she caught it. The second time she caught it faster.
A hundred and fifty.
She stopped thinking. She stopped feeling the ache. There was only the cold air in her lungs, and the knock of wood on wood, and the numbers going round in her head, and Oren's face, gray-bearded, steady, swinging, swinging.
A hundred and eighty.
She missed once more. At a hundred and ninety-one. The stick came down a little faster than the others, because Oren had made it come faster, on purpose, she knew it, to catch her. And it caught her. On the arm.
And she did not reach.
She noticed it a moment later. The way you notice that a sound has stopped. Her arm stung, and her guard was down, and she stood there with the stick hanging from one hand, and there had been no reaching. Nothing. Her hand had not gone for the fire at all. It had just stayed where it was, open, tired, empty, and let the blow come.
She looked at Oren.
He was looking at her. He had seen it too.
He did not say anything. He lifted his stick.
She lifted hers.
A hundred and ninety-two. Ninety-three. Ninety-four. Her arms came up and up. Angled. Knock. Knock. Ninety-eight. Ninety-nine.
Two hundred.
She stood there, with the stick up, waiting for the next one.
Oren swung. The two hundred and first. The same plain overhand cut.
She parried it. Her arms did. Angled. Clean. His stick slid off hers with a dry knock and went past her shoulder.
And Oren lowered his stick, and stood it on its end, and leaned on it, and looked at her for a long time, with his arms folded on top of it, the way he had looked at her across the yard at Crown Walk a thousand times, when she was eight, and nine, and twelve.
"Better," he said.
That was all.
She dropped the stick. Her arms would not hold it anymore. It fell on the frozen ground at her feet with a clatter. And she sat down beside it, all at once, on the cold earth, with her arms hanging, and laughed, and could not stop.
Oren picked up her stick, and his own, and put them both over his shoulder.
"Two hundred and one," he said. "I counted too."
And he went off toward the fire, and left her sitting there in the dusk, laughing, with the goats looking at her over the fence.
