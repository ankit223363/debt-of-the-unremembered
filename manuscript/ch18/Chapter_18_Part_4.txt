Chapter 18: Father and Son, Part 4 of 5: Dunmore
Dunmore
The carriers had gone up the hill to the tavern. The dock was nearly empty now.
Only the ice was left. The last barge sat low and black against the jetty, with its hatches open and its hold half full of gray-white blocks that would not be moved until Monday. A thin cold mist had begun to rise off it, like breath, and to creep along the planks in the lantern light. Somewhere under the jetty the river slapped and sucked at the wooden posts. The posts were old, and green with weed up to the high-water line, and above the line they were white with frost.
Mr. Dorsey, the foreman, came down the plank with his tally book under his arm and a pipe in his teeth. He nodded at Briggs. Briggs nodded back. Mr. Dorsey looked at Corin, at the gray coat and the white face and the book on his knee, and seemed about to say something, and then did not. He went up the quay toward the little brick office with the lit window, and the door banged behind him, and they were alone.
Corin opened the Ledger.
His fingers were stiff with cold. The pages were stiff too. They cracked a little when he turned them. He went back past the slag-room, past the board with Crates, 6 on every line, past Pip's charcoal hills and the doctor's casebooks and the green book with the oak leaf in it. Back and back. To Thursday night. To Bram's kitchen, and the lamp, and the six books with their brown paper covers, and the note.
He had copied it that night, word for word, letter for letter. He had copied the shape of it too, as well as he could, because the shape had seemed important. Big square capitals in soft pencil, pressed hard into the paper. No commas. No full stops, except one. The letters leaning a little to the left, as if the person writing them had been tired, or old, or in a hurry, or all three.
He turned the Ledger round and held it out.
Briggs did not take it at first. He looked at it, in the lantern light, from where he sat on his barrel. Then he wiped his big hands on his coat, slowly, one and then the other, as if they were dirty, and took the book from Corin as carefully as if it were a baby, and tilted it toward the light.
He read it.
Corin watched his lips move.
HIS NAME IS CORIN. HE WONT REMEMBER ME. DONT TELL HIM. KEEP EVERYTHING HE WRITES. — D.
Briggs read it twice. Then a third time. Then he lowered the book onto his knee and sat looking at nothing, out over the black water, toward the far bank where the warehouse lamps were coming on one by one in a long yellow line.
"Where did you get this?" he said.
"From a man who has kept my books for six years," said Corin. "Every book I have ever written. Six years of them. I didn't know he had them. I didn't know he was keeping them. I thought I lost them, or burned them, or threw them away. I don't remember." He paused. "That note was inside the first one. On the inside of the cover. In pencil."
"Six years."
"Six years ago," said Corin, "somebody brought me to his door. In the middle of the night. I was full of ash. Somebody else's ash. Burned memories, packed into me so tight I couldn't think. I couldn't talk. I didn't know my own name." He looked at the Ledger on Briggs's knee. "The man who brought me wrote that. And signed it D. And went away. And never came back."
Briggs said nothing for a long time.
The mist moved along the planks. The lantern above them creaked on its hook.
"Dunmore," said Briggs.
He said it the way a man says a word in a language he has not spoken since he was a boy. Carefully. As if he was not sure it would still fit in his mouth.
"Old Dunmore," he said. "Saints." He looked up at Corin, and there was something new in his small pale eyes. Not fear. Not quite. Something like a man looking at a door he had nailed shut long ago, and finding it open. "He's alive?"
"I don't know," said Corin. "He was alive six years ago."
"Six years ago." Briggs breathed out, long and slow, and his breath went up white past the brim of his hat. "I heard he was dead. Years back. Somebody told me. I don't remember who. Fell in the river, they said, drunk, off one of the bridges. I thought—" He stopped. "I didn't think anything. I made myself not think anything. It was easier."
"Tell me about him," said Corin. "Please. Anything."
Briggs turned the Ledger over in his hands and back again. He ran his thumb along the edge of the pages, where Corin's careful lines of writing ran out to the very margin, and he seemed to be looking for the right place to begin.
"He was the sergeant," he said at last. "Our sergeant. Eleventh Company. He was old even then. Older than any of us. Forty, maybe. Fifty. When you're eighteen, everybody over thirty is a hundred." A small dry sound came out of him that might once have been a laugh. "Gray beard. Cut short. A face like a boot. He'd been in the army since before I was born. He'd been everywhere. He never talked about any of it."
"Was he kind?"
Briggs thought about it.
"No," he said. "Not kind. Fair. That's different. He'd shout you deaf if your kit was dirty. He'd have you running round the camp in your boots at midnight for talking back. But he never hit a man. Not once. And he never lied to us. Not once." He paused. "That's why they left him behind, I think. With the rest of us. With the eleven. Because he wouldn't lie."
"Lie about what?"
Briggs's hands went still on the book.
"About the registration," he said.
He was quiet for a moment. Out on the river, a lamp went by on a small boat, low on the water, and the sound of oars came to them, slow and steady, dip and pull, and then went away again into the dark.
"I don't remember much," said Briggs. "I told you. It's mostly gone. But I remember this. The night before. When the orders came down. They gathered all the sergeants in the captain's tent, and when Dunmore came out he was a different color. Gray, like the wall. He didn't say anything to us. He just went and sat by the fire with his back to us, and he sat there all night. I know, because I was on watch. I walked past him every hour. He didn't move. He didn't sleep. Every time I came round he was looking at the same place. At the house by the river."
Corin felt the cold go into him. Down through the gray coat and the shirt and the skin and into the bones.
The house by the river.
The teacher. Her two.
"And in the morning," said Briggs, "when the companies were gone, and the village was empty, and we were standing round the well like fools, I said to him, Sergeant, where is everybody? And he told us." Briggs swallowed. "He didn't dress it up. That was his way. He just said it. He said, They've taken the children up the road in wagons. They've taken the grown folk with them, or tried to. Our lot have gone up to the ridge. And they've left us here because we asked too many questions, lads, and I'm sorry for it. And then he said—"
Briggs stopped.
His face had changed. It had gone smooth and blank, like a pond after a stone has sunk. Corin had seen that face before. He had seen it many times, on the other side of a glass, on a hundred different people. It was the face of someone reaching for a thing that had been sold.
"I don't know what he said," said Briggs quietly. "It's gone. I can see his mouth moving. I can't hear it." He shook his head slowly. "That's how it is. That's how it's been for twenty years. I can see the shapes of things. I can't hear them."
"It's all right," said Corin.
"It isn't," said Briggs. "But thank you."
He was silent a while.
"I remember the end, though," he said. "After. After the gold. When it was all over."
Corin waited.
"The gray went back," said Briggs. "Up the valley. It didn't go away, exactly. It just stopped coming. It sat on the ridge like a cloud for a day and a night and then it was gone. And the gold went too. It faded out, slow, like a lamp running out of oil. And the soldier—" His voice caught. "The whistling one. He was on the ground under the tree. On his back. With his eyes open. Breathing. But—"
"Empty," said Corin.
Briggs looked at him.
"Yes," he said. "Empty. Like your Slates. Like Felix. He was looking up through the leaves and there was nothing in his face. Nothing at all. And Dunmore knelt down beside him and took his hand and said his name, over and over, and the man didn't know it."
"Do you remember the name?"
"No." Briggs's big fist came down on his own knee, once, hard enough that the barrel rocked under him. "No. I've tried. Saints know I've tried. I hear Dunmore saying it. I hear the shape of it. It's short. It's—" He shut his eyes. "It's gone."
Corin said nothing. He did not trust himself to.
"Then the army came back," said Briggs. "Down off the ridge. Officers. Men in gray coats I'd never seen before. Not soldiers. Clerks. With ledgers." He glanced down at the book on his knee and his mouth twisted. "They walked round the village writing things down. They walked round us. They asked us what we'd seen. And one by one we told them. And one by one they took us into a tent, and when we came out, we'd seen a bit less."
"They took it from you."
"Some of it. Not all. They were in a hurry. I think they did it quick and rough, the way you'd sweep a floor before company comes." He shrugged his heavy shoulders. "I sold the rest myself. Later. I told you."
"And Dunmore?"
Briggs was quiet.
"Dunmore wouldn't go in the tent," he said.
Corin looked up.
"He stood outside it," said Briggs. "With his arms folded. And when the clerk came out with the ledger and said, Sergeant, it's your turn, he said, No. Just that. No. And the clerk said it was an order, and Dunmore said he'd been taking orders for thirty years and this was the first one he'd ever said no to, and the clerk could write that down in his book if he liked."
A ghost of something crossed Briggs's face. It might have been pride.
"They didn't make him," he said. "I don't know why. Maybe they were scared of him. Maybe they thought nobody would believe an old drunk sergeant anyway. Maybe they had other things to do. But they didn't make him. And when we marched back down to the capital, a month later, he walked at the back of the line on his own and he didn't talk to anybody. Not one word. Not for a month."
"And then?"
"And then we got to the barracks," said Briggs, "and he handed in his coat."
The lantern creaked. The river slapped at the posts.
"I was at the gate," said Briggs. "On guard. It was raining. He came out of the quartermaster's with a sack on his shoulder and no coat, in the rain, in his shirt. Fifty years old. And he stopped by me at the gate, and he looked at me for a long time, and I thought he was going to shout at me for something. The way he always did. My boots or my buttons." He swallowed. "And he said, Fenn. Somebody has to remember them."
Corin's breath stopped in his throat.
"I said, Who, Sergeant?" said Briggs. "And he said, The children. All of them. Every one. If nobody else will, then I will. And he walked out of the gate into the rain and down the hill toward the river, and that was the last time I ever saw him."
He held out the Ledger.
Corin took it. His hands were shaking again. He let them.
"He found me," he said. His voice did not sound like his own. "Six years ago. He found me somewhere. Full of ash. And took me to a man who would keep my books." He looked down at the square pencil letters. HE WONT REMEMBER ME. "He was remembering me. That's what he was doing. All that time. He was remembering us."
Briggs said nothing.
"Where would he go?" said Corin. "An old soldier with no coat and no money. In this city. Twenty years ago. Where would he go?"
Briggs rubbed his face with both hands.
"Under a bridge," he said at last. "Where all of us go, in the end. When there's nothing left." He paused. "There was a story. Later. Years later. A docker told me. I don't know if it's true. He said there was an old soldier living under one of the big bridges, down by the water, where the arches meet the bank. Keeping to himself. Not begging. And people would bring him things."
"Food?"
"No." Briggs frowned, as if he was trying to see it. "Not food. Names. That was the story. The docker said people would come down to the arches at night and tell the old man a name. Somebody they'd lost. Somebody who'd been emptied, or sold off, or forgotten. And he'd write it down. On the stones. On the underside of the arch, where the rain doesn't reach. In chalk." He shook his head. "The docker said there were thousands. All over the stones. Up and up into the dark, where you couldn't see."
Corin sat very still.
"Which bridge?" he said.
"I don't know."
"Please. Think."
"I don't know, Vane." Briggs's voice came out rough. Then he breathed in and let it out and spoke more gently. "It was a story. A docker's story, in a tavern, after a long shift. I didn't ask. I didn't want to know. If I'd known which bridge, I'd have had to go and look. And if I'd gone and looked—" He stopped.
"You'd have had to look for their names," said Corin quietly.
Briggs nodded. Once. Slowly.
Corin looked down at the Ledger. He turned forward through the pages. Past Thursday. Past Friday. To the doctor's casebook, copied out in his own small careful hand in the crypt of Saint Bride's, by candlelight, with the fever-children coughing around him.
He asks me to meet him under the Lantern Bridge, at the landing on the riverside, at eleven tomorrow night. He says nobody goes there.
Nearly the last thing Doctor Aske ever wrote. He says nobody goes there.
Under the Lantern Bridge.
He did not say it aloud. He did not say anything. He took the pencil from the spine of the Ledger, and he turned to a clean page, and he wrote, slowly, pressing hard:
Dunmore. Sergeant. Eleventh Company. Gray beard. Would not go into the tent. Handed in his coat. "Somebody has to remember them."
Names in chalk, under an arch. Thousands.
The Lantern Bridge??
He drew a box around the last line. And then another box around that.
Beside him, Briggs had taken off his hat. He sat on the barrel in the cold with his thin fair hair lifting in the river wind, turning the hat round and round in his big hands by its brim, the way a man does in church.
"Is it bad," he said, "that I'm glad he's not dead?"
"No," said Corin.
"Even though I never looked for him."
"No."
Briggs nodded. He put his hat back on. He pulled it down low over his eyes.
"He'd have shouted at me," he said. "For not looking." And there it was again, that small dry sound, that might once, a long time ago, have been a laugh.
Two Coppers
The bells of the city rang seven.
They came from everywhere at once, the way they always did on a cold, still night. Big ones and small ones. Near ones and far ones. Saint Agatha's on the hill, deep and slow, like a man clearing his throat. The bell of the Customs House, high and quick and a little cracked. The fat brass bell on the corner of Pike Lane that the dockers called Old Mother, because it nagged. And under all of them, three quarters of an hour late and not caring at all, the far-off, patient voice of Old Tide, still ringing six from across the water.
Briggs counted them under his breath. Then he stood up.
He did it slowly, like an old man, with one hand on the barrel and one on his knee. His joints cracked in the cold. He stamped his boots on the planks, left and then right, to get the blood back into them, and the frost flew off the toes in little white puffs.
"He'll knock off soon," he said, nodding at the jetty. "They'll stop for the night when that last barge is half done. Dorsey won't pay for lamp oil past eight." He pulled his small green coat tight across his chest. "I always go before he comes off. I don't like him to see me twice in one day. He gets confused. He thinks he ought to know me. It worries him."
He reached into the inside of his coat.
Corin had thought he was reaching for a pipe, or a flask, or a handkerchief. It was none of those. What Briggs took out, slowly and carefully, with both hands, was a tin.
It was an old tobacco tin. Flat and square, about the size of a man's palm, with rounded corners. Once it had been painted red, with gold letters on the lid, but nearly all the paint had worn away. Now it was mostly bare gray metal, polished smooth and bright at the edges by years and years of a thumb rubbing at it. Only a little red was left, deep in the letters, so that Corin could still just read them. CAPTAIN'S BEST. FINE CUT.
Briggs held it for a moment. Then he held it out.
"Go on," he said. "Open it. I've never shown anybody."
Corin took off one glove with his teeth and took the tin. It was warm from Briggs's chest. The lid was stiff. He worked his thumbnail under the edge of it and pushed, and it came up with a little tired squeak of metal on metal.
The tin was full of paper.
Small slips of it. Hundreds and hundreds of them. They were packed in so tightly, standing on their edges, side by side, like the pages of a book with no cover, that not one more could have gone in. Corin pulled one out from the front. It came reluctantly, with a whisper of paper against paper.
It was a Bank slip. He knew them well. He had seen a thousand of them, pinned to a thousand jars, on the shelves above the slag-room. Pale yellow paper, cheap and thin, about the length of a finger. A printed line at the top: BANK OF ECHOES. LANTERN QUAY INTAKE. A printed box for the date, and a printed box for the glass, and a printed box for the price. And at the bottom, a printed line for the Warden to sign.
On this one, in the date box, somebody had written in blue-black ink: Tues. the 3rd. In the glass box: 11. In the price box: 2c.
And on the line at the bottom, in a big, slow, careful hand, pressed so hard that the nib had nearly gone through the paper:
F. Briggs.
And under that, in smaller letters, in the same hand, two words that were not printed on any Bank slip anywhere:
Bought back.
Corin looked up.
Briggs had sat down again on the barrel. He was not looking at the tin. He was looking at the river.
"Every Tuesday," he said. "Like I told you. He comes up the quay and stands in my line and puts his hand on the third glass from the end. And sells whatever he's got. And the glass goes cloudy for a minute, just a bit, just a little, the way it does when there's not much in a person. And the clerk gives him two coppers. And he says, Thank you, miss. And he goes back to the docks."
He rubbed his thumb along the seam of his trousers.
"And at the end of the day," he said, "when the clerks have gone, and the doors are shut, and the glasses are all wiped down, I go up to the shelf in the back where the day's jars are waiting for the Bank's wagon. And I find his. Number eleven. Tuesday. Two coppers. It's always the smallest one on the shelf. You could hold it in your fist." He stopped. "And I take two coppers out of my own pocket. And I put them in the till. And I write in the book that the jar was bought back by the Warden, at full price, for his own use, which a Warden's allowed to do, it's in the rules, though nobody ever does. And I sign the slip. And I put the jar in my coat. And I take it home."
Corin looked down at the tin in his hand. At the hundreds of yellow slips, packed tight.
"How many?" he said.
"Four hundred and twelve," said Briggs. "As of last Tuesday. I count them every Sunday." A pause. "Eight years. He's never missed one. Not once. Not when he had the fever, the winter before last. He came up the quay with a face as white as that ice, shaking so hard he could hardly stand, and stood in the line for an hour, and sold the fever, I suppose, or whatever he'd had that week, which was mostly fever. And I bought it back."
"And the jars?"
"In my cellar. At home. On shelves." Briggs's voice was very even, as though he were reading out a list of stock. "I put up the shelves myself. Pine. From the timber yard on Cooper Street. Twelve shelves. Thirty-six jars on a shelf, if you put them close. I've filled eleven." He paused. "I've bought the wood for a thirteenth."
Corin did not say anything.
He was thinking of a stone cellar under a little house he had never seen, somewhere in the dark streets behind the Lantern Quay. Of twelve rough pine shelves along the walls, in the light of one candle. And on them, in rows, close together, four hundred and twelve tiny glass jars, each one no bigger than a fist, each one with a yellow slip pinned to it. Each one with a faint, faint glow inside it, so faint you would only see it with the candle out. Like stars, on a night with a little mist.
Eight years of Tuesdays. Eight years of pies, and gulls, and barges going by.
"Do you look at them?" he said.
"No," said Briggs. Then: "Sometimes." Then, after a long while: "I go down on a Sunday, after I've counted the slips, and I sit on the bottom step with the candle, and I look at them. I don't open them. I don't know how. And I wouldn't, if I did. They're not mine." His jaw moved. "I just sit there. With him."
The ice ground in the river. Out on the jetty, Felix laughed at something one of the other carriers said. A clear, easy, empty laugh, like a bell with nothing to ring for.
"Why?" said Corin quietly. Not unkindly. He wanted to know.
Briggs was quiet for so long that Corin thought he would not answer.
"Because they're his," he said at last. "The only things that are. He hasn't got anything else, Vane. He hasn't got a name. He hasn't got a mam or a dad. He hasn't got the cat, or the letters, or any of it. He's got a bunk on Pike Lane and a hook for his coat and whatever happens to him between one Tuesday and the next. That's all. That's his whole life, now." His voice dropped. "And every week he sells it. All of it. For two coppers. And it goes on a shelf in the back of my hall, and the Bank's wagon comes for it at midnight, and that's the last anybody ever sees of it."
He shook his head.
"I couldn't let it go," he said. "Not his. Not after—" He stopped. "So I keep them. That's all. I don't know what for. Maybe for nothing. Maybe one day somebody'll come along who knows how to—" He stopped again, harder this time, like a man walking into a wall in the dark. "No. That's daft. Nobody can put it back. Everybody knows that. You can't put it back."
Corin looked at the slip in his bare fingers. Bought back.
He thought of his own rod, cold in his inside pocket. Of the thick brown jar beside it, wrapped in bandage, humming its one low note. Of the way the slag came up out of the glasses every morning in the cold room, gray and dull and dead, and how, when it touched the silver, it sometimes lit. Just for a moment. Just a flicker. A face. A sound. A smell of bread.
He thought of Pip, in the crypt. Of the chalky gold line, from hill to hill. Of the old voice coming out of a child's mouth.
You can't put it back. Everybody knew that.
He did not know that. Not for certain. He did not know anything for certain. That was the whole trouble with him.
He slid the slip carefully back into its place in the tin, between Tues. the 27th and Tues. the 10th, and pressed the lid down with his thumb until it squeaked shut. He held it out.
Briggs took it. He held it for a moment in both hands, the way he had held the Ledger. Then he put it back inside his coat, on the left side, against his chest, and buttoned the coat over it.
"Right," he said. His voice had changed. It had gone back to the Warden's voice, flat and hard and tired. "That's enough of that. You didn't come down here to look at my tin."
"No," said Corin.
"You came down here about the wagon."
"Yes."
Briggs nodded slowly. He looked up the quay, toward the Lantern Quay, where the long black roof of the intake hall stood against the sky, with its row of tall windows dark now, all but one. The little window over the yard gate. The night porter's window. One small yellow square in all that black.
"Eleven o'clock," he said. "Every night. Like a clock. It comes along the river road from the east, with no lamps, and it turns in at the yard gate, and old Puckett opens up for it without being told, because he was told once in July and that was enough. And it backs up to the slag-room door. And the two big ones get down."
"The ones whose breath doesn't smoke."
Briggs's mouth tightened.
"I've watched it four times," he said. "From the porter's room. With the lamp out. Puckett thinks I'm checking up on him." He was quiet a moment. "They don't talk. Not to each other. Not to Puckett. They go down the steps into the slag-room and they come up with the crates, one each, on their shoulders. Two trips. Three. Six crates. Never more. Never less. They put them in the back of the wagon. And they get up on the box. And the wagon goes back out of the gate the way it came. Ten minutes. Never more."
"Where does it go?"
"East." Briggs shrugged. "Along the river. That's all I know. I've never followed it. I'm not a fool."
Corin waited.
He had learned, over the last week, that there was a way of waiting that made people talk. Not looking at them. Not asking. Just sitting, quietly, as though there were all the time in the world, and nowhere else to be. He did it now. He sat on the cold barrel with his hands in his pockets and his eyes on the dark water, and he waited.
It did not take long.
"You want to watch it," said Briggs. Not a question.
"Yes."
"From the yard."
"From somewhere I can see the slag-room door," said Corin. "And the crates. I need to see the crates, Warden. I need to know what's in them." He paused. "I need to see them."
Briggs let out a long breath.
"Saints," he said. "You're as daft as he was."
Corin did not ask who. He thought he knew.
Briggs sat with his elbows on his knees and his big hands hanging between them, looking at the planks under his boots. Frost had grown on the planks while they sat there, in the hour since the sun went down. Fine white feathers of it, along every crack and knot in the wood, so that the whole jetty looked as though it had been drawn in chalk.
"There's a coal hole," he said at last. "In the yard. On the left as you come in, behind the water butt. It goes down under the wall into the old coal cellar, that we don't use anymore since they put the stoves in. There's a grating at the top. Iron. You can lift it from underneath if you push. And if you stand on the coal heap at the bottom, with your face up against the grating, you can see the whole yard. The gate. The slag-room steps. The lot."
"How do I get into the coal cellar?"
"From inside. From the hall. There's a door at the bottom of the slag-room stairs, painted over. Behind the mop bucket. Nobody's opened it in ten years." Briggs reached into his trouser pocket and took out a ring of keys. Big iron keys and small brass ones, on a loop of greasy string. He went through them with his thick fingers, one by one, slowly, until he found the one he wanted. A long thin iron key, black with age, with a bent tooth. He worked it off the string. It took him a long time. Corin did not help.
Briggs held it out.
"The side door of the hall," he said. "The little one, on Tallow Row. This opens it. Puckett won't hear you. He's deaf in one ear and asleep in the other by ten." He did not let go of the key yet. "You go in at half past ten. Not before. You go down the slag-room stairs in the dark, and you don't light anything, not a match. And you go through that door behind the bucket and you shut it behind you and you stand on that coal and you don't move. Not a hair. Not till that wagon's gone out of the gate and you've counted to a thousand."
"Yes."
"I mean it, Vane." Briggs's small pale eyes held his. "Don't let them see you. Whatever you do. Whatever you see. If one of them so much as turns his head toward that grating, you get down off the coal and you lie flat and you stop breathing. You hear me?"
"Yes."
"And you don't come out after." His fingers were still tight on the key. "You don't follow it. You don't run after it down the river road like some lad in a penny story. You watch, and you count, and you go home to your bed. And on Monday morning you come in at six and you clear my glasses, like nothing happened. Because if they ever find out who let you in—"
He did not finish.
He did not have to. Corin was looking at his coat. At the left side of it, over his heart, where the tin was.
Four hundred and twelve.
If they came for Fenn Briggs, there would be nobody to buy back the four hundred and thirteenth.
"I won't let them see me," said Corin. "I promise."
Briggs let go of the key.
It lay in Corin's bare palm. Cold. Heavy for its size. He closed his fingers round it and felt the bent tooth press into his skin.
"And you," he said. "Tonight. Where will you be?"
"At home," said Briggs. "In my bed. Where a Warden ought to be, at eleven o'clock on a Saturday night. With the door locked." He paused. "And the slips counted."
"If anyone knocks—"
"Three slow ones," said Briggs. "You said." His mouth twisted. "I heard you, Vane. I've been hearing it all afternoon. Every time a gull lands on the roof." He stood up. His knees cracked again. "If anyone knocks three slow on my door tonight, I won't open it. I'll go down the cellar with the candle. And I'll sit on the bottom step. And I'll wait."
He brushed the frost off the seat of his trousers, slowly, with the back of his hand.
"Same as always," he said.
