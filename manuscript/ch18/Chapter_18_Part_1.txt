Chapter 18: Father and Son, Part 1 of 5: The Lantern Quay
The Lantern Quay
The line was longer on a Saturday.
Corin had read that in his Ledger, a week ago, on a page about rent day. Saturday the line goes all the way down the steps and along the quay to the Salt Stairs. People sell on Saturday so they can pay on Monday. He had not understood it then. He understood it now, coming along the river road from the Cut in the thin white winter sun, with his collar up and his hands in his pockets and the doctor's jar humming faintly against his ribs.
The intake hall stood at the end of the Lantern Quay, where the river road met the water. It had been a warehouse once, the Ledger said, for wool, and you could still see that. It was long and plain and tall, made of yellow brick gone brown with soot, with a row of high barred windows along the top of it and a great pair of double doors at the front, painted Bank green, standing open. Over the doors, carved into a stone set in the brick, there was a sign. Not an eye. The Bank did not use the Ministry's eye. A pair of scales, with a coin in one pan and a small round stone in the other. And under the scales, the words.
THE BANK OF VERIDIA. INTAKE. FAIR WEIGHT, FAIR PRICE.
And from the doors, down the six white steps to the quay, and along the quay beside the river wall, past the bollards and the coiled ropes and the frozen puddles, past the fish-boxes and the lobster pots, all the way to the head of the Salt Stairs at the far end, there was the line.
Hundreds of people. Standing. Waiting. Shuffling forward a step at a time and stopping again. Men in dock boots and women in shawls and old people wrapped in blankets and children. A great many children. Holding hands with their mothers, or with each other, or with nobody, standing on their own in the cold with their hands tucked into their armpits. Nobody talked much. Nobody pushed. The line went along the river wall in the thin sun like a long gray rope, and every few minutes, at the front, it moved a step, and all along its length people lifted their feet and put them down again, one step closer to the green doors.
He walked along beside it, toward the doors.
Nobody looked at him. Or rather, everybody looked at him once, quickly, at his long gray Ministry coat, and then looked away again, the way people look away from a dog they are not sure about. He walked past them all, along the river wall, and up the six white steps.
There were two watchmen at the doors.
He had not expected that. They had never been there before, the Ledger said. Blue coats. Brass on their hats. One young, one old, standing on either side of the green doors in the cold, stamping their feet, with their hands behind their backs and their breath going up in clouds. The young one was reading a broadsheet. Corin could see the top of it as he came up the steps. Big black letters.
THORNE HEIRESS FLED.
And under it, smaller: Watch seek Lyra Thorne, 20, and a Ministry harvester believed to be with her. Reward offered.
The young watchman lowered the paper and looked at Corin.
Corin did not stop walking. He did not slow down. He did not look at the paper, or at the watchman's face. He walked up the last two steps the way he had walked up them every morning for a week, the Ledger said, tired and a little bent, with his hands in his pockets, and he nodded to the old watchman, who was nearest, and went past him between the green doors.
Nothing happened.
Behind him, he heard the young one say, "Was that—" and the old one say, very bored, "That's the slag man. He's here every day. Ministry sends him. Read your paper."
And then the warm air of the hall closed round him, and he was inside.
The intake hall was long and high and bright.
That was what always surprised him. The Ledger said so, every time. It is bright inside. Very bright. Brighter than anywhere you know. Down both sides of the hall, on iron brackets fixed to the walls, there were lamps. Big ones, with blue stones in them, burning steadily, so that the whole hall was full of a clean white-blue light, like the light on snow. The walls were painted cream. The floor was white tiles, scrubbed, and smelled of polish. There were long wooden benches down the middle for the old people to sit on while they waited. There was a stove at each end, roaring. It was warm. It was clean. It was, Corin thought every time, the cleanest and warmest and brightest room that most of the people in that line would ever stand in.
And down the left side of the hall, from the doors almost to the far wall, there was the counter.
Long and dark and polished, with a brass rail along the front of it. And on the counter, set into it at an angle, one after another, like the windows of a long train, there were the glasses.
Twenty of them. Each one the size of a tabletop. Each one perfectly clear. And behind each glass, on the other side of the counter, a clerk. Young men and young women in Bank green, with white gloves, sitting on tall stools, one behind every glass. And in front of every glass, on the near side of the counter, a person from the line.
Corin had watched it a hundred times, the Ledger said. He watched it again now, from the doorway, because he could not help it.
A woman in a brown shawl put her bare hand flat on the glass in front of her. The clerk behind it laid her own white-gloved hand on the other side, against the woman's palm, without looking at her, and said something, very quietly. And the glass woke. A soft blue light came up in it, round the woman's palm, and spread, a few inches, like ink spreading in water. And in the light, very faint, very small, there were shapes. Moving. A room. A window. A man's face, laughing.
The woman's mouth trembled. Her eyes were shut.
The clerk took a small round cloudy stone from a tray beside her and pressed it against the glass, in the middle of the blue. And the blue flowed into it. Out of the glass and into the stone, the way water goes down a drain. And the glass went dark. And the stone went bright.
The woman opened her eyes. Her face was smooth and calm. She looked down at her hand on the glass as if she had never seen it before. The clerk put a coin in it. Silver. One.
"Next," said the clerk.
The woman went away down the hall toward the side door, where the people went out after they had sold. She was not crying. She did not look as if she would ever cry again. She looked like somebody walking out of a warm room into a pleasant afternoon.
Corin looked away.
Warden Briggs was at his ledger stand by the slag-room stairs, the way he always was.
Heavy and gray, in his dark green coat a size too small, with the brass plate sticking out over his stomach like a shelf. WARDEN. INTAKE. BRIGGS. His heavy gray face was bent over his big ledger, and there was a pen behind his ear and another in his hand, and an old gray wool scarf wound round his thick neck, darned in two places. He looked up as Corin came along the hall.
His small pale eyes went to Corin's face. And stayed there.
For a long moment, he did not say anything at all.
"Vane," he said.
"Warden."
"You're late." Briggs did not look at the clock. He did not need to. "Noon, I said. It's twenty past." He looked at Corin for another long moment. At his face. At his torn coat. At the dried mud on his boots, gray and glittering, from the Flats. "You look like you slept in a ditch."
"I didn't sleep."
"No." Briggs turned a page in his ledger. "No, I don't suppose you did." He dipped his pen. And then, without looking up, very quietly, in a voice so low that it did not carry past the ledger stand: "There's two watchmen on my door. Since seven this morning. With broadsheets. Asking every man who comes in if he's seen a girl with short black hair and a sword. Asking if a Ministry harvester works here."
Corin did not move.
"I told them yes," said Briggs. "I told them a Ministry harvester clears my slag. Every day, noon till the second bell. I told them he's a sad thin forgetful sort of fellow who couldn't find his own front door without a book, let alone run off with a Councillor's niece." He made a mark in the ledger. "They laughed. They said that didn't sound like their man at all." He looked up. "Was I right, Vane? Couldn't find his own front door?"
"Yes," said Corin.
"Good," said Briggs. "Then I didn't lie to the Watch." He jerked his head at the slag-room stairs. "Four glasses. Clear four and go at the second bell. Same as always." He looked down at the ledger. "And Vane."
"Warden?"
"Don't hum."
Corin went down the stairs.
Four Glasses
The slag room was cold and blue and silent.
It was the same as it always was. The Ledger said so, and his body said so, the moment he came down the last of the brick steps and the cold closed on him like water. A long low cellar under the back of the old warehouse, with a brick ceiling and no window, lit by a single gemstone lamp in an iron cage on the wall that gave off a thin, steady, bluish light. And along both walls, on wooden racks, the glasses. Dozens of them. Each the size of a tabletop. Each gone cloudy from inside, milky and still, like sheets of frozen breath.
But it was not the same.
He stood at the bottom of the steps and knew it at once, the way you know a room has been moved round in the dark. It was not the glasses. It was not the lamp. It was him. He stood there in the cold blue light and looked at the clouded glasses in their racks, and he knew, now, where they went.
On a cart. At midnight. Moggs, with the piebald horse. Along the river road with his lamp out. Down the rutted track to the Flats. Into the pits.
And a child picks through them in the dark for a farthing a pound.
He stood there for a long time.
Then he took off his coat, and folded it, and laid it on the bench by the door, carefully, with the jar of violet wrapped inside it in its strip of bandage, where he could see it. And he took out the rod.
The first glass was easy.
It was a new one. Only a few weeks of slag in it. He held the rod against it, flat, with the carved spiral tip just touching the cloud, and let it listen. And the slag came. Slowly. Thread by thread. Out of the glass and into the silver, the way water goes into a cloth. And as it went, he felt it pass. The crumbs. The corners. A smell of bread. A dog barking, very far off. A word in a language he did not know. Half of somebody's mother's face.
He had cleared a dozen glasses like this. More. He had never thought about it.
He thought about it now.
Whose bread? Whose dog?
Nobody's. That was what Briggs said. That was what the clerks said. Bits. Bits that didn't go in the stones. Pieces of people. Nobody's. But they had been somebody's. Every one. Somebody had stood at that counter upstairs with their bare hand on the glass and sold something they could not afford to keep, and this was what fell off it. The crumbs. The bits the stone did not want. Like the crusts of a loaf that the baker sweeps off his board at the end of the day and throws out for the gulls.
The glass cleared behind the rod, like a window clearing when the sun comes up. He stood back. It was clean. Perfectly clear. He could see the brick wall through it.
The rod in his hand was a little heavier.
You are going slowly today, said Malakor.
"Yes."
You are thinking about the children.
"Yes."
The rod was silent for a moment.
It will not help them, said Malakor, to clear the glasses slowly.
"I know."
Then why?
Corin did not answer at once. He went to the next glass, and laid the rod against it, and let it listen.
"Because every bit of this," he said, "is going to end up in a child's cut hand." The slag came into the rod, thread by thread. A man's voice, singing in a tavern. The smell of wet wool. "And I want to know what I'm giving them."
Malakor did not say anything to that.
At the end of the room, by the door, there was a shelf.
He had seen it every day for a week. The Ledger said so. A shelf by the door. For the full slag-jars. The carter takes them. It was a long plain wooden shelf, fixed to the bricks at the height of his chest, and on it, in a row, there were jars. Plain glass jars with wax seals, like the jars in the Harvesting Section. Each one full of a thin gray glittering dust, faintly shining. Twelve of them. Thirteen. Waiting.
At the end of the shelf, on a nail in the brick, there was a hook. And on the hook, there was a board. A plain wooden board, like a clerk's clipboard, with a sheaf of papers clipped to it.
He had never looked at it.
He looked at it now.
The top sheet was a printed form. Cheap gray paper, with lines, and boxes, and the Bank's scales at the top. DISPOSAL. SLAG AND SPENT GLASS. INTAKE HALL, LANTERN QUAY. And under it, columns. A date. A number of glasses. A number of jars. And a signature, in a box at the end of each line. The same signature, every line, all the way down the page. Big and square and heavy, pressed hard into the paper, the hand of a man who had signed the same thing so many times that his pen did it by itself.
F. Briggs. Warden.
Corin turned the page.
The next sheet was the same. And the next. A week to a page. Glasses, clouded: 9. Jars: 14. F. Briggs. Glasses, clouded: 11. Jars: 16. F. Briggs. He turned them back, slowly, week after week. Back through the autumn. Back to the end of the summer. And the numbers stayed much the same.
Then, at the beginning of July, there was a new line.
It had been added by hand. Squeezed in under the printed columns, in the same big square heavy writing, in a space where there was no box for it.
Also received for disposal, from the Hill, under seal: crates, 4.
And under that, the next week. Crates, 6. And the next. Crates, 6. And every week since, every single line, all the way to yesterday. Crates, 6. Crates, 6. Crates, 6. Every night. Six crates. Signed for, every night, by F. Briggs. Warden.
And pinned to the back of the board, behind the last sheet, where nobody would see it unless they lifted all the others, there was a different paper.
It was not gray. It was white. Thick, smooth, fine white paper, much too good for a disposal board in a slag room. And it had been folded in three, and unfolded, so many times that the creases had gone soft and furry. And it was not printed. It was written. In a neat, small, slanting, perfectly even hand, black ink, with every letter exactly the same height.
To the Warden, Intake Hall, Lantern Quay.
From this date, and until further notice, you will receive each night from the Hill, by closed wagon, six crates under the seal of this office. You will not open them. You will not record their contents. You will sign for them as "crates, under seal." You will see that they are carried with the night's slag to the place of disposal, and broken, and covered.
You will speak of this to no one.
By order of the Office of the Archon.
And under that, in the same small even hand, two letters. Very neat. With a small flourish under them, like a tail.
S. K.
Corin stood in front of the board in the cold blue light and looked at those two letters for a long, long time.
He did not need the Ledger. He did not need anything.
A pale man sitting on a clockmaker's counter, swinging his foot. Unwrapping a barley sugar. Folding the wrapper. Smaller. Smaller. Smaller. I do so hate untidiness. Don't you, Mister Rook?
A name in the Ledger that made Toby go white to the lips.
A slim pale figure on a riverbank, twenty years ago, fishing a kitchen knife out of the shallows with a stick, for fun.
He did not remember that last one. He did not know where it had come from. It was simply there, in his head, very clear and very small, like a picture in a drop of water, and then it was gone, and he could not get it back.
Boy, said Malakor, very quietly.
"I know."
Copy it. Quickly. And put it back exactly as it was.
He copied it. Every word. Into the back of the Ledger, standing at the board with the pencil skidding in his cold fingers, and the slag-jars glimmering on the shelf beside him, and the clouded glasses watching him from their racks. To the Warden. Six crates under the seal of this office. You will not open them. You will speak of this to no one. S. K.
And under it he wrote: Pinned behind the disposal sheets in the slag room. Every night since July. Six crates. F. Briggs signs.
F.
Then he folded the white paper again, along its soft furry creases, exactly as it had been, and pinned it back behind the last sheet, and let the others fall over it, and hung the board on its nail.
And went back to his glasses.
He cleared four.
It took him until nearly the second bell. He did not go fast. He went slowly, the way he had started, letting every crumb pass through the rod and through him and into the silver, and he looked at every one. A tavern song. A wet dog. A boat. A garden with a swing. A woman's hands, plaiting a child's hair. A cart going over a bridge. A child laughing.
None of them anybody's.
All of them somebody's.
When the fourth glass was clear, he stood in front of it for a long moment. Then he went to the shelf by the door, and unstoppered an empty slag-jar, and held it to the tip of the rod, and let the day's crumbs slide out of the silver into it in a thin gray glittering stream. He sealed it. He set it on the shelf at the end of the row. Fourteen.
Tonight, at midnight, Moggs would come.
He stood and looked at the jar for a while. Then he took the stub of pencil out of the Ledger's spine and, on the plain paper label, under the printed SLAG, very small, he wrote:
A tavern song. A wet dog. A garden with a swing. A woman plaiting a child's hair. A child laughing. I'm sorry.
He did not know who he was saying sorry to. He thought, perhaps, it was a girl called Pip.
Then he put on his coat, with the jar of violet inside it, against his ribs, still humming its one low note. And went up the stairs.
The Fiddler
He heard it on the stairs.
Halfway up, between the cold blue dark of the slag room and the warm white light of the hall, with one hand on the rough brick wall and the jar of violet humming against his ribs. A sound. Coming in from outside, through the great green doors at the far end of the hall, over the shuffle of feet and the murmur of the clerks and the soft next, next, next.
A fiddle.
Thin, and a little out of tune, and very sweet. Playing slowly. A tune that went up, and up, and paused at the top, as if it were looking at something, and came down again.
Corin stopped on the stair.
He did not know the tune. He was sure of that. He had never heard it in his life. But something in his chest had gone tight and hot and painful, like a fist closing round his heart, and he stood quite still on the brick stair with his hand flat on the wall and listened, and could not move.
He knew this feeling. Not the tune. The feeling. It was in the Ledger. On one of the very first pages, from a cold morning a lifetime ago, in his own hurried hand. Saw a busker on Lantern Bridge. He played a tune you didn't know. You stood and listened to it for a long time and you don't know why. It made your chest hurt.
Something about a fisherman and the moon.
He went up the last of the stairs.
The hall had gone quiet.
Not silent. The clerks were still murmuring at their glasses, put your hand flat, please, and think of it, and the glasses were still waking and going dark, one after another, all along the counter. But the line had gone quiet. The people in it had stopped shuffling. All down the hall, from the doors to the far end, they were standing still, with their heads turned toward the doors, listening. An old woman on one of the benches had shut her eyes. A docker near the front had taken off his cap. A little girl holding her mother's hand had begun to sway, very slightly, from foot to foot, the way children do when they hear music and do not know they are dancing.
Outside, on the top of the white steps, framed in the open green doors with the thin winter sun behind him, there was a man with a fiddle.
He was old. Thin and stooped and very shabby, in a coat with more patches than coat, and a red scarf wound round and round his neck, and fingerless gloves, and a hat with no crown. He was standing at the top of the steps beside the two watchmen, with his fiddle under his chin and his eyes shut, playing to the line. There was an upturned hat at his feet with three coppers in it.
He played the tune through once. Up and up, and pause, and down. And then again, a little louder. And at the high part, the part where the tune climbed and climbed and stopped, as if it were holding its breath, as if something at the top were begging to be let go, half the line in the hall stopped breathing with it.
And Warden Briggs dropped his pen.
Corin saw it go. He was standing at the top of the slag-room stairs, three steps from the ledger stand, and he saw the pen fall out of the Warden's thick fingers and hit the open ledger and roll, leaving a long black blot across the page, and drop off the edge of the stand onto the white tiles with a small clean click.
Briggs did not pick it up.
He was standing quite still behind his ledger stand. His heavy gray face had gone grayer. Not the gray of tiredness. The gray of wet ash, of a fire that somebody has thrown a bucket of water on. His small pale eyes were wide open, and they were looking at nothing. Not at the fiddler. Not at the doors. At a place in the air, halfway between the stand and the far wall, where there was nothing at all to see.
And his hand had come up to his chest.
Flat. Pressed hard against the front of his dark green coat, over the brass plate. WARDEN. INTAKE. BRIGGS. Pressed so hard that the knuckles had gone white. As if there were something under the brass, under the coat, under the skin, that hurt so much he had to hold it in.
The fiddle climbed. Up and up. Paused.
Briggs flinched.
It was not a small thing. It went through his whole heavy body, from his boots to the top of his head, the way a man flinches when he is struck. His shoulders jerked. His head turned aside, sharply, as if from a blow. His other hand came up and caught the edge of the ledger stand and held on, and the stand rocked on its iron foot, and the big ledger slid an inch toward the edge.
And from somewhere deep in his chest, so low that Corin barely heard it over the fiddle and the murmuring glasses, a sound came out of him. Not a word. Not a groan. Something lower than both. Like the sound an old door makes, in a house nobody has lived in for a long time, when the wind gets behind it.
The tune came down from the top. Slowly. Into the quiet part at the end.
And Briggs let go of the stand, and turned, and walked down the hall.
Not fast. He did not run. He walked, heavily, the way he always walked, past the end of the long counter, past the benches, past the line, toward the green doors. The people in the line moved back to let him by. Nobody looked at his face.
At the doors, he stopped.
The fiddler had come to the end of the tune. He opened his eyes, and lowered the fiddle, and smiled, a gap-toothed hopeful smile, at the big gray man standing in the doorway in front of him.
"Like it, guv'nor?" he said. "Old country tune. My gran used to sing it. Something about a fisherman and the—"
"Move on," said Briggs.
The fiddler's smile faltered.
"Guv'nor?"
"Move on." Briggs's voice was quite flat. Quite quiet. "You can't play here. Not on these steps. Not in front of my doors." He turned his head toward the two watchmen. "There's a rule. No hawkers, no beggars, no music, within twenty yards of the intake doors. It's on the board. Move him on."
The old watchman looked at the young one. The young one shrugged.
"Come on, grandad," said the old one, not unkindly. "You heard the Warden."
The fiddler looked at Briggs for a moment longer. Then he bent, slowly, and picked up his hat with its three coppers in it, and put the coppers in his pocket and the hat on his head, and tucked the fiddle under his arm. He went down the white steps one at a time. At the bottom, he stopped, and looked back.
"You know it, don't you, guv'nor," he said. Not cruelly. Wonderingly. "I can see it on you. You know that one."
Briggs did not answer.
The fiddler went away along the quay, past the line, toward the Salt Stairs, with his red scarf flapping. After a little way, Corin heard him start to play again. Very faint. The same tune. Getting smaller and smaller and further away, until the sound of the river covered it.
Briggs stood in the green doorway with his back to the hall until it was gone.
When he came back to his stand, his face was its ordinary gray again. Heavy and tired and closed. He bent, stiffly, and picked up his pen from the white tiles, and wiped the nib on his scarf, and looked at the long black blot across the open page of his ledger. He took up his blotter and pressed it down on it, hard, the way he had pressed it on the black scratch on Tuesday. And held it there.
He did not look at Corin.
"Four?" he said.
"Four," said Corin.
Briggs made a mark. Under the blot.
"Go on, then."
Corin did not go.
He stood beside the ledger stand in the warm white light, with his coat on and the jar against his ribs and his hands in his pockets, and looked at the Warden's bent gray head. At the old darned scarf. At the hand on the blotter, which was not quite steady. At the brass plate on the dark green coat, sticking out over his stomach like a shelf.
WARDEN. INTAKE. BRIGGS.
And at the open ledger beside the blot. At the line at the top of today's page, in the same big square heavy hand as the slag-room board, where the Warden had signed in at seven that morning, as he must have signed in every morning for twenty years.
F. Briggs.
"Warden," said Corin. "What's the F for?"
Briggs's hand stopped on the blotter.
He did not look up. For a long time he did not move at all. He stood there with his heavy head bent over his ledger and his hand pressed flat on the blotter, and the clerks murmured at the glasses, and the line shuffled forward a step, and the stove at the end of the hall roared softly.
Then he said, without lifting his head:
"Fenn."
He said it very quietly. Very roughly. As if it were a word he had not said aloud for so long that it had rusted in his mouth.
"Fenn," he said again. "That's my name. Fenn Briggs." He breathed out through his nose. "Nobody's called me that in twenty years. Not since…" He stopped. "Not since I was a soldier."
Corin did not say anything. He stood very still beside the stand.
Briggs lifted the blotter. He looked at the blot underneath. It had soaked through, into the page below, and the one below that, a long black stain like the shadow of a bridge.
"Why'd you want to know?" he said.
"It's on the slag-room board," said Corin. "Every night. F. Briggs. Warden. By the crates."
Briggs went very still.
He looked up, at last. His small pale eyes came up from the blotted ledger to Corin's face, slowly, and they were not tired anymore. They were very sharp. And underneath the sharpness, deep down, very far away, something that Corin had seen once before, on the face of an old clockmaker standing over a scorched corner of blue paper. Something that looked very much like fear.
"You've been reading my board," said Briggs.
"Yes."
"All of it?"
Corin looked at him for a long moment.
"All of it," he said.
Briggs did not say anything for a long time.
Then he closed the big ledger. Slowly. With both hands. The way you close a door on a room you do not want to look into anymore. And he took off his old gray scarf, and wound it round his neck again, tighter, and looked down the long bright hall toward the clock over the doors.
It said a quarter past two.
"Shift's done," said Briggs.
It was not done. It was not done till six. Corin knew that. Everybody in the hall knew that. The nearest clerk looked up from her glass, startled, and looked down again quickly.
Briggs did not seem to care. He took his hat down from a hook behind the stand. A battered brown hat with a greasy band. He put it on. He picked up a paper parcel from the shelf under the ledger, wrapped in string, about the size of a loaf.
"Mr. Abbot," he said, to the senior clerk at the first glass. "The hall is yours till six. Sign for the slag at midnight. You know the form."
The senior clerk opened his mouth. Then he shut it again, and nodded.
Briggs turned to Corin.
"Walk with me, Vane," he said. "If you're going to ask me about my board, you're not asking me in here."
