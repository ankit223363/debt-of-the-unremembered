Chapter 18: Father and Son, Part 2 of 5: The Wagon from the Hill
The Wagon from the Hill
They went out by the side door.
Not the great green doors at the front, where the line was, and the watchmen, and the white steps. The side door, low and plain, halfway down the hall, where the people went out after they had sold. It opened onto a narrow cobbled yard with a high wall round it, and a gate in the wall, and beyond the gate an alley that ran down between two warehouses to the river.
The people who had sold were in the yard.
Not many. A dozen. Standing about in the cold sun, in ones and twos, in no hurry to go anywhere. Corin had seen them before, the Ledger said, every day, and never looked. He looked now. A woman with a baby, rocking it, smiling down at it with a smooth calm pleasant face. An old man sitting on an upturned barrel, holding a silver coin in his open palm and turning it over and over as if he could not think what it was for. A boy of about fourteen, leaning against the wall with his hands in his pockets, whistling. Very badly. A tune that was not a tune at all. Just notes, one after another, going nowhere, like a bird that has forgotten its song and is trying to remember by guessing.
Briggs walked through them without looking at any of them, and opened the gate, and went out into the alley.
Corin followed.
The alley went down to the river in steps, and at the bottom it came out onto the wharf, and the wharf went along the water past the backs of the warehouses toward the docks. Briggs walked along it, heavily, in his battered hat, with his paper parcel under one arm and his other hand in his coat pocket. He did not talk for a long time. Corin walked beside him and did not talk either.
The river was busy again. Brown and fast and full of ice, gray plates and white, turning and grinding and bumping against the stones of the wharf. Barges were going up and down, slowly, through the ice, with men on their decks pushing it away with long poles and shouting. Gulls wheeled over them, screaming. The air smelled of tar and wet rope and coal smoke and the cold.
"July," said Briggs at last.
He did not look at Corin. He was looking straight ahead, along the wharf.
"End of June. Beginning of July. Hot. Stinking hot. The river was green." He walked on. "I came in one morning at seven and there was a man waiting for me. By my stand. Before the doors were open. I don't know how he got in. The doors were locked. I'd locked them myself the night before."
"A pale man," said Corin. "In gray. With gray gloves."
Briggs turned his head and looked at him.
"You know him."
"I know of him."
Briggs looked at him a moment longer. Then he looked ahead again.
"He was eating a sweet," he said. "Barley sugar. Out of a little tin. He gave me that letter, the white one, and he stood there while I read it, sucking his barley sugar, folding up the paper it came in. Smaller and smaller." Briggs's mouth twisted. "And when I'd read it, he said, You do understand, don't you, Warden? Not a word. Not to anyone. Not even to that boy of yours on the docks."
He stopped walking.
He stood in the middle of the wharf, in the cold sun, with the gulls screaming over his head and the ice grinding in the river beside him, and he did not move for a long time.
"That boy of yours," he said. Very low. "He knew. He knew about Felix. I've never told anybody about Felix. Not in eight years. Not one soul." His hand had come up to his chest again. "And this pale fellow I'd never seen in my life stood by my stand at seven in the morning with a sweet in his cheek and said it. That boy of yours on the docks. Like he was asking after my health."
He started walking again.
"So I sign," he said. "Every night. Crates, six. I don't open them. I don't ask. I sign."
"Who brings them?" said Corin.
Briggs did not answer at once.
They had come to the end of the warehouses. The wharf opened out here, into a wide cobbled space where three streets met the river, with a stone horse trough in the middle frozen solid, and a cab stand with no cabs on it, and a chestnut seller's brazier glowing red on the corner. Briggs stopped by the trough. He set his parcel down on the edge of it. He took his hand out of his pocket. It was shaking.
"A wagon," he said. "Closed. Black. No crest on it. No name. Two black horses. It comes down from the Hill at eleven, every night, along the river road, and into my yard by the back gate. And it waits there till Moggs comes at midnight. And they put the crates on his cart. And it goes away again."
"Who drives it?"
"A driver." Briggs looked at the frozen trough. "Just a driver. Ministry man. Gray coat. He doesn't talk either." He paused. "And two more. Sitting up behind. On the back."
He did not go on.
Corin waited.
"I don't look at them," said Briggs. Very quietly. "The two on the back. I don't look at them, Vane. I sign Moggs's sheet, and I stand by my door with my lantern, and I look at my boots till the wagon's gone." His hand pressed flat on the cold stone of the trough. "I looked at them once. The first night. And I haven't looked since."
"What did you see?"
Briggs turned his head and looked at him. And his heavy gray face, in the thin cold sun, was the face of a man who has seen something in the dark and has spent four months telling himself he did not.
"They're tall," he said. "Too tall. Taller than any man I ever saw. Seven foot, sitting down, near enough. In long gray coats, with the collars up, and hats pulled down over their faces so you can't see them." He swallowed. "And they don't move. Not ever. Not when the wagon stops. Not when the crates come off. Not when the horses stamp. They sit up there on the back of that wagon in my yard for an hour every night, stiller than the wall. Like they're waiting to be told to do something." He breathed out. "And it's cold, Vane. It's been cold every night since September. You can see my breath. You can see Moggs's breath, and the horses', and the driver's, like steam off a kettle."
He stopped.
"Theirs doesn't smoke," said Briggs.
Corin did not move.
He was standing by the frozen trough in the cold sun, with the gulls screaming and the ice grinding in the river and the chestnut seller's brazier glowing on the corner, and he was in a white room behind a clock face, with the snow-light coming through the backward numbers, and an old man with white hair sitting in the only chair, saying:
Tall. Taller than Captain Oren. Seven feet, and thin. In long gray coats, to the ankle, with high collars. So that in a street, at night, in the fog, you might think they were only very tall men, walking.
They have no faces.
Six. I built six.
"Warden," he said. And his own voice sounded very strange to him. Very far away. "Do they ever get down? Off the wagon?"
"No." Briggs looked at him. "Never. Why?"
"If they ever do," said Corin. "If they ever get down. If they ever come to your door." He swallowed. "And knock."
Briggs's eyes had gone very narrow.
"Three times," said Corin. "Slowly. If they ever knock on any door you're behind, three times, slowly, don't open it. Don't wait to see who it is. Go out the back. Go anywhere. Just go."
Briggs stared at him.
For a long moment he did not say anything at all. His face did not move. And then something in it did. Something small. A flicker, deep in the small pale eyes, there and gone, like the shadow of a bird going over a window.
"You know what they are," he said.
"I know who made them," said Corin. "And I know who they belong to." He looked at the Warden. "The same man who knows about your boy on the docks."
The chestnut seller on the corner called out, cheerful and loud, "Hot chestnuts! Hot chestnuts, a ha'penny a bag!" A gull landed on the edge of the frozen trough, an arm's length from Briggs's parcel, and looked at it with one hard yellow eye.
Briggs picked up the parcel.
He held it against his chest, under his arm, the way a man holds something he is afraid to drop. And he looked away from Corin, along the wharf, past the cab stand and the brazier, to where the wharf curved round toward the long wooden jetties of the Pike Dock, and the barges tied up there, and the cranes, and the men.
"He's working today," said Briggs. "Saturdays he does the ice barges. From the north. Unloading." He did not take his eyes off the jetties. "I take him his dinner."
He started walking.
Corin walked with him.
The Ice Barges
The Pike Dock was a long wooden jetty that stuck out into the river like a finger, with three more like it beside it, black and tarred and crusted white with frost, and between the jetties the water was full of barges.
Not ordinary barges. Ice barges. Corin had never seen one. They were long and low and flat, painted a dull dark red, and they sat very deep in the water, so deep that the brown river slopped over the edges of their decks. And they were full of ice. Not the gray broken plates of ice that turned in the river. Clean ice. Blue-white ice, clear as glass, cut into great square blocks the size of a man's chest, stacked on the decks in rows, five and six high, packed with straw and sawdust between them. In the thin cold sun the blocks shone. You could see right into them. You could see the bubbles caught inside, tiny and silver, like stars, and the faint blue in the heart of each one.
"From the north," said Briggs. "Off the mountain lakes. Past the end of the river. They cut it in the winter and float it down on the snowmelt, and sell it all summer to the big houses on the Hill for their ice-boxes." He snorted. "This year the snow came early and the price fell through the floor. Nobody wants ice when there's ice on every step. So they're unloading it into the ice-house here, to keep till summer." He looked along the jetty. "Saturday work. Pays a silver for the day, if you're strong."
There were men on the barges. A dozen. Big men, mostly, in leather aprons and leather pads strapped over one shoulder, and gloves, and iron hooks. Two men to a block. One on each side, with the hooks set into the ice, swinging it up between them off the stack and onto the shoulder of a third, who carried it, bent nearly double under the weight, up a plank from the deck to the jetty, and along the jetty to the open doors of a great stone ice-house at the landward end, where it was dark and cold and steaming.
They did not talk much. The work was too hard for talking. But they shouted, now and then. Mind your end. Mind the plank. Hup. And they swore, and spat, and slapped each other on the back when a block went up the plank and into the dark of the ice-house without falling. The foreman stood at the top of the plank with a slate and a stub of chalk, making a mark for every block. Thirty-one. Thirty-two.
Briggs stopped behind a stack of empty barrels at the head of the jetty, where he could see the plank and the barges and the ice-house doors. He did not go any closer. He did not wave, or call out. He stood with his back against the barrels and his parcel under his arm and his battered hat pulled down, and he watched.
Corin stood beside him and watched too.
And after a moment, without being told, he knew which one.
He was the one carrying.
Up the plank, from the deck of the nearest barge, with a great block of blue-white ice on the leather pad on his right shoulder, so big that it hid half his head. He was young. Twenty, perhaps. He was tall, nearly as tall as Briggs, and broad in the shoulders, but thin everywhere else, too thin, with long arms and big raw red hands that stuck out of the cuffs of a coat that was too short in the sleeves. He wore a knitted cap pulled down over his ears, gray, and under the cap his hair was brown and curly and stuck out in tufts. And at his belt, on a loop of leather, there was a hook. A dock hook. Black iron, curved, with a wooden handle worn pale.
He carried the block up the plank without stumbling. Steady. Easy. As if it weighed nothing. At the top, he did not stop for breath, the way the other carriers did. He went straight along the jetty toward the ice-house at an even, unhurried pace, his boots thudding on the frozen planks, and as he went, Corin saw his face.
He was smiling.
Not a big smile. A small one. Soft, and gentle, and quite pleasant, at the corners of his mouth. The smile of somebody who is thinking about something nice, a long way away. It did not change when he passed the foreman. It did not change when one of the other carriers shouted at him, Oi, Smiler, mind your feet. It did not change when a corner of the block on his shoulder grazed the doorpost of the ice-house and a shower of white chips sprayed down over his cap and his face and stuck in his eyelashes. He did not blink. He went into the dark of the ice-house with the block on his shoulder and the ice in his eyelashes and the soft pleasant smile on his face, and was gone.
Corin knew that smile.
He had seen it in the Ledger, a hundred times, in a hundred different hands. On every page about the intake hall. The woman's face went smooth and calm. He smiled at nothing. She looked like somebody walking out of a warm room into a pleasant afternoon.
A Slate.
Not a little emptied, like the woman in the brown shawl, who had sold one bad memory and walked out smiling. Not half-emptied, like Old Terrence, who sat by Mrs. Marta's stove and rubbed a key that was not there. Emptied. All the way down. The smile of somebody with nothing behind it. A clean glass. A swept floor. A house with every room bare, and the windows open, and the wind going through.
Corin looked at Briggs.
The Warden was standing very still against the barrels. His heavy gray face did not move. His small pale eyes were on the dark doorway of the ice-house where the young man had gone. And his hand, the one that was not holding the parcel, had come up, the way it always did. Flat. To his chest.
"Felix," said Corin.
Briggs did not look at him.
"Felix," he said. Very low. "Felix Briggs. My boy." He did not take his eyes off the doorway. "Twenty in the spring."
Felix came out of the ice-house without the block.
He came out into the thin sun with the white chips still in his eyelashes, and his empty hands swinging, and the smile, and he walked back along the jetty toward the plank, at the same even pace. He passed within ten yards of the barrels. He did not look at them. He did not look at anything. He went down the plank onto the deck of the barge, and turned his shoulder, and two men swung another block up onto it with their hooks, hup, and he took the weight without a sound and came back up the plank.
"He's strong," said Corin. He did not know why he said it.
"He was always strong." Briggs's voice was quite flat. "Even as a little lad. He could carry the coal scuttle up three flights when he was six. His mother used to say he'd be a docker or a blacksmith or a soldier, one of the three, and she'd rather he was a blacksmith because they come home at night." He was quiet a moment. "He wanted to be a teacher."
Corin looked at him.
"A teacher," said Briggs. "Of all the daft things. Twelve years old, and big as a bullock, and he wanted to stand in front of a room of little ones with a slate and a stick of chalk and teach them their letters." His mouth moved. Not a smile. "He used to teach the cat. Sit it on the table and hold up the slate and say, That's an A, Puss. A for apple. Say it." He swallowed. "Cat never learned. He never gave up."
Felix went past them again with the block on his shoulder. Smiling. The ice glittering in his eyelashes.
"Does he know you?" said Corin.
Briggs did not answer for a long time.
Then he pushed himself away from the barrels. He walked out from behind them, slowly, heavily, into the open, to the top of the plank, where the foreman stood with his slate and his chalk. And he held out the paper parcel.
"Same as always, Mr. Dorsey," he said.
The foreman was a short stout man with a red face and a cloth cap and a chalk-white thumb. He took the parcel from Briggs without looking surprised at all. He weighed it in his hand.
"Pie?" he said.
"Mutton."
"He'll like that." The foreman tucked the parcel under his arm with his slate. "He always likes the mutton." He looked at Briggs for a moment. Not unkindly. The way a man looks at another man he has seen do the same thing every Saturday for a very long time, and has stopped asking why. "Stopping to watch, Warden?"
"No," said Briggs.
"Right you are."
And Briggs turned round, and walked back to the barrels, and stood behind them again, with his back against the wood and his hands in his pockets.
The foreman waited until Felix came out of the ice-house again. Then he put two fingers in his mouth and whistled, short and sharp, and Felix turned his head, slowly, toward the sound. Still smiling.
"Smiler!" said the foreman. "Dinner! Your man's been!"
Felix came along the jetty to him. The foreman put the parcel into his big raw hands. Felix looked down at it, the way the people in the yard looked down at their silver coins, as if he were not quite sure what it was or why he had it. Then he sat down, right there, on the edge of the jetty, with his long legs dangling over the brown water and the ice grinding below them, and undid the string, very slowly, with his clumsy cold fingers, and unfolded the paper.
A pie. A big one, round and golden, with a crimped edge and a little hole in the top where the steam had come out. It was not steaming now. It had cooled on the walk. Felix picked it up in both hands and looked at it, turning it in the sun, with the smile on his face. Then he bit into it.
And chewed. Slowly. With his eyes half shut.
And then, still chewing, he lifted his head, and looked round. Along the jetty. At the barges. At the cranes. At the ice-house. At the stack of empty barrels at the head of the jetty, where a big gray man in a battered brown hat was standing with his hands in his pockets.
Felix looked at him.
And his smile got a little bigger. Just a little. And he lifted one hand, the one with the pie in it, the way a polite young man lifts his hand to a stranger who has done him a kindness.
"Thank you, sir," he called. His voice was pleasant and light and quite empty. "It's very good. Thank you."
Briggs lifted one hand out of his pocket.
Just a little. Just to the height of his chest. And let it fall.
Felix turned back to his pie.
And Corin, standing beside the Warden behind the barrels, saw that the big gray man's face had not changed at all. Not one line of it. It was quite still, and quite gray, and quite closed. It was the face of a man who had stood behind these barrels every Saturday for eight years and done exactly this, and had worn the feeling off it, the way a step is worn smooth by feet.
But down the side of it, from the corner of one small pale eye, into the gray stubble on his heavy jaw, there was a long wet line.
"Every Saturday," said Briggs. He did not wipe it. "He thanks me every Saturday. Very polite. Very nice manners. His mother taught him that."
He watched his son eat.
"He thinks I'm a man who brings pies," he said.
