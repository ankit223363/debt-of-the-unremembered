Chapter 18: Father and Son, Part 3 of 5: Forty Silver
Forty Silver
They sat on the barrels.
Briggs did not ask. He simply sat down, heavily, on the end of an upturned barrel behind the stack, where he could still see the plank and the jetty and his son, and Corin sat down on the next one. The barrels were cold and damp and smelled of salt fish. The sun had gone behind the warehouses. The light on the river was going from white to gray, and the ice in the water had gone from white to blue, and the wind coming off it had teeth.
On the edge of the jetty, Felix finished his pie. He folded the paper it had come in, very carefully, into a square, and put it in his pocket. Then he got up, and went back to the plank, and two men swung a block of ice onto his shoulder, and he carried it up.
Briggs watched him.
"Nell," he said.
Corin waited.
"My wife." Briggs did not take his eyes off the plank. "Nell. She had a cough. The winter Felix was eleven. Just a cough, at first. Then it got into her chest, and it wouldn't come out. The wet kind. The kind you hear through the wall at night." He was quiet a moment. "Doctor said there was a medicine. From the south. Expensive. Very. A silver a bottle, and she'd need a bottle a week, all winter, and all the next."
"And you didn't have it."
"I was a warden's man, then. Not a warden. Under-porter. Eight coppers a day." Briggs's mouth twisted. "No. I didn't have it."
He watched Felix go into the ice-house.
"So I borrowed it," he said. "From the Bank. They lend. That's what they do, upstairs, in the other office, the one with the carpet. They lend to anybody. They're very kind. Very easy. They don't even ask what it's for." His voice was flat. "They only ask what you've got."
Corin did not say anything. He thought of a brass stamp, in a small upright perfect hand. The Bank lends. It does not buy. Clause four.
"Forty silver," said Briggs. "Over the winter and the spring. Forty bottles. And she got better. She did. By the summer she could walk to the market. She could sing again. She used to sing in the kitchen, Nell did. Terrible voice. Like a gate." Something that was almost a smile moved his heavy mouth. "And then in the autumn the Bank wrote. Very polite letter. The term of your loan has ended. Kindly present yourself at the intake hall, Lantern Quay, at your earliest convenience, to settle."
The wind came off the river. Briggs pulled his old darned scarf tighter round his neck.
"I knew what that meant," he said. "I worked there. I'd stood at the doors and watched them settle for twelve years. You go in, and you put your hand on the glass, and they take what they reckon it's worth. Forty silver. That's not a bad memory. That's not one afternoon. Forty silver's a lot of a man." He breathed out. "I sat up all night at the kitchen table, working it out. What I'd lose. What forty silver of me looked like. And I thought, it'll be Nell. It'll be her. All of her. The first time I saw her, and the wedding, and the boy being born, and her singing in the kitchen like a gate. That's what I've got that's worth forty. That's all I've got."
He was quiet for a long time.
"And I thought, well. She's better. She's alive. That's what it was for. I'll go in on Monday, on my day off, and I'll do it, and I'll come home, and there'll be a woman in my kitchen I don't know, singing, and I'll learn her again. People do. I've seen them. They come in married and go out strangers and some of them, some of them, learn each other again." His big hands had closed into fists on his knees. "So I told Nell. That night. At the table. And she cried. And I told her not to. And we went to bed."
He stopped.
"And Felix was on the stairs," said Corin.
Briggs did not answer.
He did not need to.
Out on the jetty, Felix came out of the ice-house again. Empty-handed. Smiling. He walked back along the planks toward the barge.
"Twelve," said Briggs. Very low. "He'd just turned twelve. The week before. We'd had a cake. Nell made it, with an egg in it, and he blew out a candle, and he wished for a slate of his own. A real one. With a wooden frame." He swallowed. "He was on the stairs, in his nightshirt, with his ear to the crack. Like boys are. And he heard it all."
The ice ground in the river.
"And on the Saturday," said Briggs, "two days before I was going in, I had a double turn at the hall. Six in the morning till ten at night. And while I was there, at the doors, at the front, in my under-porter's coat, watching the line, Felix walked in at the side."
Corin did not move.
"By the yard gate. Where the ones who've sold come out. He'd come in the wrong way, by the way out, so nobody stopped him, and nobody saw him. And he walked up to a glass at the far end. The third from the end. And he put his hand on it."
Briggs's voice did not change. It was flat, and low, and very steady, the voice of a man reading from a page he has read so many times that the words have no meaning left in them.
"He was twelve," he said. "A child can't sell, Vane. Not on his own. Not under fourteen. That's the law. There has to be a parent, or a guardian, or the debt has to be his. But he was big. He was always big. He looked fifteen. And the clerk on that glass was new. First week. A girl. Frightened of her own shadow. And Felix stood at her glass and said, I've come to settle a debt. Forty silver. In the name of Briggs." Briggs breathed out. "And she looked in the book, and there it was. Briggs. Forty silver. Due. And she didn't look at him. They don't. They're told not to. They look at the glass."
He was quiet.
"And she said, What will you offer, please? And he said—" Briggs stopped. His fists on his knees had gone white. "He said, Everything I've got. Is that enough?"
Corin shut his eyes.
"It was enough," said Briggs. "A boy's whole life. Twelve years. Every day of it. His mother's face, and mine, and the cat, and the cake with the egg in it, and the candle, and the slate. Every letter he ever learned. Every time he ever laughed." His voice had gone very quiet. "It came to forty silver and four coppers. The girl gave him the four coppers. Change."
The light was going. On the barges, the men had lit a lantern, and hung it on a pole at the top of the plank, and it swung in the wind and threw its yellow light up and down the jetty.
"I didn't know," said Briggs. "Not that night. I came home at eleven and Nell was asleep and his door was shut and I went to bed. And in the morning—"
He stopped.
He sat on the barrel with his big hands on his knees and his battered hat pulled down and the gray light on his gray face, and for a long time he did not go on. Corin did not ask him to. He sat on the next barrel and waited, with the cold coming through his coat and the jar against his ribs, humming its one low note.
"In the morning he came down the stairs," said Briggs at last. "In his clothes. All washed and buttoned, very neat, the way Nell could never get him to do. And he stood at the bottom of the stairs, in the kitchen door, and looked at us. Me at the table. Nell at the stove." He swallowed. "And he smiled. That smile. The one he's got now. And he said, Good morning, sir. Good morning, madam. Thank you for letting me stay. Is there anything I can do to help?"
The lantern swung on its pole. Out on the jetty, Felix went up the plank with a block of ice on his shoulder, into the yellow light, and out of it.
"Nell screamed," said Briggs. "I've never heard a sound like it. Not before, not since. Not in twenty years at those doors. She screamed and screamed and he stood there in the doorway with that smile and said, I'm sorry, madam. I'm sorry. Have I done something wrong?"
He put his hand over his face.
Not to cry. Corin could see that. He was not crying. He put his big thick hand over his face the way you put your hand over a lamp, to keep the light out of your eyes. And he sat there like that, quite still.
"There was a note in his room," he said, from behind his hand. "On the table by his bed. In his writing. The last thing he ever wrote, I suppose, before he went in. Big round letters. The way he taught the cat." He took his hand away. "Dear Mam and Dad. I've paid it. Now Dad won't have to forget you. Love from Felix."
The wind came off the river. The lantern swung.
"And four coppers," said Briggs. "On the table. Next to the note. In a little pile."
Corin did not say anything for a long time.
There was nothing to say. He knew that. There was not one word in the Ledger, or in any Ledger, or in any book in the Ministry of Records or the Deep Archive or anywhere else in the world, that would have been worth saying.
So he sat on the cold barrel beside the Warden, and watched Felix Briggs go up and down the plank in the lantern light, with the ice on his shoulder and the smile on his face, and said nothing.
"Nell died that winter," said Briggs after a while. "The cough came back. I couldn't borrow again. The Bank won't lend twice to a man who's settled by… by family. It's a rule." He looked at his hands. "She wasn't sorry, at the end. She said she'd rather go knowing him than live with the boy who called her madam."
"And Felix?"
"Felix went to the docks." Briggs nodded at the jetty. "There's a dockers' house on Pike Lane. Bunks. A copper a night. He went there the week after the funeral, all on his own, very polite, and asked for work. Said he didn't know his name, and could they give him one. And they called him Smiler. And he's been there ever since." His voice was flat. "He's a good worker. Never late. Never drunk. Never complains. The foreman says he's the best man on the dock."
He watched his son.
"And every Tuesday," said Briggs, "he walks up the quay, past my doors, and stands in my line, and comes to the third glass from the end. The same one. And puts his hand on it. And sells whatever he's got that week. Whatever little he's made since last Tuesday. The taste of a pie, maybe. A gull he watched. A barge going by." He swallowed. "There's never much. There's never enough to make the glass light up properly. Two coppers. Every week. For eight years."
"Why?"
"Because it's the only thing he remembers how to do," said Fenn Briggs. "Selling. It's the last thing he ever learned. He walked into my hall and put his hand on my glass and sold himself, and it went in so deep that it's the only thing he's got left." He was quiet for a long moment. "Every Tuesday. And I stand at my stand by the slag-room stairs, and I watch him do it. And I sign the book."
He looked at Corin, at last.
His small pale eyes were quite dry.
"That's my boy," he said. "That's Felix. That's what forty silver of a Briggs looks like."
The Soldier Who Whistled
The foreman's whistle went, out on the jetty. Short and sharp. Last barge. The carriers groaned and swore and went down the plank again into the lantern light.
Briggs did not move from his barrel.
"You knew my name," said Corin. "A week ago. The first day. When I told you. You said, Knew a Vane once. A teacher. A schoolhouse with a bell. A big oak tree."
Briggs did not look at him.
"I don't remember saying it," said Corin. "I read it. In my book. And under it I wrote, A schoolhouse. A bell. An oak. And I didn't know why my hands were shaking." He took the Ledger out of his coat. He did not open it. He held it on his knee, in both gloved hands. "Warden. You were a soldier. Twenty years ago. You said so. In the hall. Not since I was a soldier."
Briggs's jaw tightened under the gray stubble.
"Were you at Oakhaven?"
The Warden did not answer for a long time.
Out on the jetty, Felix came up the plank with the last-but-one block of ice on his shoulder, into the swinging yellow light of the lantern and out of it again, into the blue dusk. The ice-house doors stood open at the end of the jetty, black, breathing out cold white mist like a mouth.
"Yes," said Briggs.
He said it to the river. Not to Corin.
"Yes. I was at Oakhaven."
He did not tell it in order. He could not. Corin understood that at once. It came out of him in pieces, like the slag out of a glass. A bit here. A bit there. With long silences between, where he sat on the barrel with his hands on his knees and stared at the river, and Corin could see him reaching, down and down into the dark, for something that was not there anymore. And not finding it. And reaching again.
"I was eighteen," he said. "New. Green as grass. Six months in. They marched us up from the capital in the summer, three companies, up the long road into the hills. Past the end of the maps, the older men said. I didn't know what that meant. I thought it was a joke."
A silence.
"There was a valley," he said. "With a river. And a village. And a tree." His hand had come up to his chest. "Big. The biggest tree I ever saw. In the middle of everything. Like the village had grown up round it, like moss round a stone."
The great oak, thought Corin. Its branches spread out over the houses like a hen's wings. Pip's charcoal on a whitewashed wall. An old shepherd's memory, on a hill, a long time ago.
He did not say it.
"And a school," said Briggs. "With a bell. I remember the bell. It rang in the mornings. For the little ones." He was quiet. "And a teacher."
Corin sat very still.
"A woman," said Briggs. "Not young. Not old. Dark hair. Ink on her fingers. She brought water out to us. To the soldiers. On the first hot day, when we were standing in the square in our coats, sweating like pigs. Nobody else did. The village didn't like us. They looked at us out of their windows. But she came out of the schoolhouse with a bucket and a ladle and went down the line, giving us water, one by one, like we were her children." He swallowed. "I said thank you. And she said, You're welcome, son. You look too young to be carrying that pike."
"What was her name?"
"I don't know." Briggs shook his heavy head slowly. "I never knew. I never asked. You don't, at eighteen. You don't think they're people." His hand pressed harder on his chest. "But the others called her the teacher. And somebody said her man had been emptied, in the town, the year before, for a debt. And she lived on her own by the river with her two."
"Two?"
"A boy and a girl." Briggs's voice had gone very low. "I saw them once. On the step of the house by the river. The evening before. The boy was cleaning a soldier's boots. A big lad, he was, the soldier. And the girl was sitting in his lap, the soldier's, listening to him sing, with her hands on her chest." He stopped. "Little thing. Four. Braids. And the boy was serious. Very serious. Like a little old man. He had the same eyes as his mother."
Corin could not speak.
He sat on the cold barrel with the Ledger on his knee and he could not speak, or move, or breathe properly. His hands on the leather cover had begun to shake again, through the doctor's tonic, through everything. He looked down at them. He made them stop.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
You were born in Oakhaven.
Briggs had turned his head. He was looking at Corin. At his face. For a long, long moment, in the blue dusk, with the lantern swinging behind him on the jetty. And his small pale eyes were very wide.
"Saints," he said. Hardly aloud. "Saints and all their fishes. You've got her eyes."
The last block of ice went up the plank.
Briggs watched it go. He did not look at Corin again for a while. He sat with his big hands on his knees and his hat pulled down and his breath going up white in the dusk.
"The night," said Corin. Very quietly. "The night of the registration. What happened?"
Briggs's hands tightened.
"I don't know," he said. "I don't. Most of it's gone." He breathed out hard through his nose. "I sold it, Vane. The year after. The winter after. I came back down to the city and I couldn't sleep. Every night. I'd shut my eyes and it would be there. And one day I walked into the intake hall, the old one, before they moved it here, and I put my hand on the glass, and I said, That valley. All of it. Take it." He was quiet a moment. "They gave me six silver. I bought a coat."
He looked down at his dark green coat. A size too small.
"Not this one," he said. "That one wore out."
"But you remember the teacher," said Corin. "And the bell. And the tree."
"Bits," said Briggs. "Bits the glass didn't take. Slag." His mouth twisted. "Like the crumbs you clear off my glasses every day. The bits that fall off." He shook his head slowly. "The teacher with her bucket. The boy and the girl on the step. The bell." He paused. "And the end."
"The end?"
Briggs did not answer at once.
When he did, his voice had changed. It had gone quieter. Younger. As if it were coming from a long way back, from a boy of eighteen standing in an empty square in the gray before dawn.
"They left us behind," he said. "Eleven of us. The ones who'd asked questions. Our companies marched up to the ridge in the night and didn't wake us. And we stood round the well in the morning with the village empty and the windows dark and I said, Sergeant, where is everybody?" He swallowed. "And the sergeant told us."
"Dunmore," said Corin.
Briggs's head came round. Fast. His eyes had gone very sharp.
"How d'you know that name?"
Corin did not answer. Not yet.
"Go on," he said. "Please."
Briggs looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked back at the river.
"There was a wall," he said. "Coming. Up the valley. From the ridge. Gray. Like fog, but it wasn't fog. Like the sky had come down and was crawling along the ground toward us." His voice was very low. "And everywhere it went, it took things. I don't know how else to say it. It went over a field and the field was still there but it wasn't anybody's anymore. It went over a cow and the cow stood there and didn't know it was a cow." He breathed out. "And it was coming for the village. For the people. For the children they'd taken in the night, to the registration, in the wagons."
The lantern on the jetty swung. Out on the planks, the carriers were coming in, the last of them, slapping each other on the back.
"And there was a soldier," said Briggs.
He stopped.
He sat on the barrel with his mouth a little open, as if the next words had stuck in it. His hand on his chest had closed into a fist, gripping the cloth of his coat.
"A big one," he said at last. "The one from the step. With the girl in his lap. I can't remember his name. I've never been able to. It's right there, and I can't get it." His face worked. "He whistled. All the time. Marching, eating, cleaning his kit. The sergeant was always on at him for it. And that morning, with the wall coming, he walked down to the tree. The big tree. In the middle of the village. And he put his back against it. And he—"
Briggs stopped again.
"He made the sky go gold," he said.
Corin did not breathe.
"Out of his own hands," said Briggs. "Gold. Like the sun coming up. It went up out of him and up and up and spread out over the top of everything and came down on every side like a bell. Over the village. Over the tree. Over all of us." His voice cracked. "And the gray wall hit it. And stopped. And couldn't come in."
The ice ground in the river. The gulls had gone quiet.
"And all the time," said Briggs, "all the time he was doing it, with the gold coming out of him and his face going white and the gray beating on the outside like rain on a window—" He swallowed. "He was whistling. That tune. Under his breath. The one the fiddler played. The fisherman and the moon. Over and over. Like it was the only thing holding him up."
He put his hand over his eyes.
"I sold everything," he said, from behind it. "Everything. The valley and the night and the wall and the children and that man's face. I sold it all for a coat." His hand did not move. "But the glass wouldn't take the tune. The clerk tried three times. She said it was the strangest thing she'd ever seen. Everything else came out of me like water. And the tune just… stayed. Right there." His other hand pressed his chest. "Every time I hear it, it's like somebody's put a knife in me and turned it."
Corin sat on the barrel for a long time.
He was thinking of a whitewashed wall in a crypt under a chapel with no roof. Of charcoal hills, and a river, and an oak with every leaf drawn. And a great curving line of soft chalky gold, from hill to hill. And an old voice, very old, out of a little girl's mouth.
The king hath put the sky over us. And they shall not come in.
Not twenty years ago. Long, long before. An old shepherd on a hill, with a staff and a child, watching a man in a plain coat lift his hands in a field.
The same valley. The same tree. The same gold.
Twice, he thought. It happened twice.
Once, a long time ago, with a king.
And once, twenty years ago, with a soldier who whistled.
He did not know what it meant. He did not know if it meant anything at all. He wrote it down later, in the Ledger, in big letters, with a box round it, and stared at it for a long time.
