Chapter 18: Father and Son, Part 5 of 5: The Song on the Jetty
The Song on the Jetty
They were halfway up the quay when they heard it.
Briggs had been walking fast. Faster than Corin had ever seen him walk. Head down, hands in his pockets, his heavy boots ringing on the frozen cobbles, the way a man walks when he wants to be somewhere else before something catches him up. Corin had to stretch his legs to keep beside him.
The quay was nearly dark. The tall warehouses on the left stood shoulder to shoulder against the sky, black and blind, their big loading doors shut and barred for the night. Here and there a small window was lit high up under the roof, where a watchman sat with his stove. On the right was the river, and the jetties going out into it one after another like the teeth of a comb, each with its one lantern at the end, swinging and creaking in the wind. The water between them was black, with gray shapes of ice turning slowly in it. The frost had come down hard. Every rope on every bollard was white with it, and stiff as wire.
And then, behind them, from the far end of the quay, from the direction of Pike Dock, a fiddle.
Briggs stopped walking.
He stopped so suddenly that Corin went on two steps without him and had to turn back. The Warden was standing in the middle of the cobbles, quite still, with his head a little on one side. Not turning round. Not yet. Just standing, the way a deer stands in a wood when it hears a twig break.
The tune came along the quay to them on the wind. Thin, and a little out of tune, and very sweet.
Up, and up. And a pause at the top.
And down.
"No," said Briggs. Very quietly. To nobody.
Then he turned round and began to walk back the way they had come.
The fiddler was on the end of the Pike Dock jetty.
Corin saw him from a long way off. A small, thin, stooped shape in the light of the jetty lantern, with a red scarf, and a hat with no crown, and a fiddle under his chin. He was standing on the very last plank, right at the end, with the black river on three sides of him and the ice going past below his boots. His back was to the quay. He was playing to the water. Or to the barge, which sat low and dark beside the jetty with its hatches still open. Or to nobody at all.
The carriers had finished. The last blocks were up on the quay, stacked on the sledges, waiting for Monday, glittering in the lantern light like a pile of cut glass. Mr. Dorsey had come out of his little brick office with a lamp and was going along the stacks, counting, with his pipe in his teeth. He looked up at the fiddler once, and frowned, and went on counting.
The men were going home. Up the plank from the barge, slapping their arms, banging their frozen gloves together, their breath going up in white clouds. Calling to each other. Night, Joe. Night, Sam. See you Monday, if I live. One by one they came off the jetty and up onto the quay and turned left, toward Pike Lane, and the warm lights of the dockers' house, and the tavern on the corner.
Felix was the last.
He came up the plank out of the barge's hold with one more block of ice on his shoulder. The very last one. Somebody must have left it, down in the dark, and he had gone back for it without being asked, because that was the kind of worker Smiler was. He came up slowly, carefully, with his free hand on the rope rail, the leather pad on his shoulder dark with wet, the block of ice gleaming blue-white against the side of his head. His cheeks were red with cold. His brown curls stuck out from under his gray knitted cap in little tufts, stiff with frost. And he was smiling. The same smile. Wide and easy and kind and empty, like a window in a house where nobody lives.
He was halfway up the plank when the tune came round to the high part again.
And Felix stopped.
Corin had come to the top of the jetty. Briggs was beside him, one hand gripping the frozen rail so hard that Corin could hear the frost crackle under his glove. Neither of them moved. Neither of them breathed.
Felix was standing on the plank, halfway between the barge and the jetty, with the ice on his shoulder and the black river moving under him. He had not turned round. He had not looked at the fiddler. He was looking at nothing, straight ahead of him, at the planks of the jetty in the lantern light. His mouth was a little open.
The fiddle climbed. Up, and up.
Paused.
And Felix hummed.
It was very soft. Corin would not have heard it at all if the wind had not dropped at that moment, the way it sometimes does on a river at night, all at once, as if somebody had shut a door. In the sudden stillness the sound came to him quite clearly across the water. Three notes. Low and rough and a little out of tune, in a young man's voice that had never sung anything in eight years. The three notes that came after the pause at the top. The three notes that brought the tune down.
Mm. Mm. Mmm.
The fiddler had heard it too. The bow stopped on the strings. The old man turned round on the end of the jetty, slowly, with the fiddle still under his chin, and looked at the young docker standing on the plank with the ice on his shoulder.
Felix's smile had gone.
It did not fall off his face. It did not break. It just slipped. A little. The way a coat slips off a peg when the hook is loose. For one moment, one single moment in the swinging lantern light, the corners of his mouth went down, and his eyes, which had been wide and blue and empty all afternoon, narrowed. As if he were trying to see something a long way off. Something very small, at the end of a very long road, in the dark.
His free hand came off the rope rail. It came up, slowly, to his chest. And pressed there. Flat.
The same way. Exactly the same way. Corin had seen it four times that day, on another man, in another place.
Beside him, Fenn Briggs made a sound.
"I know that one," said Felix.
He said it quite loudly, and quite clearly, in a puzzled voice, to nobody, to the night. The way a man says something when he wakes up and does not know where he is.
"I know that one," he said again. Softer. "Don't I?" He frowned at the jetty. "Somebody used to—"
He stopped.
The frown stayed on his face for a breath. Two breaths. Three. He stood on the plank, with the ice on his shoulder and his hand on his chest, and he reached for it. Corin could see him reach. Down and down into the dark inside himself, after something that was not there. The same way his father had reached, on the barrel, an hour before. The same face.
And not finding it.
The frown went away. Slowly. Like breath going off a cold window.
The smile came back.
"Sorry," said Felix, to nobody. And he laughed, a little, the easy empty laugh. "Daft. Miles away." And he put his hand back on the rope rail, and shifted the ice on his shoulder, and came on up the plank to the jetty, and carried the last block of ice along the planks past the old fiddler, and nodded to him politely, and set it down on the end of the nearest sledge with the others. Carefully. Square. The way Smiler always did.
Then he straightened up, and rolled his shoulders, and came back along the jetty toward the quay. Toward them.
Briggs could not move.
Corin saw it. The Warden was standing at the top of the jetty in the lantern light with one hand on the rail and the other hand at his side, and his face had gone the color of the frost. He had meant to be gone before Felix came off. He had said so. I don't like him to see me twice in one day. But he had turned back for the fiddle. And now he could not run, and he could not hide, and he could not do anything at all except stand there and watch his son walk toward him along the frozen planks with that smile on his face.
Felix saw them.
He slowed. Then he smiled wider, in a pleased, friendly way, and touched his cap.
"Evening, sir," he said. "You're the gentleman with the pie."
Briggs opened his mouth. Nothing came out.
"It was a good pie," said Felix. "Thank you. I meant to say. Mutton. I don't often get mutton." He looked from Briggs to Corin and back, still smiling, with no idea at all that anything was wrong. "Was it you knocked a pen down, sir? This afternoon? Up at the Lantern Quay?" He frowned a little. "No. No, that's not right. I wasn't up there today." He shook his head and laughed. "Sorry. I get muddled. They say I'm a bit muddled. But I'm a good worker. You ask Mr. Dorsey."
"I know," said Briggs.
It came out as a whisper. Felix did not seem to notice.
"Funny old tune, that," he said, nodding back over his shoulder at the fiddler. "Did you hear it, sir? Gave me a turn. I had a feeling I'd heard it before. Somewhere. A long time ago." He laughed again. "But I can't have. I haven't been anywhere. I've always been on the docks."
He waited, politely, in case the gentleman with the pie wanted to say something. The gentleman with the pie did not.
"Well," said Felix. "Goodnight, sir. Goodnight, sir." He touched his cap to each of them in turn. "Mind the ice on the steps. It's bad tonight."
And he walked past them, up onto the quay, and turned left, toward Pike Lane. His boots rang on the frozen cobbles. His gray cap went along under the warehouse windows, in and out of the little pools of light, getting smaller. Corin heard him begin to whistle. Not the fiddler's tune. Nothing. Just a few notes, up and down, the way a young man whistles walking home after work, without thinking about it at all.
At the corner of Pike Lane he went round, and was gone.
Briggs sat down on the frozen planks.
He did not decide to. Corin could see that he did not decide to. His knees simply stopped holding him, all at once, the way a sack of flour goes down when the rope is cut. He sat down hard on the planks at the top of the jetty, with his back against the bollard, and the frost on the bollard cracked and fell around him like sugar.
And he wept.
Not loudly. Corin had thought, if Fenn Briggs ever wept, that it would be loud. A big man's crying, rough and shaking, like a dog howling. It was not. It was almost silent. He sat with his knees drawn up and his big arms wrapped around them and his face pressed down onto his knees, into the cloth of his trousers, and his heavy shoulders shook, and that was all. No sound. Just the shaking. And every now and then, a long, slow, terrible breath in, as if he were trying to pull the whole night into his chest and could not get it to fit.
Corin knelt down beside him.
He did not touch him. He did not say anything. He did not know what to say, and he knew that there was nothing, and he knew that the Warden would not want him to try. So he knelt on the frozen planks beside the big man in the small green coat, and he stayed there, close enough that Briggs would know he was there, and he waited.
The lantern swung over them. The river went past under the jetty, black and cold, with the ice turning in it.
"He hummed it," said Briggs, into his knees. His voice was thick and wet and very small. "Did you hear? He hummed it. He knew the end."
"I heard," said Corin.
"Nell used to sing it to him. When he was a baby. Every night. At the window. I'd forgotten that. I'd forgotten that, Vane, I'd forgotten it till just now." He lifted his face. It was wet and red and crumpled, like a child's, and his small pale eyes were swimming. "She learned it off me. I used to whistle it, in the first years after. I didn't know where it came from. I didn't know it was his. The soldier's. I just whistled it, because it was stuck in me and the glass wouldn't take it. And she liked it, and she learned it, and she sang it to our boy." He took a breath that shook all the way down. "And he sold her. And he sold me. And he sold the cat and the letters and his own name. Everything. Everything he had. For forty silver and four coppers."
He pressed his fist to his chest.
"But not that," he said. "Not that. The glass couldn't take it off me. And it couldn't take it off him either."
Corin felt something go through him. Cold and bright, like a needle of ice.
The glass wouldn't take the tune.
Twice now. Two men. A father and a son. Twenty years apart. The same tune. The same glass.
He did not know what it meant. He did not know if it meant anything. But he thought of the jar against his ribs, humming its one low note. Of Pip's empty eyes and the old shepherd's voice coming out of her. Of the gold going up over a valley, twice, and a man with his back to an oak, whistling, whistling, as if the tune were the only thing holding him up.
Perhaps it had been.
Perhaps it still was.
The fiddler came along the jetty.
He came slowly, with his fiddle under his arm and his crownless hat in his hand, and he stopped a little way off from them, the way you stop near a man who is crying, not too close. In the lantern light he looked even older than he had on the white steps of the intake hall. His face was all lines, like a map that has been folded too many times. His eyes were very bright.
"I'm sorry, guv'nor," he said gently. "I didn't mean to upset you. Not twice in one day."
Briggs did not look up.
The old man stood there a moment longer. Then he held out his hat to Corin. Not begging. Just holding it out, upside down, the way he would have held it on the steps. There was nothing in it.
Corin put his hand in his pocket. He had three coppers. He took one out and dropped it into the hat. It made a small dull sound on the old felt.
And as the hat tipped toward the lantern, Corin saw the inside of it.
The lining had been white, once, a long time ago. Now it was the color of weak tea, stained with grease and sweat and years. But somebody had drawn on it. In chalk. Small, near the band, where nobody would see it unless they were looking down into the hat. A shape Corin had seen before. On a kitchen door, in an alley behind the Bourse, on Thursday night, in a pool of lamplight.
An eye. Wide open.
The fiddler saw him looking. He did not pull the hat back. He did not say anything about it. He just looked at Corin for a long moment, with his bright old eyes, and then he put the hat on his head and settled it with both hands.
"A young fellow gave me a silver," he said, quite casually, to the river. "Last week. Up by the Bourse. A silver, if you'll believe it. To learn that tune off me proper, and play it. Up and down the quays. Wherever there's people." He tucked his fiddle more firmly under his arm. "Said there's folk who've lost things. Said the tune might help them look."
"Who was he?" said Corin.
The fiddler smiled his gap-toothed smile.
"Didn't ask," he said. "It was a good silver." He touched his hat brim. "Goodnight, gentlemen. Mind the ice."
And he went away along the quay, the other way, toward the Salt Stairs. After a little while, Corin heard him begin to play again. Not the moon tune. Something quick and jolly, a jig, the kind of thing people dance to at weddings. It went away into the dark, getting fainter, until the river covered it.
Corin did not go back to Saint Bride's.
Not yet. He walked Briggs to the end of Tallow Row first, because the big man could not walk straight, and kept stopping, and putting his hand on the walls. They did not talk. At the corner, under a lamp, Briggs stopped for the last time and stood looking down the narrow street toward a little house with a green door and no light in its windows.
"Half past ten," he said. His voice was the Warden's voice again. Rough. Tired. Steady enough. "The side door. Not before."
"Not before."
"And don't let them see you."
"I won't."
Briggs nodded. He put his hand on his chest, on the left side, where the tin was. He held it there for a moment. Then he walked away down Tallow Row without looking back, and Corin heard the green door open, and close, and the bolt go across. And then another bolt. And then a third.
He stood under the lamp and took out the Ledger.
His hands were steady now. That surprised him. He opened the book against the cold lamp-post, in the circle of yellow light, and found the pencil, and turned to the clean page after TWICE, and wrote.
Saturday. Pike Dock. After dark.

He put his hand on his chest. Same as his father. His smile went away. For about three breaths. He said, "I know that one. Somebody used to—" Then it was gone.

He stopped. He looked at what he had written. Then, under it, in smaller letters, he added one more line.
He thought he knew it. For three breaths, he was nearly someone.
He closed the Ledger and put it back inside his coat, against his heart, next to the brown jar and the cold silver rod. He put the key Briggs had given him in his glove, against his palm, where he would feel it. He had two coppers left.
Then he turned his collar up, and walked away from the lamp, back toward the river, and the long black roof of the Lantern Quay.
Behind him, across the water, Old Tide was striking seven. Three quarters of an hour late, and not minding at all.
It was a little after a quarter to eight. He had nearly three hours to wait.
He found that he was humming. Very softly. Three notes. Low and rough, and a little out of tune.
He did not know where he had learned them.
