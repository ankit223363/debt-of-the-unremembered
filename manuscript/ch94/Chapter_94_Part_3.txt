Chapter 94: He Knows Now, Part 3 of 5: The Fifth Step
The Fifth Step
The house on Mercer Street was tall and narrow and gray, squeezed in between a lawyer's and a shop that sold maps, and it had a black front door with a brass knocker in the shape of a fist holding a ring, and nobody ever used the knocker. Kross had a key. Wren had a key too. She had been given it on her first day, two years and five months ago, by Mrs. Oddie, without a word, on a loop of string.
Mrs. Oddie opened the door anyway, before Kross could get his key out.
She was standing in the hall when it swung open, as if she had been standing there all morning. Which, Wren thought, she probably had. A short, wide woman in a black dress and a white apron, with gray hair scraped back under a white cap, and arms like hams, folded across her chest. She had cooked for Kross for eighteen years. She had never, in Wren's hearing, said more than four words together.
She looked at Kross. At his face. At the mud on his coat.
She unfolded her arms. She held out her hands. Kross took off his coat and gave it to her, and she took it, and held it up, and looked at the mud on it, and her mouth went thin.
Then she looked at Wren.
She looked for a long time. At Wren's face, and at her hands, and at the white rose with no thorns that Wren was still holding, without knowing she was holding it.
Then Mrs. Oddie came across the hall and put one of her great ham arms round Wren's shoulders, and pulled her in against her white apron, hard, for about as long as it takes to say a name. And let her go. And turned and went off down the back stairs to the kitchen with Kross's coat over her arm, without saying anything at all.
Wren stood in the hall and could not move for a while.
The hall smelled of beeswax and coal smoke and peppermint. It always smelled of peppermint. There was a little glass dish of them on the hall table, the white ones, striped with pink, the way there was a little glass dish of them on every table in the house. Wren took one without thinking and put it in her mouth. It was sharp and sweet and cold. It tasted of every evening she had ever come home to this house.
She had not had one in fifteen days.
She stood in the hall with the peppermint in her mouth and the rose in her hand and began, very quietly, to cry.
Kross did not say anything. He did not look at her. He went past her to the stairs and up them. Not to the top. He went up four steps and sat down on the fifth.
The stairs were narrow and dark and very steep, with a worn red carpet held down by brass rods, and a banister of black wood. And the fifth step was different from the others. You could see it, if you looked. The carpet on it was worn almost through, in a long oval in the middle, the shape of a man sitting. Eighteen years of a man sitting. And the wood of the step under the carpet, where it showed at the edges, was worn too. Smooth and pale. Like the step of a church.
Kross sat on it with his back against the wall and his long legs stretched down across the four steps below, and his hands on his knees, and looked at nothing.
Wren stopped crying after a while. She wiped her face on her sleeve. She did not feel better. She did not feel anything much.
She looked down at the rose in her hand.
For your book. Press it between the pages. It'll keep.
She took the brown book out of her coat. It was small, and thick, and the brown cover was soft and dark with handling. She had bought it on Quill Row for fourpence when she was fifteen. She had filled three of them since. This was the fourth. She opened it to the middle, to the place where her account of the Glass began. Monday. The Ridges. In her own plain hand.
She held the rose over the page.
And could not put it down.
She stood in the hall with the book open in one hand and the rose in the other, over it, for a long time. She tried. Her hand would not do it. She did not want it there. She did not want it in her account of the Glass, between the farmer under his sack and the man on his knees under the dome. She did not want it in her book at all. It would leave a mark. Roses did. A brown shape on the page, and a smell, for years.
She shut the book.
She stood there with the rose.
Then she went down the back stairs to the kitchen.
The kitchen was big and warm and low, with a stone floor and a black range and copper pans on the walls, and Mrs. Oddie was at the big table in the middle of it, with Kross's coat spread out on the scrubbed boards, brushing the mud off it with a stiff brush, in long hard strokes. She looked up when Wren came in.
Wren went to the range. She opened the little iron door at the front of the firebox. The heat came out at her, red and roaring.
She put the rose in.
It went at once. The white petals curled and went brown and then black and then were not there. The stem hissed. There was a smell, very sweet, for a moment, and then just the smell of smoke.
She shut the iron door.
When she turned round, Mrs. Oddie was looking at her across the table. With the brush in her hand. Her face did not change at all.
Then she nodded. Once.
And went back to brushing the coat.
It was dark when Wren came up from the kitchen again. She had eaten soup at the big table with Mrs. Oddie, without talking, and washed her face, and changed her collar. She had slept for a while, sitting up, in the chair by the range. Now it was night, and the house was quiet, and the gas lamp in the hall was turned down low.
Kross was still on the fifth step.
He had a cup of tea beside him, on the step above. Mrs. Oddie must have brought it. It was cold. There was a skin on it. He had not drunk any. The gray packet from Lucian was beside the cup, unopened, with its gray wax seal.
Wren sat down on the bottom step. With her back against the banister. Not near him. Four steps down. Where she always sat, the nights she sat with him at all.
They sat there for a long time.
"You did well," said Kross at last. Very quietly. "Today."
"I told the truth."
"That's what I meant."
Wren looked at the black-and-white tiles of the hall floor.
"He knew about the step," she said. "This one. He said that step of yours."
"He knows a great many things."
"How?"
Kross did not answer.
"And he knew about her. The old woman. That she came by the short way. With Brin." Wren swallowed. "There was nobody there but us. And Ferrand."
"Ferrand had birds," said Kross.
"I know. I saw."
"Then you know how."
Wren sat very still.
"What's the Vault?" she said.
Kross was quiet for so long that she thought he was not going to answer.
"A place under the old Customs House," he said at last. "On the river. West of the Fish Stairs. Where the Ministry keeps what it doesn't want the Bank to see." He looked at the cold tea. "Nobody who goes into the Vault comes out of it the same as they went in, Halloway. Some don't come out."
"And he wants her taken there."
"He wants to talk to her."
"Is that the same?"
Kross did not answer that either.
Wren drew her knees up and put her arms round them.
"Will you do it?" she said.
She had not meant to ask. It came out of her into the dark hall, small and cracked, the way Inquisitor had come out of her in the carriage.
Kross turned his head and looked at her. Down the four steps. In the low light of the turned-down lamp his face was all hollows.
"Do you know why I sit on this step, Halloway?" he said.
"No."
"Because from here, you can see the front door. And the back stairs. And the window on the landing. All three. Anybody comes in, any way, I see them first." He looked at the front door. "I've sat here for eighteen years. Every night that I'm in this house. Waiting to see who comes in."
"Has anybody?"
"Not yet."
He looked at the gray packet beside him. At the seal.
"I'll do what I'm told," he said. "I always have. That's what I'm for." He breathed out. "And I'll find her. Because if I don't find her, someone else will. Someone who finds things less carefully than I do."
Wren looked at him.
She did not understand. She thought she understood. She did not know which was worse.
"Go to bed, Halloway," said Kross.
She went up the stairs past him. She had to step over his long legs to do it. On the fifth step, as she went past, she looked down and saw that his left hand was flat on his waistcoat pocket again. Over his heart. And that his eyes were shut.
Room Nine
On Tuesday, before the Cleansing Office, before anything, he went to Room Nine.
Wren went with him, because she always did. Up the Ministry's front steps in the gray first light, past the porter who stood up so fast his stool fell over, across the great cold hall with its floor of black and white squares and its echo. Up the main stair to the second floor. Along the long corridor that smelled of floor wax and ink and old paper, past door after door with brass numbers on them. Past Seven. Past Eight.
Nine.
It was a plain door. No different from the others. Dark wood, a brass number, a brass handle. Kross had the only key. He unlocked it and went in, and Wren went in after him, and he shut it behind them, and the noise of the Ministry waking up went away all at once, as if somebody had put a hand over its mouth.
Room Nine was white.
White walls. White ceiling. White painted floorboards, scrubbed. A white tiled stove in the corner, cold. One tall window with white blinds, half drawn, so that the light came in gray and soft and lay on everything like dust. A long table of pale wood, scrubbed almost white, and two chairs, plain, facing each other across it. And nothing else. No pictures. No books. No papers. Nothing on the table at all but a small brass bell and a glass of water.
People came out of Room Nine different. Everybody in the Ministry knew that. They sat on one side of the long pale table and Kross sat on the other, and he took off his right glove, and asked them questions, quietly, and they answered. And when they came out they had told him everything. Every one of them. Nobody ever knew how.
Wren knew. A little. She had sat in the corner of Room Nine on a stool for two years and five months, writing things down. She had watched him do it. He did not take anything. Hardly ever. He only put his bare hand flat on the table, near theirs, not touching. And waited. And they felt it. The way she felt people. They felt him there, the old house with all its shutters closed and something moving in it, and they could not bear it, and they talked.
She sat down on her stool in the corner now.
Kross did not sit down. He went to the cold white stove. He opened its door. And he took something out of his coat, from the inside pocket on the right side, and held it for a moment in his gloved hand.
A notebook. Small. Black. Bound in soft leather, with a strap. Worn gray at the corners.
Ferrand's.
Wren had last seen it on the bunker stair under the Eye. In Ferrand's own pale gloved hand. Open. With a silver pencil moving across it in square, separate, unjoined letters, writing the word that made iron walk.
Kross stood in front of the open stove with the notebook in his hand.
She thought he was going to burn it. She thought that was what the stove was for. She sat very still on her stool and waited for him to strike a match.
He did not strike a match.
He undid the strap. He opened the notebook. And he stood there, by the cold stove, in the gray light from the half-drawn blind, and turned the pages. Slowly. One at a time. Reading.
She watched him read for a long time.
He did not read it the way he read the lists. Fast, page over page. He read it the way you read a letter from somebody who is dead. Slowly. Going back. Stopping at some pages for so long that she thought he had forgotten he was reading at all.
Once he stopped at a page and his face changed.
Not much. She could not have said how. Only that something in it went still in a different way. And he stood with the book open in his hand and looked at that one page for longer than he had looked at anything.
Then he turned round, and held the book out to her.
Open. At that page.
"Look at it, Halloway," he said. "Don't touch it. Look."
She got up off her stool and came across the white floor and looked.
It was a drawing. In Ferrand's silver pencil, very fine and very careful, the way he did everything. A rod. A silver rod, the length of a man's forearm, drawn from the side, with every turn of its twist shown exactly, going round and round it from one end to the other. Turning to the left. And beside it, in his square separate letters, a few words. And an arrow, pointing from the words to the rod.
She read them.
Left. Not to take. To write. Spire workshop. Six made. Two lost on the Glass.
And under that, a line on its own. Smaller.
For the Choir. For the Slate.
Wren read it three times.
She did not know what it meant. She knew the words. Choir. The quiet children, the carts with the blue open hand. Slate. A thing you wrote on, at school, with chalk. A thing you wiped clean.
To write.
She thought about Ferrand's word. The worn sound she could never catch, that he said under his breath when he wrote, and that made the iron men turn their heads together. She thought about the left-turning rod at his hip, always, that he never used on anybody, that she had never once seen him draw.
Not to take. To write.
She looked up at Kross.
"What does it mean?" she said.
Kross took the book back. He closed it. He did up the strap.
"I don't know yet," he said.
She thought it was the first time he had ever said that to her. I don't know. She had heard him say I know a thousand times. Find out, a thousand more. Never that.
He did not burn the notebook.
He went to the long pale table. He knelt down beside it, on the white floor, at the end where he always sat, and felt along the underside of the table top with his gloved fingers. Wren heard a small click. A thin drawer slid out from under the edge of the table, so thin and so well made that she had sat in this room for two years and five months and never known it was there.
He laid the notebook in the drawer. He slid it shut. Click.
Then he stood up and brushed off his knees.
"You didn't see that," he said.
"No, Inquisitor."
"Write it down anyway."
She looked at him.
"In your book," said Kross. "Not mine." He went to the door. "If anything happens to me, Halloway, somebody should know where it is. And what it says. And that I didn't burn it."
She did not ask what might happen to him. She did not need to. She had seen a page in a book, a year ago, with a name at the top and a gray circle beside it and no line through.
She sat down on her stool in the corner and took out her brown book and wrote it. All of it. The drawing. The words. The drawer under the table. And at the end, because he had told her to, He did not burn it.
When she looked up, he was standing at the window, with the blind half drawn, looking out at the gray roofs of the city. Toward the Hill. Toward the long low white house at the top of it, with its green glass room full of lemons and roses, where it was always summer.
Returned
The Cleansing Office was in the old chapel of the Ministry, on the ground floor, at the back.
Nobody had prayed in it for years. The altar had been taken out and a long desk put in its place, and the pews had been taken out and rows of clerks' tables put in theirs, and the tall narrow windows with their colored glass had been whitewashed over on the inside so that the light came through them gray and flat and the saints in the glass could not see what was being done under them. But it was still a chapel. You could tell. The roof went up high and dark, in arches, and every sound in it went up into the arches and came back down again, a little later, softer, like an echo in a well. And on the wall behind the long desk, where the altar had been, there was a paler shape on the plaster, tall and narrow, where something had hung for a very long time and been taken down.
There were forty clerks in it. Wren counted. At forty tables, in rows, with forty green-shaded lamps, writing. The sound of their pens went up into the roof and came back down like rain.
And at the long desk where the altar had been, on Tuesday morning, there was Kross.
He had brushed his coat. Mrs. Oddie had brushed it, all night, Wren thought, by the range, because it was gray and clean and smooth again when he came down in the morning, and he had shaved, and his face was his own face again, very nearly. Tired. Lined. Still. He sat at the long desk with the under-secretaries standing in a row behind him, three of them, in good black coats, looking at the back of his head the way men look at a storm coming in from the sea.
Wren sat at a little table to one side, by the pale shape on the wall. With her brown book, and a pen. To write down what she saw.
There were papers on the long desk. Piles of them. The lists. Every list the Office had sent out since Saturday, and every line that had come back from the Bank, and every report from the gray coats who had gone out with lamps, and every name of every person who had been brought in and was sitting now in the Bank's pens, six floors under the river, waiting for Sunday.
Kross read them.
All of them. One after another. He read the way he always read. Very fast. Not moving his lips. Turning each page over face down onto a new pile when he had done with it, so that one pile went down and the other went up. He did not say anything. He did not ask anything. The under-secretaries stood behind him and waited, and shifted their feet, and one of them cleared his throat, and Kross did not look round.
Wren watched his hands.
They turned the pages. Steady. Even. Over, and down. Over, and down. Like a clock.
And then, a little before noon, they stopped.
He had a page in front of him. A page of the Saturday list, with the Bank's lines on it, in black. Wren could not read it from where she sat. She could see the shape of it. A column of names on the left, in brown. A column of lines on the right, in black. Very neat. Very small. A good hand.
Kross was looking at one of the lines.
He looked at it for a long time. Then, very slowly, he put his finger on it. And moved his finger along it, word by word, the way a child does. The way Wren had never seen him do.
Then he took his finger away.
And the corner of his mouth moved.
It was so small that the under-secretaries behind him did not see it. They were looking at the back of his head. But Wren was looking at his face, from the side, and she saw it. The corner of his mouth went up. A very little. And stayed there.
"Under-secretary," he said.
The nearest of the three men jumped.
"Inquisitor?"
"This line. Number four. Saturday." Kross did not turn round. He held the page up over his shoulder. "Read it to me."
The under-secretary took the page. He read it. His face went red.
"No name given," he read. "The Charter requires a line be set against a name. The Auditor cannot set a line against a name not known. Returned to the Office." He lowered the page. "Inquisitor, the Office has already corrected—we sent it back on Sunday, called Dutch, this is the name he is known by, in the plainest—"
"And what did she send back on Monday?"
The under-secretary went redder.
"I don't—the Monday lines haven't—"
"They have." Kross held up another page, without looking. "Read it."
The under-secretary took it. He read it. He read it twice.
"A calling is not a name," he said. His voice had gone small. "Ruling of Auditor Penhallow. The Charter. The fourth leaf. Returned to the Office."
Nobody said anything. The rain of the clerks' pens went on, up in the arches.
"Who is Auditor Penhallow?" said the under-secretary at last. Helplessly.
"Dead," said Kross. "A hundred and sixty years." He took the page back and laid it face down on the new pile. "Which is why nobody can argue with him."
He went back to reading.
Wren looked down at her brown book. She found she had written, without meaning to, on the open page: A calling is not a name. In her own hand. And underlined it.
She did not cross it out.
In the afternoon, Kross went upstairs, to the under-secretary's room on the second floor, and sat down in the chair by the fire, and did not come down again.
Wren sat on a stool by the door. The under-secretary, the red one, sat at his desk and tried to work and kept looking at Kross out of the corner of his eye. Kross did not look at anything. He sat in the chair by the fire with his long legs crossed and his gloved hands folded on his knee and his eyes on the coals, as if he had come there to rest and did not mean to say a word to anybody all evening. Which was what he did.
Once, a little after the fourth bell, a clerk brought in a page. The under-secretary read it, and went pale, and looked at Kross, and Kross did not look up, and the under-secretary put the page down on his desk and covered it with his hand. Wren saw it, a little, before he covered it. A page of the Tuesday list. Near the bottom. A name, and a mark beside it in the margin. Faint. Gray. A little circle.
She went cold all over.
She knew that mark. She had seen it before. Once. On a page in a book she was not supposed to see. A page with a single name at the top in a careful upright hand, and beside it, the same small faint gray circle, drawn in one stroke, without lifting the pencil.
Kross, S.
She looked at Kross.
He had not moved. His eyes were on the coals.
At the sixth bell another clerk came. Out of breath. From across the square, from the Bank. He had been sent to the Bank's register room, he said, on the first floor, about a line. Number fifty-nine. The old man. Hale, L. The Auditor's line said the lodging was paid. The under-secretary had sent him to see if it was.
"And?" said the under-secretary.
"It's paid, sir," said the clerk. "Two months. In full. This afternoon. By the Fourth Desk."
The under-secretary stared at him.
"By the Fourth Desk?"
"That's what it says, sir. In the register. Paid this day in full by the Fourth Desk. In the first-floor clerk's hand. And a note pinned to it. In the Auditor's." The clerk swallowed. "So the line's true, sir. He's not to be brought in."
The under-secretary opened his mouth. And shut it.
And from the chair by the fire, without moving, without taking his eyes off the coals, Kross said:
"And who paid it before?"
The whole room went still.
The clerk looked at him. He had not seen him till then, Wren thought. A tall thin quiet man in a good gray coat, sitting by the fire. The clerk's face went blank.
"I—I don't know, sir. I didn't look. I can go back and look. I'll go now—"
"Don't trouble yourself," said Kross. "I know."
He stood up.
He took his hat off the hook by the fire and put it on. He went to the door. Wren stood up off her stool, quickly, and he went past her, and she went after him, out and down the stairs, three flights, and across the empty chapel with its whitewashed windows and its forty dark tables and the pale shape on the wall, and out into the snow.
He did not say anything all the way back to Mercer Street.
But on the doorstep, with his key in the door, he stopped. And stood there in the snow and the blue light of the street lamp, with his hand on the key, not turning it.
"Eleven years," he said. Not to her. To the door. "Every month. C. Vane. Paid."
Then he turned the key and went in.
Wren stood on the doorstep in the snow for a long time after the door had shut behind him.
