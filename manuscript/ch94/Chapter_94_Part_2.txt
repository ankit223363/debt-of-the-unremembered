Chapter 94: He Knows Now, Part 2 of 5: The Fire
The Fire
Lucian came back with bread.
A whole round loaf, still warm, on a wooden board, with a knife. And butter in a white dish. And a little pot of something dark and sweet that turned out to be plum jam. And a jug of hot water, and a jug of milk, which he set down beside Wren's cup without a word, as if she had asked for it, and looked at it, and moved it a little closer to her, and looked at it again.
"Thank you, Lucian," said the Archon.
"Archon." Lucian stood by the table with his papers under his arm. He did not go. He seemed to be waiting to be told whether to go.
"Stay," said the Archon. "Write. I'll want it written."
Lucian sat down. Not at the table. On a little stool by the nearest lemon tree, a few steps away, with his papers on his knees and his pen out. He uncorked a tiny bottle of ink from his pocket. He dipped the pen. He checked the nib. He dipped it again.
The Archon cut the bread himself. Three thick slices. He buttered one and spread it with the plum jam and put it on a plate and set it in front of Kross. He did the same for Wren. He did not take one himself.
"Eat," he said.
Kross ate.
Wren had never seen him eat like that. Quickly. Without looking at the food. The way a soldier eats, she thought, or a dog. He ate the whole slice and the Archon cut him another and he ate that too. And then he sat back in his white iron chair with his hands on his knees, and the color had come back into his face a little, under the gray of the stubble.
Wren ate hers slowly. She could not taste it.
The Archon watched them eat. He did not seem in any hurry. The little yellow bird came down from its vine and sat on the edge of the fountain and drank, and flew away again. The water ran. The stoves ticked.
Then, when Kross had finished, the Archon said:
"Tell me about the fire, Silas."
He said it in exactly the same voice he had used for you look tired. Kind. Quiet. Interested. As if he were asking about the weather on the road.
Wren did not move.
Kross did not move either. He sat with his hands on his knees and looked at the Archon across the little white table. His face did not change at all.
"It was in the Eye," he said. "Under the dome. The command room. Sola's room."
"Go on."
"There were lamps. On the walls, in brackets. Her lamps. Sixty years old. Oil lamps." Kross's voice was flat and even. The voice he used for reports. "There was still oil in them. Thick. Gone to something like tar. And the maps on the walls were paper, and the map on the table was vellum, and the chairs were wood. All of it dry. Very dry. Sixty years in the dark with no air moving."
"And it burned."
"It went up very fast," said Kross.
The Archon looked at him.
Wren held her breath.
On the Glass, she thought. Nothing burns on the Glass. Everybody knows that. You can put your hand in a fire on the Glass and it's cool as water. Brin showed us. The Ashborn showed us. Nothing burns.
"On the Glass," said the Archon gently.
"Not on the Glass," said Kross. "In the Eye." He did not hurry. "The Eye is not the Glass, Archon. That's the whole of it. That's what Sola made it for. Inside the dome, the hum stops. Everything works there that doesn't work outside. Fire burns. Hands take." He paused. "Ferrand's word worked there. That's why he went in."
The Archon was quiet for a moment.
"Ah," he said. "Of course. The hum stops."
"The hum stops."
"I had forgotten that." The Archon smiled. "How strange. I read it in Malakor's own letters, forty years ago. I knew it once. And I had quite forgotten." He picked up his tea and looked into it. "One forgets things, Silas. Even I."
Kross did not answer.
Wren sat very still in her chair by the window with her plate in her lap and the slice of bread on it, half eaten.
She had been there.
She had been there, in the Eye, under the dome, on the last day. Not in the command room. She had not gone in. Stay behind me. Do not go in. Whatever you see, write it down. Exactly. She had stayed behind him, in the passage, by the great door, and she had seen what she had seen, and she had written it all down in her brown book that night, every word, in a plain hand, with I in it.
She had not seen a fire.
She had not seen smoke. She had not smelled burning. She had seen Sola's room through the door, a little, from the passage, by lamplight. The table. The map. A silver rod lying on it, pointing north. And no fire. Not then. Not after.
But she had not seen everything. She had been in the passage. Behind him. She had not seen the whole of the room. She had not been there every hour.
She did not know.
She did not know if there had been a fire.
She found, sitting in the warm green room with the bread on her plate, that she did not want to know.
"And the journal?" said the Archon. Still gently. "Malakor's journal. It was in that room?"
"On the table," said Kross. "Under the map."
"And it burned."
"I put it in my report."
"You did." The Archon nodded slowly. "The journal destroyed in the fire. Lucian read it to the Council. Your very words." He turned his cup a little on its saucer. "Such a pity. Forty years I have wanted that book. Malakor's own hand. Everything he knew about the old work. Everything Sola told him." He sighed. "Well. If it burned, it burned. Nothing to be done."
He set his cup down.
And then he turned his head, and looked at Wren.
"Did you see it burn, Cadet?"
The whole green room went quiet.
Wren could hear the water in the fountain. She could hear the stoves. She could hear, a few steps away, under the lemon tree, the scratch of Lucian's pen stop.
She could feel Kross, across the table. The old house with its shutters closed. Very still now. Nothing moving in it at all.
She looked at the Archon. At his pale clear eyes. At his kind tired face.
She thought about the brown book. In her coat. In the inside pocket, against her ribs, where it always was. Write it down. Exactly. And then decide whose report you sign.
"No, Archon," she said.
She heard her own voice. It was quite steady. That surprised her.
"I didn't go into the room. The Inquisitor told me to stay in the passage. Behind him. So I did." She swallowed. "I didn't see the room burn. I didn't see it not burn. I wasn't there."
The Archon looked at her for a long time.
She felt him looking. She felt it the way you feel the sun on the back of your neck. And when she reached out for him again, without meaning to, the way you reach for a wall in the dark, there was the still pool again. Cool. Clear. Showing her own face.
"Thank you," said the Archon at last. "That's very honest. Lucian, write that the cadet was in the passage."
Lucian wrote. Wren heard the pen.
"You keep a book, I think," said the Archon. "Don't you? A little brown one. Silas told me once. In a letter. She writes everything down." He smiled. "I should very much like to read it, someday. An account of the Glass by somebody who went. There are so few."
Wren's hand had gone to her coat. To the place over her ribs. She had not meant it to. She took it away again.
"The cadet's account is in my report," said Kross.
He said it quite quietly. Quite evenly. But it was the first time he had spoken without being asked.
The Archon looked at him.
"Of course," he said. "Of course it is. I wouldn't dream of it." He turned back to Wren. "Keep your book, Cadet. A person should have one thing that is her own."
Wren could not answer.
"And poor Ferrand," said the Archon. More quietly. He looked down at the white rose lying on the cloth beside the tea tray. "On the stair. You were with him?"
"At the end," said Kross.
"Was it quick?"
"Yes."
"I'm glad." The Archon touched the rose with one gloved finger. Very lightly. On a petal. "He was a very loyal man. Not a warm one. But loyal. I shall miss him." He was quiet. "He always carried a little notebook. Black. He wrote everything in it. Like your cadet. I don't suppose—"
He stopped.
Kross waited.
"No," said the Archon. "No, I don't suppose it matters now." He took his finger off the rose. "Let him keep his notebook, wherever it is. He earned it."
Kross did not say anything.
But Wren, looking at him across the table, saw his left hand move. Off his knee. Slowly. To his coat. To the waistcoat under it. To the pocket on the left side, over his heart, where she knew he kept a little glass vial with a pink ribbon round its neck, and a page from his own black book, folded eleven times.
His hand lay flat over the pocket. And stayed there.
The Archon did not look at it. He was looking out of the window, at the city, with his kind tired face.
But Wren thought that he did not need to look. She thought that he had seen it anyway. The way you see things in a still pool. Without turning your head.
The Cleansing
"Well," said the Archon. "That's the north done with."
He said it the way you would close a book you had finished. Not sadly. Not happily. Only done. He picked up the silver pot and found it empty and set it down again and did not ask for more.
"And now there's the city," he said.
Kross waited.
"You'll have heard," said the Archon. "On the road. The Council met on Friday."
"The Cleansing," said Kross.
"The Cleansing." The Archon looked pleased, a little, as if Kross had got a sum right. "Under the old law of need. Every scribe not on the list. Every Ink-Binder. Every harvester not of the Ministry. Brought in, and weighed, and their books and needles and jars held by the Bank. By Midsummer." He folded his gloved hands on the white cloth. "It is going rather slowly."
"It's been three days."
"It has been three days, and the Cleansing Office has sent out a hundred names, and brought in thirty. And the Auditor has begun sending back the lines." He smiled. "Did you know that? Of course you didn't. You've been on the road. The Auditor of the Fourth Desk has begun sending back the Office's lines. Very politely. Every one of them true. No such room at the address given. The name does not match the register." He seemed to find this delightful. "The under-secretaries are beside themselves. They come to Lucian three times a day. Lucian has started checking them four times."
Under the lemon tree, Lucian's pen stopped, and started again.
"You want me to stop her," said Kross.
"Stop her?" The Archon looked surprised. "No. Saints, no. Leave her be. Leave her quite alone. I want to see what she does." He looked out of the window. Across the river. At the Bank, white on its hill, with its lions. "She is learning, Silas. Do you know how long it has been since anybody at that desk learned anything? I should hate to interrupt her."
Kross did not say anything.
Wren looked at him. His face was quite still. His left hand was still flat over his waistcoat pocket.
"No," said the Archon. "The trouble is not the Auditor. The trouble is the Office. The Office is full of clerks." He sighed. "Good men. Careful men. They count very well. But they cannot find anything. You give them a name and an address and they go to the address and knock on the door, and if nobody comes, they write not found and go home." He turned back to Kross. "I need somebody who finds."
Kross looked at him for a long moment.
"You want me to run it," he said.
"I want you to run it. All of it. The Office. The lists. The gray coats. The carts. Whatever you need." The Archon spread his gloved hands a little on the cloth, palms up. "I've told the under-secretaries. They'll answer to you from today. Lucian has the papers."
"Here," said Lucian, from his stool, and held up a packet. Sealed. Gray wax. He checked the seal. He checked it again.
Kross did not look at the packet.
"Why me?" he said.
It was quite quiet. Quite even. But Wren had been with him two years and five months, and she had never heard him ask anybody why. Not once. He asked who, and where, and when. He asked what was his name. He did not ask why.
The Archon looked at him with great kindness.
"Because you're the best I have," he said simply. "You always were. Since you were nine." He paused. "And because you've just come back from the north, and you're tired, and you've lost a colleague, and you'll want something to do with your hands. I know you, Silas. You'd only sit on that step of yours all night and drink cold tea."
Wren went very still.
That step of yours.
The fifth step, in the house on Mercer Street. With the ring worn into the wood by eighteen years of Kross sitting on it. Mrs. Oddie had told her. Nobody else knew. Wren had not known anybody else knew.
Kross's face did not change.
"Where do I start?" he said.
"Wherever you like. You'll know best." The Archon smiled. "But since you ask—there is one name I should like found quite soon. If you would. As a kindness to me."
Kross waited.
"An old woman," said the Archon. "Small. Gray hair, cut all anyhow. Hands blue to the wrist, with indigo. Writing all up her arms." He said it slowly, as if he were remembering a face from a long time ago. "An Ink-Binder. The best there ever was in this city, they say. She went away a long time ago. Twenty years. And now she has come back." He looked at Kross. "I'm told she came over the Glass. By the short way. With an Ashborn. A big one." He paused. "I believe you've met her."
Kross did not answer.
Wren could not breathe.
She had seen her. On the Glass. Under the Eye. A small old woman with blue hands and a sharp black glare, who had looked at Wren once, across the long room, the way you look at a cut that will not heal. And had looked away.
I'm told, the Archon had said. I'm told she came by the short way.
Who had told him? Nobody had been there but them. The heron and his people. The Ashborn. Kross. Wren. Ferrand, and Ferrand was dead. Harrow and Pike, and they were days behind, on foot, with the carts.
Ferrand wrote everything down.
She thought about Spire birds. Gray pigeons, in a wicker box on the back of Ferrand's cart. She had seen Ferrand let one go, at the Ridges, with a little roll of paper on its leg. And another, at the edge of the bowl. Before the Eye. Before the end.
"She'll go to her old shop," said the Archon. "She'll want her things. Her inks. Her needles. People always go back for their things." He said it quite sadly. "It's under the Dye-Works arches. The last one, by the old vats. The Office went along there on Saturday, but of course they didn't find it. They found a padlock and went home." He looked at Kross. "You'll find it."
"Yes," said Kross.
"Gently," said the Archon. "She's old. She's done a great deal of good in her time, in her way. I should like her brought in gently. Not hurt. Not frightened, if it can be helped." He paused. "Bring her to the Vault, Silas. Not the Bank. Not the pens. The Vault. I'd like to talk to her myself."
Kross looked at him.
Something happened in his face. Wren saw it. So small that nobody who had not been with him two years and five months would ever have seen it. A little movement, at the corner of his mouth. As if he had bitten down on something.
Then it was gone.
"Yes, Archon," he said.
"Thank you." The Archon smiled. He leaned back in his white iron chair. "And the other one. The harvester. Vane." He said the name lightly. "Spent, your report says. And gone on over the far side."
"Gone on over the far side," said Kross.
"Then we shan't see him again."
Kross did not answer that.
The Archon looked at him for a long moment. Kindly. A little sadly. The way you look at somebody you are very fond of, who has told you something you both know is not quite true.
Then he stood up.
"Go home," he said. "Sleep. Brush your coat, Silas, for the Saints' sake. You look like a man who's been dragged backward through a hedge." He picked up the white rose from the tablecloth. "Lucian will give you the papers."
Kross stood up. Wren stood up too. Her knees did not feel like hers.
The Archon came round the table to her. He held out the white rose.
"For you, Cadet," he said. "For your book. Press it between the pages. It'll keep."
Wren looked at the rose.
She did not want it. She did not want to touch it. She found her hand going out anyway. Because he was holding it out. Because you did not refuse the Archon anything. Because her hand did not know how.
She took it.
Its stem was cool and smooth. He had cut every thorn off it. Every single one. Very neatly, with his little silver scissors, so that there was nothing left on the stem that could hurt.
"Thank you, Archon," she said.
"Thank you, Cadet. For being honest." He smiled at her. "It's rarer than you'd think. Mind the step."
And he turned back to his roses.
Lucian walked them to the door.
He went in front, fast, down the pale hall with its plain old chairs, with the gray packet under his arm and his pen behind his ear. At the front door he stopped, and checked the packet, and gave it to Kross, and checked that Kross had it. Then he opened the door for them himself, because the man in the plain dark coat was not there.
The cold came in. After the garden room it was like a slap.
Kross went out onto the step. Wren went to follow him.
And Lucian put out his hand. Not to touch her. He held it in the air, beside her arm, not quite on it, the way you would hold out a hand to stop a child from stepping off a curb.
She stopped.
He was looking at her. Really looking. For the first time, she thought, since they had come in. Not at his papers. At her. His face was white and thin and very tired, with dark marks under the eyes like bruises, and his smooth dark hair had one piece sticking up at the back that he had not noticed.
"He liked you," he said. Very low. Very fast. "The Archon. He liked you. I could tell. He gave you a rose."
"Yes."
Lucian's eyes went to the step, where Kross was standing with his back to them, looking out at the snow. Then back to her.
"Be careful," said Lucian, "when he likes you."
Wren stared at him.
Lucian's hand, in the air beside her arm, was shaking. She could see it. A fine fast shake, like a leaf. He looked at it as if it belonged to somebody else. Then he put it behind his back.
"I've worked for him eleven years," he said. "I check everything three times. Do you know why?"
"No."
"Because the first time I check," said Lucian, "it's what I wrote. And the second time, it's what I wrote. And the third time—" He stopped. He swallowed. "Sometimes the third time, it isn't. Sometimes the third time, it's a little different. A word. A number. A name I don't remember writing." His voice had gone to almost nothing. "And I never, ever know whether it was me."
The cold came in through the open door. Out on the step, Kross had not turned round.
"So I check three times," said Lucian. "And then I check again."
He stepped back. He was himself again, all at once. Pale and quick and jittery, with his papers.
"Good morning, Cadet," he said. Quite loudly. "Mind the step. There's ice."
And he shut the door.
Wren stood on the step beside Kross in the cold, with the long low white house behind her, and could not make her feet go down.
"Inquisitor," she said.
"I heard," said Kross.
He went down the steps to the carriage. After a moment, she went after him.
