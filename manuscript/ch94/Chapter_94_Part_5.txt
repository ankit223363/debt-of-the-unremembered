Chapter 94: He Knows Now, Part 5 of 5: No Whistle
No Whistle
The steps up to the Weavers' Road were behind a boatyard at the Cut end, just as he had said. Old stone steps, very steep, with no rail, and snow on every one, and nobody's footprints in the snow. Nobody came up here. There was no reason to. The road did not go anywhere anymore.
Wren went up them slowly, with one gloved hand on the wall.
At the top, the road went away east in a long straight line, along the tops of the arches, between two low broken parapets. It was wide. Wide enough for two carts to pass. The paving stones were heaved up and cracked where the frost and the roots had got at them, and there were little trees growing out of the cracks, bent and black and bare, and dead grass sticking up through the snow in tufts. On the left, over the parapet, there was the river. Gray and wide and slow, with ice along the edges. On the right, there were the backs of warehouses, and chimneys, and the roofs of the lower town going away into the gray.
It was very quiet up here. Quieter than anywhere in the city. Wren could hear her own boots crunching. She could hear the river. She could hear, a long way off, the bells of the Ministry beginning the noon bell, one after another.
She walked along the road. Slowly. Looking down over the left-hand parapet as she went.
The arches were below her. She could not see into them from here. Only the tops of their fronts, where they met the underside of the road. Boards. Tin. Iron grilles. And in front of them, the narrow strip of snow and stone at the edge of the river, where people walked, or used to.
She came to the third arch from the river end, and stopped.
She could see that one. The front of it had been broken in. Green boards, smashed, hanging. A white anchor painted on a door that was off its hinge. And in the snow in front of it, something small and dark and flat. She did not know what it was. She looked at it for a long time. Then she thought she did know. It was a lamp. A little tin one. Trodden flat.
She wrote it down.
She took the brown book out and wrote it, standing at the parapet with the wind on her face, with the stub of pencil from her pocket. The third arch. Broken in. Green boards, white anchor. A tin lamp in the snow, trodden flat. Exactly.
She went on.
The brick under the road began to change color. She could see it on the outside of the piers, going down to the water. Red, and then a faint blue, and then more blue, darker, until the last piers were nearly black. And the snow on the ledges was blue too, in the shadow.
And there were the vats. Three of them, round, stone, on a platform at the edge of the river. Full of snow.
And beyond them, the last arch.
She stopped at the parapet over it. Where a little bare tree grew out of a crack in the stones, bent over by the wind. She put both her gloved hands on the parapet, on the cold broken stones, and looked down.
An iron grille. Old. Heavy. With a padlock on the gate, as big as two fists, red with rust. Behind the grille, a wall of gray boards. No window. Just boards, very old.
In front of the grille, snow. Trodden. A great many boot marks, frozen hard. Saturday's. The Office's. And a long bright scrape down one of the bars, where somebody had tried the padlock with an iron bar and given up.
She looked at the boards. At the cracks between them.
She did not know why she looked at the cracks. There was nothing to see. They were black. Just black lines between gray boards. But she looked at them for a long time. And she felt something.
Not a thing she saw. A thing she felt. The way she had felt Lucian, and Kross, and the Archon. The way you feel the heat off a stove with your eyes shut.
There was somebody in there.
Two somebodies. Behind the boards. Very still. Very close. One was small and bright and frightened, like a bird held in your two hands. And one was old. Very old. Old and sharp and full of things, so full, so many things packed in so tight, layer on layer, like the pages of a book that has been written in and written in and written in until there is no white left on any page. Like a book bound in blue.
Wren held on to the parapet.
She made herself look away from the boards. Down. At the snow in front of the vats.
And there were marks.
Not Saturday's. New ones. Soft. Not frozen. Going round the nearest vat, the one right against the blue wall. Three people's. She could see them quite clearly from up here, in the clean snow. Two small sets of prints, and one very big one. Going round to the back of the vat, where she could not see. And not coming back.
And at the back of the vat, against the blue wall, between the vat and the arch, very still, there was a post.
She had not seen it at first. It was dark against the dark brick. It was very tall, and very still, and it had snow on the top of it, a little white cap, and on its shoulders.
Posts did not have shoulders.
Wren looked at it for a long time. And it did not move. And she understood that it knew she was looking. That it had known since she came to the parapet. That it was standing there in the snow against the blue wall with a long thing held upright in its hands, waiting to see what she would do. The way Kross waited, on the fifth step.
She thought about the Glass. The dome under the Eye. A huge Ashborn with a round shaved head and gray on both cheeks, standing by the great door with a spear, who had looked at her once, across the dark, when she was carrying Kross's bag. Not angrily. Just looked.
Brin.
She put her hand inside her coat.
She did not decide to. Her hand went. Into her coat, to the inside pocket, the left one. Where her whistle was. Her cadet's whistle, brass, on a cord, that every cadet of the Long Office carried, that she had been given on her first day with her key and her coat. Three short blasts meant here. It was what it was for. You blew it, and gray coats came. That was all. That was what you did.
Her fingers closed round it.
It was very cold. The brass was cold through her glove.
She stood at the parapet with her hand inside her coat, round the whistle, and looked down at the post with snow on its shoulders. And the gray boards. And the two somebodies behind them, the bird and the book.
She thought about a farmer under a sack, with his boots sticking out, at a gate in pieces. Nobody comes through my yard.
She thought about a child's face at a barred window on Lantern Bridge, not frightened, not anything.
She thought about the Archon's kind tired face, and the still pool, and a white rose with every thorn cut off.
Bring her to the Vault, Silas. I'd like to talk to her myself.
She thought about the tin lamp in the snow in front of the third arch. Trodden flat.
Write it down. Exactly. And then decide whose report you sign.
She took her hand out of her coat.
Empty.
She stood there a moment longer, with both her hands on the parapet again. Looking down at the post, which did not move. At the boards, which did not move. At the snow, which had begun, very lightly, to fall again. Small dry flakes, coming down out of the gray, settling on the marks round the vat. Softening their edges.
Then she turned away and walked back along the Weavers' Road, the way she had come. Not fast. Not slow. With her head down and her hands in her pockets. Her left hand round the brass whistle. Her right hand round the stone with the white ring.
She did not look back.
At the top of the steps behind the boatyard, she stopped, and took out the brown book, and wrote. Standing in the snow, with the pencil stub. Her hand was shaking. She wrote it anyway. Exactly.
The last arch, by the vats. An iron grille, padlocked, rusted. Gray boards. Saturday's boot marks, frozen.
New marks in the snow by the nearest vat. Three people. Two small. One very big. Going round to the back. Not coming out.
A big man standing against the blue wall behind the vat. Very still. Snow on his shoulders. I think it was Brin. He saw me. I saw him.
Two people inside the arch. I felt them. One frightened. One very old, and very full.
I put my hand on my whistle.
She stopped. She looked at the last line for a long time.
Then she wrote one more.
I did not blow it.
The Weavers' Road
She read it to him in the kitchen, at the fourth bell, standing up.
She had thought about how she would do it, all the way back from the boatyard steps. She had thought she would tell him. In her own words. Leave things out, perhaps, or put them softly. She had thought about not telling him at all. About saying the padlock is rusted, nobody has been there in twenty years, which was true, and walking out of the kitchen and up to her narrow bed and shutting the door.
But when she came in, and he was sitting at the scrubbed table with a cup of tea in front of him, hot, for once, and Mrs. Oddie at the range with her back to them, and he looked up at her and did not say anything, only waited, she found she could not do any of those things.
She took out the brown book. She opened it at the page. And she read it to him. Exactly. Every line.
The third arch. Broken in. Green boards, white anchor. A tin lamp in the snow, trodden flat.
The last arch, by the vats. An iron grille, padlocked, rusted. Gray boards. Saturday's boot marks, frozen.
New marks in the snow by the nearest vat. Three people. Two small. One very big. Going round to the back. Not coming out.
A big man standing against the blue wall behind the vat. Very still. Snow on his shoulders. I think it was Brin. He saw me. I saw him.
Two people inside the arch. I felt them. One frightened. One very old, and very full.
I put my hand on my whistle.
She stopped.
Mrs. Oddie, at the range, had gone very still.
Wren looked at the last line. Her voice did not want to say it. She made it.
I did not blow it.
She shut the book.
Nobody said anything for a long time. The range ticked. The kettle on the back of it began, very softly, to sing.
Kross sat at the table with his gloved hands round his cup and looked at her. His face did not change. It did not go angry. It did not go anything.
"Thank you, Halloway," he said at last. "That's a true report."
Wren stood there with the book in her hands.
"Will you sign it?" she said. "Will it go in yours?"
"It's yours," said Kross. "You sign it."
She did not understand. Then she did. She stood in the warm kitchen and understood it all the way down, the way you understand cold water when you have fallen into it.
She took the pencil out of her pocket. She opened the book again, at the page. And under I did not blow it, very small, she wrote: W. H.
She looked at it.
Then she shut the book and put it back inside her coat. Over her ribs. On the right side. Away from the whistle.
Kross drank his tea.
The carriage came round at the seventh bell. The plain one. Gray, with no crest on the door, and Samuel on the box muffled up to his eyes, and the two horses blowing clouds in the cold.
Kross came down the steps of the house on Mercer Street in his brushed gray coat and his gray hat and his gray gloves. He stopped at the carriage door, and looked back at the house, at the black front door, at the brass fist holding its ring.
Wren was standing in the doorway.
"Come if you're coming, Halloway," he said.
She came.
They did not talk in the carriage. It went down through the city, through streets gone quiet with the dark and the cold, past lamps burning blue, past shuttered shops, past the river. Over the Cut by the stone bridge, where there was a white barrier, and two gray coats who saw the carriage and lifted it without being asked. Along behind the boatyards. And then up. Onto a ramp of old stone, cracked and heaved and full of snow, that the horses did not like. Samuel spoke to them, low, and they went up it, slipping, blowing, and came out on top.
The Weavers' Road.
It was white, under the moon. The clouds had gone. The snow had stopped. Everything was very sharp and very still, the way it is on a clear night after snow. The little bare trees growing out of the cracks stood up black against the white like letters on a page. The river on the left was black and silver. And far off, along the road, at its east end, where the arches stopped and the vats were, there was a little bare tree bent over by the wind.
Samuel drove toward it. Slowly. The wheels made almost no sound in the snow.
And as they went, Wren looked down over the parapet on the river side, at the strip of snow along the front of the arches, and saw the lamps.
They were at the river end. Far back. Just starting along the front of the arches. Five of them. Six. Blue. Swinging on their poles. And under them the dark shapes of men, a great many of them, in a line, walking.
And she heard them. Even from up here. Even through the carriage glass. Boots on the frozen snow, all in step. Crunch, and crunch. And at the back of them a heavy sound. Iron on iron. A clank, at every step, like a sack full of tools.
A smith's sack.
She watched them come.
They came very slowly. Much more slowly than gray coats walked. And the lamps were held high, on their poles, high up, where you could see them a long way off. And the clank of the smith's sack was very loud. Louder, she thought, than it needed to be. Somebody was carrying it so that it would swing. So that the tools in it would hit each other at every step.
Anybody in the arches would hear that. Anybody would see those lamps. From a long way off. Long before the men got there.
Wren turned her head and looked at Kross.
He was sitting in his corner of the carriage with his hat tipped down and his gloved hands one on top of the other in his lap. He was not looking at the lamps. He did not need to.
"You told them to come like that," she said. "Slowly. With the lamps up. With the sack."
Kross did not answer.
"So they'd hear," said Wren. "So they'd see. So they'd have time to—"
She stopped. She did not know how to finish it.
To get out. To go down the dark way under the vats and along the river and away. She did not know if there was such a way. She thought there must be. She thought he knew there was. She thought he had always known.
The carriage stopped.
It stopped by the little bare tree, at the parapet, over the last arch. Samuel said something to the horses, low, and they stood, blowing.
Kross opened the door. The cold came in.
He got out. He stood in the snow on the Weavers' Road, in the moonlight, with the river behind him. He did not shut the door. He stood beside it for a moment, looking down into the carriage at her.
"Stay in the carriage, Halloway," he said.
"Whatever I see," said Wren. "Write it down. Exactly."
He looked at her for a long moment.
"Yes," he said.
He shut the door. Very quietly. Wren heard the latch.
She watched him through the glass.
He walked to the parapet. To the place where she had stood at noon, by the little bare tree. He put his gloved hands on the broken stones. And he looked down.
Not along the arches, at the lamps coming. Straight down. At the last arch. At the grille, and the gray boards behind it.
Wren pressed her face to the cold glass of the carriage window and looked down too, over the parapet, at the boards.
And saw the light.
A line of it. Very thin. Yellow. A finger's width. Along the very top of the boards, where they went up into the curve of the brick. Coming out of the dark arch like a thread of gold. The only warm light anywhere in the whole cold blue night.
She stopped breathing.
Kross stood at the parapet and looked at it.
Then, slowly, he took off his right glove.
Finger by finger. The way she had seen him do it under the Eye, the day he stepped onto the drowned roofs and did not put it on again until they were out on the open Glass. He folded the glove. He put it in his coat pocket. And he laid his bare hand flat on the cold stone of the parapet, and stood there, very still, with his bare hand on the stone, looking down at the yellow line of light.
Behind him, along the front of the arches, the blue lamps came on. Slowly. Clanking. Crunch, and crunch.
Wren took out her brown book in the dark of the carriage. She could not see to write. She wrote anyway. Big, so she would be able to read it after.
He took his glove off.
When she looked up again, Kross had turned from the parapet. And was walking, not fast, not slow, along the Weavers' Road toward the old stone steps that went down to the river at the vat end.
And down.
