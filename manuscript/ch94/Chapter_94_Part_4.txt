Chapter 94: He Knows Now, Part 4 of 5: The Folded Page
The Folded Page
She could not sleep that night.
She lay in her narrow bed at the top of the house with her eyes open and looked at the slope of the ceiling over her head, where the plaster had a crack in it shaped like a river with two branches. She had looked at that crack every night for two years and five months. She had named the branches once, when she was new and could not sleep: the Kross and the Halloway. She had thought that was very funny. She had been seventeen.
She did not think it was funny now.
She thought about a small gray circle in the margin of a page. Drawn in one stroke, without lifting the pencil.
She thought about a page in a book in Room Nine, a year ago, the one and only time she had looked at something on Kross's desk that he had not given her to look at. A thin black book, not his own, lying open by the inkstand while he was out of the room. With a page headed in a small upright careful hand that was not his. And one name at the top of it. And beside the name, that circle.
Kross, S.
She had shut the book. She had never told him. She had never told anybody. She had not let herself think about it for a year.
She thought about it now.
She got up.
She put her coat on over her nightgown, because the stairs were cold, and went down in her stockings, quietly, past the second floor and the first. The gas lamp in the hall was turned down so low it was only a blue bead. The house was dark and still and smelled of beeswax and peppermint and coal.
Kross was on the fifth step.
He had not moved, she thought, since she went to bed. He was sitting exactly as he had sat for eighteen years, with his back against the wall and his long legs down the stairs and his hands on his knees. The cup of tea was on the step above, cold, with its skin. Lucian's gray packet was beside it.
But the packet was open now. The gray seal was broken. The papers were in a neat pile on the step, squared off at the edges.
And on top of the pile, there were two other things.
A little glass vial. Long and thin, like a finger, with a cork in it, and a pink silk ribbon tied round its neck in a bow. Empty, as far as she could see. Or with something in it so pale she could not see it in the dark.
And a page. Folded small. Very small. Smaller than a playing card. Folded and folded and folded again, until it was a hard little square no bigger than her thumbnail.
She stopped on the bottom step.
Kross did not look round. He knew she was there. He always knew.
"You should be asleep, Halloway," he said.
"I know."
"Sit down, then."
She sat down on the bottom step. Four steps down. With her back against the banister and her bare feet in their stockings on the cold tiles of the hall.
They sat for a while.
Then Kross reached out, without looking, and picked up the little folded square from the top of the pile. He held it in his gloved fingers for a moment, turning it over. Then he began to unfold it.
He did it slowly. One fold at a time. Wren counted. She could not help it. She had counted them going in, in the tent by the Eye, by lamplight, with the song coming across the Glass. She counted them coming out.
One. Two. Three. Four.
The paper was creased so hard that it did not want to open. It made a small dry crackling sound at every fold, like a leaf.
Five. Six. Seven. Eight.
Nine. Ten.
Eleven.
It lay open on his palm. A page torn from a small black book. Narrow, with a ruled line down the left side for a margin, and a torn edge on the right where it had come out of the binding. Covered in creases, so many that it looked like the map of a city.
He held it out to her. Down the four steps.
She did not take it. She leaned forward, and looked at it, in the light of the blue bead of the hall lamp.
There was a name at the top. In his hand. Small, and slanting, and very black.
Wren Halloway.
And under it, nothing. No words. Only, in the margin, on the left, beside her name, a mark.
A small circle. In gray pencil. Drawn in one stroke.
And through the middle of the circle, from top to bottom, hard, in black ink, a line.
Wren looked at it for a long time.
She could not speak. Her throat had closed. She sat on the bottom step with her hands round her knees and looked at her own name, and at the circle beside it, and at the black line through the circle, and she felt the cold of the tiles come up through her stockings into her feet and her legs and her whole body, as if she were turning to stone from the bottom up.
"Ferrand had a page," said Kross.
He said it very quietly. In the voice he used for reports.
"In his notebook. At the back. In the Archon's hand. A list." He did not look at her. He was looking at the front door. "Names. For when we came home. People he wanted—" He stopped. "Tidied. People who had seen too much on the road, or might have. People who might write things down." He breathed out. "Harrow was on it. Pike was on it. Two of the drivers."
Wren could not breathe.
"You were on it," said Kross. "With a circle."
The house was very quiet. Somewhere, far down, in the kitchen, the range ticked as it cooled.
"So I took it out of his notebook," said Kross. "The page. The night before the stair. While he slept. And I copied the names into my own book, one to a page, so I would know what I owed. And I put a line through every circle. In ink." He looked down at the page in his hand. "And then I tore out yours, and folded it, so I would carry it. So I would feel it. Every time I put my hand there."
He laid his gloved hand flat on his waistcoat, over the pocket, over his heart. The way she had seen him do a hundred times. On the Glass. In the carriage. At the Archon's table.
She understood.
She understood all at once, the way you understand a word you have been reading for years without knowing what it meant. Every time his hand went there. Every time.
"What does it mean?" she said. Her voice was a whisper. "The line. Through the circle."
Kross was quiet for a long time.
"It means I have the pen," he said. "For now."
It means somebody else has the pen. Wren did not know where she had heard that. Some old street saying, from the Fish Stairs, from when she was small. Somebody else has the pen. It meant you were done for. It meant your name was written somewhere and you could not get it back.
And he had taken the pen.
"For now," she said.
"For now." He began to fold the page up again. One fold. Two. "The Archon knows there was no fire, Halloway. He knew it before we came off the Glass. Ferrand's last bird told him. Journal on the table. Harvester alive. Kross on the stair. That was the last thing Ferrand ever wrote." Three. Four. "He knows I lied. He knows I have the notebook. He knows I have this." Five. Six. "And he gave me the Cleansing anyway. All of it. And sent me after the old woman."
"Why?"
"To see what I'll do." Seven. Eight. "Same as the Auditor. He likes to watch. He likes to see which way a thing will fall, when you let go of it."
Nine. Ten.
"And your name," said Wren. "Kross, S. There was a page. In Room Nine. A year ago. I saw it. I'm sorry. I shouldn't have looked. With a circle."
Kross's hands stopped. The page was nearly folded. One fold left.
He did not seem surprised. He did not seem angry.
"Yes," he said. "I know."
"Is there a line through yours?"
Kross looked at her then. Down the four steps. For the first time that night.
"No," he said.
And he made the last fold. Eleven. And put the little hard square back in his waistcoat pocket, on the left, over his heart, with the vial with the pink ribbon. And laid his hand flat over it.
"Go to bed, Halloway," he said. "You've a long day tomorrow. You're going to go and look at the arches for me."
She did not move.
"Inquisitor," she said. "Who's the vial for?"
Kross did not answer.
He had turned back to the front door. To the black door, with the brass fist holding its ring. Waiting to see who would come in.
Wren went up the stairs past him. She stepped over his long legs on the fifth step. She went up to her narrow bed under the crack shaped like a river, and lay down in her coat, and did not take it off, and did not sleep, until the window over the chimneys went gray.
Orders
In the morning he gave her her orders at the kitchen table, over porridge, the way he gave her all her orders.
Mrs. Oddie had made the porridge thick, with salt, and put a pat of butter on top of each bowl, and a spoon of brown sugar, the way Wren liked it, which she only did on the mornings after Wren had been away. She set the bowls down on the scrubbed table and went back to the range and stood with her back to them and her arms folded, the way she always stood when she was listening and did not want to seem to be.
Kross ate. He ate the whole bowl, slowly, and then he put the spoon down and folded his hands on the table and looked at Wren across it.
"The Weavers' Road," he said. "Do you know it?"
"The old east road. Over the Dye-Works arches."
"Go up onto it at noon. From the Cut end, by the steps behind the boatyard. Walk along it to the far end, over the last arch, by the vats. And look down."
"At what?"
"At everything," said Kross. "Whatever there is to see. Then come back here and tell me what you saw." He paused. "Exactly."
Wren looked at him.
"Is she there?" she said. "The old woman?"
"I don't know," said Kross. "That's why you're going to look."
It was not true. Wren knew it was not true. She had known him two years and five months, and he had never once sent her to look at a thing he did not already know about. He sent her to look so that she would see. So that it would be in her book. So that there would be more than one report.
She did not say so.
"And if she's there?" she said instead.
"Then you'll have seen her."
"And then what?"
Kross looked at her across the scrubbed table for a long moment. At the porridge in front of her that she had not touched.
"And then decide whose report you sign," he said.
He got up. He took his hat off the peg by the kitchen door. And at the door he stopped and said, to Mrs. Oddie's back, without turning round:
"I'll want the carriage at the seventh bell, Mrs. Oddie. Tonight. The plain one. And tell Samuel to give the horses a feed at the fifth. They'll be standing in the cold for a while."
Mrs. Oddie did not turn round. She nodded once.
And Kross went out.
Wren sat at the table and looked at her porridge.
The seventh bell. Tonight. Standing in the cold for a while.
He had already decided. Before he sent her. Before she had looked at anything at all. The carriage was ordered. The horses were to be fed.
She did not know why that made her want to cry again. It should have made it easier. Whatever she saw at noon, whatever she wrote, whatever she said when she came back, it would not change anything. He was going to the arches tonight anyway. It was not up to her.
And yet he had sent her. To look. And to decide.
Mrs. Oddie came and took the bowl away from in front of her. She looked down at the porridge, untouched, with the butter melted into a little yellow pool on top and the brown sugar gone dark.
She did not say anything. She put the bowl in the warming oven at the side of the range, where it would keep. And then she came back, and stood beside Wren's chair, and put her great ham hand on Wren's shoulder, and left it there.
"Mrs. Oddie," said Wren. "Has he ever—"
She stopped. She did not know how to ask it.
"Has he ever not done what he was told?"
Mrs. Oddie's hand stayed on her shoulder. She was quiet for so long that Wren thought she would not answer. She never answered things. Four words, Wren had thought. Never more than four words together.
"Once," said Mrs. Oddie.
Wren turned her head and looked up at her.
Mrs. Oddie was looking at the kitchen window. At the gray light. At the snow on the area railings.
"The winter of the fever," she said. "Eighteen years back. He went north. With men in gray. And came back." Her voice was low and rough, from not being used. "Alone. On foot. In the first frost. With his boots worn through." She breathed out. "And sat down on that step. And didn't get up. Three days."
Wren did not move.
"What did he do?" she said. "Up north? What was he told to do, that he didn't?"
Mrs. Oddie looked down at her.
"Never said," she said.
And she took her hand off Wren's shoulder and went back to the range and stood with her back to the room and her arms folded, and did not say anything else at all.
Wren went the long way to the Weavers' Road.
She did not mean to. Her feet took her. Down from Mercer Street to the river, and along the quays, and past the Fish Stairs, and into the narrow lanes behind them. Where the old fish market was, and the net lofts, and the sail-makers.
She stopped at the bottom of Gull Lane and looked up at a window.
A small one. On the second floor of a narrow house with a sail-maker's shop on the ground floor. The shop had a new name over it now. The window had new curtains. Blue ones, with a pattern of little ships.
She had looked out of that window every morning until she was fifteen. She had looked out of it the morning her father did not know her. She had stood at it with her old ringed stone in her hand and watched the gulls on the roof across the lane and not cried, because there was nobody to cry to.
She stood at the bottom of Gull Lane in her gray coat and looked up at the new blue curtains for a long time.
Then she put her hand in her right coat pocket, where the new stone was, the one from the beach under the Eye, with its white ring. And closed her hand round it.
And went on, to the Weavers' Road.
Harrow
She did not get to the Weavers' Road as soon as she meant to, because on the quay below the Fish Stairs, coming the other way, there were the carts.
She heard them before she saw them. The creak and rumble of iron-shod wheels on the cobbles, slow, heavy, and the blowing of tired horses, and a man's voice, low and hoarse, saying steady, steady there. And then they came round the corner by the old fish market, out of the snow, one behind another. Three of them. Big wagons with canvas tilts, gray with the dirt of the north road, with the canvas torn in places and lashed with rope. The horses were thin. Their heads were down. Their breath came out in long clouds.
And walking beside the first wagon, with one hand on the near horse's bridle, there was a big man in a black Enforcer's coat gone gray with mud, with a broken nose, broken more than once, and a short carbine slung across his back.
Harrow.
Wren stopped dead in the middle of the quay.
She had not seen him since the Eye. Since the morning after, when Kross had sent the Enforcers and the carts back south by the long road, on foot, and ridden on ahead with Wren, to Haverstock and the hired carriage and the fen road. Seven days. He looked as if it had been seven years. His face had gone thin and gray under the stubble, and there was a cut over one eye, half healed, and he walked as if his feet hurt him, which, she thought, they must.
He saw her.
He stopped. The horse stopped with him. The wagon stopped. Behind it, the other two wagons stopped, one after another, creaking, and the drivers looked up.
Harrow looked at her across the cobbles. At her clean gray coat. At her face.
"Cadet," he said.
"Sergeant Harrow."
They looked at each other for a while. Neither of them seemed to know what to say. The snow came down between them, lightly.
"You're back," said Wren. Which was stupid. She could see he was back.
"We're back." He looked down the line of the wagons. "Most of us. Pike's in the second one. His feet went. Frostbite. The doctor at Haverstock says he'll keep them, if he's lucky." He breathed out. "He's not lucky. But he might keep them anyway."
"I'm glad."
"Aye." Harrow rubbed his face with his free hand. "The Inquisitor?"
"At the Ministry. He's—" She stopped. "The Archon's given him the Cleansing. All of it."
Harrow did not say anything for a moment. Something moved in his face. Then it was gone.
"Has he," he said.
He did not say anything else. He did not need to.
Wren looked at the second wagon. At the canvas tilt, gray and torn. She could not see inside it. She thought about Pike, nineteen, with his big ears and his heron neck and his burned palm wrapped in a rag, lying in the back of it with his feet wrapped in more rags. Who had begged her, on the Glass, to take a little girl's face out of him. And she had said no.
She thought about a page in Ferrand's notebook, at the back, in another hand. A list of names. For when we came home. Harrow was on it. Pike was on it.
She opened her mouth.
She did not know what she was going to say. She could not say it. Not here, on the quay, in front of the drivers. Not anywhere. There was no way to say it that would not be worse than not saying it. Sergeant, your name is on a list with a circle beside it. But don't worry. The Inquisitor has drawn a line through it. In ink. For now.
She shut her mouth.
Harrow was looking at her.
"What is it, Cadet?"
"Nothing," said Wren. "I'm just glad you're back."
He looked at her a while longer. He had been an Enforcer eleven years. He knew when people were lying to him. She could see him know it.
He let it go.
"There's something," he said instead. "I wanted to tell somebody. Out there, on the road. I've been carrying it the whole way." He looked down at the cobbles. "The man on the stair. Under the Eye. The one the girl cut."
Wren went still.
She remembered him. A big square man in black, with a beard. Who had been going to throw a stone at an old woman on a stair. And the Thorne girl had come out of the dark with a bent sword in her bandaged hand, and the cut had gone through the air with a sound like wind, and the man had gone down. And Harrow had walked out past the spears to him afterward, and knelt down, and said something. A name.
"His name was Brann," said Harrow. "Tobias Brann. He was from Pewter Lane, by the Tanners' Bridge. He had a wife called Bet, and a little girl, three, called Nan." He said it slowly. As if he had been saying it to himself for seven days, on the road, under his breath, so as not to lose it. "He wasn't a bad man. He was a frightened one. He'd seen what the Ashborn did at the Ridges, and he thought the old woman was going to do it too." He looked up. "That's why he picked up the stone."
Wren did not say anything.
"I'll have to go and tell Bet," said Harrow. "Tonight. Pewter Lane. Somebody has to." He breathed in through his nose. "I thought somebody else should know his name too. In case. I don't know why."
Wren took out her brown book.
She did not ask. She opened it on her palm, there on the quay, in the snow, and wrote it, while he watched. Tobias Brann. Pewter Lane, by the Tanners' Bridge. His wife is Bet. His girl is Nan, three. He was frightened. That's why he picked up the stone.
She showed him.
Harrow looked at it for a long time.
"Thank you, Cadet," he said. Quite quietly.
He turned back to his horse. He clicked his tongue. The horse put its head down and leaned into the collar, and the wagon creaked, and moved. And the second one moved after it, with Pike inside, and the third. They went past her along the quay, slowly, toward the Ministry, toward the yard with the iron doors, toward whatever was waiting for them there.
Wren stood on the quay and watched them go.
She thought about a girl with a burned hand and a bent sword, somewhere on the road south, a long way off, who had cut a frightened man on a stair and did not know his name.
She looked down at her book.
Tobias Brann.
She did not know how she would ever give it to her. She did not know if she ever could. She thought she would try.
She put the book away. And went on, at last, to the Weavers' Road.
