Chapter 94: He Knows Now, Part 1 of 5: Lantern Bridge at First Light
Lantern Bridge at First Light
The city came up out of the dark like something rising out of deep water.
Wren Halloway watched it come, through the little square window of the carriage, with her forehead against the cold glass. First there was nothing. Only the black of the road and the black of the fields on either side, and the snow, gray in the dark, going past and past. Then a glow, low down on the edge of the sky ahead. Blue. Faint. Like the light under a door. Then the glow got wider, and higher, and began to have shapes in it. Roofs. Chimneys. A tower. And then the road came over the last low hill, and there it was, all of it, spread out below them along the river in the gray before dawn.
Veridia.
Every street lit blue. Every quay. Every bridge. Long lines and curves of blue light, going away in every direction, crossing and branching like the veins on the back of a hand. And the river black in the middle of it all. And the Spire, on its hill, black against the first gray of the sky, with one red light burning at its very top, like an eye that never closed.
She had been away fifteen days. It felt like fifteen years.
She did not know what she had expected to feel, seeing it again. Glad, perhaps. Safe. She had been born in it. She had grown up in it, down by the Fish Stairs, in two rooms over a sail-maker's, with her father's voice in the next room and the gulls on the roof. She had thought about it, out on the Glass, lying on the warm black smoothness with her eyes open, unable to sleep. She had thought about the smell of the river. The bells. Mrs. Oddie's peppermints on every table. Her own narrow bed in the room at the top of the Inquisitor's house, with the window that looked out over the chimneys.
She did not feel glad. She did not feel safe.
She felt the way you feel walking back into a room where something bad has happened. Where you know something bad has happened. Even though the room looks just the same.
The carriage went down the hill and through the north gate. The gate was open. It was always open. But there were more gray coats at it than there had been, six instead of two, and a new white barrier across the road, waist-high, freshly painted, and a brazier, and a table. One of the gray coats came out from behind the table with a lamp and held it up to the carriage window and looked in.
He saw who was inside. His face changed. He stepped back so fast that he nearly fell over the brazier, and lifted the barrier with his own hands, and half-saluted, and the carriage went through without stopping.
Wren looked across at the man in the other seat.
Inquisitor Silas Kross had not moved. He had not moved, as far as she knew, since the inn at Haverstock, two days ago. He sat in the corner of the carriage seat with his long legs folded and his hat tipped down over his eyes and his gloved hands lying one on top of the other in his lap. His gray coat was buttoned to the throat. There was mud on it, dried, in a long stain up one side, from the Glass road, from the fens. He had not brushed it off. Kross always brushed his coat. Every night. She had watched him do it in a hundred inns, with a little stiff brush from his bag, slowly, carefully, until the gray cloth was smooth.
He had not brushed it since the Eye.
He was not asleep. She knew that. She had learned, in two years and five months of riding in carriages with him, how to tell. When he was asleep his hands came apart in his lap, just a little. When he was awake, they stayed one on top of the other, exactly, like two pages of a closed book.
They were one on top of the other now.
The carriage went down Gate Street and along the Saltmarsh road and came out onto the north end of Lantern Bridge.
It was the biggest bridge in the city. Seven arches of white stone, and a wide road over the top, with footways on both sides, and lamps along both parapets, the old kind, house-shaped, with little roofs, burning blue. On the first lamppost there was a plaque that everybody passed and nobody read anymore. LANTERN BRIDGE. BUILT IN THE REIGN OF—and then a space, where the stone had been chiseled smooth, as if a word had been cut out of it—. RESTORED BY THE GRACE OF THE MINISTRY. Wren had stopped reading it when she was six.
She read it now, as they went past. She did not know why. And she looked, for a long moment, at the smooth empty place in the stone where the word had been.
There were barriers on the bridge. She had known there would be. They had been there before she went north. But there were more of them. White, waist-high, across both footways at each end, so that people on foot had to stop and be looked at before they could cross. There were gray coats at every one. Four at the north end. Four at the south. And in the middle of the bridge, by the parapet, there was a cart.
A black cart. Closed. With a cover tied down over a frame. And on the side of the cover, painted in blue, an open hand.
It was standing still. Waiting. Its horse had a blanket over its back and its head down, and its breath went up in white clouds. There was a man on the box, muffled in a plum-colored scarf, and the smell of peppermint came in through the carriage window as they went past, very strong, very sweet.
Wren knew that smell. Mrs. Oddie's peppermints. Every table in Kross's house.
She looked at the cart. She could not help it. And the cart had a window. A little one, high up, in the back, with bars across it. And at the window, very small, very white, there was a face.
A child's face. A girl, perhaps. Five or six. Looking out through the bars at the bridge, and the lamps, and the river.
She did not look frightened. That was the worst part. She did not look anything. She looked the way Wren's father had looked, at the end. When he was sitting in his chair by the window over the sail-maker's, with his hands in his lap, and Wren came in and said Da, and he turned his head and looked at her and did not know who she was.
The carriage went past. The cart was behind them.
Wren turned her head to look back. She could not see the window anymore. Only the black cover, and the blue hand.
She turned round again.
Kross had tipped his hat up. Only a little. Enough to see out from under the brim. He was not looking at her. He was looking out of his own window, on the other side, at the river. At the black water going past under the white arches.
His gloved hands were still one on top of the other in his lap.
But the top one had closed.
"Inquisitor," said Wren.
She did not know what she was going to say. She had not decided anything. The word just came out of her, into the cold carriage, small and cracked.
Kross did not turn his head.
"I know, Halloway," he said.
That was all. His voice was quite quiet. Quite ordinary. The voice he used for weather, and inn bills, and asking the way.
I know.
The carriage came off the south end of Lantern Bridge and turned, not left, toward the Ministry and the Mercer Street house, but right. Up. Onto the long wide road that went up the side of the Hill in great slow curves, between high walls with snow on them and the bare black branches of trees showing over the tops.
Wren had only been up this road twice before in her life.
She knew where it went.
She sat back in her seat and folded her own hands in her lap, one on top of the other, the way he did. And held them very still. And watched the gray light come up over the walls, and the snow on the branches, and the gates of the great houses going past one after another, until the road reached the top of the Hill, and the carriage turned in at the last gate of all.
The Garden Room
The house at the top of the Hill was not the biggest house on the Hill. Wren had always thought it would be. It was not even the grandest. It was long and low and white, with a plain front and a plain door and no columns, and a slate roof with snow on it, and a row of tall windows along the ground floor with their curtains drawn. It looked like the house of a schoolmaster who had come into a little money. A careful man, with simple tastes, who did not like to be looked at.
There was no guard at the gate. There was no guard at the door. There was a man in a plain dark coat who opened the door before they had knocked, and bowed, and took Kross's hat, and did not ask for Wren's.
And there was Lucian.
He came down the hall toward them very fast, the way he always moved, as if he were late for something that had happened yesterday. The Archon's secretary. Tall and thin and pale, with smooth dark hair brushed flat to his head and a face that looked as if it had not slept since it was born. He had a sheaf of papers under one arm and a pen behind one ear and another pen in his hand, and he was looking at the papers as he came, and then at Kross, and then at the papers again.
"Inquisitor," he said. "Good. Good. You're early. He said you'd be early." He looked at the papers. "He said you'd be early, and you are. Good." He looked at Kross again, at the mud on his coat, and his eyes went wide, and then went back to the papers, as if the papers might have an answer for the mud. "This way. He's in the garden room. He's always in the garden room in the mornings. This way."
He turned and went back down the hall, fast. Then he stopped, and turned round, and looked at Wren.
"And the cadet," he said. "Halloway. Yes. He said bring the cadet." He checked the papers. He checked them again. He checked them a third time, running his pen down a line and stopping, and moving his lips. "Yes. Bring the cadet."
He went on.
Wren had heard about Lucian. Everybody in the Ministry had. They said he checked everything three times. Every letter, every list, every number. They said he had once sent a whole cart of Council papers back to the printer because a comma was wrong, and then checked the corrected ones three times, and found the comma had been right all along, and sent them back again. They said it as a joke. They laughed.
Wren did not laugh. She felt him, going down the hall in front of her.
She could feel people again. Since the Glass. Since the moment they came off it, at the Edge, and the hum stopped and her own gift came back into her like a wet glove pulled on too tight. She could feel them the way you can feel heat off a stove with your eyes shut. Not their thoughts. Not quite. The shape of what was in them. The weight of it. The color.
Lucian felt like a room full of clocks, all ticking at once, all slightly wrong.
She followed him down the hall. Kross walked beside her. His boots left mud on the pale floor. Nobody said anything about it.
The hall was very plain. White walls. A dark wood floor, polished. No pictures. No carpets. A few chairs against the walls, plain wooden ones, very old, very good. And a smell. Warm. Green. Like a hothouse. Like summer in a box.
At the end of the hall there was a door with glass panes in it, misted over on the inside. Lucian opened it.
And warm air came out, so warm and wet and green that Wren gasped at it, after fifteen days of cold, and her eyes watered. She had to stop and blink. It was like walking into a cloud.
The garden room.
It was a long room made almost entirely of glass. Glass walls, in small square panes held by thin white iron. A glass roof, sloping, with the snow lying on it in a soft white blanket so that the light came through it pale and even and very gentle, like light through paper. And under the glass, everything was green.
Trees. Small ones, in great stone tubs, with dark shining leaves and little white flowers, and among the flowers, fruit. Lemons. Real lemons, yellow, in the middle of winter. Wren could smell them. And ferns, tall ones, taller than her, with long feathery arms that arched over the paths and dripped. And vines going up the white iron of the walls, and along the roof, hanging down. And flowers. Everywhere. In pots, in beds, in baskets hung from the iron on chains. Red ones. White ones. Purple ones like bells. Flowers she did not know the names of. Flowers that should not have been blooming anywhere in the world in the Frost month.
There were iron stoves along one wall, three of them, black, low, glowing. That was where the heat came from. And somewhere in the green there was water running, a soft continuous sound, a fountain or a channel. And birds. Small ones. She could hear them. She looked up and saw one, a little yellow bird, sitting on a vine near the roof, looking down at her with its head on one side.
It was the most beautiful room she had ever been in.
She stood just inside the door with the warm wet air on her face, and the lemons, and the little yellow bird, and she wanted to cry. She did not know why. She thought it was because she had been cold for fifteen days. She thought it was because she had seen a farmer under a sack with his boots sticking out, and a child's face at a barred window, and a man on his knees under a dome. And this was a room where none of that had ever happened. Where nothing bad had ever happened at all. Where it was always summer.
"Mind the step," said Lucian. "There's a step. There." He pointed. "Mind it."
There was a path. Pale stone, between the beds and the tubs, winding away into the green. Lucian went along it. Kross went after him. Wren went after Kross.
The path went round a bed of red flowers, and under an arch of the tall ferns, so that their wet feathery tips brushed Wren's hair, and past the fountain. It was a small round pool, of white stone, with a little stone girl standing in the middle of it holding a jug, and the water coming out of the jug in a thin silver stream. There were fish in the pool. Gold ones, slow, turning.
And beyond the fountain, at the far end of the garden room, where the glass wall looked out over the snowy roofs of the city all the way down to the river, there was a table.
A small round table, of white iron, like the walls. With a white cloth on it. And three chairs round it, white iron too, with cushions. And on the table, a tea tray. A silver pot, and a jug, and a bowl of sugar. And cups.
And beside the table, with his back to them, there was a man.
He was bending over one of the flower beds. A bed of roses. White ones. He had a small pair of silver scissors in one hand, and he was cutting a rose. Carefully. Turning the stem to find exactly the right place. He cut. And straightened up, with the white rose in his hand, and turned round.
Wren had seen him before. Twice. Once at a parade, from a long way off, at the back of a crowd, when she was ten. Once in the Council chamber, from the clerks' gallery, through a pillar. She had never been closer than the length of a street.
She was close now.
He was not tall. She had thought he would be tall. He was a little under the middle height, and thin, and very upright, the way some old men are upright, as if there is an iron rod down the back of their coat. His hair was white. Cut close to his head, very short, very neat. His face was long and pale and smooth, with very few lines on it for a man of his age, whatever his age was. Wren could not have said. Sixty. Seventy. More. His eyes were pale too. Gray, or nearly. Very clear. And he was dressed in plain gray. A plain gray coat, a plain gray waistcoat, a plain white shirt. No chain. No ring. No medal. Nothing.
And gloves. Gray ones. Pale. Very fine. Kid, she thought. So fine and pale that from a little way off, in the soft white light of the garden room, you might have thought his hands were bare.
He smiled at them.
It was a kind smile. Tired, a little. Pleased. The smile of a man whose friend has come back from a long journey, and who has been worried, and is glad.
"Silas," said the Archon. "There you are."
The Chipped Cup
"Archon," said Kross.
He bowed. Not low. A short bow, from the neck. The bow you would give an old schoolmaster you met in the street.
The Archon came across to them, along the path, with the white rose in his gloved hand. He walked slowly, as if there were all the time in the world. He stopped in front of Kross and looked up at him, because Kross was much the taller. He looked at Kross's face for a long moment. At the lines in it. At the mud on his coat.
"You look tired," he said.
"It was a long road."
"It was. It was a very long road. Longer than any of us thought." The Archon's voice was dry and quiet and quite ordinary. The voice of a man at a breakfast table. "Sit down, Silas. Before you fall down. Lucian, tell them more hot water. And bread. And something with it. The Inquisitor hasn't eaten since Haverstock, I should think."
"Since Haverstock," said Lucian. "Yes. Bread. Something with it." He wrote it down. He checked it. He went away fast down the path, checking it again.
The Archon turned to Wren.
She had not moved. She was standing on the path behind Kross with her hands at her sides and the warm wet air on her face, and she found she could not move. She did not know why. He was only an old man in a gray coat with a rose in his hand. He was smiling at her.
"Cadet Halloway," he said.
She made herself bow. It came out wrong. Too fast, too low. Like a child bobbing to a lady in church.
"Archon."
"You've been a long way," he said kindly. "Further than most people go in their whole lives. Over the Glass and back again." He looked at her with his pale clear eyes. "Was it very strange?"
She did not know what to say. She opened her mouth.
"Yes," she said. "Archon."
"I'm sure it was. I have never been. I have always wanted to." He smiled. "Sit down too. Sit there, by the window. You'll want to look at the city. People always do, the first time. It's the best view of it there is."
There were three chairs at the little white table. Wren sat down in the one by the window, because he had told her to. The cushion was soft and dry and warm from the stoves. Kross sat down in the one across from her, slowly, the way he did everything now, as if his body had to be asked first. And the Archon sat down in the third. Between them. With his back to the green, and his face to the window, and the snow-light on him.
He laid the white rose on the cloth beside the tea tray.
Then he picked up the silver pot, himself, and poured.
Wren watched him do it. She had never seen a man of any importance pour his own tea. Kross did. But Kross was Kross. The Archon poured the way her mother used to pour, at the table over the sail-maker's, when there were people. Carefully. Holding the lid of the pot down with one gloved finger. Tipping it slowly so the tea came out in a thin brown stream and did not splash. He filled one cup. Then another.
Then he picked up the third cup, and looked at it, and smiled.
It was not like the other two. The other two were very fine. Thin white china, so thin that the light came through it, with a little band of gold round the rim, and a gold line on the handle. This one was thicker. Plainer. Cream, not white, and heavy, with no gold at all. The kind of cup you might find in a farmhouse kitchen, or a soldiers' mess. And on its rim, on one side, there was a chip. A small one. A little half-moon, the size of a fingernail, where something had knocked it once, a long time ago. The edges of the chip were worn smooth.
The Archon filled it, and set it down in front of Kross. With the chip turned toward him. Toward his right hand. As if he knew exactly which way Kross liked it.
"Yours," he said. "As always."
Kross looked at the cup.
He did not pick it up at once. He sat with his gloved hands on his knees and looked at it. At the chip. At the steam going up from the tea in a thin gray line and bending in the warm air.
"You kept it," he said.
"Of course I kept it. Where else would I keep it?" The Archon picked up his own cup, the fine one with the gold, and held it in both gloved hands without drinking. "You were nine, the first time you sat at this table. Do you remember? Your feet didn't reach the floor. You sat on your hands because you didn't know what else to do with them, and you wouldn't drink the tea because it was in one of these." He touched the gold rim of his own cup. "You said you'd break it. So I sent down to the kitchen for something you couldn't break." He smiled. "And you broke it anyway. The very first week. Knocked it against the sugar bowl."
Wren looked at Kross.
She had never thought of him as nine. She had never thought of him as anything but what he was. Tall, and gray, and tired, and kind in a way that nobody ever saw. She tried to see him with his feet not reaching the floor, sitting on his hands, at this table, in this green room, with this man. She could not.
Kross picked up the cup.
He held it the way you hold something you have held a great many times. His thumb found the place by the handle. His fingers found the curve. He turned it, a little, so that the chip was away from his mouth. And drank.
"Thank you," he said.
"You're very welcome." The Archon drank too, at last. A small sip. He set his cup down. "Your grandfather would be proud of you, Silas. I've always thought so."
Kross did not answer.
Wren picked up her own cup, because she did not know what else to do. It was so thin that she was afraid her fingers would go through it. The tea was very hot and very good, strong and dark, with something in it that tasted of smoke. She drank, and it went down into her like a lit coal. She had not had tea since Haverstock either.
And while she drank, she felt the Archon.
She did not mean to. She never meant to. It was not something she did. It was something that happened, the way you hear a sound in the next room. She was sitting at a little table in a warm green room with two men, and she could feel the shape of what was in them, the weight of it, the color, the way you can feel the heat off two stoves with your eyes shut.
Kross, across from her, felt the way he always felt. Like a very old house with all the shutters closed, and something moving about inside, slowly, in the dark, from room to room. Heavy. Tired. A great many rooms.
And the Archon.
She felt for him the way you put your hand out in the dark for the wall.
And there was no wall.
There was nothing. Not nothing like Ferrand, out on the Glass, who had felt like a man made of glass, so that she could see through him to the other side and there was nothing on the other side either. Not that. This was different. This was like putting your hand into a pool of water so still and so clear that you could not tell where the air stopped and the water began. Cool. Smooth. No weight at all. No color. And when she tried to feel further, to feel what was at the bottom of it, she found that what came back to her was her own face. Her own fear. Her own tiredness. Her own hand, reaching. As if the water were a mirror.
She took her hand back.
She put her cup down on its saucer. It rattled, a little. She put her other hand on top of it to stop the rattle.
The Archon was looking at her.
Not at her hands. At her face. With his pale clear eyes, kindly, a little curious. As if she were a book he had been meaning to read.
"The tea is too strong for you," he said gently. "I'm sorry. I forget, sometimes, that not everybody likes it as Silas does. Lucian will bring milk."
"No," said Wren. Her voice came out very small. "No, Archon. Thank you. It's very good."
"Good." He smiled. "Look at the city, Cadet. While we talk. It's what the window is for."
Wren turned her head and looked out of the window.
The city lay below them, all of it, white with snow, going down in steps and roofs and chimneys to the river. The river black, with the bridges over it, one after another. The blue lamps going out now, one by one, as the day came. The Bank, white, on its own hill across the water, with its lions. The Ministry, gray and square. The Spire, very near, on the next hill, so near that she could see the shape of the windows in it, tall and narrow, going up and up. And the red light at the very top, still burning, though it was day.
She looked at the city and did not see it.
She was thinking about a pool of water so still that it showed you your own face.
