Chapter 3: The Thief in Silk, Part 1 of 5: The Open Door
Arc 1: The Siphon's Ledger.
The Open Door
Corin stood very still outside the small back door of the Inkwell, with the rain running off the brim of his hat, and listened.
Clink.
A pause.
Clink. Clink.
Metal on metal. Small and heavy. The sound of something being lifted out of a drawer and set down on a hard surface, carefully, one piece at a time. Corin knew that sound. He had never heard it in his life, and he knew it the way he knew the shape of his own hand. He had heard it through another man's ears, a night ago, in this same building. Big old-fashioned letters of black metal, as long as a finger, being lifted out of a drawer marked Q.
Someone was in there. Someone was emptying the drawer.
And Old Joss, deaf as a stone, was asleep somewhere among the presses in his nightshirt, and would not hear a thing.
Well, said a dry voice behind his eyes. That's inconvenient.
Corin did not answer. He was thinking. Or trying to. His head still felt crowded and strange, full of a dead man's week, loud and bright and not his own. And under it, a cold, slow tiredness, like water in the bottom of a boat. He had not slept. He had nearly died that morning. His hands had not stopped shaking all day.
He put his hand into his coat pocket, the left one, for the keys, without thinking about it.
His fingers touched glass.
The Jar in the Pocket
He took it out and held it up to the faint blue glow that came round the corner of the building from the nearest streetlamp.
It was a jar. Small, the same size as the three Toby had given him that morning, with a brass lid screwed down tight. But it was not empty. There was something in it. A mist. Blue-white and thick, much thicker than the thin thread of Pell's last minutes. It filled the jar from the bottom almost to the lid, and it was not turning slowly like a sleeping cat. It was moving. Fast. Swirling round and round the inside of the glass in quick, restless, angry little loops, like a wasp in a bottle. When he held it, he could feel it through the glass. A faint buzzing against his palm. A heat.
There was a label on it. His own untidy handwriting.
KESTLE, J. "KETTLE." Bare-knuckle. Lower Docks pits. 31 fights, 29 wins. Died in the ring.
Volatile. For emergencies.
And under that, smaller, pressed hard:
He will make you angry. Let him.
Corin looked at the jar for a long time.
He did not remember putting it in his pocket. He did not remember taking it from the box under the bed, the box he had told himself that morning not to open unless he meant it. But there it was, in his coat. In the pocket where his hand would go without thinking. Where a man who knew he was going out alone, at three in the morning, to a place where people had been killed, would put the one thing he might need.
He must have done it while he was writing. Without noticing. The way he had walked to Bram's shop that morning without meaning to.
Your hands know more than you do, said Malakor. They usually do. That's why you're still alive.
Corin put the jar back in his left pocket. Carefully. He kept his hand on it.
Then he pushed the small back door open, slowly, with two fingers, and went in.
Inside the Inkwell
The dark inside the printing house was thicker than the dark outside. It was a different kind of dark. Not empty. Full. Crowded with shapes that he could feel more than see, standing all around him in the black, enormous and silent, like animals asleep in a stable.
The presses.
He had seen them through Pell's eyes, by the light of Old Joss's candle. Long rows of them, iron and wood, taller than a man, with their great flat plates and their wheels and their long curved levers. Now there was no candle. There was only the faintest gray glow from a row of high, dirty windows along the top of the far wall, where the light of the streetlamps outside came through, weak and blue and filtered through years of soot. It lay along the tops of the presses in thin pale lines, picking out an edge here, a wheel there, the curve of a lever. The rest was black.
And above, hanging from the lines strung across the ceiling, the paper. Thousands of sheets of it, drying. Pale, faintly gray in the window-glow, hanging perfectly still in the dead air. Like a ceiling of ghosts. Like a forest of white leaves that had stopped falling halfway down.
The smell was overwhelming. Ink. Not the bitter waste of Gutter Lane. Fresh. Strong. Oily and black and metallic, so thick in the closed air that Corin could taste it on his tongue. Under it, hot metal and machine oil and wet paper and, very faintly, the smoke of a fire that had burned out hours ago.
And silence. Except for the rain on the roof, a soft endless patter far above. And, somewhere ahead of him, deep in the dark, to the left:
Clink.
Corin began to walk.
He went slowly. He went the way he had come out of his room that morning, one step and then the next, letting his feet find the floor before he trusted his weight to them. The floor was stone, smooth and slightly sticky, and there were things on it. Scraps of paper that whispered under his boots. A coil of cord. Something small and metal that rolled away from his toe with a tiny skittering sound, and he stopped, and held his breath, and waited.
Ahead, in the dark, the clinking had stopped too.
Nothing moved. The ghost-paper hung still overhead. The rain pattered.
Then, after a long moment:
Clink.
Corin let his breath out, slowly, and went on.
He went between two of the great presses, sideways, so close that the iron of one brushed his shoulder and left it cold. Past a long table covered in stacks of paper, square and neat. Past a cold iron stove, its door hanging open. Past a heap of something soft, sacks or blankets, and in the heap, a sound. Breathing. Slow and deep and rattling, with a faint whistle on the out-breath, the breathing of a very old man in a very deep sleep.
Old Joss. Curled up in his blankets beside the stove, asleep, deaf to everything.
Corin did not wake him. He went on.
The presses ended. There was a clear space of stone floor, perhaps ten paces wide, and then the wall. And against the wall, rising up out of the dark, so tall that its top was lost in the shadows under the ceiling, the type cabinet. Hundreds of shallow wooden drawers, row upon row upon row, each with its small brass label catching the faint gray window-light, so that the whole cabinet seemed to glitter, very dimly, like a wall of tiny stars.
And in front of it, standing on a stool, reaching up to the top corner, there was someone.
Corin stopped at the edge of the shadow of the last press. He did not breathe.
The figure was not tall. Not short. Slim, and very straight, standing on the stool with one hand braced flat against the side of the cabinet and the other reaching up into the open drawer at the top. The faint light from the high windows fell across them in a single pale bar, and in it, Corin could see a long dark coat. A hood, pulled up. And something else.
A sheen. A soft, rippling, shifting gleam, where the gray light touched the coat. Not leather. Not wool.
Silk.
The figure lifted another heavy black letter out of the drawer, and turned, and set it down very carefully on top of the cabinet beside them, on a growing row of others.
Clink.
Then they reached into the drawer again, deeper this time, all the way to the bottom. And went very still.
Corin heard their breath catch. A small, sharp sound in the dark. The sound of someone who has been looking for something for a very long time, and has just touched it.
The figure drew their hand out of the drawer.
There was something in it. Something small and flat and dark. Corin could not see its color in the gray light. But he did not need to. He knew it the way he knew his own name, which is to say, he knew it because he had read it, in his own handwriting, an hour ago.
A red book. With a rose on the cover, wrapped in thorns.
The figure stood on the stool, holding it in both hands, close against their chest. They did not move. They did not open it. They only held it, and bent their hooded head over it, and stood there, very still, in the single pale bar of light, as if they were praying.
Corin stepped out of the shadow.
"That isn't yours," he said.
The Thief
She moved before he had finished the last word.
Corin did not see it happen. That was the thing he remembered afterward, when he wrote it down. One moment there was a hooded figure standing on a stool with a red book clutched to its chest. The next, the stool was rocking, empty, on its legs, and the figure was on the floor in front of him, crouched low, and there was something cold and thin and sharp resting against the side of his throat, just under the jaw, exactly where the pulse beat.
He had not heard her land. He had not heard the blade leave its sheath. He had heard nothing at all except the soft rustle of silk, like a bird settling its wings.
"Don't," said a voice. Low. Hard. A woman's voice. "Don't move. Don't shout. Don't put your hand in your pocket."
Corin did not move. He did not shout. His left hand was already in his pocket, resting on the jar marked Volatile. He did not take it out.
"Take your hand out of your pocket," said the voice. "Slowly. Empty."
He took it out. Slowly. Empty.
The point of the blade did not move. It rested against his skin with a terrible, perfect steadiness. Not pressing. Not cutting. Just there. He could feel the fine edge of it, colder than the night, colder than the rod in his coat, and he understood, in the clear calm way you understand things in a dream, that the person holding it could open his throat with less effort than it takes to turn a page.
"Who are you?" said the voice.
"Corin Vane. Ministry of Records."
The blade did not move. But something in the dark changed. A breath, drawn in sharp.
"Ministry," said the voice. It made the word sound like something you scrape off a boot. "Of course. Of course you are." A pause. "Then it's even less yours than mine."
The Face in the Hood
His eyes were getting used to the dark. The faint gray light from the high windows behind her lay along the edge of her hood, along one shoulder, along the long thin line of the sword running from her hand to his throat. He could see her a little now. Not well. Enough.
She was young. Older than Toby, younger than him, perhaps. Twenty-four, twenty-five. Her face, inside the shadow of the hood, was pale and narrow, all sharp lines: a straight nose, a hard jaw, high cheekbones like the edges of a knife, and a mouth pressed into a thin flat line, as if she were holding something behind it that she did not intend to let out. Her eyes were light. Gray, he thought, though in that light they could have been any color. Steady. Watching him the way a hawk watches a field.
A lock of hair had fallen out of the hood across her forehead. Black. Cut short. Cut badly, Corin noticed, in a ragged line, as if someone had done it themselves, in a hurry, with a knife.
The coat was silk. He had been right about that. A long riding coat, dark green or dark blue or black, cut close to the body and flaring at the hips, with a high collar and a row of small covered buttons down the front. It was a beautiful coat. It had been made for a great lady, by someone who knew exactly what they were doing, and it had cost more than Corin earned in a year.
And it was old. Very old. Worn thin at the cuffs. Shiny at the elbows. And mended. Everywhere. Small, careful, almost invisible mends, the stitches so fine and so close that you would only see them if you were looking for them, in the right light. A tear at the shoulder, closed. A burn on the sleeve, patched with a scrap of silk so close in color that it almost disappeared. A whole new hem, sewn on with stitches as small as grains of sand.
Someone had loved that coat very much. Someone had kept it alive.
The sword was plain. That surprised him. He had expected something to match the coat, a jeweled hilt, a silver guard. It was not. It was long and slim and straight, a duelist's blade, with a simple guard of dark steel and a grip wrapped in black leather worn smooth by her hand. On the pommel, where a family crest might once have been, there was only a rough, scratched patch, as if something had been filed away.
He looked at the red book in her other hand. She was holding it against her chest, pressed flat, her fingers spread over the cover. Over the rose. Over the thorns.
"You're a thief," he said.
The gray eyes did not blink.
"So are you," she said. "You came here to take it too. At three in the morning. Through the back door. You knew which drawer." The blade shifted. A hair's width. The cold edge moved along his jaw, very slightly, like a fingertip. "How did you know which drawer?"
"A dead man told me."
She went still.
Not the stillness of a fighter, waiting. Something else. The stillness of someone who has been slapped and is deciding whether to believe it.
"What dead man?" she said.
Pell
"His name was Pell Dorran," said Corin. "He was a scribe. Grade Three. First Basement of the Ministry of Records. He was twenty-six. He laughed like a donkey. He found that book hidden inside the cover of an old Treasury ledger, a week ago, and he hid it here. In that drawer. Under the Qs. With a deaf old printer's help."
He did not look toward the heap of blankets by the cold stove. He did not need to. He felt her eyes go there, quickly, and come back.
"And?" she said.
"And someone sent him a note. On good paper. With a red wax seal. A rose wrapped in thorns." He watched her face. "It said, you have found something that belongs to my family. It said, I will pay you. I will tell you what it means. It said, come to Gutter Lane, before dawn, alone. It was signed T."
The gray eyes had gone very wide.
"So he went," said Corin. "Yesterday morning. At the fourth bell. To the lane right outside this wall. And there was no one called T waiting for him. There was a tall woman in a white mask who smelled of lilies, and a big man with hands like sacks of flour. And she put a silver cup against his head and drank everything he was. Every memory. Every face. His little sister. His gran. How to breathe." His voice was very flat. "His heart stopped because there was nothing left in him to tell it to beat. And then the big man broke his skull, so it would look like a robbery. And they left him on the rubbish heap at the end of the lane with his eyes open, smiling at the sky."
The sword did not move. But the hand holding it had begun, very faintly, to shake.
"I know," said Corin, "because I breathed what was left of him this morning. I was him, for a while. I sat at his desk. I cut open that ledger with his little knife. I carried that book in here under his shirt. I lay on that heap and felt his heart stop." He paused. "So yes. A dead man told me which drawer."
For a long moment, she said nothing at all.
The rain pattered on the roof far above. The ghost-paper hung still. Somewhere behind Corin, in his heap of blankets, Old Joss let out a long, whistling, rattling snore.
Then she said, very quietly, "You're a harvester."
"Yes."
"I've heard of you. People like you." Her lip curled, just slightly. "Ash-breathers. Grave-robbers. You crawl into dead men's heads and wear them like coats." She looked at his face, close, in the gray light. "I can see it in your eyes. The blue. Round the edges. Like frost on a window."
Corin did not know that his eyes looked like that. He had not looked in a mirror that morning. He did not know if he owned one.
"He didn't know," he said. "Pell. He didn't know what he'd found. He didn't know whose it was. He just knew his own name was in it." He held her eyes. "He burned the whole week, at the end. Everything he'd learned. So she couldn't take it. So she'd never find this drawer. He used the biggest thing he had to make one flash of light, and he ran four steps, and then they caught him."
He looked at the red book against her chest.
"He died so nobody would take that," he said. "And now you've taken it."
The sword shook harder.
Then, very slowly, the cold thin point slid away from his throat.
She did not put it away. She held it low, at her side, point down, angled toward the floor between them, the way you hold a sword when you have decided not to kill someone yet but have not decided not to later. But it was not at his throat anymore.
Corin let out a breath he had not known he was holding.
"I didn't know," she said. Her voice was very low. "That he was dead. I didn't know." She swallowed. "I was told a scribe had found it. A scribe called Dorran. I was told he'd hidden it here. I was told to come tonight, before anyone else did." Her free hand tightened on the red book until the knuckles went white. "Nobody told me anyone had died."
"Who told you?"
She looked at him for a long moment.
Then she reached up with her free hand, the one that was not holding the sword or the book, and pushed back her hood.
