Chapter 3: The Thief in Silk, Part 2 of 5: Lyra
Lyra
Without the hood, she looked younger. And harder.
The black hair was short all over, cropped close at the back and sides and left a little longer on top, where it fell across her forehead in a ragged line. It had been cut with a knife. Corin was sure of it now. He could see the uneven places, the little tufts that had been missed. It did not look like the hair of a great lady. It looked like the hair of someone who had once had a great deal of it, long and heavy and beautiful, and had one day taken a knife and cut it all off, because she no longer had anyone to brush it, or the time to do it herself, or because she was angry. Or all three.
The gray light lay across her face. It was a face that had been made for portraits. Pale and fine-boned and proud. And it was very tired. There were shadows under the gray eyes like bruises. There was a small, healing cut on her chin. There was a line between her black eyebrows that looked as if it had been there a long time and had no plans to leave.
"My name," she said, "is Lyra Thorne."
She said it the way some people would throw a stone. To see if it would hit.
It did.
Thorne. There was something in the broadsheets. His house went bankrupt. Overnight. And he went mad, they said. Burned his own mind out for some reason nobody knows. His daughter sold the townhouse.
Toby's voice. In the boiler room. That afternoon.
"You're his daughter," said Corin. "Lord Thorne's."
"I'm his daughter." Her chin came up a little. "Lord Aurelian Thorne. Of House Thorne. Of the rose and the thorn." There was something bitter in the way she said it, like a joke she had stopped finding funny a long time ago. "You've heard of us. Everyone's heard of us. We're the family that fell off the top of the world in a single night, and nobody knows why."
She held up the red book. Not to show him. To show herself, he thought. As if she still could not believe it was real.
"Except my father," she said. "He knows why. Or he did. Before."
"Before what?"
"Before he burned it all." The words came out flat and quick, as if she had said them many times and had worn all the feeling off them. "Two years ago. In his study. In the middle of the night. Every secret he ever kept. Every name he ever knew. Every memory of my mother, and my brother, and me. All of it, at once, in one spell. They found him in the morning sitting in his chair by the cold fire with his hands in his lap." She paused. "He smiles now. All the time. He's very polite. He taps on the wall with one finger. And he doesn't know who I am."
The rain pattered on the roof. Old Joss snored in his blankets.
Corin thought of Old Terrence on the second-floor landing. Good morning. Smiling at the gray sky.
He thought of something else, too. A low, warm, beautiful voice in a black lane, like honey poured over a cold stone.
Lord Thorne is not well, I'm afraid. He has not been well for some time. He sends his regrets.
"She knew," he said slowly.
Lyra frowned. "Who?"
"The woman in the lane. With the mask. When Pell said she wasn't Lord Thorne, she laughed. She said Lord Thorne is not well. He has not been well for some time. She knew about your father. She knew he couldn't have written that note." Corin looked at the red book in Lyra's hand. "She knew he couldn't have written anything at all. That's why she could use his seal. Nobody would ever be able to ask him."
Lyra's face went very white in the gray light.
The Message
"You asked who told me," she said, after a long moment. Her voice was not quite steady. "Nobody told me. Not to my face. A message came. Last night. At dusk."
She put the sword back in its sheath. Not all the way. Just enough that she could take her hand off the grip without it falling. Then she reached into the front of her silk coat, under the high collar, and took out a folded scrap of paper and held it out to him.
Corin took it. He turned it toward the gray light from the high windows.
It was cheap paper. Gray and thick and soft, torn from the edge of a bigger sheet. Proof paper, he thought. The kind of paper that printers use to try out a page before they print it properly. And on it, in big, clumsy, square capital letters, as if they had been written by someone who had learned his letters very late in life, from the backs of printed sheets, and had never been taught to join them:
THORNE GIRL.
THE SCRIBE DORRAN HAD YOUR FATHERS RED BOOK. ROSE ON IT. HE HID IT AT THE INKWELL. CABINET BY THE WALL. TOP CORNER. Q.
GO TONIGHT. LATE. BEFORE THE OTHERS COME.
BURN THIS.
There was no name at the bottom. But in the bottom corner there was a mark instead. A thumbprint. Black. Pressed into the gray paper in printing ink, every line and whorl of it as sharp and clear as if it had been engraved. And all around it, tiny black specks, like a spatter of pepper.
Corin looked at the thumbprint for a long moment. Then he turned his head and looked at the heap of blankets by the cold stove.
"A girl brought it," said Lyra. "A little thing, in a boy's coat and cap. She came down off the roof of the stable where I live. Over the gutter, down the drainpipe, as easy as you'd come down a flight of stairs. She put it in my hand and said for the Thorne girl, from a man who can't hear you say thank you, and held out her hand for two coppers, and went back up the drainpipe before I could ask her anything."
"Brass key on a string?" said Corin. "Round her neck?"
Lyra stared at him.
"How do you know that?"
"I met her too," said Corin. "An hour ago. She told me to go to bed."
Joss
Behind them, the snoring stopped.
There was a rustle of blankets. A creak of old bones. A long, rattling, phlegmy cough. And then a small, sharp, yellow light bloomed in the dark beside the stove: a match, struck against the iron, lifted to the wick of a candle stub in a tin holder.
Old Joss sat up in his heap of blankets with the candle in his hand and looked at them.
He looked even smaller than he had through Pell's eyes. Smaller and older and more bent, in his nightshirt and his leather printer's apron, with his bald speckled head shining in the candlelight and his little black eyes going from one of them to the other. From Lyra, with her silk coat and her half-sheathed sword and the red book clutched against her chest. To Corin, standing in the dark in his long gray Ministry coat, with his collar up and his hat dripping and his hand gone, without thinking, back into his left pocket.
The old man's eyes stopped on Corin's coat. On the long gray wool. On the brass buttons, with their open book and their closed eye.
He put the candle down very carefully on the floor beside him. Then he reached under his blankets and brought out a composing stick. A long, flat, heavy steel tool, as long as a man's forearm, with a sharp square edge, the kind printers use to set lines of type. He held it across his knees in both hands. Not raised. Just there.
"Ministry," he said. Too loud, the way deaf people speak, flat and cracked like an old bell. "In my shop. In the middle of the night." His little black eyes went to Lyra. "He with you, girl?"
Lyra opened her mouth. Then she looked at Corin. And something went across her tired face, quick and hard to read.
"I don't know yet," she said.
She said it facing the old man, clearly, so that he could see her lips.
Old Joss looked at her for a long moment. Then back at Corin. His speckled face was very still.
Corin took his hand out of his pocket. Slowly. Empty. He took off his dripping hat and held it against his chest. And he said, facing the old man, as clearly as he could, letting his lips shape every word:
"Two quick. One slow. Two quick."
Old Joss went rigid.
"Q," said Corin. "Gothic. Thirty-six point. Nobody's set a page in that face in forty years. Nobody's opened that drawer since your master died." He paused. "Good choice, boy."
The composing stick slid out of the old printer's hands and fell into his lap with a thud.
"He told you," whispered Old Joss. It was the first time Corin had heard him speak quietly. "Pell. Before—he told you—"
"He didn't tell me. He showed me." Corin crouched down, so that his face was level with the old man's in the candlelight. "I'm a harvester, Joss. I breathed what was left of him. Yesterday. In the lane, outside your wall." He touched his own chest. "He's in here. A little. For a while. The last week of him. He came to you in the night, and you let him in, and you didn't ask. You said, I'm deaf, see. Very deaf."
The old man's face crumpled.
It happened all at once, the way a sheet of paper crumples in a fist. All the speckled lines and folds of it pulled together, and his little black eyes squeezed shut, and his thin shoulders under the nightshirt began to shake.
"Heard it at noon," he said, in his cracked loud voice, to nobody, with his eyes shut. "Whole Quarter heard. A scribe, they said. In the lane. Head stove in. Ministry boy." He opened his eyes. They were wet. "I knew. I knew before they said the name. I saw his face, the night he came. I saw the trouble on him. And I said I'll not ask. I said better I don't know." He hit his own knee with his fist, once, hard. "Better I don't know. Saints."
Nobody said anything for a while.
Then Lyra knelt down too, on the old man's other side, in her mended silk, on the inky floor. She did not touch him. But she bent her head so that he could see her face.
"You sent me the message," she said. "Why?"
Old Joss wiped his eyes with the back of his speckled hand.
"Saw the book," he said. "That night. When he put it in the drawer. Saw the rose on it. The rose in the thorns. I know that rose. Printed it, forty years back, on the invitations to your grandfather's wedding. Gold leaf on cream card. Five hundred of them." He sniffed. "It's your family's book. Not his. Not mine. Not the Ministry's." He looked at Corin, hard. "And whoever did that to the boy in the lane would come here next, sooner or later. For it. So I thought, the rose girl's still alive. Read it in the broadsheets. Living over a stable in the High Quarter, they said, poor thing, and selling the silver." He shrugged his thin shoulders. "Thought she'd want her father's book back. Before the others came."
"What others?" said Corin.
The old printer looked at him. And for the first time, Corin saw fear in the small black eyes. Real fear. Old and deep.
"There's been men," said Old Joss. "Today. In the street. Outside. Standing about. Big men. Watching my door. Pretending not to." He licked his lips. "Didn't like the look of them. Didn't like the marks on their hands."
"What marks?"
Old Joss lifted his own speckled hand, and with one finger, drew a small shape in the air. A circle. With a line through it.
"A coin," he said. "Cut in half. Inked on the back of the hand, here." He tapped the skin between his own thumb and forefinger. "I know that mark too. Everyone in the Quarter knows that mark."
"The Black Bank," said Lyra, very softly.
And from the front of the printing house, from the great double doors on Needle Street that were barred every night with a beam of oak as thick as a man's leg, came a sound.
Boom.
The whole building shook. The ghost-paper overhead swayed on its lines, all at once, rustling, like a forest in a sudden wind.
Boom.
Old Joss could not hear it. But he felt it, through the floor, through his blankets, through his old bones. Corin saw him feel it. Saw his face go gray.
Boom.
And the oak beam across the great front doors began, with a long, slow, groaning creak, to crack.
Her Father's Handwriting
For one long moment, none of them moved.
The great doors at the front of the printing house were far away, at the other end of the long dark room, beyond the rows of sleeping presses. Corin could not see them. He could only hear them. The deep, slow, heavy boom of something striking the wood from outside, again and again, steady as a heartbeat. And under it, after each blow, the long groan of the oak beam that barred them, bending.
A ram, said Malakor, quite calmly. A log, or a beam, or a cart-shaft. Four men on it, at least. They're not trying to be quiet anymore.
Boom.
"Is there another way out?" said Corin.
Old Joss was watching his lips. He nodded quickly and pointed with his speckled hand. Not toward the small back door Corin had come in by. The other way. Deeper into the dark. Toward the far end of the type cabinet, where the wall seemed to end in shadow.
"Coal hatch," he said, too loud. "Behind the cabinet. Down into the cellar. Cellar goes under the yard to the rope-walk next door. Old smugglers' way. Nobody's used it in years."
"And the back door?"
The old man shook his head. "They'll have it. If they've any sense. Two men, outside, waiting for whatever runs out." His little eyes went to Corin. "You came in that way."
"It was open," said Corin. He looked at Lyra. "You left it open."
"I didn't." Her voice was sharp. "I came in through the roof. The skylight over the drying room. I never touched the back door."
They looked at each other.
Somebody opened it, said Malakor softly. Before either of you came. Somebody wanted it open. Somebody wanted to see who would walk through it.
Boom.
The crack in the oak beam was louder this time. A sharp, splintering crack, like a bone breaking.
The Book
Lyra was still kneeling on the inky floor beside the old printer's blankets, holding the red book against her chest.
Corin saw her look down at it. He saw her hesitate. He saw her think, quickly, the way he had seen Pell think on the boiler-room step with the note in his hands: Not now. There's no time. Run.
And then he saw her decide, the way Pell had decided, that she could not.
She opened it.
She did not open it at the beginning. Her fingers went, as if they knew the way, straight to the back. To the last page. The candle on the floor beside Old Joss threw a small, warm, wavering yellow light up across the soft red leather and the thin pages, and in that light, Corin saw the words. He had seen them before. Through another man's eyes, under a cold blue lamp in an empty Copying Hall, a week ago.
Witness. K.
For my children, if I forget.
A.T.
Lyra Thorne went perfectly still.
She did not make a sound. She did not cry out, or gasp, or speak. She only knelt there in her mended silk coat on the dirty floor of a printing house in the Ink Quarter, with a battering ram shaking the walls around her, and looked at the eight words written fast and hard in the bottom corner of the last page of a book of household accounts. And her face, in the small warm candlelight, was the face of someone looking at a letter from a person they had already buried.
"That's his hand," she said.
It was barely a whisper.
"His real hand. Not the one on the note. Not the copy." She touched the words with one fingertip, very lightly, as if they might smudge. "He wrote like that when he was angry. Or frightened. Or late. He always pressed too hard. He broke nibs. Mother used to tease him." Her fingertip moved along the line. For my children. "He wrote me a letter like this. Once. When I was nine. I'd gone to stay with my aunt in the country and I'd fallen off a horse and broken my arm, and he couldn't come, because of the Council. He sent a letter. Two pages. In this hand. Pressing so hard the paper tore."
She stopped.
"I don't have it anymore," she said. "I sold the box I kept it in. With everything else. To pay the doctors."
Boom.
"Lady Thorne," said Corin quietly.
She did not look up. "Don't call me that. Nobody calls me that anymore."
"Lyra."
She looked up.
"There's writing under the writing," he said. "On every page. Between the lines. Pell saw it. He couldn't read it. I don't think anyone can, without help. But it's there." He looked at the red book in her hands. "Your father didn't write a book of candles and flour and wine and hide it inside the cover of a Treasury ledger in the Ministry of Records. He wrote something else. Something he wanted to keep. For you. In case he forgot."
Her lips parted.
"And then he forgot," said Corin.
Crack.
The sound of the oak beam breaking was not like the sound of the blows. It was sharper. Higher. Final. It ran through the whole building like a shout, and the hanging paper overhead swung and rustled in a long wave from one end of the room to the other, and somewhere at the front of the printing house there was a great crash of wood, and the groan of heavy iron hinges swinging inward.
And light.
Yellow light. Moving. Swinging. Lanterns.
And voices.
The Stones
Lyra snapped the red book shut.
As she did, something slid out from between its pages. Small and black. It dropped onto the inky floor between them and lay there in the candlelight. A twist of black silk. Loosely tied. And through a gap in the silk, Corin saw them. Three small oval shapes, smooth as river pebbles, pale blue and dark inside and cold, each one sealed in black wax.
He picked them up before he had decided to. His hand simply did it, the way it had put the volatile jar in his pocket without asking him. The three cold stones in their black silk lay in his gloved palm.
They were heavier than they looked. Much heavier. As heavy as lead. And cold. Not the cold of stone or metal. The cold of the rod in his coat, when he first picked it up. The cold of the Ash. A deep, slow, patient cold that came up through his glove into the bones of his hand and seemed to listen.
Oh, said Malakor.
Just that. Just one word. But Corin had never heard the dry old voice sound like that before. Not mocking. Not bored. Not amused.
Afraid.
"Those are mine too," said Lyra. She held out her hand.
Corin looked at the stones. U.W. Pressed into the black wax with the point of a pin, on each one. He did not know what it meant. He did not know why Malakor, a ghost in a silver rod, who had laughed at drowning and called his own death inconvenient, had just gone quiet with fear.
He closed his hand around them.
"Later," he said.
Her gray eyes flashed.
But there was no time. The voices were in the room now. Rough. Loud. Not bothering to be quiet. Boots on the stone floor, a lot of them, spreading out among the presses. The yellow light of the lanterns swinging this way and that, throwing long black shadows of the great iron machines across the walls and the ceiling, so that they seemed to lean and lurch and stagger like drunk giants.

And over them all, a voice Corin knew.
He had heard it once before. In a black lane. Very close to his ear. Rough and bored, smelling of beer and onions.
"Spread out," it said. "And find me the little clerk's book."
Lyra's sword came out of its sheath with a sound like a breath drawn in.
Old Joss pushed himself up off his blankets, his composing stick in one hand and his candle in the other, his thin legs shaking under his nightshirt. He looked at Corin. At Lyra. Then he blew out the candle.
And in the sudden dark, he said, quite quietly for once, "Coal hatch. Behind the cabinet. Go."
Nobody went.
