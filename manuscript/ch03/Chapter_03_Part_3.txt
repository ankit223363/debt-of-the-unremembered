Chapter 3: The Thief in Silk, Part 3 of 5: The Burn
Lanterns
They did not go because there was no time to go.
Old Joss had already turned toward the end of the type cabinet, his composing stick in his fist, shuffling fast on his bare old feet. Lyra was half up off her knees, the red book shoved into the front of her silk coat and her sword low in her hand. Corin had the three cold stones in his left fist and his right hand on the jar in his pocket. All three of them were moving toward the shadow at the end of the cabinet, toward the coal hatch and the cellar and the old smugglers' way.
And a lantern came round the end of the last press.
It was held high. A big tin lantern with a glass front, its wick turned up so that it blazed. The yellow light swung across the clear space of floor in front of the cabinet like a broom sweeping, across the stool, across Old Joss's heap of blankets, across the three of them, and stopped.
The man holding it stopped too.
He was young. Younger than Corin. Big in the shoulders, with a thick neck and a shaved head and a broken nose, in a sheepskin jerkin and leather breeches and heavy hobnailed boots. He had a short iron bar in his other hand, as long as his forearm, wrapped at one end with rags for a grip. And on the back of that hand, in the skin between the thumb and the first finger, inked in black, there was a small round shape with a line through it.
A coin, cut in half.
For a moment, he only stared at them, with his mouth a little open. As if he had walked round a corner expecting an empty room and found three ghosts.
Then he grinned.
"Tull!" he bellowed over his shoulder, without taking his eyes off them. "Back here! By the letters! Got 'em! Got all three!"
The Black Bank
They came quickly. Corin counted them as they came, the way his hands had counted the jars that morning, without being asked.
Two more round the end of the same press, behind the first, with lanterns and clubs. Then two from the other side, squeezing between the presses, one with a long knife and one with a length of chain wrapped round his fist. Then one more, very fast and light on his feet, who came over the top of a press instead of round it, swinging up onto the great iron plate and crouching there like a cat, looking down at them, with a pair of short curved knives in his hands.
Six.
They spread out in a half circle across the clear space of floor, between the three of them and the rest of the printing house. Their lanterns made a ring of swinging yellow light. Their shadows jumped and lurched across the type cabinet behind Corin, huge and black. They smelled of wet wool and sweat and cheap gin and lamp oil. And every one of them, on the back of the hand that held a weapon, had the same small black mark.
And then the seventh came.
He did not hurry. He walked round the end of the last press slowly, the way a man walks into his own kitchen. He did not carry a lantern. He did not need to. The others' lanterns lit him from every side.
He was the biggest man Corin had ever seen.
Not tall, exactly. Wide. As wide as a door. His shoulders were so broad and so heavy that his head seemed small on top of them, a round, bald, red head with little ears and little eyes and a small, wet, pink mouth. His arms were as thick as a normal man's legs. His hands, hanging loose at his sides, were the size of shovels, with fingers like iron bars.
Hands like sacks of flour.
Corin knew those hands. He had felt one of them close on the back of his neck, in a black lane, and lift him off the ground like a kitten.
The big man stopped at the center of the half circle. He looked at Old Joss, trembling in his nightshirt with the composing stick raised. He looked at Lyra. Then at Corin. His little eyes went over Corin's Ministry coat, the brass buttons, the high collar, the dripping hat. They were small, pale, wet eyes, like a pig's. Not stupid. Not stupid at all.
"Well, now," said Tull.
Beer and onions.
Terms
"Ministry man," said Tull. He sounded pleased. "Didn't expect a Ministry man. And the old deaf one. And—" His small eyes moved to Lyra and stayed there. His wet pink mouth stretched slowly into a smile. "Well. Well, well. Look at that. Silk."
Lyra said nothing. Her sword was held low, the point angled at the floor between them. She did not seem to be breathing.
"I know you," said Tull. "Don't I? Seen your face. On a broadsheet. Two years back. Thorne Heiress Sells Family Silver. That's it." He laughed. It was a deep, wet, rumbling laugh, like gravel in a bucket. "The Thorne girl. The mad lord's daughter. Living over a horse. Out at three in the morning. Climbing in through windows." He shook his round bald head slowly. "Your father'd be ashamed. If he knew who you were."
Lyra's knuckles went white on the grip of her sword.
Corin felt the cold stones in his left fist. He felt the jar in his right pocket, buzzing faintly against his palm through the glass, angry, eager. He did not take it out. Not yet.
Count, said Malakor softly. Count them again. Where are they standing? Where are the lanterns? Where is the floor clear?
Corin counted.
"Here's how it goes," said Tull. He spread his huge hands, palms up. Reasonable. Friendly. "You've got something that belongs to my lady. Little red book. Little stones. The clerk's leavings. Give them here, and we all go home. Nobody hurt. Old deaf one goes back to bed. Ministry man goes back to his Ministry. Thorne girl goes back to her horse." He smiled his small wet smile. "My lady might even be generous. She likes the Thornes. Always has. She might send round a little something. For the silver."
"Your lady," said Corin.
Tull's little eyes moved to him.
"Tall," said Corin. "White mask. Like the inside of a shell. Fur collar. Silver-gray. Necklace of little glass vials, all glowing. Smells of lilies." He paused. "Carries a silver cup."
The half circle of men went very quiet. The cat-man crouched on top of the press shifted his weight. One of the lanterns swung.
Tull did not stop smiling. But the smile had changed. It had gone stiff at the edges, like gravy going cold.
"Now how," he said slowly, "would a Ministry man know a thing like that?"
"Because I was in the lane," said Corin. "Yesterday. Before dawn. I was lying on the rubbish heap when you picked me up by the back of the neck. I felt your hand. I smelled your breath." He looked at the huge hands. "You hit me with a club, afterward. Where it would show. Because she told you to. But I was already gone by then."
One of the men in the half circle made a small sound. The one with the chain. He took a step back.
Tull's little eyes had gone very narrow.
"Harvester," he said.
He said it the way the crowd on Needle Street had said it. Like a curse. Like a disease.
"You're one of the breathers. You went in him. You wore him." He spat on the inky floor. "Saints. My lady said there'd been a harvester. Said she saw it in the broadsheet this morning. Ministry Harvester Attends Ink Quarter Death. Said, Tull, that harvester will have seen my face, if he's any good." The small wet smile came back, slowly, but it was not friendly anymore. It was not anything. "Said, Tull, if you meet him, make sure he's no good."
"Then she should have come herself," said Lyra.
It was the first thing she had said. Her voice was low and cold and perfectly steady.
Tull looked at her.
"She's a lady," he said. "Ladies don't come to printing houses in the middle of the night." A pause. "Except you, it seems."
He lifted one huge hand. Not fast. Almost lazy. The way a man lifts his hand to call a waiter.
"Book and stones," he said. "Now. Or we take them off you. And then we take the rest."
Lyra did not move. Old Joss did not move. Corin did not move.
The rain pattered on the roof, far above. Somewhere among the presses, a drop of ink fell from something and hit the stone floor with a tiny sound. Tick.
Tull sighed. It was a long, deep, rumbling, regretful sigh.
"Have it your way," he said.
And dropped his hand.
The Burn
The young one with the iron bar came first. He was closest, and he was eager, and he had been the one to find them, and he wanted it to be him.
He came at Lyra.
Corin saw him come. He saw the iron bar go up, high, over the shaved head, the rag-wrapped grip clenched in a big fist, and come down, fast, in a great swinging overhand blow meant to break a skull.
It did not land.
Lyra did not seem to move. She did not step back, or duck, or raise her sword to block. She simply was not where the bar came down. She was a step to the left of it, turned sideways, as if she had been standing there all along and the young man had just been too stupid to see. The iron bar hit the stone floor where her head had been with a great ringing clang and a burst of sparks, and the shock of it went up the young man's arm and he yelled.
And as he yelled, the flat of her sword came round and hit him across the side of the head.
Not the edge. The flat. A sharp, smacking crack, like a schoolmaster's ruler on a desk. The young man's eyes rolled up. His knees went. He folded down onto the floor in a heap, on top of his own iron bar, and lay still.
It had taken less than a breath.
The half circle of men stopped moving.
Lyra stood over the fallen man with her sword held low and loose at her side, the point resting just above the floor. She was not breathing hard. Her face was perfectly calm. Only her eyes moved, quick and light, going from one man to the next, to the next, to the cat-man crouched on top of the press, and back.
"Next," she said.
Five
They came all together, then. They were not stupid. They had seen what she could do to one. So they did not come one at a time.
The man with the long knife came from her right. The man with the chain from her left. One of the lantern men came straight at her, swinging his club, and the other came round wide, behind the press, to get at her back.
And the cat-man, on top of the press, leaped.
Corin saw it all. It seemed to happen very slowly, the way things happen in dreams, or in Ash. He saw Lyra turn. He saw her sword come up and meet the long knife, steel on steel, with a high bright shing, and turn it aside. He saw her twist, and duck under the swinging chain, so close that it whipped through the short black hair on top of her head. He saw the club come down and her shoulder roll away from it, and the club hit the type cabinet behind her, and a hundred drawers rattle, and somewhere a drawer burst open and a spray of little metal letters went scattering across the floor like hail.
She was fast. She was so fast. Faster than anyone he had ever seen, though he could not remember anyone he had ever seen. She moved like water moves, like a flame moves, never where you expected, always where you did not. Her sword was a gray line in the lantern light, here, there, gone.
But there were five of them. And she was one.
The chain came round again, low this time, at her legs. She jumped it. And as she came down, the cat-man came down too, out of the air, on top of her, with his two short curved knives flashing.
She got her sword up. She caught one knife on the guard. The other one went past her face so close that it cut a lock of her hair, and the black hair fell, slowly, glinting, in the yellow light.
She hit the floor on her back. The cat-man landed on top of her. The man with the long knife was coming. The chain was coming. The club was coming.
And Corin, three steps away, with his hand on the jar in his pocket, saw her face.
It was not afraid. That was the strange thing. It was not afraid of the knives, or the men, or the floor. It was afraid of something else. Something inside her. Something she was about to do.
He saw her close her eyes.
And he saw her reach.
The Needle
Corin had felt Pell reach, in the lane. He knew what it looked like, from inside. A hand going into a drawer, past everything else, for the one thing that would be enough.
He did not know what Lyra reached for. He would not know until much later, when she told him, sitting on a cold step by a river at dawn with her hands in her lap.
But this is what it was.
A room. A small, warm room, high in a tall house, with a window looking out over a garden full of roses. Late afternoon light, gold and slanting, lying across a wooden floor. A woman in a blue dress, sitting in a chair by the window, with a wooden hoop in her lap and a needle in her hand. Dark hair, piled up and pinned. Long, pale, clever fingers. And beside the chair, on a little stool, a small girl of six or seven, with long black hair down her back, and a hoop of her own, and a needle, and her tongue sticking out of the corner of her mouth.
In, said the woman, softly. And through. And pull. Gently. Not too tight, or it puckers. There. Her pale clever fingers closed over the small girl's fingers on the needle. In. Through. Pull. You see? It's only patience, my love. Every great thing is only patience. Small stitches. One after another. Until it's done.
And on the woman's hoop, in silk thread the color of roses, half finished, there were words. Small and neat and curling. The girl could not read them yet. The woman read them out to her.
Fold it small, she said. That's what it says. It's what my mother used to say, and her mother before her. When something is too big to carry, you fold it small. Very small. And put it somewhere safe. And carry it anyway.
The girl did not understand. She was six.
She was twenty-four now, lying on her back on a dirty floor in the Ink Quarter with a man's knee in her chest and a curved knife coming down at her throat.
She still did not understand. She had never understood. But she had embroidered those words herself, years later, on the corner of every handkerchief she ever owned, in thread the color of roses, without ever knowing why.
She took the room. The gold light. The woman in blue. The hoop. The needle. In. Through. Pull. Every stitch she had ever set, every flower and leaf and letter, every hour of patient, careful, beautiful work, every memory of her mother's fingers closing over hers.
And she burned it.
Silk
Corin felt it happen. Even from three steps away. Even through the cold stones in his fist and the buzzing jar in his pocket and the dead man's week crowding his head.
It was like a breath going out of the world.
The air around Lyra Thorne went still. Completely still. The lantern flames stopped flickering. The swinging chain stopped in the middle of its swing, as if it had hit something soft and invisible. The cat-man's curved knife, an inch from her throat, stopped.
And her sword moved.
It did not move like a sword. It moved like a needle. Small. Quick. Precise. In. Through. Pull. So fast that Corin did not see it go, only saw where it had been, a thin bright silver line drawn on the air, hanging there for a moment like a thread of light.
The cat-man's knife fell in two pieces. The blade, and the handle, cut clean apart. He stared at the stump in his fist.
The silver line went on. Through the air. In. Through. It touched the swinging chain, and the chain came apart, every link of it, a great iron rattling rain falling to the floor. It touched the long knife, and the long knife fell. It touched the club, and the club split down its length like a log under an axe. It touched the lantern the man was holding over his head, and the lantern was suddenly two halves of a lantern, falling, and the flame inside it went out in midair.
And it went up.
Pull.
The silver thread of light went up, through the dark above the presses, through the forest of hanging paper. And every line it touched, every one of the hundreds of cords strung across the ceiling from wall to wall, parted at once with a sound like a harp string snapping.
The paper fell.
All of it. Thousands upon thousands of sheets, at once, from one end of the Inkwell to the other. A great, soft, white, rustling avalanche. It came down out of the dark like snow, like a flock of white birds all landing at once, sheet after sheet after sheet, drifting, turning, sliding, on the presses, on the floor, on the men, on everything. It filled the air. It put out two more lanterns. It buried the young man lying unconscious on his iron bar. It was so thick that Corin could not see across the clear space of floor anymore, only white, falling, endless white, and the yellow glow of the last lanterns somewhere behind it, and shapes, shouting, stumbling, blind.
And in the middle of it, rising up off the floor with her sword in her hand and the cat-man sprawled stunned at her feet, Lyra Thorne.
Her face was white. Whiter than the paper. Her lips were pressed together so hard they had disappeared. She was staring at her own left hand.
Corin saw her try to move the fingers. One at a time. Thumb. First finger. Second. As if she were checking that they were all still there.
They were all still there. They moved. They worked.
But she looked at them the way you look at a house you used to live in, and now strangers live there.
"Lyra," said Corin.
She did not look at him. She closed her left hand into a fist. Opened it. Closed it.
"I've lost embroidery," she said. Very quietly. Very calmly. As if she were telling him the time. "All of it. I can't remember how to thread a needle." She swallowed. "I can't remember my mother's hands."
And through the white falling storm of paper, from somewhere off to the left, there came a deep, wet, rumbling laugh, like gravel in a bucket.
"Pretty," said Tull. "Very pretty, Thorne girl. My lady'll pay a fortune for that, when we take it out of you."
And something huge came through the paper toward them.
The Old Printer
Not all of them had gone for Lyra.
In the white, falling, rustling storm of paper, Corin heard boots behind him. Close. Coming fast from the left, from the dark between the cold stove and the end of the type cabinet. He turned his head.
One of the lantern men. The second one, the one who had gone round wide behind the press to get at Lyra's back. His lantern had gone out in the paper. He had dropped it. He had a club in one hand, a short thick stick of black wood with a knob on the end, and he was not going for Lyra anymore.
He was going for Old Joss.
The old printer was standing in front of his heap of blankets in his nightshirt and his leather apron, with his composing stick raised in both shaking hands, like a boy with a wooden sword. He could not hear the man coming. He could not hear anything. He was staring at the place where Lyra had been, at the white chaos of falling paper, with his mouth open.
Corin opened his mouth to shout. Then he remembered that shouting was no use.
He moved instead. Two steps. He was too slow. He was tired, and his head was full, and his legs did not want to move as fast as he told them to. The man with the club was already there, already swinging, the black knob coming round at the side of the old man's bald speckled head—
And Old Joss ducked.
Not because he heard. Because he felt. Corin saw it happen. The old man's bare feet, on the stone floor, felt the thud of the heavy boots coming up behind him, the way they had felt the ram on the front doors. Sixty years beside the great iron presses had taught Old Joss to feel the world through the floor. And he dropped, quicker than Corin would have believed a man that old could move, into a crouch, and the club went over his head and hit the side of the cold stove with a great clanging bong that shook soot down the chimney.
And as he came up, the old man swung the composing stick.
It was a short, flat, heavy bar of steel, as long as a man's forearm, with a sharp square edge. It hit the lantern man across the shin, just below the knee, with a crack like a branch breaking. The man screamed. He went down on one knee in the paper.
Old Joss did not stop. He shuffled backward, fast, toward the nearest press. Not to hide behind it. To get to it. His speckled hand found a long iron lever on the side of the machine without looking for it, the way Corin's hand found the Ledger in his coat, and pulled.
The press woke up.
It was the loudest sound Corin had heard all night. Louder than the ram on the doors. A great iron groan, and a clank, and a deep, heavy, thudding clack-THUMP, as the huge flat plate at the top of the machine came down, all at once, on the bed beneath it, the way it had come down ten thousand times a night for sixty years to print a page.
The lantern man had reached out to catch himself as he fell. His hand had landed on the edge of the press bed.
He snatched it back just in time. The great plate came down an inch from his fingers. But the sleeve of his coat was caught under it. Pinned to the iron bed, flat, as firmly as if it had been nailed there. He pulled at it, howling, kneeling in the paper with his broken shin and his arm stretched out, held fast by a printing press.
Old Joss looked down at him. He was breathing hard. His bald speckled head shone with sweat in the light of the last lanterns.
"Sixty years," he said, too loud, to the man who could not get away. "Sixty years I've run these. You think I don't know where to put a man's arm?"
And he hit him once more with the composing stick, quite gently, on the top of the head, and the man stopped howling and lay down.
The old printer looked up and met Corin's eyes across the white falling paper.
He did not smile. But something in his small black eyes was fierce, and bright, and very much alive.
"Go on," he said. "Your girl needs you."
Corin turned.
