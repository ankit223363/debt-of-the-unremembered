Chapter 60: The Road to the Glass, Part 1 of 5: Thursday
Thursday
He read the first page before he opened his eyes.
He could do that now. He had not been able to, in the autumn. In the autumn he had woken every morning with nothing, the way you wake in a strange inn, and lain there in the dark until his hand found the Ledger under the pillow, and opened it, and his eyes found the square capitals at the top. READ THIS FIRST. And only then had he known who he was.
Now he knew the page by heart. Not the things on it. The page. The way the lines lay. Where the words went. He could see it on the inside of his eyelids, in the dark, before he was properly awake, the way you see the shape of a window after you have looked at it too long.
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin Vane. You are twenty-eight years old. You were born in a place called Oakhaven. You do not remember it. You are a harvester.
And the names. And the rules. And near the bottom, in his own capitals, and under them, in a small quick hand that leaned forward as if it were in a hurry:
LYRA THORNE. PARTNER. RULE ONE: NO LIES BETWEEN US. NOT EVEN THE KIND ONES.
He opened his eyes.
The crypt of Saint Bride's. The round stone arches, like the inside of a loaf, gray now with the very first of the morning coming down the twenty-three steps from the door at the top. The brazier, low and red. Mrs. Doyle's broom against a pillar. The smell of coal smoke and bread.
And on the floor by the fire, in blue chalk, big, the way Gideon had said he would do it every morning, two words.
LYRA.
READ.
And an arrow. Pointing at him.
He lay on his blankets and looked at the words for a long time.
Then he turned his head.
She was not on her blankets by the far pillar. The blankets were there, folded square, with Silas's old black coat on top of them. But she was not.
He sat up. Too fast. The crypt swung.
"She's gone up," said Oren.
He was sitting against the far wall where he always sat, with his sword across his knees. Awake. Corin did not think he had slept.
"At the first bell," said Oren. "Before it was light. To the Hill. To him." He did not look at Corin. "She wouldn't let me go with her. She said the Watch would let her through, and they'd not let me, and she didn't want to waste the morning arguing at a corner." A pause. "She's right. They'd not let me."
Corin did not say anything.
"She said to tell you," said Oren. He stopped. His gray-bearded face moved in a way Corin had not seen it move before. "She said to tell you she'd read it. What you wrote. On the steps. Last night. She read it before she went up. By the lamp. She said to tell you she read it twice."
Corin looked at him.
"That's all," said Oren. "That's what she said."
Corin opened the Ledger.
To the last page with writing on it. SHE IS COMING AFTER. And the small red spot beside the R, dried brown now. And under it, his own crooked capitals from last night. I THINK IT MEANS YES. I DO NOT KNOW HER. BUT I KNOW THAT.
And under that, nothing. The page was full. All the way to the bottom. There was no room for anything else.
He turned it over.
There was no next page.
He sat and looked at the inside of the back cover. Old brown board, with the paper of the cover folded over it at the edges and pasted down. HARBOUR TIDE TABLES, it said on the outside. He had bought it secondhand on Quill Row. He did not remember buying it. It was in the book. On the inside of the front cover, in his own capitals: LEDGER VII. BEGUN AT THE FIRST FROST. QUILL ROW. TWO PENCE.
And now it was full.
Seven. He had filled seven of them. In six years. Since the night he took the rod out of the drawer at the Ministry, and the voice behind his left ear said its first word. Seven books. The first six were somewhere. In a box under the floor of his room on Tallow Street, perhaps, if Kross's men had not found them. Or burned. Or on a shelf at the Ministry, with a number on them. He did not know. The Ledger did not say. Perhaps he had not wanted it to.
And this one was full. On the morning he was leaving the city. With SHE IS COMING AFTER on the last line.
Good, said Malakor. That's a good last line. Better than most.
Corin shut the book.
Boy.
I'm here.
Before you go, said Malakor. Tonight. Before the dyke road. There's one thing. A long pause. The dry rustle of an old man shifting in a chair. I've never asked you for anything. Not in six years. Not really. I've told you what to do, and shouted, and called you a fool. But I've never asked.
No.
I'm asking now.
Corin waited.
The key, said Malakor. Linus's key. The one with the oak leaf on it. In the Ledger, behind the back cover, where you put it on Sunday.
Corin opened the back cover again. And there it was. Pushed in under the pasted-down paper at the edge, where the board had come unstuck. A small key. Old. Iron, gone brown. With a bow shaped like an oak leaf, the veins of the leaf cut very fine into the iron. He had not known it was there. It was in the Ledger, a line from Sunday, but he had not known where he had put it.
It opens a door under the Ministry, said Malakor. On a level the Ministry will tell you does not exist. Linus stood in front of it four times and never opened it. He said so. He wrote it on his last page. He was afraid.
Of what?
Of me.
Corin sat very still on his blankets.
It was my room, said Malakor. Before. Long before. Before the Ministry built its house on top of it. Before there was a Harvesting Section, or a Senior Investigator, or a drawer for a rod. It was my room. I worked there. I wrote there. I cut rods there, and taught Corvell, and Corvell taught Linus. A pause. And when I went away, for the last time, to the place where I didn't come back from, I locked it. And gave the key to Corvell. And Corvell gave it to Linus. And Linus gave it to you.
And you want me to open it.
I want you to go in, said Malakor. Before you leave this city. Because you won't come back to it for a long time. Perhaps never. And there's a thing in there you'll need. Where you're going.
What?
Malakor did not answer at once.
Not the thing itself, he said at last. The thing itself isn't there. I took it with me. When I went. But there's a page in there that says where I took it. In my own hand. In the old letters. The dry voice was very quiet. I can't tell you where. I've tried. I've tried for six years, boy, on and off, when you were asleep. It won't come out. It's folded. They folded it in me, before I died. Or I folded it myself. I don't know any more. I only know that when I try to say it, there's a crease where it ought to be.
Like K's face.
Like K's face, said Malakor. Yes. But a page isn't a face. A page you can read. If you're standing in front of it.
Corin looked at the oak-leaf key in his gray-gloved palm.
The Ministry, he said. Today. With Kross's list going up at noon.
Before noon, said Malakor. At the second bell. Before the clerks are all in. There's a boy there. A clerk. In the old Harvesting rooms. You know him. He's in the book.
Corin turned back the pages of the Ledger. A long way back. Into the autumn. And there it was. In his own capitals, from before he ran.
TOBY. JUNIOR CLERK. HARVESTING SECTION. SITS AT THE DESK BY THE OLD CUPBOARDS. NINETEEN. FRECKLES. ALWAYS EATING. HONEST. I TRUST HIM. DO NOT GET HIM INTO TROUBLE.
And under it, in the small quick hand, added later: You got him into trouble.
Corin looked at the line for a long time.
Then he said, out loud, to the crypt, "Juno."
Juno sat up on her blankets with her whistle in her fist.
"What?"
"How fast can you get to the Ink Quarter and back?"
Juno looked at him. At the key in his hand. And her small fierce face, which had been gray and closed and miserable since the dome, came suddenly open, the way a door opens.
"Fast," she said. "Faster than anybody."
"There's a boy called Toby," said Corin. "A clerk at the Ministry. He lodges over a pie shop on Bell Alley, behind Sallow's warehouse. I don't know which floor. It's in the book." He held out the Ledger, open at the page. "Can you read that?"
Juno looked at it. Her lips moved.
"T," she said. "O. B. Y." She frowned. "I know the letters. I don't know the word. It's not Tallow Street. It's not Rope."
"It says Toby. Freckles. Always eating. Over the pie shop."
"I'll find him." Juno was already up, already pulling the sacking tighter round her frostbitten feet. "I'll find a boy with freckles who's always eating over a pie shop. That's easy. What do I tell him?"
Corin thought.
"Tell him," he said slowly, "that the man who used to sit at the desk by the window, with the gray hands, who always left him the crust, wants to see the old cupboards. At the second bell. Before the others come in. By the back way, through the Records yard." He paused. "And tell him he doesn't have to. Tell him he can say no."
Juno looked at him.
"He won't say no," she said.
"Tell him anyway."
She nodded. And went up the twenty-three steps two at a time on the sides of her feet, and out of the door, and was gone into the gray morning.
Oren was looking at him from the far wall.
"The Ministry," said Oren. "Today."
"Yes."
"With your face on the list at noon."
"Before noon."
Oren looked at him a moment longer. Then he got up, slowly, the way an old soldier gets up after a night on a stone floor, in stages, and buckled his sword belt, and settled the sling of black shawl on his bad arm.
"Then I'm coming," he said. "She said not by yourself. To you. Yesterday. On the Hill." He looked at Corin. "You wrote it down. I saw you."
Corin looked down at the Ledger in his lap. Full. To the last line.
SHE IS COMING AFTER.
"All right," he said.
The Ministry of Records
The Ministry of Records stood at the top of the Old Town, behind Ferrule Street, where the hill went up steep from the river toward the Crescent. It had always stood there. It was older than the Bank, older than the Bourse, older than the Council. A great square block of gray stone with no ornament on it at all, five stories high, with rows of tall narrow windows like the slots in a dovecote, and a flat roof, and a door in the middle of the front that was twice the height of a man and painted black.
Corin had gone in at that door every working morning for ten years.
He did not remember it.
He stood across the street from it, at the second bell, in the gray light, in Fenton's heavy coat and Silas's gray wool gloves and a wool cap pulled down over his ears, leaning on the ash crutch. And looked at it. And it was a building. A big gray building with a black door. He knew it was the Ministry. The Ledger said so. THE MINISTRY OF RECORDS. TOP OF THE OLD TOWN. BLACK DOOR. HARVESTING SECTION, THIRD FLOOR, BACK. He knew he had worked there. He knew he had had a desk by a window, and a junior clerk called Toby who was always eating, and a drawer with a rod in it that he had opened one night six years ago and taken out.
He did not remember any of it.
But his feet did.
That was the strange thing. He did not decide to go round the side of the building. He did not think about it. His feet took him. Off the pavement, across the cobbles, round the corner to the left, down a narrow lane between the high gray side wall of the Ministry and the back of a stationer's, with Oren beside him. And at the end of the lane, a gate. An iron gate, in a high wall, standing a little open. And beyond the gate, a yard.
The Records yard. Cobbled. Square. Full of handcarts, and stacked crates, and bundles of paper tied with string. With a loading door at the back of the building, low and wide, and a ramp going up to it, and a lamp over it. Ash lamp. Brass. Burning blue.
Corin stopped at the gate.
He looked at the lamp.
Don't, said Malakor. Don't go near it. It'll go white for you from ten paces. It went white for you every morning for six years, and you walked past it every morning for six years with your hands in your pockets, because they knew what you were and they didn't care. You were theirs. A pause. You're not theirs now.
There was somebody standing under the lamp.
A young man. Thin, and not tall, in a clerk's black coat that was too big in the shoulders, with ink on his cuffs. A round freckled face. Red hair, sticking up at the back. And in one hand, a half-eaten bun.
He saw Corin at the gate, and stopped chewing.
And stared.
And then he put the bun in his coat pocket, very carefully, as if it were important not to lose it, and came down the ramp and across the cobbled yard to the gate, fast, with his black coat flapping. And stopped in front of Corin. And looked at him. At the wool cap, and the heavy coat, and the crutch. And the gray wool gloves.
"Mister Vane," said Toby.
His voice cracked in the middle, the way a boy's voice cracks.
"Toby," said Corin.
He knew him. Not from the book. From before. The way he knew Oren and Juno and Wren. A face that had been in front of him every day for a long time, across a desk, in a gray room, with a bun in its hand. He did not remember the room. He did not remember the desk. But he knew the face.
"They said you were dead," said Toby. "In the autumn. After you ran. And then they said you weren't. And then they said you'd taken a lord off a barge. And then they said you'd held up the roof of the Bourse." He was breathing fast. "Half the Ministry's been talking about nothing else since Tuesday. The Inquisitor won't say. He just goes in and out with his hands behind his back." He looked at Corin's gloves. "Was it you? The roof?"
"Yes," said Corin.
Toby looked at him for a long moment.
Then he looked at the gloves again. And something went across his round freckled face, very fast, the way a cloud goes over a field. And he swallowed.
"How far up?" he said.
Corin did not answer.
Toby did not ask again.
"The girl came," he said instead. "Your girl. The small one, with the whistle, and the feet. At the first bell. She banged on the pie shop door till Mrs. Gammon came down in her nightcap with a rolling pin. And then she told me." He almost smiled. "She said you said I could say no."
"You can."
"I know," said Toby. "I'm not going to." He looked over his shoulder, at the ramp and the loading door and the blue lamp. "Come on. Quick. Old Abernethy comes in at the third bell and he always comes through the yard, because his knees can't do the front steps. And the Long Office is half in by half past."
"The lamp," said Corin.
Toby looked at the lamp.
Then he looked at Corin.
And then he did a thing that Corin would write in the Ledger that night, in crooked square capitals, under THOSE I OWE, and underline twice. He took off his black clerk's coat. The one that was too big in the shoulders. And walked up the ramp in his shirtsleeves, in the cold, to the brass lamp. And reached up. And hung the coat over it. All of it. So that the coat covered the lamp from top to bottom, and the blue light went out under it like a candle under a snuffer.
"Mister Pocock does that," said Toby, coming back down the ramp. "When he wants to smoke his pipe in the yard and not have the lamp tell on him. Everybody knows. Nobody says." He shivered. "It'll keep for five minutes. Then the coat starts to smell of burning."
They went in.
Up the ramp. Through the loading door. Into a long low passage with a stone floor and whitewashed walls and a smell of paper and dust and ink. And then a stair. Narrow, and stone, and steep, going up into the building in a square turn. Toby went first, fast, in his shirtsleeves. Then Corin, on the crutch, a step at a time. Then Oren, with his good hand on his sword.
First floor. Second. Corin heard voices, behind doors. Clerks coming in. A man laughing. A door banging. The scrape of a chair. Toby did not stop. He went on up.
Third floor.
And Toby pushed open a door at the top of the stair, a plain wooden door with a brass plate on it, and stood aside.
HARVESTING SECTION.
Corin went in.
It was a long room. Long and narrow, at the back of the building, with a row of tall narrow windows down one side looking out over the roofs of the Old Town toward the river. There were desks. Six of them. Old and heavy and dark, with green leather tops worn pale, and brass lamps, and inkwells. Five of them had papers on them, and pens, and coats on the backs of the chairs. One did not.
The last one. At the far end. By the last window.
It was quite bare. The green leather top had been cleaned. The inkwell was empty. The drawer in the front of it had been pulled out, all the way, and lay on the floor beside it, upside down. And the chair had been pushed in, square, the way you push in a chair at a table where nobody is going to sit again.
Corin looked at it.
He did not remember it.
But his hand, on the grip of the crutch, in its gray wool glove, closed very tight.
"They took everything," said Toby, beside him. Very low. "The day after you ran. Two men from the Inquisitor's office. Every paper. Every pen. Your cup. The drawer." He looked at the drawer, upside down on the floor. "They looked in the drawer for a long time. I don't know what for. It was empty. You'd taken whatever was in it."
The rod, thought Corin. Malakor's rod. In the long right pocket of my coat. For six years.
He did not have the coat now. He had Fenton's coat. The rod was—
He did not know where the rod was.
He went cold.
It's in the crypt, said Malakor. Under your blankets. Wrapped in a shirt. Where Oren put it on Tuesday morning when he took your coat off you to see to your hands. You've had it under your head for two nights. A dry pause. I'm not in the rod, boy. I haven't been in the rod for a long time. I'm in you. I moved in, the night you held up the dome. There was room.
Corin stood very still in the Harvesting Section of the Ministry of Records.
There was room, said Malakor again. More quietly. I'm sorry. I didn't ask. There wasn't time to ask. You'd have gone up in the gray like a dry field, if there hadn't been somebody in the empty place to hold the door.
Corin did not say anything.
He looked down the long room. Past the bare desk. Past the last window. To the end wall.
There were cupboards along it. Tall and old and dark, like the desks, from the floor to the ceiling, with brass handles. Five of them. The kind of cupboards where a Ministry keeps the files it does not want, and does not want to throw away. Their doors were shut. Their brass handles were dull with dust. Nobody had opened them for a long time.
The last one, said Malakor. On the left. Behind it.
"Toby," said Corin. "The cupboards. At the end. The last one on the left."
Toby looked at the cupboards.
And frowned.
"There are four," he said.
Corin looked at him.
"Four cupboards," said Toby. "At the end. I've sat at that desk by them for two years. I dust them on Fridays. There are four." He counted them, with his finger, in the air. "One. Two. Three. Four."
There were five.
Corin could see them. Five. Quite clearly. Tall and dark and old, from the floor to the ceiling. The fifth one, at the far left, was a little narrower than the others. A little darker. Its brass handle was not dull with dust. It was bright. As if a hand had been laid on it, from time to time, over many years. Four times, perhaps. By a small bent old man with a stick and blue fingers, who had stood in front of it and been afraid.
The clerks do not see it, Linus had written. You will see it. You have the eyes for it. I gave you those too, a long time ago, before you can remember.
Oren was looking at the end wall too. His face was quite blank.
"Four," said Oren.
"Five," said Corin.
He went down the long room on the crutch, past the bare desk, past the last window. To the fifth cupboard. The one nobody else could see.
And put his gray-gloved hand on the bright brass handle.
And pulled.
It was not a cupboard. It was a door. It swung open on silent hinges, heavy, old, and behind it there were no shelves. There was a stair. Stone. Narrow. Very old, older than the Ministry, older than anything in the building, the steps worn into deep dips in the middle by feet that had gone up and down them for a very long time. Going down. Into the dark.
And cold came up out of it. Old cold. The cold of a place that has been shut up a long time and not let anybody in.
Toby made a sound behind him.
Corin turned round.
Toby was staring. Not at the stair. At Corin. At the place where Corin's hand was, on the bright brass handle of a door that, to Toby, was not there.
"Mister Vane," he said. His voice had gone very small. "You're holding nothing. You're holding the wall."
"I know," said Corin. "It's all right." He looked at Toby. At the round freckled face, white now under the freckles, and the red hair sticking up at the back, and the shirtsleeves in the cold. "Go back down. Get your coat off the lamp before it burns. Sit at your desk. Eat your bun. If anybody asks, you came in early, and you didn't see anybody, and the coat was Pocock's idea."
Toby looked at him.
"Will you come back up?"
"Before the third bell."
"And if you don't?"
Corin did not answer that.
Toby stood there a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, quickly. And turned. And went back down the long room, past the five desks with their coats and papers, to the door. And stopped there. And looked back.
"Mister Vane," he said. "You always left me the crust. Off your bread. At noon. Every day. You said you didn't like crusts." He swallowed. "You did, though. I saw you eat them, when you thought I wasn't looking. You just left them for me because I was always hungry."
Corin did not remember.
But he believed it.
"Go on, Toby," he said.
And Toby went.
And Corin turned back to the door that was not there, and the old stone stair going down into the cold, and took the oak-leaf key out of the Ledger.
"Oren," he said. "You can't see it."
"No."
"Then hold on to my coat."
Oren looked at the wall. Blank. Then, slowly, he reached out with his good hand and took hold of the back of Fenton's heavy coat, in a fist, the way a blind man takes hold of the coat of the man who is leading him.
And they went down.
