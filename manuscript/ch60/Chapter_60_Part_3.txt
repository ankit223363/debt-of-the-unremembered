Chapter 60: The Road to the Glass, Part 3 of 5: Fourteen
Fourteen
The white room was very quiet.
That was the first thing. After the carriage, and the drumming of the wheels on the bridge, and the singing coming down off the Spire. It was so quiet that Corin could hear the snow beginning again outside the tall narrow window. Not falling. Touching. Small dry flakes, touching the glass, one at a time, and sliding down.
She was on her knees by the bed.
On the white boards, on the far side, by the window, with her back to the white stone of the Spire. In her mother's burned gray gown. With Silas's old black coat on the floor beside her where she had let it fall. Holding her father's right hand in both of hers, on the white blanket. The open one. Not the closed one. The closed one lay on the other side of him, by the wall, on the blanket. Still closed. The knuckles white.
She did not look up when Corin came in.
He stood in the doorway on the crutch, with Oren behind him on the landing, and Wren behind Oren, and the small old nurse in gray behind Wren, with her notebook shut in her hands and her pale water eyes on the floor. And Kross at the bottom of the stair, in the hall, not coming up.
He looked at the man in the bed.
He had seen him before. Twice. In the cellar on Saltmarsh Row, in the dark, a thin shape on a heap of sacks that he had carried out over his shoulder, the Ledger said. And on the barge, in the reeds, the Ledger said. He did not remember either time.
But he had been him.
For three hours. In a warm study in the rain, two autumns ago. He had been inside this man's chest, and felt it go tight at a shut door on the second floor, and loosen at the other one. He had sat in his chair, with the dip in the seat that fitted him. He had worn his rings. He had folded with his hands, and felt it burn.
He knew this man better than he knew anybody in the world.
And the man was dying.
Corin could see it from the door. The fallen face. The blue under the eyes and round the mouth. The breath going in, very shallow, with a small catch at the top of it, like a man going up a stair who has to stop on every step. And going out, slowly, all the way, as if it might not come back. And then, after a long time, coming back.
"Come in," said the girl by the bed. Without looking up. "Shut the door. Sit on the other side. By his hand."
Corin came in.
Oren shut the door behind him, from the landing. Corin heard him not go away. He heard him put his back against the door on the other side, and stand there.
Corin went round the end of the white iron bed, on the crutch, slowly. To the other side. By the wall. Where the closed hand lay on the blanket. There was a white chair there. The nurse's chair. He lowered himself into it, a little at a time, with the crutch, and leaned the crutch against the wall, and sat.
And looked across the bed at her.
She was looking at her father's face. She had not looked anywhere else since Corin came in. Her lips were moving. Very slightly. Without sound.
Counting.
His breaths. He knew that without being told. She was counting her father's breaths. In. One. Out. And the long gap. In. Two.
"How long?" he said. Very low.
"Since the first bell," said Lyra. "I've counted since the first bell." Her voice was quite steady. Quite flat. "Four thousand and something. I lost count once, about the tenth bell, when he opened his eyes. I had to start again from a round number." She did not look up. "The gaps are longer now. Since noon. They're getting longer."
Corin did not say anything.
He looked down at the closed hand on the blanket, by his knee.
It was a long hand. Thin. Pale. With the plain gold ring on the third finger, worn thin. And the knuckles white, from holding. From holding on.
He had folded with that hand. In the stone. He had felt the paper go small inside it.
He did not touch it.
"Put your hand on it," said Lyra.
He looked up.
She was looking at him now. Across the bed. Over her father's thin chest going up and down under the white blanket. With her dark eyes too big for her face, and the small worried line between her brows.
"On his hand," she said. "The closed one. Put yours on it. Please." She swallowed. "He should have somebody holding both. And you're the only one who's been him."
Corin took off the gray wool glove.
His hand under it was gray. Slate-gray, to the wrist and beyond, with the white linen on the black ends of the fingers. He looked at it. And then he put it, very gently, over the closed hand on the blanket. His palm over the white knuckles. Like a lid.
He could not feel it with his fingers.
He could feel it with his palm. A little. Cold. Bones. The ring.
And as he laid his hand there, the man in the bed opened his eyes.
Gray eyes. Pale. Looking up at the white ceiling. And then moving. Slowly. Across. To the wall side. To the white chair. To the man sitting in it, with a gray hand over his closed one.
And stopping there.
Corin did not breathe.
The gray eyes looked at him. And there was something in them. Far back. A light at the end of a long road, very small. And it was not the look you give a stranger. It was not the look you give anybody you know, either. Corin did not have a word for it.
It was the way you look at a man who has been in your house when you were not there. Who has sat in your chair. Who knows where the floor creaks.
The closed hand under Corin's palm moved.
Not opening. It stayed closed. But one finger moved, inside the fist, against the inside of Corin's palm. And tapped.
Four.
Two.
Nine.
Three.
Witness. Hold. Fold. Home.
Corin knew what to do. He did not know how he knew. It came up out of him the way the six notes had come up out of him in the carriage. He bent his own gray finger, the one he could not feel, and tapped. On the white knuckles. Once.
And then twice, quick.
Heard.
The gray eyes looked at him a moment longer.
Then they closed. Slowly.
And the breath went out. All the way. Slowly.
And the gap was very long.
Corin looked across the bed. Lyra had not moved. She was kneeling on the white boards with her father's open hand in both of hers, and her eyes on his face, and her lips still. Not counting. Waiting.
The breath came back.
Shallow. With the catch at the top.
And Lyra's lips began to move again.
They sat like that for a long time. Corin did not know how long. The light from the tall window moved across the white boards. The snow touched the glass. Outside, on the landing, he could hear Oren breathing, against the door. And under it, very faint, a small sound like a pencil moving on paper, and stopping, and moving. The nurse, who could not not hear.
The gaps got longer.
Corin began to count them too. He could not help it. Without meaning to. In. Out. And the long gap. And in. He did not know he was doing it until he found that his lips were moving, and he was counting the same numbers as the girl on the other side of the bed. In time with her. The same breath. Across the bed. Like two people singing the same song in two different rooms and finding they are in step.
And then the open hand, in Lyra's, moved.
Corin saw it. The long pale fingers. Coming up out of hers. Slowly. Like a sleeping child's hand coming open. Until her palm was lying in his. Upturned. And his finger came down on it.
One.
She went very still.
Two.
Three. Four.
Corin watched it. The long thin finger, pressing, and lifting, and pressing, on the girl's palm. Slowly. With a long gap between each one. As if each one were a stair, and he had to stop on every step.
Five. Six. Seven.
She was not counting the breaths now. She was counting the taps. Her lips moved with them. Eight. He could see her mouth make the word. Nine.
The gaps between the taps were longer than the gaps between the breaths.
Ten.
Eleven.
Corin's hand was still over the closed hand on his side. He felt it. Through his palm. The closed fist, under his, holding. Tighter. As if it knew.
Twelve.
The finger lifted off her palm.
And hung there.
A long time.
Corin did not breathe. He saw that she did not breathe either.
And came down.
Thirteen.
And lifted.
And hung there.
And the breath went out of the man in the bed. All the way. Slowly. The way the tide goes out, at the very edge of the sand, a long way off.
And the finger came down. On her palm. Very lightly.
Fourteen.
And stayed there.
And the breath did not come back.
Corin sat in the white chair with his hand over the closed one and waited for it. In. He waited. It did not come. He waited for the catch at the top. It did not come. He waited for a long time, a very long time, longer than any of the gaps, and the snow touched the glass of the tall window, and slid down, and touched it again.
It did not come.
Across the bed, the girl in the gray gown knelt on the white boards with her father's finger on her palm, on the fourteenth tap, and did not move.
She did not cry out. She did not make any sound at all.
She only said, very quietly, to the man on the bed, in a voice Corin had never heard her use, the voice of a girl of nine on a wide marble stair in her stockinged feet:
"Fourteen. That's the landing, Father. That's all of them. You can stop now."
And bent, and put her forehead down on his open hand on the white blanket.
And stayed there.
Corin did not move.
Under his palm, the closed hand did not open. It had gone very still. Quite cold. But it did not open. The fingers stayed shut over whatever was inside them, the knuckles white, the little finger first and the thumb over the top of them all. Holding. The last room. The children.
It would not open now. Corin knew that. Not for anybody. Not ever.
Whatever K was, K had gone under.
After a long time, he took his hand away from it.
And put his glove back on. And took the stub of blue chalk out of his pocket. Gideon's chalk. Warm, still. And bent down, slowly, in the white chair, over the white painted boards of the floor by the bed. And wrote. In big square crooked capitals, because his fingers could not feel the chalk. On the white floor, in blue. So that she would see it when she lifted her head. So that she could read it, if she could not write it.
AURELIAN THORNE.
HE KEPT THE CHILDREN.
FOURTEEN.
He sat back.
And outside the white door, on the landing, he heard the small dry sound of a pencil stop moving on paper. And not start again.
And then, below, at the bottom of the scrubbed pale stair, in the empty white hall, very faint, he heard something else.
A man's voice. Quiet. Even. A voice that had never once in its life needed to shout.
Saying, to nobody, very low:
"Fold it small, Aurelian. And let it go under."
And then a door, closing. The study door.
And then nothing.
When Lyra lifted her head at last, it was nearly dark. The snow had thickened outside the window, and the white stone of the Spire had gone gray, and the room was gray, and the blanket was gray. Only the blue chalk on the floor was still bright.
She looked down at it.
She read it. He watched her read it. Her lips moved. Every word.
Then she looked up at him, across her father, across the bed.
Her face was white and dry and quite still.
"Thank you," she said.
And Corin, who did not know her, who did not know her face or her name or the sound of her step, found that his own face was wet. And that he had not felt it start. And that he could not make it stop.
He was not crying for her. He knew that. He did not know her.
He was crying for the man in the bed. Whose chair he had sat in. Whose rings he had worn. Who had stood in a cold hall two autumns ago and not been able to say anything to a girl on a stair with her shoes off, because he knew why she counted, and she did not.
She knew now.
And he was gone.
Ledger VIII
Kross was in the hall when they came down.
He was standing at the foot of the scrubbed pale stair, in the gray light from the open study door, in his long plain gray coat now, buttoned to the throat, with his hat in his hand. As if he were about to go out. Or had just come in. Wren was by the front door, in her servant's cloak, very straight. The small old nurse was not anywhere. Corin did not know where she had gone.
Lyra came down first. Slowly. Counting. Oren behind her, close, with his good hand not quite on her shoulder. Then Corin, on the crutch, a step at a time.
She stopped at the bottom.
Kross looked at her.
He did not say I'm sorry. He did not say anything. He looked at her for a long moment, with his tired gray eyes, the way you look at somebody across a grave.
Then he held out his hands.
There was a book in them. Small. A little bigger than a hand. Bound in green cloth, faded at the corners to the color of moss. With a tree on the front, in gold thread, and a crown in its branches. And under the tree, in small silver stitches that leaned back as if they were looking over their shoulder, the words.
The dark is the day with its eyes shut.
"It's time," said Silas Kross.
Lyra looked at the book.
She did not take it. Not at once. She stood at the bottom of the stair in her mother's burned gown and looked at it in his hands.
"You said no," she said. "Yesterday. You said not that."
"Yesterday it wasn't time." His voice was very quiet. "It was to stay on his desk until it was time. And then come to me. And stay with me until it was time again." He did not look away from her. "And then go to you. When he was gone. Not before." A pause. "That was what she asked. I've done what she asked. All of it. For eleven years."
"Who asked?"
He did not answer.
He did not have to. The silver stitches were looking over their shoulder at her from the green cloth.
Lyra put out her hands, in Silas's old black gloves, and took the book.
She held it against her chest. With both arms. The way Gideon held his jars. And stood there, at the foot of the stair, with her eyes shut.
Kross put on his hat.
"The carriage will take you down," he said. "To the bottom of Saint Bride's Lane. Not further. My driver doesn't go over the river after dark." He went to the front door. Wren opened it for him. The snow was coming down thick now in Steeple Lane, gray in the last of the light. "I'll see to him. Tonight. He'll go to Saint Clement's, to the Thorne vault, beside her. Beside the box they buried that has nothing in it." He paused on the step. "Your brother will be there in the morning. And your aunt. I'll tell them myself."
"Thank you," said Lyra.
She said it without opening her eyes.
Kross stood on the top step of his plain gray house with the snow coming down on his hat and his shoulders, and did not turn round.
"Don't thank me, Miss Thorne," he said. "I put him in this house. Whatever else I did. I put him here." And then, very low: "And don't come to the vault in the morning. Be gone by then. By the dyke road. All of you."
And he went down the five steps into the snow, and up Steeple Lane toward the Spire, with his hands behind his back. Not hurrying. And the snow closed behind him.
Wren shut the door.
And then, with her back against it, in the gray hall, she said, in a voice that was not correct at all, that was only a girl's voice, nineteen:
"He sat on the fifth step all night. Last night. In the dark. With a cup of tea he didn't drink. I heard him. He never went to bed."
Nobody said anything.
"I just thought somebody should know," said Wren.
The crypt was full of candles when they came back.
Mrs. Doyle had done it. She had gone up to the church while they were on the Hill and taken every candle she could find off every saint in every niche, the stubs and the half-burned and the new ones nobody had lit, and brought them down the twenty-three steps in her apron, and set them all round the crypt, on the floor, on the ledges, along the bottoms of the round stone arches. Fifty of them. Sixty. Burning. So that when Lyra came down the steps with the green book against her chest, the whole low round crypt was full of small warm yellow lights, like a field at night full of stars come down to sleep on the ground.
She stopped on the bottom step and looked at them.
Mrs. Doyle was standing by the fire with her broom.
"For your father," she said. It was the first thing Corin had ever heard her say. "It's what you do. Where I come from. When somebody goes." She looked at the floor. "One for every year, my mother said. I didn't know how many years. So I took all of them."
Lyra looked at her.
Then she came down off the bottom step, and crossed the crypt, between the candles, to the old woman by the fire. And put her arms round her. With the green book between them. And held on.
Mrs. Doyle stood very still, with her broom in one hand. And then she put the other hand on Lyra's back. And patted it. Twice. The way you pat a horse.
Then she let go, and went back to sweeping.
Later, when the others were eating, Lyra came and sat down beside Corin, against his pillar, by the fire.
She had the green book in her lap.
She did not say anything for a while. Neither did he. They sat side by side, not close, not far, and watched the candles burn down.
Then she opened the book.
Corin had thought it would be full. A family book. Births, and marriages, and deaths, the way the Oakhaven register had been, year after year, in a careful hand. It was not. Only the first page had writing on it.
In ink. Brown now. In a hand that leaned back. A woman's hand. Small, and even, and looking over its shoulder.
Aurelian Thorne and Isolde, his wife.
And under that, three lines.
Lyra. Born at the turn of the year, in the snow.
Rowan. Born in the spring, in the rain.
And a third line.
It had been written. Corin could see that. The same brown ink. The same leaning hand. A name, and a few words after it. And then it had been scratched out. Not with a line through it. Scratched. With the point of something sharp. Over and over. Until the paper was rough and furred and the ink was only a brown smudge in the middle of a scrape, and you could not read a single letter.
Under the scrape, very small, in the same leaning hand, in the same brown ink, there was a mark.
A small square. Folded. Drawn, the way you would draw a letter folded small. A square, and inside it, lines, crossing and crossing. Creases.
Lyra looked at the scraped line for a long time.
She did not say anything about it.
Neither did Corin.
After that, the pages were blank. All of them. Page after page of thick creamy paper, good paper, the kind the Hill buys, with a faint watermark in one corner. Nobody had written on them. Not in eleven years.
Lyra turned them over slowly. All of them. To the end.
Then she turned back to the first blank page. The one after the scraped line.
And held the book out to him.
"Your other one's full," she said. "Oren told me. On the stair. He said you'd filled it this morning. To the last line."
Corin looked at the book in her hands.
"It's your mother's," he said.
"Yes."
"It's the only thing you've got of hers."
"Yes." She did not take it back. "And she'd want it written in. She didn't stitch those words on the front of a book so it could sit on a desk with nothing in it for eleven years." Her voice was quite steady. "I can't write in it. You can. So you write in it."
Corin did not move.
"Rule one," said Lyra. "No lies between us. Not even the kind ones." She looked at him. "So here's the truth. I want my mother's book to have you in it. Because you were my father for three hours. And because you held up the sky. And because I can read, and I'll read it every day, and I want to read you."
She held it out.
Corin took it.
It was light. Lighter than the old Ledger. The green cloth was soft under his gray-gloved hands. He could feel it a little, through his palm. Like through a glove.
He opened it on his knee. To the first blank page.
And took out the stub of pencil.
And looked at the clean creamy paper for a long time, by the candlelight.
Then, at the top, in square capitals, crooked, because his fingers could not feel the pencil, he wrote:
LEDGER VIII.
BEGUN AT SAINT BRIDE'S, THE NIGHT AURELIAN THORNE DIED. IN HIS WIFE'S BOOK. GIVEN ME BY HIS DAUGHTER.
And under it:
READ THIS FIRST.
And then he stopped.
Because he had written the first page of a Ledger seven times before. In six years. He must have. And he did not remember any of them. And he did not know how it went.
He looked at the girl beside him.
"Read it to me," he said. "The old one. The first page. You read. I'll write."
And she took the old Ledger, Ledger VII, HARBOUR TIDE TABLES, out of his coat. And opened it, on her knee, to the first page. And read it to him. Slowly. Line by line. In her voice. Quick, and a little cross, and a little cracked down the middle, like a cup.
"Your name is Corin Vane."
He wrote it.
"You are twenty-eight years old."
He wrote it.
"You were born in a place called Oakhaven. You do not remember it."
He wrote it. His hand stopped, for a moment, on the last word. And went on.
"You are a harvester."
And the names. And the rules. And THOSE I OWE. All of it. Line by line. Her voice reading. His hand writing. In the candlelight. With the others eating bread by the fire and not looking at them, the way people do not look at two people who are doing something that matters.
And at the bottom of the page, she stopped.
Corin waited.
She was looking at the line near the bottom of the old first page. In his capitals. And under it, in her small quick hand, the hand she did not have any more.
LYRA THORNE. PARTNER. RULE ONE: NO LIES BETWEEN US. NOT EVEN THE KIND ONES.
She did not read it.
She sat with the old Ledger on her knee and looked at it.
"Read it," said Corin.
She looked up.
"It's the next line," he said. "I can see it from here. Read it. I'll write it."
She looked at him for a long time.
Then she read it. In a voice he had kept when he burned everything else. Very quietly.
"Lyra Thorne. Partner."
He wrote it.
"Rule one. No lies between us."
He wrote it.
"Not even the kind ones."
He wrote it.
And then he did not stop. He went on writing. Under it. His own line. That was not on the old page. That had never been on any page before.
I DO NOT REMEMBER HER. SHE READ THIS PAGE TO ME. SHE IS COMING AFTER.
He turned the green book round on his knee, so that she could see.
She read it.
And she did not say anything. She did not have to. She put out her right hand, the one in Silas's old black glove, worn through at one fingertip, and put one finger on the page, under the last line. And pressed. Not hard.
And took it away.
And there was a small red spot on the creamy paper. Under the R of AFTER. From the cut on her thumb, that kept opening.
Her mark.
The only one she had left.
