Chapter 60: The Road to the Glass, Part 4 of 5: Leave-Taking
Leave-Taking
"I'm coming," said Juno.
She said it at the ninth bell, when Oren stood up and buckled on his sword, and Silas came down the twenty-three steps with a sack over his shoulder full of bread and cheese and a jar of the green-leaf honey for Corin's hands. She stood up off her blankets with her whistle in her fist and her feet in fresh sacking and her small fierce face set like a door.
"I'm coming," she said again. "I walked fourteen miles in the snow to find you. I lay in a gutter on top of the Bourse for four hours. I can climb anything. I can whistle. I can do all the letters." She glared at Corin. "You'll need somebody who can do the letters."
Nobody said anything.
It was Lyra who answered. She was sitting by the fire with the green book in her lap. She had not moved much since Corin wrote the first page in it. She looked up.
"Your mother," she said.
Juno's face changed.
"Mrs. Marta," said Lyra. "On Tallow Street. With the clocks. She hasn't seen you since you went to the barge. Since before the Bourse." Lyra's voice was very gentle. Gentler than Corin had heard it. "She sat on that step every night for a week, Wren says. Sweeping. Waiting. With Terrence rubbing his thumb."
Juno did not say anything.
"And your feet," said Silas, from the steps. Quietly. "You've two toes gone already. The Glass is colder than anything you've ever walked on. It'll take the rest. And then your feet. And then your legs." He put the sack down. "I'm sorry, Juno. I'm a doctor. That's what I'm for. To say the thing."
Juno stood very still in the candlelight with her whistle in her fist.
Then her face crumpled, all at once, the way a paper bag crumples when you close your fist on it. And she sat down on her blankets and put her face in her knees.
Corin got up.
It took a long time. Nobody helped him. He got up, and went across the crypt on the crutch, between the candles, which had burned very low now, and stood over her. And then, slowly, because his knee would not bend and it hurt more than anything had ever hurt, he got down. On the stone. Beside her. With his bad leg stuck out straight.
And took the old Ledger out of his coat.
Ledger VII. HARBOUR TIDE TABLES. Full to the last line.
And held it out to her.
Juno lifted her face out of her knees. It was wet and red and furious.
"What's that?"
"The old one," said Corin. "It's full. I've got a new one now." He did not look at Lyra. "I can't take both. Too heavy. And if they catch us on the road, they'll take whatever's in my coat. And then they'll know everything that's in here. Everybody. Mother Grue. Silas. Toby. Your mother. You." He held it out. "So somebody has to keep it. Somewhere safe. Somebody who can do the letters."
Juno looked at the book.
"I can't read it," she said. "Not words. Only letters."
"You'll learn."
"Who'll teach me?"
Corin looked across the crypt.
At Gideon. Sitting by the fire against his father's side. With his father's great gray hand in his lap. Looking at them. With his head on one side, the way he looked at chalk marks.
"He's learning," said Corin. "He knows five words now. Silas is teaching him. You could learn together."
Gideon looked at Juno.
Juno looked at Gideon.
"He only knows Lyra and read," said Juno. "And V."
"And Da," said Gideon. "Silas showed me this afternoon. And Saul." He did not look away from her. "That's five."
Juno did not say anything for a long time.
Then she reached out and took the old Ledger in both hands. And held it against her chest. Like Gideon held his jars. Like Lyra held the green book.
"I'll keep it," she said. Her voice was hoarse. "I'll keep it under my mattress at Mum's. And I'll learn all of it. Every word. And when you come back I'll read it to you. All of it. From the start." She glared at him. "So you'd better come back."
"Yes," said Corin.
"Promise."
He looked at her.
"I can't," he said. "Rule one."
Juno's face went very still.
Then she nodded. Once. Hard. As if he had given her the right answer, and she hated it.
"Then try," she said.
"I'll try."
She put her arms round his neck. Quickly. Hard. With the book between them. And then let go, and turned away, and lay down on her blankets with her back to him and the book under her, and did not move.
Corin got up. It took even longer than getting down.
Gideon was standing by the fire.
He had let go of his father's hand. He had stood up. And he was standing in front of the brazier, in his boots with no big toes in them, with his white curled hands over the front of his coat, over the two jars. Looking at Corin.
"I'm staying," he said.
"I know."
"I said I'd find out how. To him. On the stair. I'll find out how. Somebody should." Gideon looked at his father, sitting against the pillar with his pale empty eyes on nothing. "Silas says nobody knows. Not how to put them back. Not once they're gone a long time. Not feelings. Not a whole man." He looked back at Corin. "But Linus Hale's book. The one you've got. It says how to put a thing back, sometimes, if you're quick, and the person is still warm. Silas read me that bit. From what you told him."
Corin did not say anything.
"He's not quick," said Gideon. "It's been nine years. But he's still warm." His dry voice did not change. "So there's a start."
Corin looked at him for a long moment.
Then he reached into Fenton's coat. Past the green book. Past the yellow page from under the Ministry. To the oilcloth packet, tied with string.
Linus Hale's book. Sixty-two years.
He held it out.
Gideon looked at it. And did not take it.
"That's yours," he said. "He gave it you."
"He gave it me to be useful. I can't use it. Silas says. A hand's width less a finger. If I try the slow ways, I'll go up like a dry field." Corin did not lower the packet. "You can't read it. But Silas can. Most of it. And the back, the old letters—" He stopped.
I'll write them out, said Malakor. Behind his left ear. Very quietly. Tell him. Tonight, on the road, when we stop. You write, and I'll say. The page about putting things back. I wrote it. I know it. I'll say it, you'll write it, in plain capitals, in the green book. And you'll tear it out and send it back to him. By Wren. Or by the Auditor.
Corin did not ask how Malakor knew about the Auditor.
"Malakor says he'll give you the page," said Corin. "The one about putting things back. In plain letters. I'll send it."
Gideon looked at him.
Then he took the oilcloth packet. In both white curled hands. And put it inside his coat. With the jars.
"I'll keep it," he said. "Till you come back."
"Yes."
"I won't open it till Silas says."
"Yes."
Gideon nodded.
Then, very slowly, as if he were not sure how it was done, he held out his hand. His right one. White and curled. That would not bend.
Corin took it. In his gray wool glove.
They held on to each other for a moment, in the candlelight, the way they had held on in Cinder Lane under the burning dome.
And then Gideon let go, and went back to the pillar, and sat down against his father's side again, and took the great gray hand in his lap. And began, very softly, to hum. The carrying tune. Slow, and heavy, and going-on, with no end to it.
Big Gideon's head turned. Slowly. Toward the sound.
Silas was standing at the bottom of the steps with the sack.
He did not say anything when Corin came to him. He only took Corin's left hand, and pulled off the gray wool glove, and pushed back the sleeve of Fenton's coat. And looked at the edge of the gray. Near the shoulder. In the light of the last candle.
He looked for a long time.
"Not a candle," he said. "Not a spark. Not a breath of wind. Not for anything. Not for anybody." He did not let go of the hand. "Promise me."
"I can't."
"I know you can't." Silas's tired voice did not rise. "Promise me anyway. Lie to me. Just once. I'm not in your book."
Corin looked at him.
"You are," he said. "Doctor Silas. Saint Bride's. He won't let them go."
Silas took off his crooked spectacles.
And rubbed his eyes with the back of his wrist. And put them back on. Still crooked.
"Then go," he said. "Go and try, and don't come back gray to the eyes, or I'll never forgive you."
He put the sack over Corin's shoulder. And the green-leaf honey in his pocket. And the gray wool glove back on his hand.
And turned away, quickly, and went to put more coal on the brazier, though it did not need it.
Lyra was the last one.
She had not moved from the fire all this time. She sat with the green book in her lap and watched them, one by one. Juno. Gideon. Silas. And when Corin came back across the crypt to her, on the crutch, with Silas's sack over his shoulder, she looked up.
And stood up.
She had taken off her mother's gray gown. Corin saw it now. She had changed, while he was with Juno. Into clothes Mrs. Doyle must have brought down from somewhere. A boy's breeches. A thick wool shirt. A sheepskin jerkin, old and worn, too big for her. And over it all, Silas's old black coat, buttoned to the throat. And boots. Heavy ones. Laced to the knee. Her short burned hair was pushed up under a wool cap like his.
The gray gown lay folded on her blankets. Very neatly. With the list pocket on top.
"You're coming," said Corin.
It was not a question.
"You wrote it," said Lyra. "In my mother's book. She is coming after." She put the green book inside the black coat, against her chest, and buttoned the coat over it. "I read it. I always do what I read."
"That's not true."
"No," said Lyra. "It isn't." She looked at him. "Rule one. Here's the true one. He's gone. There's nothing to stay for. Ro has Aunt Cressida, and Aunt Cressida has Ro, and they'll go to the vault in the morning and stand in the snow, and I won't be there, and Ro will think I've left him again." Her voice did not shake. "And I'll write to him from the Glass and tell him why. Or you will. In crooked capitals."
She picked up the gray gown from the blankets. And held it for a moment. And then gave it to Mrs. Doyle, who was standing by the pillar with her broom.
"Keep it," said Lyra. "Please. It was my mother's. I can't take it where we're going." A pause. "There's a list in the pocket. A vial, and a letter, and a strip of paper. Don't read them. Give them to the Auditor at the Bank, if she ever comes. Only to her."
Mrs. Doyle took the gown.
She did not say anything. She folded it over her arm, very carefully, the way you fold a flag.
And Lyra looked round the crypt one last time. At the candles, burned down to nothing. At the brazier. At the round stone arches. At Juno, with her back to them, and the old Ledger under her. At Gideon, against his father's side, humming. At Silas, by the fire, with his back to them, putting coal on.
And at the words on the floor, in blue chalk, big.
LYRA.
READ.
And under them, smaller, new, written while they were on the Hill, in letters that leaned and wobbled because the hand that made them had only just learned how:
DA. SAUL.
She looked at them for a long time.
Then she counted the twenty-three steps, going up. Out loud. So that everybody heard.
One. Two. Three.
And Corin came after her. On the crutch. A step at a time.
And Oren came after him.
Wat's Boat
The dyke road began where the city stopped.
Not at a gate. Corin had thought there would be a gate. There was one, the Ledger said, the North Gate, with a toll and a lantern and Enforcers in it day and night since the week the Thorne abduction went up on the posters. But the dyke road did not go through it. The dyke road went round it. It began behind the last of the tanneries on the north edge of the Saltmarsh, where the cobbles stopped and the mud began, and went up onto the top of a long high bank of earth that kept the river out of the fields. And ran along the top of the bank, north, straight as a rule, into the dark.
On one side of the bank, the river. Wide and black and slow, with ice at the edges, going down to the sea. On the other side, below, a long way down, the fields. Flat. White with snow. Going away into nothing. And between the fields and the foot of the bank, a channel. Narrow. Black. Still. A ditch of water, half frozen, with reeds along it.
The Tanners' Ditch, the Ledger called it. It went north too. Beside the dyke. Under it, almost. All the way to the reeds.
They came up onto the dyke at the tenth bell, the three of them, in the snow.
Lyra first. Counting. Corin did not know what she was counting. Steps, perhaps. Or snowflakes. Or the bells, going behind them, very faint, from the city. Then Corin, on the crutch, in Fenton's coat and Silas's wool gloves, with the sack over his shoulder and the green book inside his coat against his ribs. Then Oren, with his sword, walking backward half the time, looking at the city.
The snow was coming down thick now. Kross had been right. Off the hills. Big soft flakes, very many, falling straight down out of a black sky with no wind, so that you could see only a few paces in front of you, and then only white, and then only dark. The tracks they made filled in behind them almost before they had made them.
He's good, said Malakor. I'll give him that. He's very good. He knows the weather better than the weather does.
They walked for an hour.
Corin did not know how. The crutch sank into the snow on the top of the dyke at every step, and the stiff leg dragged, and his knee was a long white scream that he had stopped listening to somewhere about the first quarter. He walked anyway. Lyra did not look back. But she did not get further than ten paces ahead of him, the whole hour. When the gap got longer than ten paces, she stopped. And counted something. And went on when he caught up.
At the end of the hour, there was a lock.
It was on the river side. A great black wooden gate in the water, with a beam across the top of it and a little stone hut beside it on the bank. The lock-keeper's hut. Dark. Empty. With its door hanging open and snow on the floor inside. Lock-keeper taken, the broadsheet had said, a week ago. Corin had read it in the Ledger. Barges searched from Saltgate to Brindle.
And on the other side of the dyke, below, on the black still water of the Tanners' Ditch, there was a light.
Small. Yellow. Low down. A lantern, shuttered almost all the way, so that only a thread of light came out of it. On the water.
Oren had his sword out.
"Wait," said Lyra.
She went to the edge of the dyke. On the field side. And looked down. At the thread of yellow light on the black water, a long way below, through the falling snow.
And then she whistled.
Not Juno's whistle. She did not have it. With her mouth. Low. Two notes. And a pause. And one.
And from the water, out of the dark, came an answer.
Not a whistle. A voice. A man's voice. Deep, and rough, and very tired. Singing. Very low. Three notes of a tune Corin did not know, that went up and hung, and came down again.
Lyra put her hand over her mouth.
"Wat," she said. Through her fingers. "It's Wat."
There were steps cut into the side of the dyke. Mud steps, with planks laid in them, slippery with snow. Oren went down first, sideways, and then reached up. And Corin came down them on his backside, one at a time, with the crutch across his knees, the way a child comes down a stair. And Lyra came last. And at the bottom, on a plank landing half sunk in the frozen reeds, there was a boat.
A punt. Long and narrow and flat, black, with a little hooped canvas shelter at one end like a cart's cover. And standing in the stern of it, with a pole in his left hand, and his right arm in a sling made from a flour sack, a man.
Big. Broad. With a red beard gone white with snow, and a face Corin did not know, and eyes red-rimmed and swollen as if he had not slept, or had not stopped crying, for a week.
"Miss Lyra," said Wat Doon.
Lyra stood on the landing and looked at him.
She did not say anything. She did not have to. Corin saw her see the sling. And the face. And the hooped canvas shelter at the front of the punt, with a lantern hung inside it, shuttered. And something under the lantern, in the shelter. A small shape. A basket, perhaps. Wrapped in blankets.
"Hessa," said Lyra.
Wat's face did not change.
"She handed him down," said Wat. His voice was very low and very flat. "Your father. Off the stack. When it was burning. To the man in gray. And then the mast came down." He looked at the water. "She'd given me the boy first. Before. She put him in my arms and said, Go. Robin first. And I went. I didn't look back. She told me not to." His good hand was white on the pole. "I didn't look back."
Lyra said nothing.
"She kept her promise," said Wat. "Robin first. And your father second. She kept both." He looked up at Lyra. "I heard he's gone. Today. On the Hill."
"Yes."
"Then that's both of them."
The snow came down on the black water between them.
"How did you know?" said Oren. Very quietly. From the landing. With his sword still out. "To come. Here. Tonight."
Wat looked at him.
"A man came," he said. "To Pask's hut. At Sedge End. Yesterday, at dusk." His mouth twisted under the white-snowed beard. "In gray. On a horse. The one who was there. At the barge. When she—" He stopped. "Vell. The one who fired the hay."
Oren's sword came up.
"He didn't come to take anything," said Wat. "He stood at the door of the hut in the snow and didn't come in. And he said, Thursday night. The third lock below Brindle. Tenth bell. There'll be three. A girl, a lame man, and the captain. Take them up the Ditch, through the reeds, past the toll on the north road, as far as the reeds go. And then go wherever you like, and take the boy, and don't tell anybody where." Wat's voice was quite flat. "And he gave me a purse. And went."
"And you came," said Oren.
"I came." Wat looked at Lyra. "Not for him. For her. For Hessa. She'd have come."
He put the pole into the water.
"Get in," he said. "Before the snow stops."
They got in.
Oren in the middle, on the floor of the punt, with his sword across his knees and his back against the side. Lyra at the front, by the hooped shelter, kneeling. Corin at the back, by Wat's feet, on a coil of rope, with the crutch laid along the bottom of the boat and his bad leg stuck out straight on top of it.
And Wat leaned on the pole, and the punt slid off the landing, out of the reeds, onto the black still water of the Tanners' Ditch. North. Very quietly. With the snow falling on them, and on the water, and on the reeds, and the dyke going up on their right like a wall into the dark, and the white fields going away on their left into nothing.
Nobody spoke.
After a while, Wat unshuttered the lantern in the shelter a little. Just a thread more. So that he could see the water ahead.
And Corin, at the back, by Wat's feet, saw the basket.
It was in the shelter, under the lantern, on a heap of sacks. A wicker basket, old, the kind you carry eggs in. Wrapped round with blankets, gray and brown, many of them. And in the basket, in the blankets, asleep, a child.
A baby. A year old, perhaps, or a little less. With red hair like duckling down, sticking up all over his head. And a round face, pink with cold. And his mouth open, and his thumb in it.
Robin.
Corin knew the name. It was in the Ledger. ROBIN. HESSA'S BOY. ELEVEN MONTHS. RED HAIR. HESSA PROMISED: ROBIN FIRST.
He looked at the sleeping child for a long time.
And then he saw the doll.
It was in the basket with the boy. Tucked in beside him, under his arm, in the blankets, the way a child holds a thing it loves even in its sleep. A rag doll. Small. Old. Very old, Corin thought, by the look of it. Made of cloth that had been white once and was gray now, and stuffed so long ago that the stuffing had gone flat and soft. It had a dress. Faded blue. The blue of a winter sky an hour before dark. And a face, stitched on in thread. Two eyes. One of them a black button, sewn on tight. The other one gone, with only a few loose threads where it had been. And a small stitched mouth.
The stitches were very small.
Very small, and very even, and they leaned back. Every one. Leaning back a little, all the same way, as if they were looking over their shoulder.
Corin looked at them.
He had seen stitches like that. Somewhere. Not long ago. He was sure of it. He could feel the edges of it, the way you feel the edges of a word that is on the tip of your tongue. Small. Even. Leaning back.
He could not think where.
"The doll," he said to Wat. Very low. So as not to wake the boy.
Wat looked down. At the basket. At the doll under the sleeping child's arm.
"Pask's," he said. "The eel-man. At Sedge End." He leaned on the pole. "It was up in the rafters of his hut, in the turf, with the eel-traps. Old Pask got it down on Tuesday, when the boy wouldn't stop crying for his mother. He gave it him. And he stopped."
"Where did Pask get it?"
Wat shrugged with his good shoulder.
"He said a lady left it," he said. "Years ago. One autumn. In a dark blue cloak, with the hood up, asking the way to the coast. She stopped at his hut for water in the rain. And she left it on his table when she went. And she said—" Wat frowned, remembering. "Pask's got no teeth. It's hard to tell what he says. But he said she said, For the one who comes after."
Corin sat very still on the coil of rope at the back of the punt.
For the one who comes after.
"Pask thought she meant his grandchild," said Wat. "He never had one. His son died. So it stayed in the rafters." He looked at the doll. "Till Tuesday."
At the front of the punt, by the shelter, Lyra was kneeling with her back to them, looking ahead. At the black water, and the falling snow, and the dark. She had not turned round. She had not heard. She was counting something. Her lips were moving.
Corin opened his mouth to say her name.
Boy, said Malakor. Very quietly. Not tonight. She's buried her father today. Let her count.
Corin shut his mouth.
He looked at the doll a moment longer. At the stitches leaning back. At the one black button eye.
Then he took out the green book. And opened it, on his knee, under the thread of lantern light, to the page after the first page. And wrote, very small, crooked:
THURSDAY. WAT'S BOAT. THE TANNERS' DITCH.
A RAG DOLL IN ROBIN'S BASKET. FADED BLUE DRESS. ONE BUTTON EYE. FROM PASK'S RAFTERS. A LADY IN A DARK BLUE CLOAK, ONE AUTUMN, ASKING THE WAY TO THE COAST, LEFT IT. FOR THE ONE WHO COMES AFTER.
THE STITCHES LEAN BACK. I HAVE SEEN STITCHES LIKE THAT. I CANNOT THINK WHERE.
ASK HER. NOT TONIGHT.
He shut the book.
In the basket, the boy stirred in his sleep. And his small hand closed on the doll's faded blue dress. And held on.
And Wat leaned on the pole, and the punt went on, north, through the falling snow, between the dyke and the fields, without a sound.
