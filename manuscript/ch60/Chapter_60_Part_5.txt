Chapter 60: The Road to the Glass, Part 5 of 5: The Dyke Road
The Dyke Road
They went up the Ditch all night.
Corin did not sleep. He sat on the coil of rope at the back of the punt, by Wat's feet, with his bad leg stuck out straight along the crutch on the bottom of the boat, and the green book inside his coat against his ribs, and watched the snow fall on the black water.
There was nothing else to see. The dyke on the right, going up like a wall into the dark. The fields on the left, white, flat, going away into nothing. The reeds along the edge of the water, standing up out of the ice, black and stiff, with snow on their heads. And the snow. Always the snow. Falling straight down, with no wind, very thick, very soft, so that the whole world was the small circle of yellow light from the shuttered lantern in the shelter, and the black water in it, and the snow coming down through it, and beyond that, nothing.
Wat poled.
He did not stop. He did not speak. He leaned on the pole with his good arm, and pushed, and drew it up, and leaned again, hour after hour, with the snow settling on his shoulders and his beard and his hat until he was white all over, like a man made of snow. Once, about the second bell, he stopped, and leaned on the pole, and stood there in the stern with his head bowed, for a long time, breathing. And Corin thought he was going to fall. And then he lifted his head, and went on.
Oren slept. Sitting up. With his sword across his knees and his eyes half open.
Lyra did not sleep either.
She knelt at the front of the punt, by the shelter, with her back to them, looking ahead. Into the snow. For hours. Corin could see her shape against the faint yellow glow of the lantern. Kneeling. Very straight. In Silas's old black coat. Not moving. And her lips, he thought, moving. Counting.
About the third bell, the boy in the basket woke, and cried.
It was a thin small crying, in the dark, in the snow. Not loud. The crying of a child who has been cold and frightened for a long time, and has got used to it, and is crying now out of habit more than anything.
Wat did not stop poling. He could not. If he stopped, the punt would drift, and catch in the reeds, and freeze in.
Lyra turned round.
She looked at the basket. And then, without a word, she reached into it, and lifted the boy out. Blankets and all. And the doll, still clutched in his fist. And held him. Against her chest. Inside Silas's coat. With the green book.
And began, very softly, to sing.
Corin did not know the song. It went up, and hung, and came down again. Slow. Simple. A tune for rocking a child. A tune that a woman sings on a barge at night, on a black river, with fog on the water.
Wat's pole stopped in the water.
Just for a moment. The punt slid on, a little way, on its own, through the snow. And Corin looked up and saw Wat's face, white with snow, in the faint light, turned toward the front of the boat. Toward the girl singing.
Then the pole went down again. And Wat leaned on it. And pushed.
And went on poling. With the snow running down his face, which was not all snow.
The boy stopped crying.
Lyra went on singing. Very low. The same song, over and over. Until he was asleep again, against her chest, inside the coat, with his fist in the doll's faded blue dress and his red head under her chin.
She did not put him back in the basket. She held him the rest of the night.
At the fifth bell, the snow stopped.
Corin did not hear it stop. He saw it. All at once, it was not there. The black water in the circle of lantern light was black and still and empty, with no flakes falling into it. And beyond the light, for the first time all night, there was something to see.
The sky.
It was not dark any more. It was gray. A pale cold gray, getting paler at the edge, ahead of them, to the right. To the east. Where the dyke went down.
Because the dyke was going down.
Corin had not noticed. He noticed now. The great earth wall on their right, that had gone up into the dark all night like the side of a house, was lower. Much lower. A man's height, perhaps. Less. Sloping down, ahead of them, until it was only a bank. And beyond the bank, no river. The river had gone. Somewhere in the night, it had turned away east, toward the sea, and left the dyke behind.
And the fields had gone too.
There were no fields. On either side. There were reeds. Only reeds. Standing up out of the ice in every direction, as far as Corin could see in the gray light. Taller than a man. Black and gold and silver with frost. Millions of them. Going away and away into the gray, flat and endless, with the channel of black water running between them like a crack in a floor.
The fens.
Wat stopped poling.
He leaned on the pole in the stern, and the punt slid on a little way, and stopped, in the black water, in the reeds. And he stood there, white with snow, and looked ahead.
"That's the toll," he said.
Corin looked.
Far ahead, through the reeds, in the gray light, there was a light. Yellow. Small. Low down. And beside it, very faint against the sky, a shape. A roof. A barrier, perhaps, across a road.
"The north road," said Wat. "It comes up from the city on the other side of the dyke, and crosses the Ditch here, on a causeway. There's a toll. A gate. Enforcers, since last week." He leaned on the pole. "We go under the causeway. There's a culvert. Low. You'll have to lie down in the bottom of the boat. All of you." He looked at Lyra. "And the boy. If he cries—"
"He won't," said Lyra.
Wat looked at her a moment longer. Then he nodded.
They lay down in the bottom of the punt. Oren with his sword. Corin with his crutch. Lyra with the boy inside her coat, and her hand over his head, and her mouth by his ear, singing without sound.
And Wat shuttered the lantern all the way, and lay down too, in the stern, and pushed with his good hand against the reeds, and the punt slid forward. Into the dark under the causeway. Into a tunnel of old stone, round, dripping, so low that Corin could have reached up and touched the roof of it from where he lay. Black. Cold. Smelling of wet stone and old ice.
Above them, on the causeway, they heard boots. Two men. Walking. Stamping their feet in the cold. And a voice.
"—all night, and not a cart. Not one. Who'd be out in that?"
"Nobody."
"Then why are we stood here?"
"Because there's a list. Thursday's list. And there's a name on it, the sergeant says, that wasn't on it last week, and the Inquisitor didn't take it off." A pause. "A harvester. With gray hands. And a limp."
"What's he done?"
"Held up the Bourse, they say. The dome. On his hands. When it was falling."
A silence.
"That's not a crime."
"It is now."
The boots went away along the causeway. The voices went with them.
The punt slid on through the dark, under the stone. And came out the other side. Into the gray. Into the reeds.
And Wat sat up, and took the pole, and pushed, and the punt went on, north, between the reeds, away from the causeway and the toll and the yellow light, very quietly. Until the light was gone behind them, and the shape of the roof, and there was nothing behind them but reeds.
And nothing ahead but reeds.
And the gray light coming up in the east.
He didn't take it off, thought Corin. My name. The list.
He said he could leave it off once, said Malakor. Maybe twice. And he didn't. Not this time. A long pause. I think he wanted you to hear that, boy. On the causeway. I think he put those two men there to say it. So you'd know. That it's real. That you can't come back.
Corin lay in the bottom of the punt and looked up at the gray sky.
Or he's just a man who keeps his word, said Malakor. I've never been able to tell, with him.
They left the punt at the seventh bell.
At a place where the reeds stopped. All at once. Like a wall. And beyond them, there was land. Rising. Gray-white with snow, and stony, with no trees on it, and no houses, and no road. Only a track. Going up. North. Into hills.
Low hills, at first. Bare and round, like the backs of sleeping animals under a white blanket. And beyond them, higher ones. And beyond those, very far off, so far that Corin could not be sure he was seeing them at all, a line along the bottom of the sky. Pale. Paler than the snow. Shining a little, in the gray morning light. Like a strip of water. Or ice. Or glass.
Wat poled the punt in against the edge of the reeds, where the land began. And held it there with the pole. And did not get out.
Oren got out first. Into the snow, up to his knees. And then reached back with his good hand and took Corin's arm, and pulled, and Corin came out of the punt onto the land, on his knees, and got the crutch under him, and stood.
Lyra got out last.
She stood on the edge of the land, in the snow, with the boy inside her coat. Asleep. She did not take him out. Not at once. She stood with her arms round him, under the coat, and looked at Wat.
Wat looked back.
"Where will you go?" she said.
"East," said Wat. "Through the fens. To the Fenners. Hessa's mother's people. Out past the last staithe, where the reeds go down to the sea. They don't have lists out there. They don't have anything." He looked at the boy's red head, just showing at the top of Lyra's coat. "They'll take him. He's Hessa's. They'll know the eyes."
Lyra nodded.
Then she took the boy out of her coat. Slowly. Carefully. Without waking him. Blankets and all, and the doll still in his fist. And knelt down at the edge of the land, in the snow, and leaned out over the black water, and put him back in his basket in the shelter of the punt. And tucked the blankets round him. And put the doll straight, under his arm.
She looked at the doll for a moment.
At the faded blue dress. At the one black button eye.
Corin saw her look.
And he saw her frown. Just a little. The small worried line between her brows. As if she had seen something, and did not know what it was, and could not stop to think about it.
And then the boy stirred, and she put her hand on his red head, and the line went away.
She stood up.
"Robin first," she said to Wat.
Wat's face moved under the snow in his beard.
"Robin first," he said.
And pushed off from the land with the pole. And the punt slid back out into the black channel between the reeds. And turned. East. Away from the hills and the track and the line of pale shining at the bottom of the sky. And went. Slowly. Very quietly. A man made of snow, leaning on a pole, in a long black boat, with a basket in the bow.
They watched it go until the reeds closed round it.
And then there was nothing but reeds. And the gray light. And the silence.
Lyra turned round.
She looked at the track, going up into the hills. At the low round hills, and the high ones behind them. At the line along the bottom of the sky, very far off, pale, shining.
"Is that it?" she said.
Corin looked at it too.
Yes, said Malakor. Very quietly. That's it. That's the Glass.
"Yes," said Corin.
Lyra looked at it a moment longer.
Then she began to walk. Up the track. Into the snow. Counting.
And Corin came after her, on the crutch. A step at a time.
And Oren came after him. Walking backward, half the time. Looking at the reeds. Looking at the way they had come. Until there was nothing to see that way but reeds, and then not even reeds, and he turned round at last, and walked forward like the others.
North.
And behind Corin's left ear, as the first of the low round hills rose up white in front of them, an old dry voice said, very quietly:
There's a road under your feet now, boy. And nobody listening.
Then tell me.
I can't. I told you. There's a crease. A long pause. But I can walk it with you. All the way. To the bunker. To the box under her map table. And when you open it, and read it, in plain letters, I'll be there. Another pause. I'll be right here. Behind your ear. Where I've always been.
And if it's me? The one who got out?
Malakor did not answer for a long time.
The track went up. The snow was knee-deep. Ahead of them, Lyra walked, and counted, and did not look back, and did not get more than ten paces ahead.
Then you'll know, said Malakor at last. And she'll know. And the man with the gentle voice will know, sooner or later, because he always does. A breath. And then we'll see what kind of a fool you are, boy.
What kind?
The kind I'd have been, said Malakor. If anybody had ever made me kind.
Elara at Her Window
The Auditor of the Fourth Desk sat at her window on the third floor of the Oblivion Bank and watched the snow stop.
It was the hour before dawn on Friday. The hour when the night is at its coldest. She had been sitting here since the eighth bell on Thursday, in her hard wooden chair with the cushion Mister Gage had brought her once without a word, in her dark green coat, with her gloves on. She had not slept. She had not tried.
The snow had come down all night. She had watched it. Thick and soft and straight, with no wind, out of a black sky, onto Lantern Quay. Onto the lamps. Onto the barges moored along the stone. Onto the black water of the river, where it lay for a moment, white, and then went dark, and sank. It had covered the ash. All of it. The gray that had lain on everything since Midwinter night was gone under it. The quay was white again. The roofs across the river were white. The lamps of Lantern Bridge, far off to her left, burning blue, had little white caps of snow on them, like old men in nightcaps.
And now it had stopped.
She had seen it stop. All at once. The air outside the glass full of falling white, and then not. Empty. Still. And the sky behind it, over the roofs on the other side of the river, not black any more but gray. A pale cold gray, getting paler at the edge. To the north and east.
She looked north.
There was nothing to see. The roofs of the Old Town, white, going up the hill. The flat square shape of the Ministry of Records at the top, with its rows of dark windows. And behind it, higher, on the very top of the Hill, the Spire. A white needle against the gray, very thin, very tall. And beyond that, nothing. Sky. Gray. The north.
She did not know why she was looking at it.
On the little table beside her chair there was a paper.
It had come up at the sixth bell on Thursday, in the Bank's internal post, in a pale green envelope with the Ministry's seal on the back. It came every Thursday. Every Thursday for six years. The Fourth Desk received a copy, for the record, because the Charter said so, and nobody had ever thought to ask why. Mister Harrow opened it, and read it, and filed it in the cupboard with the brass 9 on its door, and she had never once in six years looked at it.
She had looked at it last night.
She picked it up again now. In the gray light from the window. And read it again.
THE MINISTRY OF RECORDS. LIST OF PERSONS SOUGHT. THURSDAY.
And under that, in two columns, names. Thirty or forty of them. Debtors. A forger. Two men from the Lower Docks who had run from the Friday cart. A woman who had sold a memory twice. The usual names. She did not know any of them.
Except the first.
It was new. It had not been on the list last Thursday. She had gone to the cupboard with the brass 9 on its door at the ninth bell last night and taken out last Thursday's, and the Thursday before, and the Thursday before that, all the way back to the autumn, and laid them side by side on her desk under the brass lamp, and read them all. It had not been on any of them. Not since the autumn. Not since the week after he ran, when it had been on every list for a month, and then, one Thursday, without a word, it had not been.
It was on this one.
At the top. First. Alone. Above the two columns. In the small, upright, careful hand that signed every list. A hand she knew.
VANE, CORIN. Harvester, formerly of this Ministry. Lame in the left leg. Gray to the shoulder. To be held on sight. By order of the Inquisitor. S. K.
She read it three times.
Then she put it down on the table. Face down.
And took out her black book.
She opened it to the last page with writing on it. Her own small neat hand. From Tuesday. Ferrule Street. Room six. Hale, L. Emptied in the night. And the count. Running total: 2,410 days. And the lines from the old man's page, before they went. Ask the giant. He was there. At the end, in the valley. He carried her out. The little one.
And between the last two pages, folded small, very small, a blank sheet of paper with a circle drawn in the corner, folded away inside where nobody could see it.
She did not take it out.
She took out her pencil, and wrote, under the last line:
Friday. Before dawn. The Thursday list. VANE, CORIN. First. Added. By S. K.
The snow has stopped.
She looked at it.
Behind her, in the dark of the long room, the bench by the door creaked.
She did not turn round.
She heard him get up. In stages. The way a very big man gets up. And come down the long room toward her, between the desks, past Mister Harrow's empty chair, past the black cupboards with their brass numbers. Past cupboard eleven, where a boy was asleep in the dark under the shelves with seven leather books and a penny notebook in his lap. Mister Gage's great soft boots made no sound on the boards. He stopped beside her chair.
And held the slate down beside her, by the window, in the gray light.
She looked at it.
He's gone.
The Auditor sat very still.
"Where?" she said.
The chalk did not move for a long time.
Then it scratched on the slate. Slowly.
North. Last night. By the dyke road. In the snow.
And under it:
To the Glass.
She looked at the word for a long time.
She knew the Glass. Everybody did. Everybody in Veridia had grown up knowing it. The great dead waste in the north, beyond the hills, where nothing grew, and nothing lived, and nothing could be burned, and nothing could be taken. Where a general had stood at the end of a war, sixty years ago, and burned the sun out of the sky. There were no ledgers in the Glass. No vaults. No Fourth Desk. Nothing to audit.
"How do you know?" she said.
Gage wiped the slate.
And did not write anything for a long moment.
Then he wrote:
Because it's where I'd have gone.
The Auditor looked up at him.
At the plain wooden face, very high above her in the gray. At the small gray eyes. He was not looking at her. He was looking out of the window. North. Over the white roofs and the Ministry and the Spire. At the gray.
She had never once seen him look at anything like that.
"You've been there," she said.
He did not answer.
"You've been to the Glass."
He did not answer that either.
She looked at him for a long time.
Then she turned back to the window. And looked north too. The two of them. At the gray sky over the white city, getting paler at the edge.
And as she looked, she saw something on the sill.
Outside. On the stone ledge, on the other side of the glass. In the snow. Where the snow had been falling all night, and had settled, smooth and white and unmarked, an inch deep.
Not unmarked.
There was a small hollow in the snow, in the middle of the sill. As if something had been laid there, very lightly, after the snow began, and before it stopped. And the snow had fallen on it, and round it, but not much. Not enough to cover it.
She stood up.
She unlatched the window. It had not been opened since the autumn. It stuck. Gage put one great hand flat on the frame above her head and pushed, and it gave, and swung outward, and the cold came in. Clean cold. Snow cold. With a smell of the river in it, and of smoke from a thousand chimneys, and nothing else. No ash.
She reached out, with her gloved hand, into the snow on the sill.
And picked it up.
It was a sweet paper.
Small. White, once. Gone soft and damp with the snow. The kind of paper a peppermint comes wrapped in, from the cut-glass bowl on a desk at the Office of Public Health, or a round silver box on a stair at Midwinter. Folded.
Folded very small.
She stood at the open window in the cold, in the gray light, with the snow-damp paper on her gloved palm, and counted the folds.
She could not help it. She was an Auditor. She counted things.
Eleven.
She stood very still.
In the inside pocket of her dark green coat, against her ribs, with her black book, there were two other papers. She had carried them since the autumn. Since the morning she went to an empty room on Tallow Street, a room that had been a harvester's, and stood by his bed and laid her bare hand on the wall and read three words scratched there. CORIN VANE. OAKHAVEN. ELARA. And then turned, and seen on the windowsill of that room, laid side by side, very neatly, as if somebody had wanted them found, two folded barley-sugar wrappers.
She had taken them. She did not know why. Her hand had done it.
She had never counted their folds.
She took them out now. With her other hand. Out of her coat. And held them on her palm, beside the peppermint paper from the sill. Three small folded squares, in the gray light, on her white kid glove.
She counted.
Eleven.
Eleven.
Eleven.
The Auditor of the Fourth Desk stood at her open window on the third floor of the Oblivion Bank, in the cold, at dawn, with three small papers on her palm, each folded eleven times, and did not move for a long time.
The Inquisitor's orders. She's not to be held.
VANE, CORIN. To be held on sight. By order of the Inquisitor. S. K.
Somebody neat.
She closed her hand over the three papers.
And put them back in her coat, against her ribs, with the black book and the blank page folded small.
And shut the window. And latched it. And sat down again in her hard wooden chair with the cushion, in the gray light, and folded her gloved hands in her lap.
Gage had not moved. He was standing beside her chair, very big and very still. Looking at her now. Not at the north. At her.
She did not ask him.
She did not ask about the papers, or the folds, or the Inquisitor, or the valley, or the little one he had carried out. She did not ask who had called her Ellie in a field of long wet grass. She did not ask what she had given away for love six winters ago, or to whom.
She had a hundred questions. She had had them for six years.
She was not afraid of them any more. That was the strange thing. She noticed it, sitting there. The way you notice that a tooth has stopped aching. She was not afraid of what he would write.
She was only waiting.
"Not yet," she said. To the window. To the north. To the gray coming up over the white roofs.
And beside her, Mister Gage lifted his slate, and wrote, slowly, with the chalk squeaking a little in the quiet of the long room. And held it down beside her, by the window, in the first real light of the morning. So that she could read it.
Not yet.
And under it, smaller, pressed so hard the chalk broke at the end:
But soon.
The Auditor read it.
And then, for the first time in six years, at her window on Lantern Quay, with the snow lying white on the whole of the city and the lamps of the bridge still burning blue in their little white nightcaps, Elara smiled. Not the smile that showed her gold tooth over the gap, that people looked at.
A different one. Smaller. That she did not know she had.
Outside, very far off, over the river, over the roofs, over the Hill and the Spire and the white fields beyond them, the sun came up.
North and east. Over the reeds. Over the hills.
Over the Glass.
