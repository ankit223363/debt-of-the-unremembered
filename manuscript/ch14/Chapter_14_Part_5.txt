Chapter 14: Thaddeus Rook, Part 5 of 5: The Clock Strikes
The Clock Strikes
There was no time to talk after that. There was only time to move.
Rook moved first. For a man so old and so bent, he was very quick. He went to the bench and swept the little silver mouse into one pocket of his dressing gown, and held out his other hand, palm up, toward the window. The brass sparrow stopped beating against the glass at once. She flew to him across the white room, a quick bright flicker of copper, and landed on his fingers, and he tucked her gently into the other pocket and buttoned the flap over her. Then he took a roll of soft gray cloth from under the bench and unrolled it on the boards. It was full of pockets. He began to fill them with tools, quickly, without looking, his shaking hands going straight to each one as if they knew the way in the dark. The smallest tweezers. The finest files. Three little screwdrivers. A lens.
"Faber," he said, without turning round. "The brake."
Faber did not move. He was standing by the stove with his hands at his sides, and his thin face was white.
"Master. If you start her—everyone will hear. The whole city. Everyone will look up at the Tide Hall and know that somebody's—"
"Yes," said Rook. "Everyone. That's rather the point, my boy. A great many people looking up at the Tide Hall at once, all asking what's happened, is a great deal harder to make disappear than one old man." He rolled up the cloth and tied it with a string and pushed it inside his coat. "Besides. I want to hear her. Just once. Even if I can't hear her the way I should." He turned round. "The brake, Faber."
Faber looked at him for one more moment. Then he went to the square hole in the floor and down the iron ladder, fast, into the room of the great still wheels.
Far below them, down the stair in the wall, there was a sound. A boot, on stone. And a voice, small with distance, echoing up the hollow tower.
"—up here, I tell you, he said up—"
Lyra had her sword out.
Corin had not seen her draw it. He never did. One moment her hand was on the hilt under her coat, and the next moment the blade was there, long and thin and plain, catching the white light from the clock face along its whole edge like a line of frost. She went to the hole in the floor and stood beside it, looking down the ladder, with the sword held low at her side.
"Mister Rook," she said. Not loudly. "How do we get out?"
"Through her face," said Rook. "At the six. There's a hatch. For cleaning the glass." He pointed with one long shaking finger at the bottom of the great milky circle, where the backward six stood, black and tall, and under it, in the iron frame, there was a small square door with a ring for a handle, no bigger than the door of an oven. "There's a walk outside. An iron walk, all round the face. And a ladder at the corner, down to the roof of the Hall. And from the roof there's a way down to the market at the back. I came up that way once, the first night, when the tide was wrong for Faber's boat." He paused. "It's very high. And it will be very slippery. And I don't think either of us should look down."
"I won't," said Juno at once. "I never do."
From the room below, Faber's voice came up the ladder. High and thin and shaking.
"Master! It's stiff! It hasn't moved in thirty years, it's rusted, I can't—"
"Then lean on it, boy," said Rook. "Lean on it the way I taught you to lean on a stuck mainspring. Not hard. Steady. Put your weight on it and wait. She'll come. She always comes, if you're patient with her."
And he went down the ladder himself, with his white hair and his long legs and his plum-colored dressing gown, as fast as a boy.
Corin went after him. He did not know why. Lyra stayed at the top, by the hole, with her sword. Juno stayed by the hatch. Minim the brass dog went down the ladder beside Corin, somehow, his short brass legs going tick tick tick on the iron rungs, as if ladders were nothing to him at all.
The brake was a lever.
It stood up out of the floor at the side of the great iron frame, as tall as a man, a thick black iron bar with a worn brass handle on the top, and it was pulled all the way back, hard against a stop, and held there by an iron pin as thick as Corin's thumb. Faber was hanging off the handle with his whole weight. His feet were off the floor. His thin face was red and his teeth were set and the lever was not moving at all.
Rook did not hurry. He went to the lever, and put one long hand on it, beside Faber's, and stood there for a moment with his head on one side, his white hair standing up, as if he were listening to something nobody else could hear. Then he took the iron pin in his other hand, and wiggled it, very gently. Back and forth. Back and forth. And slowly, with a long thin squeal of old rust, the pin came out.
The lever did not move.
Then it did.
It moved all at once, with a great shuddering clunk that Corin felt in his teeth, and Faber fell off it and sat down hard on the stone floor, and the lever swung forward on its pivot and stopped, and for a moment nothing else happened at all.
And then, high up in the dark of the tower, something groaned.
It was a deep sound. Very deep. Lower than any sound Corin had ever heard a man make, or an animal. It came down out of the dark over their heads like the creak of a great ship's timbers in a storm. And as it came, the chains began to move.
The great chains, hanging down from the frame, gray with thirty years of dust. They shivered. Dust fell off them in long soft streams, like smoke. And the great round iron weights at the bottom of them, hanging down through the holes in the floor into the dark below, began, very, very slowly, to sink.
And as they sank, the wheels began to turn.
Not fast. So slowly at first that Corin was not sure he was seeing it. The biggest wheel, the mill wheel at the bottom, as tall as a cart, moved one tooth. Just one. With a heavy iron chock that shook the floor. Then it stopped. Then it moved another. Chock. And as it moved, the wheel beside it moved, and the wheel above that one, and the one beside that, and the small ones behind them, all the way up the great iron frame, tooth to tooth, wheel to wheel, like a long row of people waking up one after another in a dark house. Chock. Chock-chock. Chock. Dust poured off them. Old cobwebs tore and drifted down through the gray light in long ghostly strands. A pigeon that had been asleep somewhere in the frame burst out of it in a clatter of wings and went flapping wildly round the room and out through one of the narrow windows.
"The pendulum," said Rook softly. "Give her a push, Faber. Just a little one. To remind her."
Faber got up off the floor. He went to the great round brass disc at the bottom of the pendulum, hanging still just clear of the stone. He put both his small gloved hands flat on it. And he pushed. Gently. Steadily. The way you would push a child on a swing.
The disc moved. An inch. Two. It swung away from him, slow and heavy, into the gray air of the room, and hung there for a moment at the end of its swing, and came back.
Faber pushed it again. A little harder.
It went further. It came back. He pushed. It went further still. And now Corin could see the whole great rod swinging, from the frame high above all the way down to the disc, slow and wide, like the arm of a giant stirring a pot. And with every swing, somewhere deep inside the frame, something went TOCK.
TOCK.
A sound like a hammer on an anvil. Like a door slamming in an empty church.
TOCK.
The whole tower shook with it. Corin felt it come up through the soles of his boots and into his knees and his chest and his teeth.
TOCK.
The pendulum swung out, wide and slow, across the far corner of the room. Across the arched opening in the wall, where the stair came out. Corin saw it swing across the dark mouth of the stair, filling it, the great brass disc going past the opening like the blade of a scythe, and then swing away again, and then come back. Across the opening. And away. And back.
And on the stair, just inside that dark arch, a man in a white coat was standing frozen with one foot raised, staring at the huge brass disc as it swept past an arm's length from his face.
He was young. Corin saw that in the moment the pendulum was away. Young and red-faced from the climb, with his mouth open and a short club in his hand. He stared at the pendulum. The pendulum came back, and filled the arch, and swept past him with a great soft whoosh of moving air that blew his hair back off his forehead. And went away again.
The young man in the white coat turned round on the stair without a word and went back down it, very fast.
From below, much further down, there came a roar. A big, deep, angry voice, all beer and onions, bellowing up the hollow tower. "What's that? What's that row? Get UP there, you useless—"
"She'll keep them out," said Rook. "For as long as she's going. Nobody comes through that arch while she's swinging, not unless they want to be swept down two hundred steps." He looked up at the great turning wheels, with the dust still pouring off them, and his face in the gray light was the face of a man looking at someone he had loved a long time ago and had not seen since. "Hello, old girl," he said softly. "Hello. Do you remember me?"
The wheels turned. Chock. Chock-chock. The pendulum swung. TOCK.
Rook turned to Faber.
"Go with them," he said. "Now."
"Master—"
"Now, Faber." The old man's voice was quite gentle. "You can't come where I'm going. You'd never fit. You were always too big for the inside of a clock." He took Faber's thin face in both his long shaking hands, very briefly, the way a father does. "Go home. Open your shop. Mend kitchen clocks. Tell everyone I'm dead." Something that was almost a smile moved his long thin mouth. "You're so good at it."
They went back up the ladder. Rook came last.
At the top, in the white room, he did not go to the hatch. He went to the bench. He stood in front of the row of brass beetles along the back of it, green and blue and red and gold and purple in the strange shadowless light, and he looked at them. Then he put out his hand, and picked up one of them. The green one. A bright green, like new leaves.
He took a tiny brass key out of his pocket, fitted it into a hole in the beetle's side, and turned it. Four times. Tick, tick, tick, tick.
Then he turned round, and held it out to Lyra.
"You had a green one first," he said. "When you were four. Do you remember? You named it Leaf."
Lyra looked at the beetle in his palm. Its six thin brass legs were moving, very slowly, walking in the air. Its two fine feelers curled and uncurled.
She did not say she remembered. She did not say anything. She held out her hand, and he put the beetle into it, and its little brass feet walked on her palm, tickling, tik tik tik tik, round and round in a small circle, the way it would have walked on a nursery table, long ago.
Her face did something then. Just for a moment. Corin looked away.
"And this," said Rook, "is yours."
He put the blue stone back into her other hand. And as it left his fingers, his hand began to shake again, at once, the way a leaf begins to shake when the wind comes back.
"Keep it close," he said. "Keep it hidden. Whatever it is, whatever Edmund put in it, he meant it for a Thorne. And he meant it for someone who wasn't born yet." He looked at her through all his lenses. "You weren't born yet, my dear. When he said it."
Then he turned away from her, quickly, and bent, and picked up the brass dog.
Minim did not struggle. He let himself be lifted, his short legs dangling, his green glass eyes on Lyra. Rook tucked him under one long arm against his dressing gown, like a man carrying a loaf.
"Come with us," said Lyra. Her voice was rough. "Please. Mister Rook. Come with us. There's room. There's Vespera's, and my rooms, and—"
"No," said Rook. "No, my dear. I'd only slow you down. And they'll be watching you now. All of you. More than ever." He shook his white head. "I have a way of my own. There are more clocks in this city than anybody knows, and I know the insides of all of them. Minim and I will be quite all right." He looked at Faber. "When it's safe, I'll stop a clock at twelve. Somewhere you can see it. You'll know."
He looked at the fish, going round and round in its bowl on the windowsill.
"Goodbye, old fellow," he said to it. "I'm sorry. You'll have to swim on your own for a while."
And he went to the far wall, where the great iron beam that held up the roof met the corner of the room, and put his hand on the wall, and pressed something. A panel swung open. Behind it was dark, and the smell of oil, and the sound of the great wheels turning. Chock. Chock-chock.
He stepped into the dark, with Minim under his arm, and his white hair the last of him to go.
The panel swung shut.
The hatch under the backward six was stiff, and Corin and Juno had to pull it together. It opened with a groan, and the cold came in through it like a knife.
Outside, there was the sky.
That was all, for the first moment. Sky. White and enormous and very close, full of slowly falling snow. And under it, a narrow iron walk, no wider than a plank, with a thin iron rail along its outer edge, running away round the curve of the great glass face. It was covered in snow. Thick, smooth, untouched snow, with a crust of ice on top of it that glittered.
Juno went out first. On her hands and knees. Then Faber, gray-faced, with his eyes shut. Then Lyra, with the beetle in one pocket and the stone round her neck again on its bootlace and her sword back in its scabbard. Then Corin.
He did not mean to look down. He looked down.
The whole city lay below him, white.
It went away on every side, roofs and roofs and roofs, white and gray and soft, under the falling snow, with the chimneys standing up out of them like the stalks of plants and the thin gray threads of smoke going straight up from a thousand of them into the still air. The river lay in the middle of it, black, winding, steaming, with its plates of ice turning slowly on the current. And there, upstream, the Lantern Bridge, low and dark, with its row of small blue lights. And beyond the bridge, on the far bank, the hills of the city going up and up, the Ink Quarter, the docks, the Hill itself, at the very top, gray and grand and far away, with the great dark shape of the Archon's temple standing on its crown.
And right beside him, so close he could have touched it, rising up out of the snow on the walk, was the bottom of the six. A great black iron numeral, taller than he was, rough and cold and furred with frost.
And above it, very high, the hands.
They were moving.
He could see them move. That was the strange thing. The long one, the minute hand, black iron as long as the mast of a ship, standing straight up at the twelve. And as he watched, with the snow falling past his face, it moved. A tiny jerk. TOCK. It shivered there at the top, as if it were gathering itself for something.
"Mister V," said Juno. Far ahead along the walk. "Come on. Come on."
He went on. On his hands and knees, along the narrow iron walk, with the snow cold through his trousers and the ice cracking under his palms and the great glass face curving up on his right like the side of the moon.
They were halfway to the ladder at the corner when the clock struck.
Corin did not hear the first stroke. He felt it. It came out of the tower behind him, out of the stone and the iron and the glass, all at once, a sound so huge and so deep that it was not like a sound at all. It was like the sky cracking. It went through him from his boots to the top of his head and shook every bone in his body, and the iron walk shook under him, and the great glass face beside him shook and hummed, and all along the edge of the walk, and the ledges of the tower, and the heads of the pillars far below, the snow jumped. It leaped off every edge at once, in long white sheets, and fell, and the icicles under the walk broke off and went down in a glittering shower, turning over and over, all the long way down to the roof of the Hall.
BONG.
And from every roof of the city round them, pigeons rose.
Thousands of them. Gray and white and blue. They went up into the falling snow from every chimney and gutter and ledge in a great soft roaring cloud of wings, and wheeled, and wheeled again.
BONG.
The second stroke. He was ready for it this time. It shook him anyway. He held onto the iron rail with both hands and shut his eyes.
BONG.
Old Tide. That was its name. He did not know how he knew. It came up out of somewhere very deep, from a part of him that had nothing to do with the Ledger. Old Tide. The great bell of the Tide Hall, that rang the hours for the ships. Somebody had told him that, once. Somebody had held him up on their shoulders, once, on a riverbank, to see it, and said its name.
And then it was gone again. As quickly as it had come.
BONG.
Across the whole white city, Corin knew, people were stopping. In the streets. In the shops. In the market. At their windows. Stopping, and turning, and looking up through the falling snow at the tall gray tower on the far bank of the river, where the clock that had not struck in thirty years was striking now.
BONG.
He opened his eyes. Lyra was ahead of him on the walk, kneeling, looking back. Her face was white and her eyes were very wide, and the snow was in her hair.
"Twelve," she said, over the stroke. "It thinks it's twelve."
BONG.
"It is twelve," said Corin. "For it. It's only just woken up."
They reached the ladder on the seventh stroke. It went down the corner of the tower, iron rungs set into the stone, crusted with ice, to the long sloping roof of the Hall below, all white. Juno went down it like water going down a drain. Faber went down slowly, with his eyes shut, feeling for each rung with his feet. Then Lyra.
Corin went last. On the twelfth stroke, with his hands on the frozen rungs and his face against the cold stone, he felt the tower stop shaking. Slowly. The great deep hum of the last stroke going on and on, inside the stone, inside him, fading, fading, like ripples spreading out across a pond.
And in the silence after it, very faint, from inside the tower, through the stone, he heard the TOCK. Steady. Slow. Patient.
Old Tide was going again.
And somewhere inside her, among the great turning wheels, in the dark, there was an old man with white hair and a brass dog under his arm, who could not hear her as music. Who had never, in his life, heard her strike.
Corin hoped he had felt it, at least. He hoped it had shaken his bones.
The Lantern Bridge
The roof of the Tide Hall was lead, under the snow, and it sloped.
That was the first thing Corin found out, when he stepped off the bottom of the ladder. His boot went into the snow and the snow went crunch, and then, under the crunch, his boot found the lead, and the lead was ice, and his foot went out from under him, and he sat down hard and began to slide.
He slid a long way. Down the slope of the roof, on his back, in a spray of snow, faster and faster, toward the edge, where the roof stopped and there was nothing but white air and the market three floors below. He grabbed at the snow. It came away in his hands. He grabbed again.
A hand caught his collar.
It jerked him to a stop so hard that his coat cut into his throat and he choked. He lay there, flat on his back in the snow, with his boots a yard from the edge of the roof and his heart going like a drum, and looked up.
Lyra was lying on her front above him on the slope, with one hand gripping the iron rim of a skylight and the other gripping his collar. Her face was red with cold and her short black hair was full of snow and she was breathing hard through her teeth.
"Nobody falls," she said, "on the Tide Hall roof. Except you."
From somewhere along the roof to their left, Juno laughed. A short, breathless, frightened laugh.
Corin did not laugh. He got his heels into the snow, and his hands, and turned over, very carefully, and crawled back up the slope to the skylight on his hands and knees. Lyra let go of his collar.
"Thank you," he said.
"Write it down," said Lyra. "In your book. So you remember you owe me."
"I'll remember."
"You won't." But something moved at the corner of her mouth. "That's why you should write it down."
The way down was at the back of the building, where the Tide Hall stood against the market. Juno found it. A great square iron drainpipe, thick as a tree trunk, going down the back wall, with iron brackets every few feet bolting it to the stone. The brackets made steps. Icy steps, a long way apart. Juno went down them like a squirrel. Lyra went down them like a soldier, steady, one at a time, never looking anywhere but at the next one. Faber went down very slowly, with his face against the pipe and his lips moving. Corin went last again, and slipped twice, and caught himself both times, and did not fall, and when his boots touched the cobbles of the lane at the bottom he stood there with his hands still on the pipe for a long moment, shaking, until Juno pulled his sleeve.
The lane at the back of the Tide Hall opened straight into the fish market.
Corin had never smelled anything like it. Even in the frozen air, even with the snow on everything, it smelled. Salt and weed and scales and cold blood and old wet wood and tar. It was a long, wide, covered market, under a high roof of glass and iron, and the glass was white with snow, so that the light under it was dim and gray and greenish, like the light at the bottom of the sea. Down both sides, and in long rows down the middle, there were stalls. Great stone slabs on iron legs, and on the slabs, packed in ice, in rows, there were fish. Silver fish and gray fish and fish with spots and fish with long ugly faces and whiskers. Crabs, red and blue, in heaps, with their claws tied. Eels, coiled in tubs. Mussels, black, in sacks. And oysters, rough gray stones in barrels, with sawdust between them.
There were people. Not many, because of the snow, but some. Women in long canvas aprons and men's boots, with their sleeves rolled up over red raw arms, shouting prices. A boy with a barrow. An old man with a pipe, sitting on an upturned crate, gutting herring with a little curved knife so fast that his hands were a blur. And every one of them had stopped what they were doing. Every one of them was standing quite still, with a fish or a knife or a coin in their hand, looking up. Through the snowy glass roof. Toward the tower of the Tide Hall, where the great deep TOCK, TOCK, TOCK was coming down through the stone and the glass and the frozen air, slow and steady, like a heart.
"It struck," said one of the fishwives. To nobody. In a wondering voice, like a child. "Did you hear it, Bet? It struck. Old Tide struck."
"My gran used to talk about it," said another. "She said you could hear it in Saltmarsh, on a still day."
"Who started it?"
Nobody knew. Nobody looked at the four people who came out of the lane at the back of the market, covered in snow, and went down the long middle row between the slabs of fish, fast, not running, with their heads down. Nobody looked at anything except the tower.
Nobody except one.
He was standing at the far end of the market, at the great arched entrance that opened onto the riverfront road. A young man in a white coat. Red-faced. Out of breath. With a short club in his hand.
The same young man who had stood on the stair and stared at the pendulum.
He saw them. Corin saw him see them. His mouth fell open. And then he turned his head and shouted, back over his shoulder, out into the road: "Here! They're here! In the market!"
"Back," said Juno. "Back, back, the side way—"
They ran.
Juno led them sideways, between two stalls, under a slab, through a curtain of hanging oilskins that slapped wetly in their faces, and out through a narrow side door into the cold white daylight of a lane that ran along the river. Behind them, Corin heard shouting. A fishwife's voice, furious. A crash, as somebody knocked over a barrel of oysters. Boots, on stone.
The lane ran upstream, along the river wall, toward the bridge. There was no other way. On one side the high blank backs of warehouses. On the other side the river wall, waist high, and over it the black water, steaming. They ran along it in the snow. Juno in front, fast and light, her big coat flapping. Then Lyra. Then Faber, gasping, his gloved hands pressed to his chest. Then Corin.
And ahead of them, getting closer with every step, the Lantern Bridge.
It rose up out of the snow in front of them, long and low and dark, its stone arches striding away across the black river, one after another, toward the far bank. The tall iron lamp-posts stood along its top at every pier, with their small blue lanterns burning. There were steps up to it from the lane. Broad stone steps, white with snow.
And at the side of the steps, going down instead of up, under the first dark arch, there was another flight of steps. Narrow and steep and black with slime, going down to the water. To a landing.
Lyra stopped.
She stopped at the top of those narrow black steps. Just for a moment. Corin, running up behind her, nearly went into her back. She stood quite still, with the snow falling on her, and looked down. Down the slimy steps, into the dark under the arch. At a flat stone landing at the bottom, at the edge of the black water, with an iron ring set into it, and a pile of old broken crates against the wall, and nothing else at all.
No lantern. No small round man on his knees. Nothing.
She looked at it for the time it takes to breathe in, and out.
Then she turned round and went up the broad white steps to the bridge, two at a time, without a word, and Corin went after her.
They were a third of the way across when the white coats caught them.
There were three of them. They came up the steps from the lane behind, at a run, the young red-faced one in front with his club, and two bigger ones behind him, older, harder, with their white coats open and their breath steaming. They came out onto the bridge and saw the four of them ahead, and the young one shouted again, and they came on, fast, down the long white middle of the bridge between the blue lanterns.
And from under the bridge, from the river, there came a voice. A big, deep voice, all beer and onions, booming up from the water.
"Don't hurt the girl! She's not to be hurt! Her Ladyship said!"
Corin looked over the parapet.
Below, on the black water, a long black boat was sliding out from under the arch. Four oarsmen in white coats, rowing hard. And in the front of it, standing up, with his legs apart for balance and his great red hands on his hips and a half-eaten pie in one of them, was a huge man in a brown coat. A man with a face like a side of beef and small, pale, cheerful eyes. Gorse.
He saw Corin looking down at him. And he grinned, and lifted the pie, as if in a toast.
And hummed. Three notes. Loud and tuneless and cheerful, booming up off the water.
Mm, mm, mmm.
Corin's whole body went cold.
"Mister V!" Juno, ahead, her voice cracking. "Mister V!"
He turned round.
The young white coat was on them. He had come down the bridge faster than the other two, so fast that he was ten yards ahead of them, and he came at Lyra with his club up, because she was the nearest, and because she was a girl, and because he was young and frightened and had not been told anything about her except that she was not to be hurt.
Lyra did not draw her sword.
She stepped to one side. That was all. A small step, quick and neat, like a step in a dance. The club came down where she had been and hit nothing, and the young man's own weight carried him forward, and as he went past her she put out one foot, and he tripped over it, and went down on his face in the snow with a grunt, and slid. And before he had stopped sliding she was standing over him with her boot on the wrist of the hand that held the club, and the club was in her own hand, and the end of it was resting lightly against the back of his neck.
"Stay down," she said. Not loudly. "Please."
He stayed down.
The other two had stopped. Ten yards off, in the middle of the bridge, between two blue lanterns. They were looking at Lyra. At the young man on his face in the snow. At the club. They looked at each other. And Corin saw them decide.
They came on together. Slower now. Careful. Spreading apart, one to the left and one to the right, the way men do who have done this before. The bigger one had a short iron bar in his fist. The other one had nothing in his hands at all, and that was worse, because he was pulling off his gloves as he came, and Corin could see that the backs of his hands were covered in fine blue writing.
A burner. Somebody who did not need a club.
Lyra saw it too. She let go of the club. Her hand went to her sword.
Something small, boy, said Malakor. Suddenly. Sharp and close inside Corin's coat. Something from today. Quickly.
Corin did not stop to think. If he had stopped to think, he would not have done it.
He reached inside himself. The way Vespera had taught him. The way the Ledger said he had done a hundred times before and forgotten every one. He reached in, past the cold and the fear and the pounding of his heart, for something small. Something new. Something that had not had time to grow roots.
And he found it.
A bowl of stew. Hot in his hands, in a dark passage that smelled of onions. Brown and thick, with lumps of potato, and bits of onion floating, and a heel of bread stuck in the side of it like a spoon. And the taste of it. That was what he took. Not the bowl. Not the passage. Not the small fierce old woman in the purple shawl watching him eat it like a hen watching a chick. Just the taste. The heat and the salt and the onion and the soft potato, going down, warming him all the way, on the coldest morning of his life.
He took it, and he burned it.
The light came out of his hands.
It was white. Pure white. Whiter than the snow. It burst out of his two open palms in a great silent flash, like a sheet of lightning, and filled the whole middle of the bridge, from parapet to parapet. It lit up every flake of snow in the air, every one, so that for one long moment they hung there all round him, frozen, blazing, like a million tiny stars. It lit the white coats of the two men, and their faces, and their wide shocked eyes, and the fine blue writing on the burner's hands.
And then it was gone.
The two men were on their knees in the snow, with their hands over their faces. The bigger one had dropped his iron bar. The burner was swearing, over and over, in a high frightened voice. I can't see. I can't see. I can't—
"Run," said Corin.
They ran.
They ran the length of the Lantern Bridge, under the blue lanterns, through the falling snow, the four of them. Past the last lamp-post, and the plaque on it where a king's name had been chiseled smooth out of the stone, and Corin did not look at it. Down the broad steps at the far end. Into the riverfront road. And Juno took them left, and right, and up, and through, into the narrow crooked lanes of the Ink Quarter, where the houses leaned together over their heads and the snow lay deep and soft and unbroken and nobody was looking up at the Tide Hall at all.
Behind them, from the river, for a long time, they could hear Gorse shouting.
And under the shouting, faint and steady, from the far bank, TOCK. TOCK. TOCK.
They stopped at last in a little covered passage between two printing shops, where the snow had not reached, and the air smelled of hot ink and oil. Faber sat down on the cobbles with his back against the wall and put his head on his knees. Juno leaned against the wall beside him, panting, with her cap in her hand. Lyra stood in the mouth of the passage, looking back the way they had come, with her hand on her sword.
Corin stood in the middle of the passage, and breathed.
His hands were tingling. The palms of them, where the light had come out. They felt the way your hands feel when you have held them too close to a fire. He looked down at them. There was nothing there. They looked like ordinary hands.
He tried to remember the stew.
He could remember the bowl. He could remember the passage, and the smell of onions, and the purple shawl, and the bread stuck in the side like a spoon. He could remember that it had been hot. That he had been glad of it.
But when he reached for the taste of it, there was nothing there. A smooth blank place, like the place where a tooth has been. He knew he had tasted it. He knew it had been good. He could not, for anything in the world, have told you what it had tasted like.
A good choice, said Malakor quietly. Small. New. It will not be missed.
"She'll make it again," said Corin. "Tomorrow. Mrs. Marta. She always makes it."
Then you will taste it again, said Malakor. For the first time. A pause. That is the only good thing about what you are, boy. Everything can be for the first time.
Lyra had turned round from the mouth of the passage. She was looking at him.
"What did you burn?" she said.
Corin thought about it.
"Breakfast," he said.
Gloves
Vespera did not ask any questions.
They came to her by the back way, the way the Ledger said Corin had come a great many times before: down a crooked flight of steps behind the printing shops, along a lane so narrow that the snow had only fallen into it in a thin white line down the middle, through a yard full of broken barrels, and in at the back door of Faber's shop, where Faber stopped, and leaned against the wall in his own dark workroom, and shut his eyes, and did not move for a long time. Then through a low door in the side wall that Corin had not known was there. And into warmth, and lamplight, and the smell of hot ink and herbs.
The back room of Vespera's shop was low and dark and very full. Hundreds of needles hung from the beams of the ceiling on fine threads, every size, from needles as fine as a hair to needles as long as a finger, and when the door swung shut behind them the draft moved through the room and set them all turning, so that the whole ceiling chimed, very softly, like rain on glass. There was a stove, red at the seams. There was a long table covered with tiny pots of ink, and cloths, and trays. And there was a big old leather chair beside the stove, cracked and worn, with one of its arms worn right through to the stuffing.
Vespera was standing in the middle of the room with her arms folded.
She was not tall. She was round and solid and square, like a well-made chest of drawers, with broad shoulders and strong forearms and a plain, wide, weather-brown face. Her sleeves were rolled up. And her hands and her forearms, all the way up to the elbow, were covered in writing. Fine black writing, and blue, and here and there a line of red or green, so small and so close that from a little way off her arms looked gray, like a stone wall covered in lichen.
She looked at them. At Juno, white and shivering, with her cap in her hand. At Faber in the doorway behind them, with his eyes shut. At Lyra, with snow in her hair and her hand still resting on her sword. At Corin.
At Corin's hands.
"Sit," she said to him. "In your chair. Before you fall down." She jerked her chin at the others. "Thorne girl, there's tea on the stove. Juno, get by the fire and take those boots off, your feet are wet through, I can see it from here. Faber." Her voice changed, very slightly. "Come in and shut the door, love. Come and sit. You're letting the cold in."
They did what she said. Everybody did, the Ledger said. Even the Ministry.
Corin sat in the leather chair. It knew him. That was the strangest thing. He did not remember it at all, but his body did. His back went into the hollow in the leather as if it had been made for it, and his right arm fell onto the worn place on the arm of the chair, and his head went back against the top, and all at once, without meaning to, he was so tired that he could not move.
Vespera knelt down in front of him. She took his hands, both of them, in her own writing-covered ones, and turned them over, palms up, and looked at them in the lamplight.
"Light," she said. Not a question.
"Yes."
"Small?"
"The taste of my breakfast."
She looked up at him for a moment with her small, dark, steady eyes. Then she nodded, once, as if he had passed a test. And she let go of his hands, and got up, and went to the stove, and that was all she said about it.
The whole city was talking about Old Tide.
Vespera had heard it all from the shop doorway. People had been going up and down Hook Lane for an hour, she said, stopping and gossiping in the snow, in a way they never did. Somebody said it was a sign. Somebody said it was the Ministry, testing it. Somebody said a boy had climbed the tower for a dare and fallen into the works. Somebody said it was a ghost. A man from the docks said the Watch had gone over in a boat to look, and found nothing but pigeons and a lot of dust and the great wheels turning on their own. And a woman from the fish market said that the bell had struck twelve at a quarter to one, and that was bad luck, everybody knew that was bad luck, a clock that struck the wrong hour was a clock that was calling somebody home.
"Fools," said Vespera, pouring tea. "All of them." She put a cup into Faber's hands. Then she looked at him. "Is he out?"
Faber held the cup without drinking.
"I don't know," he said. "He went into the works. Into the wheels. With Minim. He said he had a way of his own." He swallowed. "He always said that. I have a way of my own, Faber. For thirty years. I never once knew what it was."
He put the cup down on the table, very carefully, so that it made no sound. Then he got up and went to the door that led through into the front of Vespera's shop, and through the shop, and out of the front door into Hook Lane. And after a moment, Corin got up out of the leather chair and followed him.
Faber was standing at the bottom of the lane. On the little stone quay, at the edge of the black water, with the snow falling on his bald head and his fringe of gray hair, and his narrow shoulders up round his ears. He was looking across the river.
Corin came and stood beside him.
From here you could see the Tide Hall clearly, across the water. The long gray front with its row of pillars. The tall square tower. And the great round face at the top of it, pale and pearly in the gray afternoon light.
And the hands.
They were not at twelve anymore. The long one had moved round to the eight. The short one stood a little past the one. Twenty minutes to two. And as Corin watched, very slowly, the long one moved again. Not a jerk this time. Steady. Patient. The way a clock's hands move when the clock is going, and means to go on going.
If you ever look up at that clock from the other side of the river and the hands are anywhere else, anywhere at all, you're not to come. You're to go home and shut your door and forget me.
Faber stood on the quay and looked at the moving hands. He did not make a sound. His face did not move. But down his thin cheek, from his good eye, a tear ran slowly into the corner of his mouth. And from his other eye, the empty one, the eye with nobody behind it, another. Exactly the same. At exactly the same time.
Corin had not known an empty eye could cry.
"He said he'd stop a clock at twelve," he said, after a while. Quietly. "Somewhere you could see it. When it was safe."
"Yes." Faber wiped his face with the back of his glove. "Yes. He did." He drew a long breath. "There are a great many clocks in this city, Mister Vane."
"Then you'd better look at all of them."
Faber turned his head and looked at him. And after a moment, something that was almost a smile came into his thin, wet face.
"Yes," he said. "I suppose I had." He looked back across the river. "Every morning. Before I open the shop."
Corin wrote it down.
He sat in the leather chair by Vespera's stove with the Ledger open on his knee and wrote for a long time. Lyra sat on a stool at the end of the long table, with her tea going cold beside her. In front of her on the scrubbed wood, the little green beetle walked round and round in a slow circle, tik tik tik tik, its feelers curling, until it ran down and stopped. And she picked it up, and turned the tiny brass key four times, and set it down, and it walked again. She did it over and over. Vespera, at the stove, watched her do it, and said nothing.
Corin wrote:
THADDEUS ROOK. Alive. Master Gearwright. Made Lord Edmund's clock with him, 30 years ago. Hid inside the Tide Hall clock for a year. Very old. White hair. Glasses on hinges. HIS HANDS SHAKE ALL THE TIME. They stopped shaking when he held the blue stone. He underlined it twice. Gone now. Into the works. With Minim, his brass dog. He says he has a way of his own. He will stop a clock at twelve when it's safe. Faber will look.
A Gearwright burns music to give machines a kind of mind. Rook gave all his music away. He can't hear tunes now. When I hummed the three notes he said it sounded like wind in a chimney.
He keeps a book. GIVEN. So he knows where everything went. Like mine.
The clock's eleven stones are eleven of his tunes. The 12th, the white one, was EDMUND'S. Edmund said: "Something I'll never need again." Lyra remembers it. It was there till 9 years ago at least. Now gone. Where?
Edmund cut the Karst letters on the drawer himself. "It's a promise." The drawer is "for someone who isn't born yet." Said 30 years ago. LYRA WASN'T BORN YET.
CRESSIDA, aged 11 or 12, tried to pick the drawer open with a pearl hatpin. "I only wanted to see, Mister Rook." Nine years ago she visited Rook and asked about the 12th stone. A MONTH LATER, SILAS KROSS CAME.
He stopped there. He looked at what he had written for a long time. Then he went on.
SILAS KROSS. Pale. Gray gloves. Eats barley sugar. Folds the wrappers small, smaller, smaller, and puts them in his waistcoat pocket. "I do so hate untidiness." He took Faber's left eye and left ear, in front of Rook, with his bare hand, to make Rook work.
Rook built SIX GRAY MEN for him, under the Hill, from an old machine soldier dug out of the Glass Wastes. Seven feet tall. Gray coats. White faces like eggs, with blue stones for eyes. Felt in their feet. They SMELL MEMORY through walls. Ten silver needles in their hands. They do not stop.
They knock three times before they come in. Rook put it in. So people have one moment.
IF YOU EVER HEAR THREE SLOW KNOCKS AT A DOOR, CORIN, DO NOT WAIT TO SEE WHO IT IS.
He underlined that three times, so hard that the pencil nearly tore the page. Then he sat and looked at it, and the needles turned and chimed softly over his head, and he did not know why his hand was shaking.
He went on.
A year ago Kross brought Rook 40 sheets of drawings. "Not hunters this time. Something that sings." Rook understood what it was for. He will not say. He burned them all, except one corner. I have it.
CHOIR ORGAN. General Arrangement. Sheet 1 of 40. Pipes: 1,2— (burned). Closed-eye stamp. "For installation: Lvl. IV." Toby says there is no Level Four. Brightwater says there was, once. The Deep Archive.
A row of little chairs in front of the pipes. Something like a crown where each head would go.
He stopped again. This time for longer.
"Malakor," he said. Under his breath. So low that only the rod in his coat could have heard it.
The rod was silent.
"You know what it is. Don't you?"
For a long time there was nothing at all. The stove ticked. The needles chimed. The little beetle on the table walked its slow circle, tik tik tik tik, and ran down, and Lyra wound it again.
Then Malakor spoke. Very quietly. And his thin dry papery voice had something in it that Corin had never heard there before, in all the pages of the Ledger. It sounded old. Not old the way Malakor always sounded old. Old the way the scar in the stone over the Tide Hall pillars was old. Old the way a wound is old, when it has never properly healed.
I know what it was, he said. Once. A long time ago. Before your city had its name. A pause. Hide that paper, boy. Hide it well. And do not ever, ever take it below the third floor of the Ministry of Records.
"What was it for?"
Ask me another day.
"Malakor—"
Another day, said the voice in the rod. I have been dead for a very long time, Corin Vane. I would like, for one more afternoon, not to remember.
And it was silent, and stayed silent, and Corin, after a while, stopped asking.
He took the scorched blue corner out of the front of the Ledger, where he had put it in the white room. He looked at it once more in the lamplight. The pipes going up and up. The little chairs. The closed eye. Then he opened the Ledger at the very back, where the stiff brown cover was lined with thick marbled paper, and he took Vespera's smallest knife from the table, and very carefully slit the marbled paper along its bottom edge. Just a little. Just enough.
He folded the blue corner in half. And in half again. And again. Until it was very small.
Fold it small, said a voice in his head. A woman's voice. Not Malakor's. Not anyone he knew. A soft, low voice, with an accent he could not place, like water. Fold it small, and keep it close.
He slid it in behind the marbled paper, and pressed the edge flat with his thumb, and closed the book.
Then, at the bottom of the last page, he wrote:
Lyra caught me on the Tide Hall roof. I would have gone over the edge. I OWE HER.
I burned the taste of Mrs. Marta's stew. Ask her to make it again. Tell her it was the best thing I ever ate. (It probably was.)
Gorse was in the boat. He hummed the three notes. He shouted: "Don't hurt the girl! Her Ladyship said!"

At four o'clock Corin put on his gloves.
Vespera had found him a clean collar from somewhere, and brushed the worst of the snow and soot and slime off his long gray coat with a stiff brush, scowling, and wiped the dirt from his face with a wet cloth as if he were six years old. She did not ask him where he was going. She looked at the gloves, and at the note from the back of the Ledger, with its tiny drawing of an open eye, and her mouth went thin. But she did not ask.
At the front door of her shop, with her hand on the latch, she said only one thing.
"Whatever she offers you," said Vespera, "say you'll think about it. Never say yes in that house. Never say no either. Say you'll think about it, and come back here, and we'll think about it together."
She opened the door.
The snow had almost stopped. Only a few last flakes were drifting down out of the low gray sky, slow and lazy, turning over and over in the still air. The light was going. Hook Lane was blue with dusk, and the icicles along the gutters had gone the color of steel, and somewhere up at the top of the lane, on the riverfront road, a lamplighter was going along with his long pole, and the lamps were coming on one by one behind him, blue and small and steady, like the lanterns on the bridge.
Corin went up Hook Lane in the dusk. He did not fall. He went carefully, step by step, with one gloved hand on the wall. At the top he turned, and looked back, once, across the river.
The great pale face of the Tide Hall clock was glowing very faintly in the dusk, the way a moon glows before the sky is dark. Someone, somewhere inside it, had lit a lamp, or the last of the daylight was coming through the glass. He could not tell which. And the black hands stood at twenty minutes to four.
They were wrong. He knew they were wrong. Every other clock in the city said nearly half past four.
Old Tide did not seem to mind.
He turned his back on the river and walked up into the city, toward the Bourse, in his clean white gloves, through the last of the snow. And as he went, all round him, from every tower and steeple on every hill, the bells of the city began to ring the half hour. Late, he thought. He was going to be late. He began to walk faster.
And far behind him, across the black water, at the wrong time entirely, three quarters of an hour behind all the rest of the world, Old Tide answered them. Once. Deep and slow. Not because it was time. It was not time for anything at all. It was as if a hand somewhere inside the tower, among the great dark turning wheels, had reached up in the dusk and pulled the rope.
