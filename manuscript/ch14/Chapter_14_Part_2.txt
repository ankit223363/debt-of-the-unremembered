Chapter 14: Thaddeus Rook, Part 2 of 5: Faber's Bench
Faber's Bench
Juno was on the roof of the hay store, exactly where Oren had said, sitting with her knees up and her arms wrapped round them and her cap pulled down over her ears. There was snow on the cap. There was snow on her shoulders, and on the toes of her scuffed boots, and a little ridge of it along the top of her rolled-up sleeves. She looked as if she had been carved there out of the roof and left out overnight.
She saw them come out of the green door, and stood up, and shook herself, so that the snow flew off her in a white cloud like flour off a baker. Then she came down. She did not use the ladder. She went along the ridge of the roof, quick and sure, and dropped off the end of it into a drift by the yard wall, and came up out of the drift brushing herself off and grinning, with her sharp brown face pink with cold and her bright eyes shining.
"You took your time," she said. "Mister V. Miss." She nodded to Lyra, short and businesslike, one worker to another. Round her neck, on its piece of dirty string, the big old brass key swung and glinted. "There's a carriage up top. Black. Blinds down. And a great big man sat up on the box with the driver, eating a meat pie out of a paper. He's had three. I counted."
"Gorse," said Corin.
"Is that his name? It suits him. Prickly and no good to anybody." Juno sniffed her grandmother's sniff. "And there's two more. White coats. One at each end of Linden Walk, stamping their feet. Trying to look like they're waiting for somebody. They're not very good at it. One of them's been waiting outside a shut shop for an hour." She jerked her head at the back of the yard. "So we're not going out the front. Come on."
The back way was through Pruett's hay store, a long dim barn that smelled of dust and summer, with the hay stacked to the rafters on both sides in great golden blocks and a narrow passage between them like a lane between two cliffs. At the far end there was a small door, and beyond the small door there was a high brick wall with the snow lying along its top in a long thin loaf, and beyond the wall, when they had climbed it, Juno first and then Lyra and then Corin, slowly and badly, with his long coat catching on every brick, there was the dye-yard.
Corin had never seen anything like it. Or if he had, it was gone.
It was a wide open yard behind the Dye-Works, and it was full of vats. Great round wooden vats, taller than a man, bound with iron hoops, standing in rows like giant barrels. Most of them were covered with boards. But some of them were open, and the snow had fallen into them, and where it had fallen it had changed color. In one vat the snow lay on the surface like a crust of deep red, the red of a winter berry. In the next it was a strong bright blue. In the next, yellow. In the next, a purple so dark it was nearly black. Steam rose off some of them, thin and slow, where the dye was still warm underneath, and it curled up into the falling snow and melted it before it landed, so that above each warm vat there was a little round space in the air where no snow fell at all. The ground between the vats was white. But it was splashed with color here and there, in long drips and splatters, red and blue and yellow, as if somebody had walked through with a paintbrush and shaken it.
And over all of it, at the end of the yard, rose the great square chimney of the Dye-Works, black against the white sky, with snow along every ledge of it and a thin gray smoke going straight up from its top into the stillness.
"Don't touch anything," said Juno, over her shoulder. "Not the vats. Not the drips. You'll be blue till spring."
They went through the dye-yard in single file, between the steaming vats, and out through a gap in a broken fence, and down a long narrow alley that ran behind a row of workshops, and then another, and then a flight of steps cut into the hill with an iron rail so cold that it burned Corin's fingers through his gloves. Juno led. She never stopped. She never looked lost. She went left and right and down and through as if she were following a line painted on the ground that only she could see. Twice she stopped at a corner, and put her hand up, and they waited, pressed against a wall, while she looked round it. Once she made them crouch behind a coal bin for a long, cold minute while a man in a white coat went past the end of the alley, slowly, looking up at the windows.
Lyra crouched beside Corin behind the coal bin. Her breath went up in quick white puffs. Her face was very calm. But her right hand was inside her coat, and he knew it was resting on the hilt of her sword.
The white coat went on. Juno counted under her breath to twenty. Then they went on too.
Hook Lane was so steep that it was nearly a staircase.
Corin knew that from his Ledger. He did not know how it would look in the snow. It looked, he thought, standing at the top of it with his breath smoking, like a frozen waterfall. It went down and down from the riverfront road between two rows of crooked, leaning, tar-black houses, and the snow lay on it in steps and ledges, deep in the hollows and thin on the edges, with the black cobbles showing through here and there like rocks in a stream. The houses on both sides leaned in toward each other over the lane, so close at the top that their gutters nearly touched, and from every gutter hung a row of icicles, long and thin and clear, so many of them that it was like walking down under a fringe of glass. At the bottom, far below, there was the little stone quay, and the dark water beyond it, slow and black and steaming faintly in the cold, and two or three small boats tied up against the stone with snow piled in them like sugar in a bowl.
They went down slowly. It was slippery. Lyra went down sideways, with one hand on the wall. Juno went down like a goat. Corin fell over once, hard, on his back, and slid three steps before he stopped, and lay there for a moment in the snow looking up at the icicles and the white sky, with the breath knocked out of him.
Juno's face appeared over him, upside down, grinning.
"Nobody falls on Hook Lane," she said. "Except you."
He got up.
Halfway down there was a small dark shop with no sign over the door. Only a symbol, painted in faded silver on the black wood. A needle, with a long thread curling through its eye. The shutters were closed. But there was a thin line of yellow light at the edge of one of them, and a smell of hot ink and herbs coming from under the door, and as they passed, the shutter moved, very slightly, the width of a finger, and closed again.
"Vespera's up," said Juno, without looking round. "She's always up."
The shop next door was a clock-mender's.
It was even smaller than Vespera's, and even darker, and it leaned so far toward the lane that it seemed to be peering down at its own doorstep. It had one window, a wide low bow window with small square panes, and one of the panes was cracked from corner to corner and mended with a strip of brown paper. Over the door, on an iron bracket, hung a wooden sign in the shape of a clock face, with the paint flaking off it. The hands painted on the sign said ten past two. There was a little ridge of snow along the top of the sign, like a white eyebrow.
Behind the window, through the small square panes, they could see a bench. A long wooden bench, scarred and stained and covered from one end to the other with the insides of clocks. Wheels. Springs. Tiny screws in saucers. Little brass plates with holes in them. Long thin rods. Coils of wire. Bits of glass. All of it lay in shallow wooden trays and saucers and old tins, sorted by size, hundreds and hundreds of tiny shining pieces, glinting in the light of a single oil lamp hung low over the middle of the bench.
And at the bench, bent over it, sat a man.
Corin opened the door. A bell rang over it. Not a shop bell. A tiny, sweet, silvery chime, like a spoon tapped on a glass, and then another, a little lower, and then a third, as if the bell were playing the first notes of a tune and had forgotten the rest.
The man at the bench did not look up.
Inside, the shop was warm and close and very full. It smelled of oil. Fine, thin, sweetish clock oil, and brass, and hot lamp, and something like the smell of a new penny. The walls were covered in clocks. Not grand clocks, like the ones in Niles's shop. Small, broken, waiting clocks. Clocks with no hands. Clocks with no glass. Clocks with their backs off and their wheels showing. A cuckoo clock with its little door hanging open and no cuckoo inside. A tall thin clock in a cracked case with a pendulum hanging still and crooked, like a broken arm in a sling. Only a few of them were going, and none of them agreed. One said nine. One said a quarter to twelve. One said twenty past four and ticked very loudly, as if to make up for being wrong.
The man at the bench was small and thin and stooped, with his narrow shoulders hunched up round his ears. He was perhaps fifty-five. He was bald on top, with a fringe of gray hair round the back of his head like a monk's, and he wore a leather apron over a brown woolen jersey, and fingerless gloves, and in his right eye there was a jeweler's glass, a little black tube with a lens in the end, screwed in tight so that his whole face was pulled up on that side. He had a pair of fine tweezers in one hand and something so small in them that Corin could not see it at all, and he was bending over a tiny open watch on the bench with his face very close to it and the tip of his tongue between his teeth.
His left side was toward the door. His left ear. His left eye.
Corin had read about Faber. Deaf in one ear and blind in one eye. He had not thought about which ear, or which eye.
He went round the end of the bench, to the right side, where the jeweler's glass was. And then he stopped, a little way off, where the man could see him. He did not want to make him jump. Not with the tweezers.
The man's right hand went still. Then, very carefully, he set the tiny thing down in the watch, and laid the tweezers in a saucer, and took the jeweler's glass out of his eye. He looked up.
His right eye was brown, and sharp, and a little red at the rim from the glass. It was an ordinary eye. A clever eye.
His left eye was not.
It was the same brown. It was the same shape. It moved when the right one moved, turning in its socket to look at Corin, to look at Lyra, to look at Juno in the doorway. It even blinked, at the same time as the other. It was not milky, or scarred, or hurt in any way that Corin could see. It looked like a perfectly good eye.
But there was nobody behind it. That was the only way he could think to say it. The right eye looked at him. The left eye only pointed.
"Yes?" said Faber. His voice was high and soft and careful, the voice of a man who spent his days talking to very small things. He turned his head a little as he spoke, to bring his right ear round toward them. "Yes? I'm sorry. I didn't hear the bell. I never hear the bell." He looked at Juno, and his face cleared a little. "Oh. It's Marta's girl. Hello, Marta's girl."
"Hello, Mister Faber," said Juno.
"Have you brought me something to mend? Your gran's kitchen clock? I told her, it's the spring, she must stop winding it so hard—"
"No, Mister Faber." Juno took her cap off, which Corin had never seen her do. Her brown hair was cropped short and stuck up all over. "This is Mister Vane. From the Ministry. He's all right. And this is Miss Thorne."
Faber's face did not change. But his hands, lying on the bench on either side of the little open watch, went very still.
"Thorne," he said.
"Yes," said Lyra.
She came forward into the lamplight, and he looked up at her. He looked at her for a long time. At her face, and her short black hair, and her gray eyes, and the shape of her nose. His good eye went over her slowly, the way it might have gone over a clock he had not seen for a long time, checking every wheel.
"Yes," he said softly. "Yes, you are. You have his nose. Lord Edmund's." He swallowed. "What do you want with me, Miss Thorne?"
Corin took out the Ledger. He opened it to the page, and turned it round, and laid it down flat on the bench in front of Faber, beside the little watch, under the lamp. He put his finger under the line.
Maker's plate inside the clock door: E. T. & T. R.
"We'd like to know who T. R. is," he said. "And where we can find him."
Faber looked at the page for a long time.
He did not touch it. He looked at it with his head on one side, so that his good eye was close to it, the way he had looked at the watch. And while he looked, his face slowly lost its color, the way a cloth loses its color in the wash, until it was the color of the snow on his windowsill.
"Where did you see that?" he said at last.
"Inside my grandfather's clock," said Lyra. "The tall one. With the twelve stones. It's at Niles's on Gutter Street. It's been there for two years."
"Niles's." Faber breathed out. "Niles's. Of course. Of course it is." He shut his good eye for a moment. When he opened it again it was wet. "I looked for it, you know. After. When the auction was in the papers. I went up to Crown Walk to look at the lots. And it wasn't there. Withdrawn. And I thought, oh, well, then. Somebody's got it. Somebody's broken it up for the stones. And I went home and I…" He stopped. "It doesn't matter what I did."
"Who is T. R., Mister Faber?" said Corin.
Faber looked up at him. His good eye was very bright.
"Thaddeus Rook," he said. "Master Gearwright. The best clock-hand in Veridia. The best there ever was, I think. I was his boy. His apprentice. For twenty-six years. I swept his floor and I sorted his wheels and I held the lamp while he worked, and I watched him. That's how you learn. You watch." He picked up the tweezers again, and put them down again, and picked them up. His hands could not keep still. "He made that clock with Lord Edmund. In Lord Edmund's own workshop, on the Hill. Thirty years ago. It took them two years. I was there. I held the lamp."
"Where is he now?"
Faber put the tweezers down very carefully in their saucer.
"He's dead," he said.
He said it quite steadily. He did not look away. He had his hands flat on the bench, on either side of the little watch, and he looked up at Corin with his one good brown eye, and said it quite steadily, the way a man says a thing he has said a great many times, to a great many people, until it has worn smooth.
"He died eight years ago. In the winter. I'm sorry."
Corin did not say anything.
He was looking at the bench. Not at Faber. At the far end of the bench, where the lamplight did not quite reach, beside a tin of screws. There was a parcel there. A small, neat, square parcel, done up in brown paper and tied with string, ready to go. The string had been tied in a very particular way, round and round and then across, with a little loop on top to carry it by.
And beside the parcel, half under it, there was a scrap of paper. The back of an old handbill, torn small. With writing on it.
He could not read it from where he stood. But he could see the writing. It was not Faber's. Faber's writing would be small and neat and careful, he was sure of it, the writing of a man who handled tiny things. This writing was large and sprawling and old, and it shook. Every line of it shook, like the line of a hill drawn by a man on a moving cart.
He looked at Lyra. Lyra was not looking at the parcel. She was looking at the shelf over the bench.
There were things on the shelf. Ordinary clock-mender's things. A row of old tins. A jar of brushes. A bottle of oil. And in among them, at the very end, almost hidden behind a tin, there was something small and round and shining. Something the size of a walnut. Brass, with a back of bright green enamel, like the shell of a beetle. Six thin brass legs folded up underneath it. And two tiny feelers, curling up from its head, fine as hairs.
It was not finished. One of its legs was missing. And the green enamel on its back was only half done, so that you could see the bare brass underneath on one side, like a bruise.
Lyra put out her hand. Slowly. She did not touch it. She stopped with her fingers an inch from it, the way you might stop before touching a bird that you did not want to frighten away.
"Mister Faber," she said. Her voice was very quiet and very strange. "Did you make this?"
Faber did not answer.
"I had one," said Lyra. "When I was little. Lots of them. A row of them, on my windowsill, in the sun. Every color. Every winter there was a new one." Her hand stayed where it was, an inch from the half-made beetle. "I'd forgotten. Until this morning. They were sold, you know. At the auction. With everything else. Some man bought the whole box of them for a few coppers. For his children, I suppose." She swallowed. "I hope he had children."
She turned round and looked at Faber.
"You didn't make this," she said. "Did you? You can't. You sort the wheels and you hold the lamp. But you can't do this. This isn't mending. This is something else." Her gray eyes were bright and hard. "He sends you the pieces that need finishing. Doesn't he? And you send him what he needs. Every week. In a parcel. With a loop on the string."
The clock on the wall that said twenty past four ticked very loudly into the silence.
Faber sat on his stool with his thin shoulders hunched and his hands flat on the bench, and looked at her. And slowly, very slowly, his face began to crumple. Like paper in a fire.
"He asks about you," he said. It came out of him in a rush, high and soft and broken, as if it had been pressing against the inside of his teeth for a very long time. "Every week. Every single week, in his letter, at the bottom. How is Edmund's granddaughter? Is she well? Does she still keep the beetles? And I never knew what to tell him. I never knew. I didn't dare go up the Hill and ask. Not after… not after what happened to them. So I wrote she's well. Every week. She's well, Master. She's well." He pressed the back of his gloved hand against his mouth. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I promised him. I promised him I'd never tell anybody. They're looking for him. They've been looking for him for a year. If they find him—"
"Who's looking?" said Corin.
Faber took his hand away from his mouth.
He did not answer. Instead, slowly, he turned his head. Not toward Corin. Toward the window. He turned his head so that his left eye, his empty eye, the eye with nobody behind it, was looking straight at them.
And then he lifted his right hand, and touched his left ear. Lightly. With one finger. The way you might touch a sore place, to see if it still hurt.
"The same people who did this," said Faber. "In front of him. To make him work."
Outside, through the cracked pane, the snow went on falling into Hook Lane, slow and soft and white, and somewhere down at the bottom of the lane a boat bumped against the stone of the quay. Once. Twice.
"He's alive, then," said Corin.
Faber looked at him for a long moment with his one good eye.
Then he got up off his stool, and went to the end of the bench, and picked up the brown paper parcel by its little loop of string.
"I take it to him on Thursdays," he said. "At noon." He looked out of the window, at the white sky. "It's Thursday."
The Tide Hall
Faber's boat was the smallest of the three tied up at the bottom of Hook Lane. It was a narrow flat-bottomed thing, painted a green that had faded nearly to gray, with two oars lying across the seats and a rusty tin can rolling about in the bottom for bailing. It had snow in it. Faber knelt on the quay and scooped the snow out with his gloved hands, carefully, the way he did everything, and then he wiped the seats with the end of his apron, and then he looked up at them.
"It holds four," he said. "Just. If nobody moves about." His good eye went to Juno. "That means you, Marta's girl."
"I never move about," said Juno, and stepped down into the boat so lightly that it hardly rocked at all.
Lyra went next. She sat in the front, in the pointed end, with her back very straight and her hands in her lap and her sword across her knees under her coat. Corin went last, and badly. The boat tipped under him and the black water slapped at its side, and for one bad moment he thought he was going in. Then Faber caught his sleeve and pulled him down onto the middle seat beside Juno, and the boat steadied, and Faber untied the rope from the iron ring in the quay and pushed them off with his foot and sat down himself in the back, facing them, and took up the oars.
He rowed well. Corin had not expected that. He was such a small, thin, careful man, with his hunched shoulders and his gloves and his one good eye. But he rowed with long, slow, steady strokes, the oars going in and out of the water without a splash, and the little boat slid out from the shelter of the quay into the open river as smoothly as a knife going into butter.
And then they were on the river.
It was very wide. That was the first thing. From Tallow Street, from the hill, it had looked like a ribbon. From down here, sitting a hand's width above the water in a little green boat, it looked like a sea. It went away on both sides into the falling snow, wide and flat and black, and the far bank was only a long dark line in the whiteness, with a few gray shapes standing up out of it that might have been buildings and might have been clouds. The near bank was going away behind them, the tar-black houses of Hook Lane getting smaller and grayer, the little quay getting smaller, until it was only a dark notch in the white shore.
The water was black. Not dark, not brown, the way the Ledger said it was in summer. Black, like ink in a well, and smooth, and very slow, and it steamed. A thin gray mist lay on top of it, a hand's breadth deep, curling and drifting, so that the boat seemed to be sliding through a low cloud. The snow fell into the mist and vanished. Every flake. It came down out of the white sky, turning, drifting, and touched the black water, and was gone, just like that, without a sound, as if the river were swallowing it.
There was ice. Corin had not known rivers could have ice on them. It lay along the edges of the water, close to the banks, in thin flat plates as big as tabletops, gray-white and cracked, turning slowly in the current. Some of them had come loose and were floating out into the middle of the river, slowly, spinning, like great white lily pads. One of them came drifting past the boat, close enough to touch. There was a gull standing on it. A big gray-and-white gull with a yellow beak and a red spot on the end of the beak, standing on one leg on its floating plate of ice, riding it down the river toward the sea like a lord in a carriage. It turned its head and looked at them with one hard yellow eye as they went by. Then it opened its beak and said something rude, and went on looking at the snow.
Juno laughed out loud. Then she put her hand over her mouth, and looked round, as if somebody might hear.
Nobody could hear. There was nobody on the river at all. No barges. No ferries. No little rowing boats full of fish. The great ships at anchor stood far off downstream in a long gray line, their masts and yards all white with snow, and nothing moved on any of them. The river belonged to the four of them, and the gull, and the ice.
Upstream, to the left, through the snow, Corin could see a bridge.
It was a long stone bridge with many arches, low and dark against the white sky, and along the top of it, at every pier, there stood an iron lamp-post, very tall, with a lantern on the top. The lanterns were still lit. In all that white daylight they were still burning, small and blue and steady, one after another, all the way across the river from one bank to the other, like a row of cold blue stars.
The Lantern Bridge, said a voice in his head. His own voice, reading from his own Ledger. The king's name has been chiseled off it.
And under one of those dark arches, at the bottom of a flight of slimy stone steps, there was a landing. He knew that too. He had stood on it yesterday. Not with his own feet. With the feet of an old man, two years ago, at night, hiding in the dark behind a pile of old crates, watching a small round man on his knees in the yellow light of a lantern.
He looked at Lyra.
She was sitting in the front of the boat with her back very straight, looking straight ahead, at the far bank. She did not turn her head toward the bridge. Not once. Not at all. She sat and looked straight ahead and her face was white and still, and the snow settled on her short black hair and on the shoulders of her filthy brown coat and did not melt.
Corin did not look at the bridge again either.
"There," said Faber, after a long time. "Look."
He did not stop rowing. He lifted his chin, very slightly, toward the far bank. They all turned and looked.
The snow had thinned for a moment. And there, rising up out of the dark line of the shore ahead of them, was a building.
It was very big. That was the first thing. Bigger than anything Corin had seen on the near bank, bigger than the warehouses, bigger than the Dye-Works. It stood right at the edge of the water, so close that the river lapped at its walls, and it was made of pale gray stone that had gone dark and streaky with age, the way a face goes streaky with tears. It was long and low at the front, with a great row of tall stone pillars all along its river side, holding up a heavy stone roof, like the front of a temple. Between the pillars, a wide flight of stone steps went down, step after step, right into the river, and disappeared under the black water. The bottom steps were green with weed. The top steps were white with snow.
Behind the pillars there were windows. Tall, narrow, arched windows, a long row of them. Every one was boarded up with planks nailed across it, gray and split and rotting.
And behind the long low front, rising up out of the middle of the building, there was a tower.
It was square and very tall, taller than any of the cranes on the docks, and it went up and up out of the snowy roof, gray stone on gray sky, with narrow slits for windows all the way up it. At the top, on all four sides, there was a clock.
The face that looked out over the river was the biggest clock face Corin had ever seen. It was round and pale and blank as the moon, as wide across as the whole of Mrs. Marta's house was tall, and it was not painted, the way a clock face usually is. It was made of glass. Thick, milky, pearly glass, set in a great iron frame, so that the gray daylight shone through it and made it glow, very faintly, like a lamp turned down low. Round its edge, in black iron, were the numbers. Twelve of them. Each as tall as a man. And in the middle, black against the pale glass, were the hands. Two great black iron hands, long as the masts of a fishing boat, with arrowheads on their ends.
They were pointing straight up. Both of them. Together. At the twelve.
It was the clock he had seen from the bottom of Tallow Street that morning. He knew it at once.
"The Tide Hall," said Faber softly, between strokes. "The old Customs House. Every ship that came up this river used to stop here and pay its tax. Every bale of wool. Every barrel of wine. Every chest of tea. They had clerks in there by the hundred, once, sitting in rows, writing it all down." He pulled on the oars. "And that clock up there told the tides. Not just the time. The tides. High water and low water, every day of the year, on a ring inside the face. The ship captains would look up at it from the river and know when to sail." He was quiet for a moment. "It stopped in the war. Like a lot of things. Nobody ever came to start it again."
"It's at twelve," said Juno.
"It's always at twelve." Faber did not look round at it. "It's been at twelve for a year. It was at nine, before. For thirty years. At nine minutes to three. Where it stopped." He pulled the oars again. "When he came here, he moved the hands. Once. Up to twelve. And he said to me, Faber. If you ever look up at that clock from the other side of the river and the hands are anywhere else, anywhere at all, you're not to come. You're to go home and shut your door and forget me." His voice was quite steady. "I look at it every morning. From the bottom of Hook Lane. Before I open the shop."
Nobody said anything.
The little boat slid in toward the great gray building through the drifting mist, and the long row of stone pillars rose up over them, higher and higher, until they had to tip their heads back to see the top. Snow lay on the heads of the pillars like caps. Between the pillars, high up, under the roof, there was a long band of carved stone, with letters cut into it. Big letters, each one as tall as a child, deep and square.
THE TIDE HALL OF HIS MAJESTY KING
And then nothing.
Not a gap. Not a space. A scar. Where the next word should have been, the stone had been cut away. Chiseled out, with hard, rough, angry strokes, so deep that the marks of the chisel still showed, all down the face of it, like claw marks. Someone had stood up there, on a ladder, a long time ago, and hacked a name out of the stone until there was nothing left of it at all. Only a long rough hollow, gray and pitted, with snow lying in it.
Corin looked up at it for a long time.
He had read about another place like this. In his Ledger. On the very first morning. The chiseled-out king's name on Lantern Bridge. He had not thought about it since. He did not know why he was thinking about it now. Except that it was the same. The same rough, angry strokes. The same hollow, where a name had been.
As if someone had gone all round the city, a long time ago, with a ladder and a chisel, cutting out one word wherever they found it.
Do not stare, said Malakor, very quietly, from inside his coat. It is only a word. It is only stone.
But his thin dry voice was not quite steady. And Corin, who had read the Ledger, and knew that Malakor was very old, and very dead, and had been alive a long time ago, when kings had names, wondered for the first time whether Malakor remembered what the word had been.
Faber did not take them to the great steps.
He rowed them along the front of the building, close in under the pillars, where the shadows were deep and the water slapped hollowly against the stone. At the far end, where the long front met the side wall, there was a low arch in the stonework, right down at the waterline. It was so low that you could not have stood up in a boat under it. An iron grille hung across it, rusted orange, with icicles hanging from its bars.
Faber shipped his oars and let the boat drift in toward the grille. He reached into his apron pocket and took out a key. A long iron key, black and old. He leaned out, and put his gloved hand through the bars, and felt about for something on the other side, and there was a click, and a groan, and the grille swung slowly inward on its hinges, with a long thin shriek of rusty iron that went echoing away into the dark under the building.
"Heads down," said Faber.
They ducked. The boat slid in under the arch, out of the gray daylight, into the dark.
It was very cold under the Tide Hall. Colder than the river. A wet, still, stony cold that smelled of weed and old water and stone that had never once in a hundred years been warm. The arch went on for a little way, low and dripping, and then it opened out, and they were in a long stone tunnel with water for a floor, black and still, and a curved stone roof close over their heads, green with slime. Somewhere far off, water was dripping. Plink. Plink. Plink. Slow and even, like a clock.
Corin took out his little brass lamp and turned it up. A small blue light bloomed in his hand.
The tunnel went on ahead of them into the dark. On both sides, along the walls, there were iron rings set into the stone, rusted orange, and old wooden posts gone black and soft, where boats had once been tied. And at the end, where the light just reached, there was a flight of stone steps going up out of the water, and at the top of the steps, a door.
Faber rowed them to the steps and tied up the boat to one of the iron rings. They climbed out. The steps were slippery with green slime, and Corin went up them on his hands and knees, and nobody laughed at him, not even Juno.
The door at the top was old, and thick, and studded with iron nails. It had a keyhole. But Faber did not use his key.
Instead, he reached up to a place beside the door, high on the wall, where a thin wire came out of a hole in the stone and ran away up into the dark. He took hold of the wire with two fingers, very gently, and pulled it. Three times. Short. Then a pause. Then twice more. Long.
Far, far away above them, so far that it was hardly a sound at all, a bell rang. A tiny bell. Ting. Ting. Ting. And then, after a pause, Ting. Ting.
They waited.
Nothing happened for a long time. The water dripped somewhere in the dark. Plink. Plink. Juno shifted her feet on the slimy step. Lyra stood quite still, with her hand inside her coat.
Then, from the other side of the door, there came a sound.
It was a small sound. A soft, quick, scratching, whirring sound, like somebody winding a watch very fast, and then a click, and then a sort of patter. Patter, patter, patter. Getting closer. Coming down the stairs, Corin thought. Something small, coming down a long stone stair, quickly, on a great many little feet.
It stopped on the other side of the door.
There was a pause. Then a snuffling noise. A long, slow, careful snuffling at the crack under the door, exactly like a dog. And then, very low, a growl.
It was not a dog's growl. It was too even for that. Too smooth. It was the growl of a wheel turning very fast against a spring. A low, steady, buzzing whirr, rising and rising, like a top being spun.
Faber bent down to the crack under the door.
"It's me, Minim," he said, in his soft high careful voice. "It's only me. And friends. Friends, Minim. Let us in."
The whirring went on for a moment. Then, slowly, it ran down, the way a clock runs down when it is not wound. Lower and lower. And stopped.
There was a click. A heavy one. Somewhere inside the door, a bolt slid back.
The door swung open.
And there on the stone floor on the other side of it, in the blue light of Corin's lamp, sat a dog.
It was made of brass.
All of it. It was no bigger than a cat, and it was shaped like a little terrier, with a square body and short legs and a short, stiff, upright tail. Its body was made of brass plates, fitted together so closely and so cleverly that they slid over each other when it moved, like the scales of a fish. Through the gaps between the plates, Corin could see wheels turning. Tiny wheels. Hundreds of them. Turning slowly, steadily, ticking very softly, like a heart. Its ears were two little brass triangles that stood up on top of its head and turned, one and then the other, toward each of them in turn. Its eyes were two round beads of dark green glass, and there was a little light inside each one, faint and greenish, like the light at the bottom of a pond.
It looked up at Faber, and its stiff little tail moved, once, from side to side, with a small clicking sound. Tick-tock.
Then it turned its head and looked at Juno. And at Corin. And at Lyra.
It looked at Lyra for a long time.
Its brass ears turned toward her, both of them at once. Its green glass eyes brightened, very slightly. And then it got up off the floor, and walked across the stone to her on its short brass legs, tick tick tick tick, and stopped in front of her, and put its square brass head on one side, and looked up at her face.
And sat down again. At her feet. And leaned its cold brass shoulder, very gently, against her boot.
Lyra looked down at it. She did not move.
"It's never done that," said Faber, in a strange voice. "Not with anyone. Not even with me."
And from somewhere high above them, from the top of a long stone stair that went up and up into the dark beyond the door, there came another voice. An old voice. A cracked, thin, shaking voice, like a reed that has been left out in the wind too long.
"Faber?" it called down. "Faber, you're early. You're never early. And Minim's stopped growling." A pause. "Who have you brought me, Faber? Who have you brought?"
