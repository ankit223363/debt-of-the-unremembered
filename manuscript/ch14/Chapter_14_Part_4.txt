Chapter 14: Thaddeus Rook, Part 4 of 5: What He Built Next
What He Built Next
"It was a summer evening," said Rook.
He had not moved in his chair. But something in him had gone small, and tight, and far away, the way a snail goes back into its shell when you touch it. His hands were still. They were still because the blue stone was in them. But he was holding it so hard now that his knuckles had gone white through the holes in his gloves.
"Very hot. The kind of evening when the whole city smells of drains and the river goes green. I'd sent the boys home. Faber had gone to get our supper from the pie shop on the corner. I was alone in the shop, at the bench, with the door open for the air. And I looked up, and there was a man sitting on my counter."
He said it very simply. There was a man sitting on my counter. As if that were the most frightening thing a man could say.
"I hadn't heard him come in. The bell over my door was a loud one. A brass one. You couldn't open that door without it ringing. But he was there. Sitting on the end of my counter, with one leg crossed over the other, swinging his foot. Like a boy on a wall." Rook swallowed. "He was slim. Not tall. Pale. Pale hair, pale skin, pale eyes, like a fish that lives in the dark. A gray coat, very well cut. Gray gloves. And he had a little tin box open on his knee. A sweet tin. And he was eating barley sugar out of it."
Lyra had gone very still on the end of the bed.
"He didn't say anything at first. He just sat and looked round my shop, at all the clocks, the way you look round a room you're thinking of buying. And he took a barley sugar out of the tin, and unwrapped it, and put it in his cheek. And then he took the wrapper—it was a little square of waxed paper, yellow—and he folded it. In half. And in half again. And again. And again. Very neatly. Very carefully, with the tips of his gray gloves. Until it was a tiny square no bigger than my smallest fingernail. And he put it in his waistcoat pocket, and patted the pocket. And smiled at me." Rook's voice had gone thin again. "And he said, I do so hate untidiness. Don't you, Mister Rook?"
Corin did not need the Ledger. The name was there already, in his mouth, cold and flat as a stone.
"Silas Kross," he said.
Rook did not answer. He did not need to.
"He said there was a little job," Rook went on. "For the Ministry. A very important job. Very secret. He said the Archon himself had asked for me, by name, because I was the best. He said I'd be paid. Well paid. He said it would only take a few weeks." He gave a small, terrible laugh, like something cracking. "I said no. I said I was an old man. I said I had orders to finish, and a shop to run, and boys to feed. I said, find somebody else. And he looked at me, and he folded another wrapper, very slowly, and he said, But there isn't anybody else, Mister Rook. That's rather the point. And then Faber came back with the pies."
By the stove, Faber had stopped moving. He stood with the brown teapot in his hands, and his back to them, and his narrow shoulders up round his ears.
"They took us both," said Rook. "That night. In a black carriage, with the blinds down. Up the Hill. Through a gate with a closed eye on it, carved in stone. Into a building I'd never seen, and I've lived in this city all my life. And down. A long way down. Stairs, and stairs, and more stairs, and a lift on a chain that went down in the dark for so long that my ears hurt." He shut his eyes behind the glass. "I don't know where we were. Under the Hill, I think. Very deep. It was cold down there, even in summer. Cold and dry. And there were doors. So many doors. Iron doors, with numbers on them, and men in gray coats outside every one."
He opened his eyes again.
"And behind the last door, there was a thing on a table."
The fat little stove ticked. Outside the great milky glass of the clock face, a gull went past, a dark flicker against the white, and was gone.
"It was a man," said Rook. "Or the shape of one. Lying on its back on a long stone table, under lamps. A big man. Bigger than a man. Twice as long as I am. Made of metal. Iron, and something darker than iron, that I'd never seen before, black and smooth like the inside of a mussel shell. It was old. So old. It was rusted orange all down one side, and the rust had eaten right through in places, so you could see inside. And it was full of sand. Fine white glittering sand, that ran out of the holes in it when they moved it, and lay in little heaps on the floor round the table, and crunched under your boots."
Corin felt the rod go cold against his ribs.
"They'd dug it up," said Rook. "Out of the desert. A long way south. Out of the Glass Wastes, Kross said. From the war before the war. Before any of us were born." He shook his head slowly. "It had been a soldier once. A machine soldier. Built to hunt. You could see that, even rusted. Its hands were… its hands were the worst. Long, and thin, with too many joints, and at the end of every finger there was a hollow silver needle. Like a siphon. Like your rod, young man. But small. Ten of them." He held up his own shaking fingers, and looked at them, and put them down again. "Somebody had made it to find people. And to take something out of them. And somebody had put something in it once, to make it go. Something like what I do. A kind of mind. I could feel it, when I put my hand on it. Old and cold and very far down, like a fire that went out a hundred years ago and still has a little warmth in the bottom of the ashes."
The Wastes, said a voice inside Corin's coat. Very faint. So faint he was not sure he had heard it. They have been digging in the Wastes. The fools. The poor fools.
He did not ask. Not now. He put his hand flat on his coat, over the rod, and kept it there.
"Kross stood beside the table," said Rook, "and ate barley sugar, and said, It used to hunt, Mister Rook. It doesn't anymore. I'd like you to make it hunt again. And then he smiled and said, And then I'd like you to make me some more. Twelve, I think. Twelve is a nice round number."
"And you said no," said Lyra.
"I said no."
"And then?"
Rook did not answer.
Instead, Faber turned round from the stove.
He still had the teapot in his hands. He held it in front of him, against his leather apron, as if it were something that could keep him warm. His thin face was quite calm. His good brown eye was quite calm. And his other eye, the empty one, the eye with nobody behind it, looked at them all, and saw none of them.
"And then Inquisitor Kross took off one of his gray gloves," said Faber, in his soft, careful voice. "And came round the table. And put his bare hand on my face."
Nobody moved.
"Just here." Faber lifted one finger from the teapot and touched his own cheek, under his left eye. Lightly. The way you touch a bruise. "Very gently. His hand was cold. And he looked at the Master, not at me, the whole time. And he said, Mister Rook. Your boy has two eyes. He doesn't need both of them, does he? Not to hold a lamp." Faber's voice did not change. "And then there was a sort of… pull. Like a thread being drawn out through the back of my head. It didn't hurt. That's the thing people don't understand. It didn't hurt at all. It was just… gone. The knowing of it. How to see with that eye. As if I'd never known. My eye was still there. It still moved. It still blinked. But the part of me that used to look out of it had been taken away, and there was only a window, and nobody standing at it."
He put the teapot down on the stove. Very carefully. So that it made no sound.
"The Master said no again," said Faber. "He was brave. He said no again. So Inquisitor Kross did my ear. The left one. And then he wiped his hand on his handkerchief, and put his glove back on, and took out another barley sugar." He looked at Rook. "And he said, Next time, Mister Rook, it'll be his hands. And then what will he be good for?"
The old man in the chair had bent his head right down, so that his great white cloud of hair hid his face. His shoulders were shaking. Not his hands. His shoulders.
"I said yes," he said, into his lap. "After that. I said yes. Saints forgive me. I said yes."
It was a long time before anyone spoke.
Juno had taken her hand off Minim's back. She was sitting very straight on the floor by the stove, with her arms wrapped round her knees and her sharp brown face gone pale under the soot, staring at Faber. At his left eye. At his left ear. As if she had walked past his shop every day of her life and never once looked at him before.
Lyra had not moved at all.
Corin found that he was holding his little brass bowl of tea so tightly that it had begun to hurt his fingers. He put it down on the end of the bench, beside the row of beetles. His hand was not quite steady.
"What did you build?" he said.
Rook lifted his head.
His long face was wet, and old, and very tired. He took the little brown book out of Corin's hands, gently. He turned the pages. Near the back, he found what he wanted. He held it out, open.
The page was black.
Not all of it. But nearly. It had a heading, at the top, in the big shaking hand. THE GRAY MEN. And under the heading there had been a list. A long list. Corin could see that it had been a list, because there were numbers down the left side, one under another, all the way to the bottom of the page and over onto the next. One to forty-one. But every line after every number had been scratched out. Over and over. With hard black strokes of a pen pressed so deep that it had torn the paper in places, line after line after line, until there was nothing left but black, and the little holes, and the numbers sitting alone at the edge of the page like the stumps of trees after a fire.
At the very bottom, under the black, in small writing that did not shake at all, it said:
Every tune I had left. Every one. So that I would never sing again.
"Six," said Rook. "I built six."
He closed the book.
"It took two years. Down there. In the deep room under the Hill. They gave us a room to sleep in, Faber and me, with a lamp that was never put out and a door that was always locked, and every morning a man in gray brought us bread and took us to the workshop. And I worked. I took that old thing on the table apart, piece by piece, and I learned how it was made. And then I made it again. Better." The word came out of him like something bitter he had to spit. "I'm good at my work. That's the worst of it. I'm so good at it."
"What are they like?" said Juno. Her voice came out small. "The Gray Men."
Rook looked at her for a long moment. As if he were deciding something.
"Tall," he said at last. "Taller than Captain Oren. Seven feet, and thin. They walk like men. Not like the old one, all stiff and grinding. Like men. Smooth. Quiet. I made their feet with felt inside the iron, so they make no sound on stone. Their bodies are plates of dark iron, laid one over another like the slates on a roof, so they can bend. And over the iron there are coats. Long gray coats, to the ankle, with high collars. So that in a street, at night, in the fog, you might think they were only very tall men, walking." He paused. "Kross wanted that. He thought it was funny."
"And their faces?" said Juno.
"They have no faces." Rook's voice was very quiet. "Only a smooth white oval. Like an egg. Porcelain. With two little holes for eyes, and in each hole, a blue stone, very small, that glows. That's how they see." He swallowed. "But mostly they don't see. Mostly they smell. That was the thing the old one could do that I'd never seen before, and I learned it, Saints help me, and I made it better. They can smell memory. The warmth of it. A mind full of things it hasn't given up yet, hidden in a cellar, in a cupboard, under a floor… they can smell it through a wall, the way a dog smells meat. And they follow it. They don't get tired. They don't get bored. They don't stop. That's what I gave them, with all my tunes. That's all they want. They want to find."
"And when they find?" said Lyra.
Rook looked down at his hands. At the blue stone, lying still in his still palm.
"The hands," he said. "Like the old one. Ten silver needles."
He did not say any more about the hands.
Corin found that he did not want him to.
"I did one thing," said Rook.
He said it after a long time. When the tea had gone cold in the cups, and the fish had gone round its bowl a hundred times, and the white light through the clock face had shifted, very slightly, so that the shadows of the two great hands lay a little further across the floor.
"One thing. That Kross didn't ask for. I put it in at the very end. In the last week. After I'd finished everything else, and they were standing in a row in the deep room, all six of them, so tall, with their gray coats on and their white faces, waiting. I opened them up again, one by one, while the man in gray was eating his lunch, and I put it in."
"What?" said Corin.
"I made them knock."
Nobody understood. Rook could see that nobody understood. He looked from one face to the next, and he lifted one shaking hand and made a little fist of it, and knocked, very softly, on the arm of his chair. Three times.
Knock. Knock. Knock.
"Before they go in," he said. "Anywhere. Through any door. Into any room. They stop, and they knock. Three times. Slowly. And then they wait. Just for a moment. Just for a breath." His voice wavered. "They can break down any door in this city. They could walk through a wall if they wanted. They don't need to knock. But they do. Every time. They can't help it. I made it so deep in them that it's like breathing."
"Why?" whispered Juno.
Rook looked at her. At her sharp small face, and her big coat with the sleeves rolled up, and the brass key on its string round her neck.
"So that whoever is on the other side has a moment," he said. "That's all. One moment. To hide something. Or to run. Or to hold somebody's hand. Or to say one thing out loud, so that the last thing they remember is something they chose." He looked down. "It isn't much. I know it isn't much. It was all I could do."
"And Kross?" said Corin. "When he found out?"
"He laughed." Rook shut his eyes. "He laughed and laughed. He stood in front of the first one and it knocked on the door of the deep room, three times, knock, knock, knock, and he laughed till he had to sit down. He said it was the most charming thing he had ever seen. He said, Oh, Mister Rook, you old softy. They'll hear them coming. What a lovely idea. It'll make it so much more interesting." He opened his eyes. "And he let me keep it."
The room was very quiet.
"Six," said Rook. "He wanted twelve. But by the end of the sixth my hands had started to shake. Like this." He held them up, the blue stone in one of them, and the other one going up and down and side to side in the white light, helpless as a leaf. "That's what it costs, when you burn too much too fast. Your hands go. And a clockmaker whose hands shake is no use to anybody. So Kross came down to the deep room one morning, and looked at my hands, and folded a wrapper, and said, Oh dear. Oh dear, oh dear. You're no use to me like that, are you, old man? And they put us in the black carriage, Faber and me, and took us back up into the daylight, and put us out on Bellfounders' Row in front of my shop at six o'clock in the morning. And drove away." He was quiet. "They'd taken my sign down while we were gone. My name, in gold. Someone had taken it down and painted over the place where it had been. As if I'd never been there at all."
He looked at Faber.
"Faber opened a little shop on Hook Lane, after," he said. "Mending. Just mending. Kitchen clocks and pocket watches. Nobody looks at a man who mends kitchen clocks. And I went and sat in my room over the shop on Bellfounders' Row for six years, with the shutters closed, and I made beetles. Because my hands only stop shaking when they're working." He looked down at the stone in his still palm. "Or when they're holding something of Edmund's."
"Six years," said Corin slowly. "And then?"
Rook did not answer at once.
He turned his head instead, and looked at the stove. At the little fat black iron stove, ticking quietly as it burned, with its crooked pipe going up and out through the hole in the backward eight. And at the floor in front of it. Where Minim the brass dog had got up, quietly, while they were all listening, and gone over to the stove on his short brass legs. And was standing there, with his square head down, pushing at something with his nose. Something small and pale, half hidden under the iron edge of the ash pan.
Pushing and pushing. Tick, tick, tick. Patiently. The way a dog does with something it wants you to see.
"And then," said Thaddeus Rook, "a year ago, he came back."

He looked at the great milky circle of the clock face, at the backward numbers and the long black shadows of the hands, as if he could see the rain on the other side of it.
"I was in my room over the old shop. On Bellfounders' Row. With the shutters closed and the lamp lit, making a beetle. A red one. And I heard someone on the stair." His voice was flat and quiet. He stopped. His thin mouth worked.
"I can't tell you what he was humming. I couldn't hear it as a tune. Only as a noise. Like a wet finger going round and round the rim of a glass." He shut his eyes. "But I knew who it was."
"Kross," said Lyra.
"He sat on the end of my bed," said Rook. "Swinging his foot. And he ate a barley sugar, and folded the wrapper, and put it in his pocket. And he said, How are the hands, Mister Rook? Better? "Drawings," said Corin.
"Forty sheets." Rook opened his eyes. "Blue paper. White lines. The way the Ministry's draughtsmen do it. Very fine. Very careful. Somebody had spent a long time on them." He shook his head slowly. "Kross said they'd been copied from something old. Something they'd found in a part of the Archive that nobody was supposed to go into anymore. He said it was a design from before the war. From before all the wars. And that nobody alive could build it, because nobody alive understood it. Except, perhaps, an old Gearwright with shaking hands who had once made a clock that could keep a secret." Rook's long face twisted. "He was very flattering. He's always very flattering."
"What was it?" said Juno.
Rook did not answer for a long time.
"He said," he said at last, very slowly, "Not hunters, this time, Mister Rook. I have enough hunters. This time I want something that sings."
The fish in its bowl on the windowsill had stopped swimming. It hung in the water, quite still, with its golden fins spread, looking out through the glass.
"He left the drawings with me," said Rook. "He said to look at them. He said to think about it. He said he'd come back in a week for my answer. And he said, Do think hard, Mister Rook. Faber's hands are still very good, you know. I asked." He swallowed. "And he went away down the stair. Humming."
"And you looked at them," said Corin.
"All night." Rook's voice had gone very small. "I sat at my bench with the lamp and I looked at every sheet. Forty of them. One after another. And at first I didn't understand. It was too big. It was too strange. It was like looking at the drawing of a whole city and trying to find your own front door. But I'm good at my work. I'm so good at my work." He said it again, the way you press on a bruise. "And by the time the sky went gray behind the shutters, I understood what it was. And what it was for."
"What was it for?"
Rook looked at Corin.
And Corin, who had sat with a great many frightened people in a great many small rooms, and had seen a great many faces in the last moment before they said something terrible, saw something he had never seen before. He saw a man decide not to say it. Not because he was afraid of Corin. Not because he was afraid of Kross. Because he was afraid of the words themselves. As if saying them out loud, in this room, would make the thing they described a little more real. A little more possible. A little nearer.
"No," said Rook. "I won't say. I'm sorry, young man. I won't. Not to you, not to anybody. I've carried it for a year and I'll carry it a bit longer." His still hands closed tight round the blue stone. "I burned them. That's all you need to know. That same morning. Every sheet. I fed them into my stove one by one and I watched them go, and when they were all ash I raked the ash and burned it again. And that night Faber came with his boat, and I came here, with Minim, and my tools, and my little ones. And I moved the hands to twelve. And I've been here ever since."
He let out a long breath.
"Kross came back to my room on the seventh day, I suppose. And found the bench empty, and the stove cold, and the drawings gone." Something that was very nearly a smile moved the corners of the long thin mouth. "I'd have liked to see his face. Just once. I'd have liked to see him find something untidy."
"Master," said Juno.
She had got up off the floor. Nobody had seen her do it. She was kneeling in front of the little fat stove, beside Minim, with her cap pushed back on her head and her sharp face close to the iron, and she had her hand under the edge of the ash pan. Where the brass dog had been pushing.
"Master," she said again. "He wants you to see something."
She drew her hand out.
There was something in it. A scrap of paper. Stiff, thick paper, a deep strong blue, the blue of a winter evening just after the sun has gone. It was not big. About the size of a playing card. It had three sides. Two of them were straight, and met in a sharp clean corner, the way the corner of a large sheet of paper meets. The third side was not straight. The third side was brown and black and curled, eaten away in a long ragged curve where the fire had taken it, and the brown went back into the blue in a soft scorched line, like the edge of a cloud.
On the blue, in fine white lines, there was a drawing.
Corin came and knelt down beside Juno to see it. He did not touch it. He held his little brass lamp close.
It was only part of a drawing. A corner. Most of it had been in the rest of the sheet, the part that had burned. But you could see the edge of it. Long straight white lines, going up, side by side, close together. Dozens of them. Each one a little taller than the one beside it. Not because of the stone. The stone was in his other hand. His hand stopped shaking by itself. As if he had made up his mind about something, and the deciding of it had been enough.
He held out the scrap of blue paper to Corin.
"You keep a book," he said. Quite calmly. "You write things down. So that you'll always know where they went." He put the paper into Corin's hand, and closed Corin's fingers over it with his own long cold ones. "Then take this. And keep it. And don't burn it. I was going to take it to my grave, young man. I thought that was the brave thing. It isn't. It's only the quiet thing." His pale eyes behind the glass were very steady. "Somebody outside should know. Somebody who isn't afraid to write it down."
Corin looked down at the scorched blue corner in his hand.
CHOIR ORGAN. SHEET 1 OF 40.
He folded it. In half. And in half again. And put it inside the Ledger, against his heart.
"And now," said Thaddeus Rook, turning round to face the great silent machinery of the room, with the snow-light on his white hair, "I think it's time we woke her up. Don't you? It's been thirty years." He looked at Faber, and something came into his long old face that Corin had not seen there before. Something bright. Something almost young. "Let's see if she remembers."
