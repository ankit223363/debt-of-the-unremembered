Chapter 14: Thaddeus Rook, Part 3 of 5: The Man Inside the Clock
The Man Inside the Clock
The stair went up inside the wall of the tower, round and round, like the thread of a screw.
It was stone, and narrow, and very steep, and every step was worn into a hollow in the middle by a hundred years of clerks' boots. There was no rail. There was only the wall on one side, curving away, cold and damp under Corin's hand, and on the other side, the dark. Not empty dark. Every few turns the stair passed a narrow slit of a window, and the gray snow-light came in through it and showed him, for a moment, what was on the other side. And what was on the other side was the inside of the tower. A great square hollow, going up and down further than he could see, with ropes hanging in it, long ropes, thick as his arm, gray with dust, swaying very slightly in the cold air. And chains. And iron rods. All of it going up, up, into the dark above, toward the clock.
Minim went first, tick tick tick on the stone, stopping every few steps to look back at Lyra with its green glass eyes. Then Faber, with the parcel. Then Lyra. Then Juno. Then Corin, last, with the lamp, counting the steps under his breath because he did not know what else to do.
He had got to two hundred and twelve when the stair came out into a room.
It was a big room. It filled the whole tower from wall to wall, square and high, with a ceiling of great dark beams far over their heads. And it was full of the clock.
Not the face. The face was higher up. This was the inside of it. The works. The heart.
Corin had seen the inside of Lord Thorne's clock at Niles's. Its wheels and its springs, small and bright and fine as jewelry. This was the same. But it was a giant's. The wheels in this room were as big as the wheels of a hay cart, as big as mill wheels, some of them, made of black iron and dark old brass, with teeth on them as long as his hand. They stood upright in a great iron frame in the middle of the room, row behind row, all fitted into each other, tooth to tooth, the way the fingers of two hands fit together when you fold them. Long iron rods ran out of the frame on all four sides, up through the ceiling, toward the four faces of the clock above. Chains hung from it. Weights hung from the chains, great round iron weights as big as barrels, hanging still in the air, down through holes in the floor into the dark below.
And in front of it all, hanging down from the frame on a long iron rod, there was the pendulum.
It was enormous. The rod was as long as the mast of a fishing boat, and at the bottom of it, just clear of the floor, hung a great round disc of brass as wide as a cartwheel, dull and green and streaked with old bird droppings. It hung perfectly still. It had hung perfectly still, Corin thought, for thirty years.
Nothing moved. Nothing ticked. The whole great machine stood there in the gray light, silent, frozen, covered in dust and old cobwebs, like a sleeping animal so big that you could walk round it for an hour and never see all of it.
And round the walls of the room, in a ring, was the tide.
That was the only way Corin could think of it. There was a great brass ring set into the walls, all the way round the room, at the height of his head, and on it, cut into the brass, there were lines. Hundreds of them. Wavy lines, rising and falling, rising and falling, all the way round. High water. Low water. High water. Low water. Next to each wave there were tiny numbers, the days of the year, and the names of the months. And the tides of the moon, drawn as little round faces, some full, some half, some thin as a fingernail. Someone, a long time ago, had stood in this room with a pencil and a lamp and worked out every tide of every day of every year, and then had cut it all into brass, so that it would never be forgotten.
"Edmund loved this room," said a voice above them. "He used to say it was the only honest room in the city. Because it couldn't lie. The tide comes when it comes."
They looked up.
There was a ladder at the back of the room. A long iron ladder, going up through a square hole in the ceiling. And at the top of it, in the square hole, looking down at them, there was a face.
It was a very old face. Old in the way that a tree is old, or a cliff, as if it had been out in the weather for a very long time and had been worn into its shape by the wind. It was long and thin and deeply lined, with brown spots on the cheeks and the forehead, like the spots on an old apple, and a long thin nose, and a long thin mouth that turned down at the corners. Round it, on all sides, stood up a great soft cloud of white hair. It stood straight up and straight out in every direction at once, fine and fluffy and silver-white in the gray light, like the seed head of a dandelion just before the wind blows it away.
And on its nose, there was glass.
A great deal of glass. A pair of spectacles in a heavy brass frame, with round lenses. And on top of the lenses, fixed to the frame by tiny hinges at the sides, there were more lenses. Three more on each side, stacked up on top of each other, flipped up out of the way onto his forehead like the visor of a helmet. Some of them were clear. One was blue. One, on the left, was a deep smoky brown. They caught the light and glittered as he moved his head, so that it seemed his eyes were full of small moons.
Behind all the glass, his eyes were pale and watery and blue. And very frightened.
"Who are they, Faber?" he said again.
"Friends, Master," said Faber. "I think. I hope." He held up the parcel by its loop of string, as if it might help. "I brought your things. The hairsprings. And the oil. And the candles, and the bread, and a twist of tea."
The old man did not look at the parcel. He was looking at Corin. At his long gray coat.
"Ministry," he said. His voice went thin and high. "That's a Ministry coat. Faber, that's a Ministry coat. What have you done? What have you—"
"No, Master. He's not—he is, but he isn't. He's not one of them."
"They're all one of them!" The old face in the hole had gone the color of paper. "They're all one of them, Faber, every one, I told you, I told you a hundred times—"
His hand came up over the edge of the hole. It was holding something. A long, thin, shining thing, like a knitting needle, but made of steel and sharpened to a point so fine that it caught the light along its whole length like a line of water. It was a clockmaker's tool. A broach, for opening holes in brass. It was not a weapon. But he was holding it like one, pointed down at them through the hole, and his hand was shaking so badly that the point of it went round and round in little circles in the air.
Corin did not move.
He stood very still at the bottom of the ladder with the lamp in his hand, and looked up at the shaking point of the broach, and the frightened eyes behind all that glass, and he thought about a man who had been hiding inside a stopped clock for a year, alone, with a brass dog for company, looking out at the river through a face of milky glass, and jumping every time a boat went by.
He did not take out the Ledger. He did not need it.
"Mister Rook," he said. "My name is Corin Vane. I work for the Ministry of Records. That's true. But they told me to stop looking into something, and I didn't stop, and I don't think they're very pleased with me." He paused. "I'm not here for you. I'm here because of a clock you made. With Lord Edmund Thorne. Thirty years ago. It ran backward yesterday afternoon at twenty-nine minutes past four. And it opened a drawer."
The shaking point of the broach went still.
Not slowly. All at once. As if something had caught hold of the old man's hand from inside and held it.
"It ran?" said Thaddeus Rook.
"It ran," said Lyra.
She stepped forward, out from behind Corin, into the gray light at the foot of the ladder. She did not say who she was. She did not need to. She stood there in her filthy brown coat with her short black hair full of snow and her chin up, and Minim the brass dog sat down beside her boot again and leaned against it. And the old man in the hole above looked down at her face, through all his lenses, for a long, long time.
Then, very slowly, he reached up with his free hand and pulled down one of the lenses from his forehead. The blue one. He set it over his right eye, click, and looked at her through it. Then he pulled down another one, a clear one, click, on top of the blue. And looked again.
And then the broach fell out of his fingers.
It fell straight down through the hole, turning over and over, glittering, and hit the stone floor beside Lyra's boot with a small bright ting, and bounced, and lay still.
"Edmund's nose," said Thaddeus Rook, in a voice that was hardly a voice at all. "Oh. Oh, my dear. You've got Edmund's nose."
The room at the top of the ladder was the strangest room Corin had ever been in. He was almost sure of it, even with all the rooms he had lost.
It was behind the face.
The whole of one wall, the river wall, was the great round clock face, seen from the back. A huge circle of thick milky glass, taller than three men standing on each other's shoulders, set in a web of black iron bars like the spokes of a wheel. The gray daylight came through it, soft and white and pearly, and filled the whole room with a strange, even, shadowless glow, like the light inside a cloud. And on the glass, standing out dark against all that whiteness, were the numbers. Backward. Twelve great black iron numbers in a ring, each one as tall as a man, all of them the wrong way round, the way writing looks in a mirror. The twelve was at the top, where it should be. But the three was on the left, and the nine was on the right, and the seven and the five had changed places.
And across the middle of it all, from the center of the glass straight up to the backward twelve, lay the shadows of the two great hands. Two long black bars, one on top of the other, like the shadow of a ladder.
Rook lived here.
He had made a home in it, the way a mouse makes a home inside the wall of a house. There was a bed against one wall, made of planks laid over two old sea chests, with a straw mattress on it and a heap of blankets and an old sailcloth, gray and stiff, over the top of everything. There was a stove. A small, fat, black iron stove with a crooked pipe that went up and out through a hole in the clock face itself, right through the middle of the backward eight, where one pane of the milky glass had been taken out and the pipe pushed through and the gap stuffed round with rags. The stove was lit. It was ticking softly as it got hot, and the warm air round it shivered.
There was a table, with a candle on it, stuck in its own wax, and a tin plate, and a tin cup, and half a loaf of bread gone hard. There was a chair, only one, with a cushion on it made from a folded coat. There was a basin. A bucket. A row of hooks with clothes on them, coats, a great many coats, all old.
And there was the bench.
It ran along the whole of the wall opposite the clock face, under the great iron beam that held up the roof, and it was the most beautiful thing in the room. It was made of dark old wood, polished smooth by years of hands, and it was covered from end to end with tools. Not thrown down anyhow, the way tools are thrown down on most benches. Laid out. In rows. Each one in its own place. Files and tweezers and screwdrivers, from the biggest to the smallest, so small at the end of the row that they were like the legs of insects. Tiny saws. Tiny hammers. Pliers with jaws no bigger than a grain of rice. Lenses on brass stands. Little lamps. Rows of saucers full of wheels and springs and screws, sorted, every one, by size and by kind. Above the bench, on a long shelf, there were drawers. Dozens of little wooden drawers, each with a brass knob and a paper label, and on the labels, in a big, sprawling, shaking hand, things like hairsprings, fine and pivots, steel and jewels, red and wheels, 40 teeth.
And on the bench, in among the tools, there were the things he had made.
Corin saw them one at a time. It took him a while, because they were small, and they did not look like anything much at first. And then they moved.
A bird. A sparrow, made of brass and copper, with a tiny copper beak and two round black glass eyes, sitting on the edge of a saucer. It turned its head as Corin came up through the hole in the floor, quick and jerky, the way a real sparrow turns its head. It looked at him with one eye. Then the other. Then it hopped, twice, along the edge of the bench, tik tik, and fluffed out its brass feathers, which were tiny thin plates laid over each other like the scales of a fish, and settled down again.
A mouse. A little silver mouse, no bigger than his thumb, sitting up on its back legs beside a tin of screws, washing its face with its two front paws. Over and over. Very fast. Very busy.
A fish. A small golden fish, swimming round and round in a glass bowl of water on the windowsill of the backward three. Its tail went from side to side. Its fins rippled. It swam to the side of the bowl and looked out at them through the glass with its round golden eyes, and opened and shut its mouth.
And beetles. A whole row of them, along the very back of the bench, against the wall. Brass beetles, the size of walnuts, with backs of bright enamel. Green and blue and red and gold and a purple like the dye in the vats. Some of them finished. Some of them only half done. None of them moving. They sat in their row, side by side, very neat, in the white light, like the beetles on a little girl's windowsill in the sun.
Lyra stopped at the top of the ladder.
She stood there with one hand still on the iron rail, and looked at the row of beetles. And did not say anything at all.
Thaddeus Rook was very tall. That was a surprise, after the face in the hole. He was the tallest old man Corin had ever seen, nearly as tall as Oren, but so thin and so bent that he did not seem it. He was bent forward from the waist, like a question mark, or like a tree that has grown up on a windy hill, so that his head and his great cloud of white hair hung out in front of him and his long thin arms dangled. He wore a great many clothes. All of them old. A gray knitted cap pulled down over the back of his hair, which made the front of it stand up even more. Two coats, one on top of the other, both too big. A long quilted dressing gown on top of the coats, plum-colored, faded, tied round the middle with a piece of rope. A scarf. Fingerless gloves of gray wool, with holes in them.
And his hands shook.
Corin saw that at once, now that the broach was not in them. They shook all the time. Not a little tremble. A great, heavy, helpless shaking, the hands going up and down and side to side at the ends of his thin arms like leaves in a wind, so that he could not keep them still for a moment. He held them together, in front of his chest, one inside the other, as if he were trying to hold them down. It did not help. They shook anyway.
He stood in front of Lyra with his shaking hands and his glittering lenses and his white hair, and looked at her.
"You're Aurelian's girl," he said. "The one who kept the beetles." His voice cracked. "You were four the first time you held the lamp for me. You stood on a chair by the clock, like Faber used to, and you were so proud. You held it with both hands and you never shook once. And you were eleven the last time. You told me you were too old for beetles now. And then you took the blue one anyway, and put it in your pocket when you thought I wasn't looking." He swallowed. "Faber says you're well. Every week. Are you well, my dear? Are you really?"
Lyra looked at him.
Corin waited for her to say yes. That was what people said. Yes, I'm well, thank you. He had heard her say it to Mrs. Hobb. To Madame Hesper. To her aunt.
"No," said Lyra. "Not really."
The old man's long face crumpled.
"No," he said. "No. I didn't think so. I didn't think Faber could know." He held his shaking hands together tighter. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. About your father. About all of it. Faber told me. I couldn't come. I couldn't—I'm not brave. I've never been brave. Edmund was brave. I only made the wheels."
"It's all right," said Lyra.
"It isn't."
"No," said Lyra. "It isn't. But you can't help it now. And I didn't come for that." She put her hand up to her collar, and drew out the brown bootlace, and the small blue stone on the end of it, and held it up in the white light from the clock face. "I came about this."
Rook went quite still.
All of him. His thin bent body, his long arms, his white hair. Even his hands. For one long moment, looking at the blue stone, his hands did not shake at all.
Then he put one of them out. Very slowly. Palm up. And Lyra, after a moment, lifted the bootlace over her head, and laid the stone in his palm.
And Corin saw a thing he would write in his Ledger that night, and underline twice.
The moment the stone touched Rook's palm, his hand stopped shaking.
Not slowly. Not a little. It stopped. All at once. As if somebody had laid a heavy weight on it. His long thin fingers, in their holey gray gloves, closed round the little blue stone, and they were as still as stone themselves. As still as the hands of a man asleep. He held it up. He pulled down one lens, and then another, and then a third, click, click, click, until his right eye was looking through a whole stack of glass, and he brought the stone up close to it, and turned it in his still fingers in the white light, slowly, round and round.
"Karst blue," he said softly. And his voice had changed too. It was not thin and high anymore. It was low and steady and sure, the voice of a man who knows exactly what he is looking at. "From the deep mines. Under the mountains, past the sea. There are only a few stones like this in all the world, and Edmund brought two of them home, in his pocket, from the war. Cut from one piece. Twins." He turned it again. "I made the settings. For both. This one, and its brother. And Edmund said, Thaddeus, one is for my boy, to wear. And one is for the clock, to keep."
He lowered the stone, and looked over the top of his lenses at Lyra.
"Its brother's cracked now, isn't it," he said. "Aurelian's. Cracked straight across, the night of Oakhaven."
Lyra stared at him.
"How do you know about Oakhaven?" she said.
Rook did not answer. He looked down at the blue stone in his still hand. And then he looked at the brass dog, Minim, sitting on the floor beside Lyra's boot, with its green glass eyes fixed on the stone and its little brass ears pricked up as high as they would go.
"That's why he went to you," he said slowly. "Minim. That's why he sat down by you and leaned. He's never done that for anyone. Not even Faber." He almost smiled. "I made him from a tune, you see. Every one of my little ones, I made from a tune. That's what a Gearwright does. And Minim's tune was one that Edmund used to whistle. At his bench. Every day. For two years. While we built the clock, and set the stones, and cut the drawer for this." He held up the blue stone. "Minim smells it on you. Edmund. Or something of him. It's in the stone."
"What was the tune?" said Corin.
Rook turned his head and looked at him. His pale blue eyes behind the stack of glass were very sad.
"I don't know," he said. "I'm sorry, young man. I don't know. I gave it to Minim. I gave it away, to make him go. And once you give a tune away like that, it's gone." He shook his white head slowly. "I don't know any tunes now. Not one. I gave them all away, a long time ago. To my little ones. And to… to other things." His voice wavered. "I can't hear music anymore. Not as music. When the busker plays on the Lantern Bridge, I hear a noise like a door creaking. When the bells ring for the Archon's festival, I hear iron being dropped down a stair. When a girl sings in the street, under my window, on a summer night…" He stopped. "I hear that she is making a sound. That's all. I know it must be beautiful. Everyone stops to listen. But I don't know why."
The little silver mouse on the bench had stopped washing its face. It sat up very straight on its back legs, with its paws folded, and looked at him.
Corin looked at the mouse. And at the sparrow, and the fish going round and round in its bowl, and the row of beetles in the white light. And he thought about a song. Three notes. A fisherman, and the moon. A song so old that every child in Oakhaven had hummed it, and a dead scribe's Ash had kept it, and a big man in a carriage hummed it without knowing why.
He hummed it.
Very softly. Just the three notes. The ones he knew. Mm, mm, mmm.
The sparrow turned its head. The fish stopped swimming. Minim's ears swiveled toward him, both at once.
Rook did not move. He stood with the blue stone in his still hand and looked at Corin with polite, puzzled, kindly eyes, the way a man looks at somebody speaking to him in a language he does not know.
"Is that a tune?" he said gently. "I'm sorry. I can't tell. It sounds to me like the wind in a chimney." He tilted his head. "Was it a nice one?"
Corin opened his mouth. Then he shut it again.
"Yes," he said. "I think it was. I think it was a very nice one."
A Box of Wheels
Faber made tea.
He did it without being asked, the way a man does a thing he has done a hundred times in the same small room. He untied the parcel on the end of the bench and took out a twist of brown paper and a tin of candles and a loaf and a small brown bottle of oil and a little flat box of hairsprings, and he laid each thing in its own place, the bread on the table, the candles on the shelf, the oil and the springs in two of the little wooden drawers over the bench. Then he filled a black iron kettle from the bucket and set it on the fat little stove, and while it began to tick and grumble he found cups. There were only two. A tin one and a china one with no handle. He looked at them, and then at the four visitors, and then he went to the shelf and took down a jam jar and a small brass bowl that might once have been part of a clock, and wiped them out with the corner of his apron.
Rook sat in the only chair. He had not wanted to. He had tried to give it to Lyra, and Lyra had said no, and he had stood there shaking, holding the back of it, until Faber came and took him by the elbow, gently, and sat him down. Now he sat folded up in it, very tall even sitting, with his knees up near his chest and his great cloud of white hair standing up round his head in the white light from the clock face. Lyra sat on the end of the plank bed. Juno sat on the floor by the stove, cross-legged, with Minim the brass dog lying beside her and her hand resting on its warm ticking back. Corin stood.
He did not know why he stood. Perhaps because he wanted to see the room. Perhaps because the shadows of the two great hands lay across the floor at his feet, black and straight, like the lines of a road, and he did not want to sit on them.
Rook still had the blue stone. He held it in his lap, in both hands, and his hands were quite still. He did not seem to notice.
"He came to my shop on a Tuesday," he said. "In the spring. Thirty-two years ago."
Nobody had asked him. He began anyway. His voice was low and slow and steady, the voice of the man who had looked at the stone, not the voice of the man in the hole.
"I had a shop then. On Bellfounders' Row. Up behind the Exchange. A good shop. Bigger than Faber's. With my name over the door in gold. T. Rook. Clocks, Watches & Fine Movements. I had three boys working for me. Faber was the youngest. He'd been with me three years." The pale eyes went to Faber, at the stove, and something almost like a smile moved the long thin mouth. "He was terrible. He dropped everything. He dropped a mainspring once and it went off like a gun and broke my window."
Faber, at the stove, did not turn round. But the back of his neck went red.
"And Lord Edmund Thorne came in," said Rook, "on a wet Tuesday, in the spring, with the rain running off his hat, and put two blue stones on my counter. Just like that. Out of his coat pocket. As if they were a pair of marbles." He looked down at the stone in his lap. "I'd never seen anything like them. I'd never seen anything like him. He was a big man. Loud. He laughed so you could hear it in the street. He had ink on his cuffs and oil on his fingers and a hole in the elbow of a coat that must have cost more than my whole shop. He couldn't keep still. Not for a moment. He picked up everything on my counter and looked at it and put it down in the wrong place, and he talked the whole time, and his hands were always moving, always, as if they wanted to be making something and didn't know what yet." Rook was quiet for a moment. "He said he'd come back from across the sea with these two stones and a head full of something he wanted to build, and he didn't know how to build it. And somebody had told him that Thaddeus Rook could make a clock that thought."
"Could you?" said Juno, from the floor.
Rook looked down at her. At the brass dog under her hand.
"Yes," he said simply. "I could."
The kettle boiled. Faber poured the water into a brown teapot with a chipped spout and stood it on the stove to brew, and the smell of it came up into the cold white room, dark and bitter and warm.
"A Gearwright," said Rook, "is a clockmaker who burns."
He said it carefully. Plainly. The way you would explain something to a child, or to a man who forgets.
"That's all it is. Anyone can make a clock go. You wind a spring, and the spring pushes a wheel, and the wheel pushes another wheel, and the hands go round. That's only pushing. A clock like that doesn't know anything. It doesn't know it's a clock. It goes until it runs down, and then it stops, and it doesn't mind." He turned the blue stone over in his still fingers. "But if you take something that you love, and you burn it, the way a mage burns, and you put the light of it into the wheels while you're building them… then the clock has something inside it. Not a mind like ours. Something smaller. Something simpler. A kind of wanting. A clock with a little bit of mind in it will want one thing, very much, and it will do that one thing over and over, for as long as it lives, and it will never get tired, and it will never forget."
He looked at the little silver mouse on the bench, sitting up washing its face.
"Bramble there wants to be clean. That's all. I made him from a skipping song. He's been washing his face for eleven years." He looked at the sparrow. "Tuppence wants to see everything. I made her from a song the fishwives sang in the market behind this building, gutting herring in the cold. She sits at the window and watches the river, and when a boat comes she tells me." He looked at the golden fish, going round and round in its bowl. "And the fish wants to swim. I don't remember what I made the fish from. Something about water, I think."
"What does Minim want?" said Juno.
Rook looked at the brass dog. Minim looked back at him with its green glass eyes, and its stiff little tail went tick, tock, once, on the boards.
"Minim wants to keep the door," said Rook. "And to know who's a friend."
Juno took her hand off the dog's back. Then she put it back again, slowly, and left it there.
"And Edmund's clock?" said Corin. "What did it want?"
Rook was quiet for a long time.
"To keep a secret," he said at last. "To keep a secret, and to give it up to the right person, at the right time, and to nobody else in all the world." He breathed out. "That's a very hard thing to want. It's much harder than washing your face. It took me eleven tunes to make it want that. And it took Edmund one."
He reached inside his plum-colored dressing gown, and inside the first coat under it, and inside the second coat under that, and took out a book.
It was small. Smaller than Corin's hand. It was bound in brown leather gone soft and black with years of being carried, and its corners were rounded with wear, and there was a piece of red ribbon sewn into its spine for a bookmark, faded nearly to pink. Rook held it in his lap for a moment beside the blue stone. Then he held it out. Not to Lyra. To Corin.
"You keep a book," he said. "Don't you, young man? I saw it. In your coat. I saw you touch it on the ladder, when you didn't know what to say. The way I touch mine."
Corin took the little book. He opened it.
The pages were thin and yellow and covered in the same big, sprawling, shaking hand as the labels on the drawers. But the first pages did not shake. The first pages were steady and black and careful, in a young man's hand, and they got older as they went on. Each page had a heading. And under each heading, a list.
The first page said, GIVEN.
And under it:
1. My mother's washing song. The one she sang at the tub on Mondays. — To the first clock I ever made that thought. A kitchen clock, for her. It wanted to wake her gently. Sold when she died.
2. The hymn at Low Chapel, harvest Sunday. — To the bell machine at St. Ivo's. It wanted to ring true.
3. The tune the carter whistled, the summer I was nine. — To a music box for a little girl on the Hill. I never knew her name.
And on, and on. Page after page. Tune after tune. Some of them had little drawings beside them, in pencil. A clock. A bird. A wheel. Some of them had a date. Some of them had nothing but the name of the tune, and what it had been given to, and a line under it.
Corin turned the pages slowly. He did not know what he was looking for until he found it.
It was a page of its own. Near the middle. The heading on it was written bigger than the others, and underlined twice, and the hand was a man's hand now, not a young man's, steady and sure.
E. T.'s CLOCK. Twelve stones. Eleven tunes. One his.
And under it:
1. Rose. — My mother's lullaby. The last of her I had.
2. Amber. — The harvest dance at Low Farm, when I was courting Annis.
3. Green. — The song the lark makes, that Annis tried to whistle and could not.
4. Gray-blue. — The rain song. Annis sang it at the window. I don't know the words.
5. Violet. — Our wedding waltz.
6. Pale gold. — The bells of St. Ivo's, Sunday morning. All twelve.
7. Milky white. — What Annis sang to the baby. Six weeks. I could not bear to keep it.
8. Sea-green. — The girls' song on the Tide Hall steps.
9. Copper. — The hammer song at the bell foundry, my father's shop.
10. Silver-gray. — The last tune my father played on his fiddle.
11. Flame blue. — The song Edmund and I sang together, very drunk, the night the clock first ran.
12. White. — Edmund's. He would not tell me what.
Corin read it twice. Then he looked up.
Rook was watching him. The pale blue eyes behind all the glass were calm. Quite calm. It was the calm of a man who had read this page so many times that it could not hurt him anymore, the way a scar stops hurting when it has been touched enough.
"I don't know any of them," he said. "Not now. Not one. I couldn't hum you my mother's lullaby to save my life. I couldn't tell you how our wedding waltz went, or what Annis sang to the baby." He looked down at the blue stone in his still hands. "But I know that I gave them. I wrote it down. Every one. So that I'd always know where they went." He lifted his eyes to Corin's face. "That's what the book is for. Isn't it? Not to remember. You can't remember what's gone. Only to know where it went."
Corin's hand went to his own coat. To the stiff new book over his heart.
"Yes," he said. "That's what it's for."
Faber gave out the tea. Rook got the china cup with no handle, and held it in both hands, and it rattled against his teeth when he drank. Lyra got the tin cup. Juno got the jam jar and burned her fingers on it and did not complain. Corin got the little brass bowl, and held it, and did not drink, and the steam came up past his face into the cold white air.
"Tell me about the drawer," said Lyra.
She had not touched her tea. She was sitting very straight on the end of the plank bed, with the tin cup in her lap, and her gray eyes were on Rook's face.
"And the time. Four, two, nine, three. And the twelfth stone. Tell me all of it. Please."
Rook set his cup down on the floor beside the chair.
"We built it in Edmund's workshop," he said. "In the house on Crown Walk. Number 3. At the back, down the half stair, where the old laundry had been. It took two years. I went there every day. Faber came with me, and held the lamp, and dropped things." Faber, by the stove, made a small sound that might have been a laugh. "And Edmund worked beside us. He was good. He was very good, for a lord. He had the hands for it. He only didn't have the patience. He wanted it all done yesterday."
He was quiet for a moment.
"And there was a girl," he said. "Who sat on a stool by the door."
Lyra's cup went still in her lap.
"Eleven. Twelve. A thin fair little thing in a white pinafore, with her hair in a long gold plait down her back. Edmund's daughter. His only daughter. Miss Cressida." Rook's long face did not change. "She came every afternoon, after her lessons, and sat on the stool and watched. For two years. She never got bored. That was the odd thing. Children always get bored in a workshop. She never did. She sat and she watched every single thing we did, with her hands folded in her lap, and she asked questions. Very polite questions. What's that for, Papa? What does that wheel do? Why is that stone a different color? And Edmund would laugh and pull her plait and tell her." He paused. "Nearly everything. He told her nearly everything."
"But not about the drawer," said Corin.
Rook turned his head and looked at him.
"No," he said. "Not about the drawer."
He picked up the blue stone from his lap, and held it up by its bootlace, and it turned slowly in the white light, deep and clear and bottomless.
"The drawer was the last thing we made. Under the twelfth claw. A little silver drawer no bigger than a matchbox. Edmund cut the letters on the front of it himself, with my smallest graver, in a writing I'd never seen. Foreign. Little hooks and lines. I asked him what they said. He said, It's a promise, Thaddeus. That's all you need to know." Rook lowered the stone. "And Miss Cressida, on her stool by the door, asked what the drawer was for. And Edmund didn't laugh, that time. He looked at her for a long moment. And he said, It's for someone who isn't born yet, Cressie. And he turned back to the bench."
Nobody said anything.
"She didn't come for three days after that," said Rook. "And on the fourth day, I came down to the workshop early, before Edmund, and the door was open. And Miss Cressida was standing at the bench in her white pinafore. With a hatpin. A long one, with a pearl on the end. Trying to pick the little silver drawer open."
He looked at Lyra.
"She heard me on the stair. And she turned round. And she smiled at me." His voice was very quiet. "I'll never forget it. Of all the things I've lost. She smiled and she said, I only wanted to see, Mister Rook. And she put the hatpin back in her hair. And she walked past me and up the half stair. And she never came to the workshop again."
The fish in its bowl on the windowsill swam round and round. The stove ticked. Down in the great room below them, in the dark, the enormous silent pendulum hung still.
"I made the drawer so it couldn't be picked," said Rook. "After that. I took it out and made it again. That's what the clock was for, in the end. That's what the eleven tunes were for. A drawer that wouldn't open to a hatpin, or a knife, or a crowbar, or a mage. Only to the time. Its own time. Its own day and its own minute." He touched the stone. "Edmund didn't choose the time. He said, Aurelian will choose it. When he needs to. A Thorne will stop the hands at the minute, and the clock will hold it, and then it will open only to that minute, told back to it. So I built it that way. Any Thorne could set the time once. By stopping the hands with his own finger, on the minute, and saying it to the clock. And after that it would only open to that time. Told on the glass. Four taps."
"Four, two, nine, three," said Lyra, very low.
"If that's what Aurelian chose." Rook nodded slowly. "I never knew. I wasn't there." He looked at her. "Was it you, my dear? Who told it?"
"My father told me," said Lyra. "He tapped it on the window. Every day. For two years. After he burned." Her voice did not shake. "He couldn't remember my name. But he remembered that."
Rook looked at her for a long, long moment.
Then he took off his spectacles. All of them, the whole heavy brass frame with its stack of lenses, and held them in his lap, and put his other hand over his eyes. He sat like that, very still, for a long time. And when he took his hand away his long brown-spotted face was wet.
"Then he was brave too," he said. "Like his father. Oh, my dear. I'm so glad. I'm so glad somebody was."
"And the twelfth stone?" said Corin.
Rook put his spectacles back on, carefully, and blinked through them.
"The twelfth was Edmund's," he said. "Not mine. I made the claw for it. Empty. And Edmund said he'd find the right thing to put in it himself, when he was ready. A Thorne thing. His own." He shook his head slowly. "It took him a long time. Years. The clock stood on Crown Walk with eleven stones, and the claw at twelve empty, and every winter when I came to clean it Edmund would say, Not yet, Thaddeus. Not yet. And then one winter I came, and there it was."
"White," said Lyra.
Rook turned and looked at her.
"I remember it," she said. "It was the brightest of all. The last to go out at night. The first to light in the morning."
"White," said Rook softly. "Yes. So white it hurt to look at. Whiter than milk. Whiter than snow. I asked him what he'd burned for it. And he only smiled and said, Something I'll never need again, Thaddeus." He was quiet. "That was the winter before he died. He died at his bench, they said. In the workshop. Late at night. With a little brass wheel in his hand." Rook's still hands closed round the blue stone. "I never knew what he was making. Nobody ever told me. There was nothing on the bench when I came for the funeral. Somebody had swept it clean."
"And the white stone?"
"It was there," said Rook. "Every winter. In its claw. Shining. Every winter for eleven more years, when I came to clean the clock. And then I didn't come anymore." He looked at Lyra. "Is it gone now?"
"It's gone," said Lyra. "The claw's empty. My aunt thinks my father took it out. Before the clock went to Niles. She said—" Lyra's mouth tightened. "She said he'd never have let it go with the twelfth still in it."
"She asked about it, then," said Rook. "Miss Cressida."
"She offered Niles a hundred gold for the clock. In May. And she asked about the twelfth stone."
Rook nodded slowly. He did not look surprised. He looked like a man hearing that a storm he had seen coming for a long time had finally reached the edge of town.
"She came to me too," he said. "Once. Nine years ago. To my shop on Bellfounders' Row. A grown woman by then. In a white coat. Smelling of flowers." His thin mouth twisted. "She smiled at me just the same. She said, Mister Rook. How lovely. Do you remember me? I used to sit on the stool. She bought a little silver watch. And while I was wrapping it, she asked me, as if she'd only just thought of it, whether Papa had ever told me what was in the twelfth stone."
"What did you say?"
"The truth. That he never did." Rook looked down at his hands. "She thanked me. And took her watch. And went away."
He was silent for a moment.
"And a month later," he said, "the Inquisitor came."
