Chapter 14: Thaddeus Rook, Part 1 of 5: Snow on Tallow Street
Snow on Tallow Street
Corin woke because the world had gone quiet.
That was the first thing. Before he opened his eyes, before he knew where he was or who he was, he knew that something was wrong with the sound of the morning. Tallow Street was never quiet. Not ever. At this hour there should have been carts. The iron wheels of the brewer's dray going down to the river, loud as a falling shelf of pans. The milk girl calling at the corner. Doors banging. Boots on the cobbles. Mrs. Marta's voice two floors below, telling somebody exactly what she thought of them.
Instead there was nothing. A thick, soft, padded nothing, as if somebody had wrapped the whole street in a blanket while he slept.
He opened his eyes.
The ceiling was gray. A low, sloping gray ceiling of old plaster with a long crack in it shaped like a river on a map. He did not know it. For a moment, lying on his back on the narrow iron bed with the thin blanket pulled up to his chin, he did not know anything at all. Not the ceiling. Not the room. Not the cold, which was very great, and which had crept in under the blanket and settled in his feet like two stones.
Then his right hand moved, by itself, the way it always did, and found the book.
It was on the crate beside the bed, where it always was. A stiff, dark-brown book, quite new, with a cover that still smelled of leather and glue. His fingers knew the shape of it before his head did. He picked it up and held it against his chest, and lay there for a moment longer, breathing, waiting for his heart to slow down.
Then he sat up and opened it.
The first page said, in big black letters, in an untidy sloping hand he knew was his own because it was the only hand he knew:
READ THIS FIRST.
Your name is Corin.
You were born in Oakhaven.
You had a younger sister named Elara.
You used to fear the dark.
And underneath, smaller, added later in a different ink:
You are an investigator for the Ministry of Records. You harvest the Ash of dead mages. You forget things. This is normal. You live at Number 14 Tallow Street, the top room.
He read it twice. Then he turned the page and kept reading.
It took a long time. It always took a long time. There was a great deal of it now, and some of the pages were crowded so close with small writing that he had to hold the book near the window to see it. He read about Pell, the scribe, dead on a rubbish heap in the Ink Quarter. He read about a jar, and a girl with a sword, and a woman with writing all over her hands. He read about a clock. He read about a list sewn into the collar of a green coat. Those I Owe. He read about a woman with pale gold hair and a red mouth.
And then he came to the last pages, the ones from last night, and his hand went still on the paper.
The writing there was big and torn. The pencil had gone through the page in two places.
CRESSIDA THORNE KILLED DOCTOR ASKE. GORSE PUT HIM IN THE RIVER. A MAN WITH A LEFT-TURNING CUP TOOK HIM FIRST. BERTRAM SAW. BERTRAM IS DEAD.
SHE KNOWS LYRA KNOWS.
IT IS SNOWING.
He looked up from the book, at the little square window under the slope of the roof.
It was white.
Not gray, the way it usually was, with the soot of a thousand chimneys on it. White. A soft, deep, even white, like a sheet of new paper. He got up off the bed with the blanket round his shoulders and went across the cold boards in his stockings and looked out.
The city had gone.
That was how it seemed, for one long strange moment. The city he had read about in his book was a black city, a brown city, a city of soot and smoke and wet cobbles and dirty brick. This city was white. All of it. The roofs across the street were white, every slate of them hidden under a smooth thick coat, so that they looked soft, like pillows. The chimney pots wore little white caps. The gutters were lined with white, and along the edges of them hung rows of icicles, thin and clear and sharp as needles, dripping very slowly in the gray morning light. The street below, the whole length of it, from the pump at the corner down to the bend where the hill dropped toward the river, was buried under snow as high as a man's ankle, and nobody had walked in it yet. Not one footprint. Not one wheel mark. It lay there smooth and clean and untouched, and the doorsteps were white, and the railings were white, and the old iron lamp-post outside the house opposite had a little white hat and a white scarf of snow along its arm.
And it was still falling. Slowly now. Big, soft, lazy flakes, drifting down out of a low sky the color of a pewter plate, turning over and over as they came, the way a leaf turns when it falls from a tree on a windless day. He watched one come down past the window. It came close to the glass, close enough that he could see it was not a blob at all but a tiny star, with six arms, each arm covered with smaller arms, like the frost on a cold window. Then it touched the glass, and was gone. Melted. A drop of water, sliding down.
He stood there a long time.
He did not remember snow. He knew that, standing there, with a strange sinking feeling, the way you know a step is missing in the dark. He knew the word. He knew it was cold, and white, and fell from the sky in winter. But he did not remember ever seeing it. Not once. Not as a child, throwing it. Not as a young man, walking through it. Somewhere, in some year he had lost, he must have stood at a window like this and watched it fall. Everyone had. But that morning was gone, with all the others, and this was the first snow of his life.
It was very beautiful.
Before the first snow, said a woman's voice in his head. A warm voice. A smiling voice. That's what we agreed.
He turned away from the window.
There was a jar on the crate beside the bed.
He had not seen it when he woke. He saw it now. A plain glass jar, the kind they used for pickled onions at the market, with a wax seal over the top and a paper label pasted on the side. On the label, in his own big untidy hand, it said:
BERTRAM ASHCOMBE. STEWARD. FORGIVE ME.
In the bottom of the jar there was a thin layer of something soft and gray. No thicker than a finger. It shone a little, very faintly, in the cold light from the window, the way the inside of a shell shines.
He looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked down at the Ledger in his hands, and turned back a few pages, and read again what he had written about the old man in the rooms over the saddler's shop. About the tobacco tin under the third stone of the hearth. About a list, in a careful old hand, with ticks and crosses.
Owed.
He put out his hand and touched the cold glass of the jar. Just once. With one finger.
"Good morning," he said to it. He did not know why. It seemed rude not to.
He cannot hear you, said a voice.
This voice was not in the room. It was not in his head, either, exactly. It was somewhere in between. A dry, thin, old voice, like paper being folded very slowly, and it came from the long thin silver rod that lay on the crate beside the jar, with a spiral of fine lines carved round and round it from end to end.
Corin did not jump. He had read about the voice in the Ledger. Many times. It had its own page, near the front, in letters even bigger than his name.
THE VOICE IN THE ROD IS MALAKOR. HE IS VERY OLD AND VERY DEAD. HE HELPS YOU. DO NOT TRUST HIM ALL THE WAY.
"I know he can't hear me," Corin said.
Then why speak?
"Because he kept a secret for two years with nobody to tell it to." Corin took his hand away from the jar. "Somebody should say good morning to him."
Malakor was silent for a moment.
You are a strange boy, he said at last. You were a strange boy the first time I met you, and you have forgotten that meeting eleven times, and every time you come back exactly as strange as before. A pause. Get dressed. It is cold, and you are standing in your stockings, and you have a great deal to do today. You have a clock to ask about. And you have an appointment at half past four.
"The Bourse," said Corin.
The Bourse. The kitchen door. Bring your gloves. The thin voice was dry. Lady Vivienne does not send messages twice.
He got dressed in the cold.
It took a long time, because his fingers would not work properly, and because the water in the jug on the washstand had a skin of ice on top that he had to break with the handle of his comb. He washed his face in it and gasped. Then he dried his face and looked at it in the little square of cracked mirror nailed to the wall over the washstand.
A thin face. Not old. Not young. Dark hair that needed cutting. Gray eyes with a little blue frost round the very edges of them, like the rim of ice on the water jug, that the Ledger said came from the Ash and would never go away. A small white scar on his chin that the Ledger did not explain.
That is you, he told himself, the way he did every morning. That is Corin Vane. And he looked at the face until it looked back at him, and nodded, and began to feel like his own.
He put on his shirt and his trousers and two pairs of stockings, one over the other. He put on his waistcoat and his long gray Ministry coat, and then, because the cold was the kind that got into your bones and stayed there, he took the thin gray blanket off the bed and folded it into a long strip and wound it round his neck like a scarf. He put the Ledger into the inside pocket of the coat, on the left side, over his heart, where it always went. He slid the rod into the long narrow pocket that ran down the lining on the right. He put the little brass lamp in his side pocket.
Then he looked at the gloves.
They lay on the end of the bed, where he must have put them last night, so that he would see them. A pair of fine white cotton gloves, a footman's gloves, very clean, with a tiny darn on the left thumb. The Ledger said a footman called Tam had put a note inside them at a party in a great house on the Hill. The note was folded into the back of the Ledger now. He took it out and read it again.
Thursday. The Bourse. Ask for Tam at the kitchen door.
And below that, very small, a little drawing. An eye. Open.
He put the gloves carefully in his other side pocket, so they would stay clean. Then he went out, and down the stairs.
The stairs were dark and narrow and steep, and they creaked on every step, and on the third landing someone had scratched two words into the plaster of the wall, low down, where a child might have done it, or a man sitting on the step.
WE REMEMBER.
Under the words there was a little bird on a string. He had read about it. He looked at it as he passed, the way you look at a friend's face in a crowd, and went on down.
Round the frame of his own door, at the top of the stairs, there had been notes, the way there always were. Strips of brown paper and the backs of old handbills, pinned to the wood with long hatpins with little glass beads on the ends, and written in purple ink in a big firm hand. He had read them all before he came down. The newest one said:
Mrs. Marta. Purple door, back of the ground floor. Your landlady. Rent due Friday. Eat the stew.
So when he got to the bottom of the stairs, and went down the short dark passage that smelled of onions, and the purple door at the end of it flew open before he could touch the brass knocker, he knew who the woman in it was. She was very small and very old, so small that the top of her head came only to the middle of his chest, with a face like a dried apple, all wrinkles, and two small sharp black eyes like currants pushed into dough. Her white hair was pulled back into a bun so tight it lifted her eyebrows. She wore a black dress and a purple shawl, and over the shawl, because of the cold, an old gray blanket pinned at the front with a hatpin. She came out at him like a cork out of a bottle.
"There you are," said Mrs. Marta. "I heard you. I heard you up there walking about in your stockings on that cold floor like a fool. You'll catch your death. Here." She pushed a bowl into his hands. It was hot. Wonderfully hot. It was full of something brown and thick with lumps of potato and bits of onion floating in it, and a heel of bread was stuck in the side of it like a spoon. "Eat that. All of it. Standing up, if you have to, but eat it."
"Thank you, Mrs. Marta," said Corin.
"Don't thank me. Eat." She folded her arms over her shawl and watched him do it, the way a hen watches a chick. "You were up half the night. I heard that too. Writing. Scratch, scratch, scratch, till the fourth bell. And then talking." Her sharp little eyes went to his face. "Who were you talking to? There's no one up there."
Corin swallowed a mouthful of stew. It burned all the way down, and he was glad of it.
"An old man," he said. "He's dead."
Mrs. Marta looked at him for a long moment. She did not seem surprised. Very little, the Ledger said, ever seemed to surprise Mrs. Marta.
"Was he a good man?" she said.
Corin thought about it. About a tobacco tin, and a careful list, and Forgive me written on the back of a ticket in a hand that shook.
"I think he tried to be," he said. "I think he got very frightened. And then he tried again."
Mrs. Marta nodded slowly, as if that was the right answer. As if it was the only answer anybody could hope for.
"That's most of us," she said. "Eat your bread."
He ate his bread.
When he had finished, and given her back the bowl, and she had looked into it to make sure it was empty, she jerked her head at the front door.
"The girl's been," she said. "Juno. Our Juno. Out the back at first light, over the wall, in all this." She sniffed. "She said to tell you, the Thorne girl's up, and the big captain's with her, and there's been a letter. She said you'd know what that meant."
Corin did not. He took out the Ledger and found the page. Lyra Thorne. Rooms above Pruett's livery stable, off Linden Walk. Her father lives there with her. He burned his whole mind two years ago. Captain Oren Vance guards them.
"A letter," he said. "Did she say what kind?"
Mrs. Marta's mouth went thin.
"She said it smelled of lilies," she said.
He opened the front door, and the cold came in at him like a wall.
It took his breath. It took it right out of his chest, so that he stood on the top step with his mouth open, gasping like a fish, and his eyes watered, and the tears were cold on his cheeks before they had gone an inch. The air smelled of nothing. That was the strange part. Tallow Street always smelled of something: the tannery up the hill, the river at the bottom, coal smoke, horse dung, frying fat, wet stone. Now the cold had frozen all of it, and the snow had covered the rest, and the air smelled clean and sharp and empty, like the inside of a new tin.
He went down the steps. His boot went into the snow, and the snow went crunch, a soft dry squeaking crunch like somebody biting into an apple, and the cold of it came up through the leather at once. He stood there with one foot in the snow and looked down at it. At the print his boot had made. The very first print on the whole white street.
Then he pulled the blanket up over his chin, and put his hands deep in his pockets, and began to walk.
The street was empty all the way down. The snow lay on everything. On the windowsills, in long soft rolls, like bread dough. On the iron railings of the area steps, in thin white lines along the top of each spike, so that the railings looked as if they had been drawn in chalk. On a barrow that somebody had left out by the pump, filling it up to the brim like a bowl of sugar. On the sign over the chandler's shop on the corner, so that the painted candle on it seemed to have a white flame.
A door opened as he passed, and a woman in a shawl looked out, and saw the snow, and said "Oh!" in a small surprised voice, like a child. Then she shut the door again.
At the bottom of the street, where it bent toward the river, he stopped.
From here you could see down over the roofs of the Lower Docks to the water. On a clear day it was a brown, busy, ugly view, all cranes and masts and warehouses and smoke. Today it was not ugly at all. The roofs went down in white steps, one below another, like a great white staircase, all the way to the river. The cranes stood up out of the snow like the bones of huge birds. The masts of the ships at anchor had white along every yard and rope, so that they looked as if they had been spun out of sugar. And the river itself was dark. Black. A long, slow, shining black ribbon winding through all that white, with gray mist lying on it and little plates of ice along its edges, turning slowly in the current.
And far off across it, through the falling snow, on the other bank, he could see a tall dark square tower with a great round face on it, pale and blank as the moon. A clock tower. Its hands were black. They stood together, both of them, pointing straight up.
He did not know what it was. He looked at it for a while, because it seemed important, the way things sometimes did, for no reason he could find. Then he turned his back on the river and went up the hill toward Linden Walk, his boots crunching in the clean snow, his breath going up in front of him in white clouds, like smoke from a chimney.
Behind him, the line of his footprints ran back down Tallow Street to the door of Number 14. One man's prints. Alone in all that white.
Anyone who wanted to know where he had gone would only have to follow them.
The Letter With the Lily Seal
Pruett's livery stable stood at the bottom of a short, crooked lane off Linden Walk, behind a tall wooden gate painted a dark green that had gone almost black with age. The gate was open. Somebody had already been out with a shovel, and a narrow path had been cut through the snow from the lane to the yard, with the snow piled up on both sides of it in two neat white walls as high as Corin's knee. The shovel was leaning against the gatepost. Its blade was still wet.
He went through.
The yard was small and square and cobbled, though you could not see the cobbles now. The snow lay over everything in a thick, smooth, rounded coat, so that the shapes underneath it had all gone soft. The stone trough in the middle was a long white loaf. The pump beside it wore a white hat, and from the end of its spout hung a single icicle as long as a man's hand, clear as glass, with a drop of water shining at the very tip of it, too cold to fall. All round the yard, the low brick buildings had white roofs, and under the eaves, where in summer the swallows had their nests, there were rows of icicles like the teeth of a comb.
The stable doors stood open. Warm air came out of them, and with it the smell of horse and hay and leather, thick and brown and sleepy. From the far end, in the dark, something big shifted its weight and blew out a long breath through its nose.
Corin looked in as he passed. In the last stall, an old gray mare with a long white face looked back at him over her half door. There was frost on the whiskers round her soft gray mouth. She had a blanket over her back, a thick checked horse blanket, red and brown, buckled under her belly. Someone had been careful about it. The brass plate on her stall door said PATIENCE.
"Hello," said Corin.
Patience looked at him with one large, dark, patient eye. Then she sighed, so that a little cloud of white went up from her nose, and turned back to her hay.
At the far side of the yard, a flight of worn stone steps with no rail went up the outside wall of the building to a green door. The steps had been swept. Every one of them. And on the top step, with a broom in one hand and a coal scuttle in the other, stood the biggest man Corin had ever seen.
He filled the little landing at the top of the steps the way a cart fills a narrow lane. He was very tall and very broad, with a chest like a barrel and shoulders that would have brushed both sides of a doorway. He was perhaps forty-five, with a short beard more gray than brown, cut close to his jaw, and short gray hair, and a face that had been handsome once, before someone had opened it with a blade from the left temple down across the cheek to the corner of the mouth. The scar was old and white and pulled his mouth a little crooked on that side. He wore a coat that had once been a uniform, dark green wool faded nearly to gray at the shoulders, with tarnished silver buttons and a badge on the left breast: a rose inside a ring of thorns, in thread gone the color of rust and straw. A short heavy sword hung at his hip. His face was red with cold. It was not a friendly face. But the eyes in it were quiet and watchful, and when they found Corin, standing in the snow at the foot of the steps, they did not change at all.
Corin's hand went to the Ledger. He did not need it. He had read the page that morning, by the window.
Captain Oren Vance. Big. Quiet. He guards Lyra and her father. He burns words to fight. Every fight takes some. Do not be hurt if he forgets your name. He is trying.
"Captain," said Corin.
Oren looked at him for a long moment more. Then he nodded. Once. Short.
"Vane," he said. His voice was deep and slow and rough, like a cart going over gravel. "You came. Good." He put down the coal scuttle, and the broom, and looked at the broom for a moment, frowning, as if he had wanted to say something about it and the word had gone. Then he let it go. "She's been up since four. Reading it. Over and over. Won't put it down." He jerked his square head at the green door behind him. "Go in. Talk to her. She won't talk to me."
"Reading what?"
Oren's face did something then. It was not a big thing. His mouth went flat, and a muscle moved at the side of his jaw, under the short gray beard. But it made Corin think of a dog he had once seen in the street, a big quiet dog that did not bark, going stiff all over when a stranger came near its door.
"The letter," Oren said. "It came at first light. A boy brought it. In a white coat." He paused. "It smells of… of…"
He stopped. He stood there on the top step with his big red hands hanging at his sides, and Corin watched him reach for the word. Watched him reach for it, and close his hand, and find nothing there.
The Ledger said Oren had burned that word himself, some days ago, in the white room behind the green door. He had used it to build a wall inside him, ready, in case Corin and his rod meant harm to Lord Thorne. He had not needed the wall. The word had gone anyway.
"Lilies," said Corin quietly.
Oren looked at him.
"Lilies," he said, slowly, as if he were tasting it. "Yes." Then, after a moment, very low: "I don't like them. I don't know why. I don't know what they are anymore. But I don't like them."
He stood aside, and Corin went in.
The long low room over the stable was warm. That was the first thing, after the cold of the yard: it was warm, warm as a kitchen, because the little iron stove in the corner was roaring, red at the seams, and someone had stuffed rags along the bottoms of both small square windows to keep out the drafts. The rag nearest the stove was steaming gently. The air smelled of coal smoke and hot iron and, very faintly, under all that, of something else. Something sweet and white and heavy, like flowers left too long in a vase.
Lyra Thorne was sitting at the plain wooden table in the middle of the room, under the dark old beams, with a sheet of paper in front of her.
She had not heard him come in. Or she had heard him and did not look up. She sat very straight, the way she always sat, with her shoulders back and her chin up, as if there were a book balanced on her head. She was wearing the plain gray wool dress with the mended cuffs, and over it, because the stove could only do so much, an old man's gray cardigan with the elbows out. Her short black hair was a mess. It stood up at the back as if she had been running her hands through it all night. Her face was white. Not pale. White, like the snow outside, and there were dark marks under her eyes, like thumbprints of soot.
Around her neck, on a brown bootlace tied in a hard knot, hung a small blue stone.
It was round, and smooth, and no bigger than the end of a child's thumb. It hung just at the hollow of her throat, against the gray wool, and it was the deepest, clearest blue he had ever seen. Not the blue of the sky. The blue of very deep water. The blue you see when you look down over the side of a boat into the sea, on a still day, and the light goes down and down and never finds the bottom. And somewhere in the heart of it, very small, very faint, there was a spark of light, like a star seen at the bottom of a well.
The Ledger said it had come out of a tiny silver drawer in a clock yesterday afternoon. The Ledger said it had been made as one of a pair.
Corin pulled out the other chair and sat down across from her.
She still did not look up. She pushed the paper across the table to him with two fingers, the way you might push away a plate of food you did not want.
He looked at it.
It was a single sheet of thick cream-colored paper, very fine, with a soft rough edge, the kind of paper that cost more than a docker earned in a day. It had been folded twice, and the folds were sharp, and on the back, where it had been sealed, there was a broken blob of white wax. He turned it over and looked at the wax. Pressed into it, very clear and neat, was a single letter. A C. With a little hook at the top, like a fishhook.
He did not need the Ledger for that either.
The smell came up off the paper as he turned it. Sweet and white and heavy.
He turned it back and read it.
The ink was violet. The hand was beautiful, all long smooth loops and graceful tails, the hand of somebody who had been taught to write by a master and had practiced every day of her life.
My darling girl,
The snow has come, just as I said it would. Isn't it lovely? I stood at my window this morning and watched it fall over the whole city, and I thought of you, all alone in that little room over the horses, and I was so sorry.
I have done what I said I would do. I always do. A carriage from the Ministry's House of Rest at Fallowfield will call at Pruett's yard for your father on Monday next, at noon. It is a very good House. There are gardens, and a lake, and nurses day and night, and nobody there will ever let him sit cold by a window again. Mrs. Hobb may go with him if she likes. The papers are signed. There is nothing anybody can do about them now, darling, so please don't try. It will only tire you.
Unless, of course, we come to an understanding before then.
You know what I would like. Bring the brooch and the portrait to Number 11. And the other little thing, the blue one, that you found yesterday in a place you should not have been. Bring them all. Bring them yourself. Come alone. And then we will burn those horrid papers together, you and I, in my fire, and drink a cup of chocolate, and never speak of any of it again.
Monday at noon, darling. Do think about it.
Your loving aunt,
C.
Underneath, in the same violet ink, there was one more line. Smaller. As if she had added it at the last moment, smiling, with the pen already halfway back to its stand.
P.S. Bertram always said you were too clever for your own good. I see he was right.
Corin put the letter down on the table.
For a while neither of them said anything. The stove roared softly in the corner. The rag on the windowsill steamed. Down in the yard, Patience stamped once, and her shoe rang on a cobble under the snow.
"She knows about the stone," said Corin at last.
"She knows about everything." Lyra's voice was quite flat. It had no feeling in it at all, the way a road has no feeling in it after a great many carts have gone over it. "She knew the clock ran. She came straight there. She knew the moment it happened. How did she know?" Her hand came up, by itself, and closed round the blue stone at her throat. "And now she knows I have this. And she wants it. More than the brooch. More than the portrait. Read it again. She put it in the middle, where it wouldn't look important. That's how you know it's the important one." She let go of the stone. "My mother taught me that. When you write a letter and there's one thing you really want, you never put it first or last. You put it in the middle, and you make it small."
Fold it small, thought Corin. He did not say it.
"Monday," he said instead.
"Monday at noon." Lyra looked at the inner door. The white door at the far end of the long room, with the worn gray patch in the paint where hands had pushed it open a great many times. It was closed now. From behind it, very faintly, came the sound of a woman humming. "Four days."
"Can she do it? Really?"
"She's a Councillor. He's a Councillor's brother with no mind. There's a law. There's always a law." Lyra's mouth twisted. "For the protection of persons unable to protect themselves. The family can ask for it. The nearest family of age. I'm not of age till spring. Rowan's sixteen. So it's her." She pressed her lips together. "Fallowfield is two days' drive into the country. Past the marshes. You have to have a pass from the Ministry to visit. A pass with her name on it."
She did not say what that meant. She did not need to.
"And if you give her the things," said Corin. "The brooch. The portrait. The stone."
"Then she'll burn the papers, and drink chocolate with me, and smile." Lyra looked up at him for the first time. Her eyes were gray. Thorne gray. The same gray as her aunt's, exactly, and he had not noticed that before, and he wished he had not noticed it now. "And a month later, or a year later, she'll write me another letter. On cream paper. With lily wax. Because there will always be something else she wants. Bertram knew that. That's why he hid the ticket. That's why he wrote do not let her have it. Bertram gave her everything, once, because he was frightened. And she killed him anyway."
Her voice did not shake. But her hands, on the table, had closed into fists.
The humming behind the white door stopped. The door opened, and a big woman came out, backward, carrying a basin of water with a towel over her arm. She was broad and red-faced and cheerful, with her sleeves rolled up over strong red forearms and a white cap on the back of her head. She turned round and saw Corin and beamed.
"Oh! It's the gentleman! The friend!" said Mrs. Hobb. "Good morning, sir. Isn't it a morning? I never saw such snow. Not so early. Not in all my born days." She set the basin down on the washstand by the door, sloshing. "His lordship's been looking at it since it got light. Just looking. Smiling. Like a little boy at a sweetshop window." Her face softened. "Do you want to go in and see him, sir? He does like a visitor. He doesn't say. But you can tell."
Corin looked at Lyra. Lyra looked at the white door. Then she got up.
The inner room was the whitest room in the city. Corin was almost sure of it.
It had been white before. The Ledger said so: a small room, whitewashed more carefully and more often than the rest, so that it seemed to glow. But now the snow-light was pouring in through its one window, gray-white and soft and very bright, and it filled the little room up the way milk fills a jug. The walls were white. The narrow bed was white, with a white cover pulled straight over it and the corners tucked in tight. The washstand was white, and the china jug on it, and the plain wooden chair by the window had been painted white so many times that the paint was thick and smooth as icing on a cake.
And in the white chair, by the window, sat Lord Aurelian Thorne.
He was thin. Not ill thin. Worn down, the way a stone step is worn down by feet going over it again and again. His hair was white, all of it, fine and soft and brushed straight back from a high forehead, and his face was long and narrow and fine-boned, shaved so carefully that the skin shone. It was perfectly calm. Perfectly peaceful. The face of a man watching something beautiful that he did not have to understand. He wore his dressing gown of dark blue silk, faded along the shoulders where the sun had lain on it day after day, over a clean white nightshirt buttoned to the throat. His feet were in soft leather slippers, side by side on the floor, very neat, and his long thin hands lay folded in his lap on top of a red wool blanket that Mrs. Hobb had tucked round his knees against the cold.
He was looking out of the window at the snow.
On the little table by his bed there stood a small wooden box, no bigger than a snuffbox, with a pattern of pale shell set into its lid. The Ledger said Mrs. Hobb dusted it every morning.
Lyra went and knelt down by the chair.
She did not say anything at first. She just knelt there, on the scrubbed boards, with her gray skirt spread round her, and looked up at her father's quiet face. Then she reached across to the little table and opened the shell box. Inside, on a fold of faded blue velvet, lay a thin silver chain, and on it a small blue stone. Round and smooth and no bigger than the end of a child's thumb. The deepest, clearest blue. And across the face of it, from one side to the other, there ran a fine white crack, like a single hair laid across a glass of water. She lifted it out by its chain. Then she took the whole stone on its bootlace from round her own neck, and held the two of them up together, very gently, in front of her father's chest.
They were the same. Exactly the same. The same size. The same shape. The same deep, clear, bottomless blue. Like two drops from one ocean. Except that one was whole, and one was broken.
Lord Thorne's eyes moved.
Very slowly. Away from the snow. Down to the two blue stones, side by side, an inch from his chest, catching the white light from the window. He looked at them for a long, long moment. His face did not change. It was still calm, still peaceful, still smiling very slightly at nothing. But his right hand came up, out of his lap. Slowly. The way a leaf comes up to the top of a pond. And his long thin fingers closed, very gently, round both stones at once. The whole one and the cracked one. He held them together in his palm, against his chest. And he went on holding them.
He did not say anything. He did not say Home, the way the Ledger said he had said it once, holding a cup. He did not say Bright. He did not tap on the glass.
He only held them. And his thumb moved, once, very softly, across the two smooth blue faces, the way you would stroke the cheek of a child who was asleep.
Lyra knelt beside him and did not move. Corin stood in the doorway and did not breathe. Behind him, in the long room, he heard Mrs. Hobb stop sloshing, and stand still.
After a long time, Lord Thorne's hand opened again, and the whole stone slipped out of it, on its bootlace, and he turned his face back to the window, and the snow.
Lyra stood up. She laid the cracked stone back in its box, on the faded velvet, and closed the lid, softly, the way you close a door on someone sleeping. Then she tied the bootlace round her neck again, in the same hard knot, and tucked the stone away under the collar of her dress, out of sight. Her face was quite steady. Her eyes were not.
"He knows it," she said, very low. "Somewhere. Somewhere a long way down. He knows it."
"Yes," said Corin.
"Then it matters." She turned and looked at him. "Whatever it is. Whatever it's for. It matters to him. And she wants it." She drew a breath. "So I'm going to find out what it is before Monday. And then I'm going to decide."
She went past him, out of the white room, and he followed her.
At the table in the long room, Corin took out the Ledger, and turned to yesterday, and found the page he wanted. He laid it flat on the table between them, beside Cressida's letter, and put his finger on a line.
Maker's plate inside the clock door: E. T. & T. R.
"E. T. is your grandfather," he said. "Edmund Thorne. Niles said so. The auction list said so. Made by Lord Edmund Thorne." He moved his finger. "But he didn't make it alone. There's another name on the plate. T. R. Somebody made that clock with him. Somebody who knew how to build a drawer that only opens when you tell the clock the time." He looked up. "If anyone knows what that stone is, and why your grandfather hid it there, it's T. R."
Lyra frowned down at the two letters.
"I don't know any T. R.," she said slowly. "I don't think I do. Grandfather died the year I was born. I never knew him. And all his friends were old men who came to dinner and talked about the war and pinched my cheek." She shook her head. "T. R. Thomas? Theodore?"
"Rook."
They both turned.
Oren was standing in the doorway, with the snow-light behind him and the broom in his hand again. He had come in so quietly, for such a big man, that neither of them had heard him. He was looking at the open Ledger on the table. At the two letters.
"Rook," he said again, slowly, in his deep gravel voice. "T. R. Mister… Thaddeus. Thaddeus Rook." His heavy brows came together. He was reaching again. Corin could see it. Reaching down into the dark for something that had been there a long time. "He came to the house. Every winter. When I was new. Before… before the first snow. To clean the… the big…" His hand moved in the air, a slow round movement, like the swing of a pendulum.
"The clock," said Lyra.
"The clock." Oren nodded slowly. "Every winter. Old Lord Edmund's friend. He'd come up the area steps with a bag of tools as big as a… as a dog. Old. Even then. White hair, sticking up. And a lot of… of glass. On his face. Little round glasses, on hinges, one on top of the other, that he could flip up and down." He touched his own face, beside his eye, with one thick finger. "He'd sit inside the clock case, nearly. With his head in it. For a whole day. Wouldn't eat. And when he came out, he'd give the little miss a…" He stopped. He looked at Lyra. "A beetle."
Lyra had gone very still.
"A brass beetle," said Oren. "With green on its back. That walked. You wound it up with a little key and it walked round and round on the nursery table, and you'd shriek." Something that was almost a smile moved at the corner of his flat mouth. "Every year a new one. Different colors. You had a row of them on the windowsill."
"I remember," said Lyra, in a strange small voice. "I remember them. On the sill. In the sun. I'd forgotten." She put her hand flat on the table, as if she needed to hold on to something. "I don't remember him. But I remember the beetles."
"Then he stopped coming," said Oren. "Eight years ago. Nine. One winter, he didn't come. Bertram said he'd died." The gray eyes went to Corin. "Bertram said a lot of people had died."
In Corin's coat, against his ribs, the long thin rod was very cold.
Bertram, said Malakor softly, also said that Doctor Aske had gone to a sister in the country.
"A clockmaker," said Corin slowly. "An old clockmaker. White hair. Glasses on hinges. Who stopped coming to the Thorne house eight years ago and was said to be dead." He shut the Ledger. "Clockmakers know each other. There's one on Hook Lane. Next door to Vespera's. Faber." He stood up. "If Thaddeus Rook is alive, or if he's dead and someone knows how, Faber will know."
Lyra was already on her feet. She took her coat from the hook by the stove. Her old brown coat, still filthy from yesterday's roofs. Under it, on the same hook, in a scabbard of worn black leather, hung a sword. She took that down too, and buckled it on, quickly, under the coat, with hands that did not need to look at what they were doing.
"Oren," she said. "Stay with Father. Don't let anyone in. Anyone. Not if they come with papers. Not if they come with the Watch. Not if they come with a cup of chocolate."
Oren looked at her. Then he looked at Corin.
"There's a carriage," he said. "At the top of the lane. On Linden Walk. Since first light. Black. With the blinds down. And a big man sitting up beside the driver, eating something out of a paper." He paused. "He's been watching the gate."
"Gorse," said Corin.
Oren did not know the name. He did not need to. He knew the kind.
"There's a back way," he said. "Through Pruett's hay store. Over the wall into the dye-yard. The little one knows it." He jerked his head toward the window. "She's been sitting on the hay-store roof for half an hour, waiting for you. In the snow. Like a… like a…"
"Like a cat," said Lyra.
"No," said Oren slowly. "Not a cat. The other thing. Black. With a sharp face. That sits on roofs and shouts."
Lyra looked at Corin. For the first time that morning, something that was almost a smile came into her white face. Small and tired and crooked. But there.
"A crow," she said.
"A crow," said Oren, and nodded, satisfied, and held the door open for them, and they went out into the snow.
