Chapter 52: Lot 41, Part 2 of 5: Gently
Gently
The old man stood in the arch of the side vault and looked at Corin for a long time.
Corin looked back. Neither of them said anything. Lyra, behind Linus Hale's shoulder, with the stone jar of water still in her arms, did not move. She had never seen Corin's face do what it was doing now. It was not anger. It was not grief. It was something like the face of a man who has been waiting at a window for a very long time for a carriage, and has given up, and gone to sit by the fire, and then hears the wheels.
"Boy," said Linus Hale.
"Master."
That was all. One word each.
Then the old man came into the vault, tapping, and Oren brought his crate across from the arch and set it down by the bench, and Linus Hale lowered himself onto it, very slowly, in stages, the way very old men sit, as if every joint had to be asked separately. He laid his stick across his knees. He put the oilcloth parcel down on the stone beside the crate, carefully, flat. And then he looked at Corin's hands.
At the gray. Dull pewter gray, from the tips of the fingers to past the wrists and on up under the cuffs of the old coat.
He did not say Ah this time. He did not say anything. He just reached out with his own two hands, and took one of Corin's gray hands between them, and held it. His own blue fingers on the gray ones. And Lyra saw that under the cuff of his dark coat, where it had ridden up as he reached, the old man's wrist was gray too. The same dull pewter. Going on up his thin arm, under the sleeve, out of sight.
Gray to the shoulders, Remy had written in his black book, about the old harvester who brought Lot 41 to the Bourse. Blue fingers. No name. No pay.
"You lied to me," said Corin. "In the autumn."
"Yes."
"For an hour. By the fire. With the tea."
"Yes," said Linus Hale. "Kindly. I lied to you very kindly, Corin. I am good at it. I have had a great deal of practice." He looked down at the gray hand between his blue ones. "I am not going to lie to you today. I want you to know that before I start. I am not going to tell you everything. But I am not going to lie."
"Why not everything?"
The old man looked up at him over the gold spectacles.
"Because some of it would kill you to carry before Monday," he said. "And I find I would rather you lived until Tuesday." He let go of Corin's hand. Gently. He put it back on the Ledger, on the cover, where it had been. "Ask me after. If there is an after."
Corin did not answer that.
Then Linus Hale turned on the crate, slowly, and looked up at Lyra. Who was still standing in the arch with the stone jar of water in her arms, because she had forgotten to put it down.
"Put that down, my dear," he said. "And sit. This part is for you."
She put it down. She sat on the floor under the grating with her back to the wall, where she always sat, in Gideon's thin jacket over her mother's gown. She folded her gloved hands in her lap. She found that they were cold. Colder than the stone.
The old man looked at her for a moment, with his head on one side.
"Two years ago," he said, "in the autumn, on the eighteenth night of the month, at the ninth bell, I came to your father's house on Crown Walk in a black carriage of the Ministry. With one other. We were let in at the front door by your father's man. Bertram, I think. A very correct old man, with white side-whiskers. He took my hat." He paused. "Your father was in his study. Downstairs, at the back, with the green curtains. He was sitting by the fire with a book. He had been expecting somebody to come, for a long time. I think he was almost glad it was me, and not the other one."
Lyra did not breathe.
"I was sent," said Linus Hale, "to take his last evening. That evening. The whole of it. From the moment he sat down by his fire with his book, to the moment I finished. Taken whole. Before the fire. Those were the words on my order." His voice was very steady. Thin and dry and gentle, like paper. "And everything that was in that evening, I was to take with it. Every thought he had while he sat there. Every face he thought of. Everything he was holding, to keep it safe, in the place where a man holds things when he knows the door is about to open."
"Who sent you?" said Lyra.
She heard her own voice. It sounded like somebody else's. Somebody very far away.
Linus Hale looked at her. And then he looked at the skulls on their stone shelf above Corin's head. At Brother Anselm, and Brother Hugh, and the one at the end with no name, only a little painted cross.
"The man whose regards I sent Corin in the autumn," he said. "I won't say his name in this room. Not because I'm afraid. Because names carry, and these walls are old, and there are fifty-one children out there who don't need it in the air they breathe."
Nobody said anything.
"I did it," said Linus Hale. "I want you to know that. I did not refuse. I did not run. I sat down in the chair across the fire from your father, where your mother used to sit, and I took out my rod, and he looked at me and said, Gently, Linus. If you would. And I said I would." His blue fingers moved on the stick across his knees. "And I did. I took it gently. Very slowly. A whole evening, taken the old way, the slow way, the way I taught Corin, so that nothing tore. It took me until the second bell after midnight. He did not make a sound."
Lyra found that she was holding her own wrists, very tightly, in her lap.
"The other one," she said. "Who came with you. In the carriage."
Linus Hale was quiet for a moment.
"A woman in white," he said. "Who stood behind your father's chair the whole time. With her hand on the back of it. And did not say one word. Not one. Until I had finished. And then she said, Is it done? And I said yes. And she said, Give it to me." He looked down at the stick. "And I did not."
The vault was very quiet.
Signed, Lyra thought. I stood behind his chair. The clerk wrote what he was told. That was what her aunt had said, on Friday night, in the white room, about the lily paper. I stood behind his chair.
She had thought her aunt meant a different night. A different chair.
"I did not give it to her," said Linus Hale. "That night. I put it in a case. A small case of black wood with a silver clasp, which had been given to me in the hall when I came in, by a man in a gray cloak, with the hood up, who did not speak. A man with long thin white hands. I do not know who he was. I did not see his face. He put the case into my hands and went out again by the front door into the dark, and Bertram shut the door behind him, and I never saw him again." He paused. "There was a mark on the clasp. A folded square. I did not know what it meant. I still don't."
Long thin white hands. Lyra had heard that before. On the barge. From Oren, telling them what Moll had told him. A hooded gentleman in a gray cloak, with long thin white hands, who taught the drum boy the call.
She did not say it. She could not. Her mouth was too dry.
"I put your father's last evening into that case," said Linus Hale, "and I closed the clasp. And the case closed itself. I felt it. It closed the way a door closes when somebody on the other side turns the key. And I could not open it again. I tried, later, in my room, that same night. It would not open for me. It would not open for anybody, I think, but one blood." He looked at Lyra. "His."
"And then?"
"And then I took it home. And kept it. For one day." His thin face did not change. "And the next night your aunt came to my door at the Lamplighter's Rest, alone, in a plain gray cloak with the hood up, so that nobody in the taproom would know her. And she came up the stairs to my room, and stood in my doorway, and held out her hand." He looked down at his own blue hands on the stick. "She did not say anything. She did not need to. She just held out her hand."
"And you gave it to her."
"Yes."
Lyra looked at him.
"Why?"
Linus Hale was quiet for a long moment.
"Because I was relieved," he said at last. Very simply. "That is the worst thing I am going to tell you today, my dear. I was relieved. I had your father's last evening in a box on my table, and the man who had sent me for it would come for it soon, and I knew what he would do with it, and I did not want to be the one to give it to him. And then a woman in a gray cloak held out her hand. And I put the box in it, and shut my door, and sat down by my fire, and I was so relieved that I wept."
He looked up at her.
"I did not know what she would do with it," he said. "I did not ask. I did not want to know. I wanted it to be somebody else's." A pause. "For two years it was. And then, last month, she sent it back to me. By her own servant. With a card. And asked me to carry it to the Lady's house, for the Midwinter sale. Under her name. By proxy. Proceeds: none. And I did that too."
Nobody said anything.
Out in the long hall, Mrs. Doyle had begun to sing again, very softly. The song about the three lamps and the long road. Polly's song now. Mrs. Doyle sang it to her every hour, in case.
"I am not asking you to forgive me," said Linus Hale. "I want that to be clear. I have not come here to be forgiven. I am eighty-one, and there is a circle by my name, and I find I do not have time for it." He took off his gold spectacles, and wiped them slowly on his scarf, and put them back on. "I have come so that it is written down somewhere that is not me. Because when they come to my room, it will not be written down in me any more."
He turned on the crate, slowly, and looked at Corin.
"Write it, boy," he said. "In your book. All of it. In the square capitals I taught you, saints know how many years ago, in my room, with your tongue between your teeth. Write it. And then read it to her. Every morning, if she wants it. Until it doesn't hurt."
Corin had already opened the Ledger.
Lyra watched him write. His gray fingers on the pencil, slow and stiff, in his square capitals. She could not read it from where she sat. She did not need to. She knew what it said.
She found that she was not crying. She had thought she would be. She was not. She was sitting on the cold stone floor with her back to the wall, holding her own wrists, and she was perfectly still, and perfectly dry, and somewhere very deep inside her, in a place she had not known was there, something had gone quiet. The way a room goes quiet when a clock that has been ticking for two years finally stops.
Gently, Linus. If you would.
That was the last thing her father had said in his own house, as himself, in his own voice, to somebody he knew. Everything after that had been tapping, and a few broken words on a barge.
And now she had it. And nobody could take it away. Because it was not hers. It was written down.
The Book
When Corin had finished writing, the old man bent down, slowly, in stages, and picked up the oilcloth parcel from the floor beside the crate.
He held it on his knees for a moment. Flat. With both blue hands on top of it. The way Lucian held his black book. The way Corin held the Ledger. As if it were warm, and he wanted to feel it for a little longer.
Then he held it out.
"Take it," he said.
Corin did not move.
"Take it, boy. My arms are old."
Corin took it. It was heavy. Heavier than it looked. He laid it on the Ledger on his knee and looked at it. Gray oilcloth, stiff and cracked at the corners, tied with ordinary brown string in a plain knot, the kind of knot you tie when you want somebody to be able to undo it without a knife.
"What is it?"
"Everything I know," said Linus Hale. "Sixty-two years of it. Since I was nineteen and Master Corvell put a rod in my hand for the first time in the old Harvesting rooms, in the basement where your Toby sits now." He looked at the parcel. "Every harvest I ever did. Every jar. Every name. The old ways, the slow ways, the ones they don't teach any more because they take too long. How to take a thing gently, so it doesn't tear. How to put a thing back, sometimes, if you're quick, and the person is still warm." His voice did not change. "And some other things. Things I saw. Things I was told not to write down, and wrote down anyway, in the back, in the old letters, where nobody looks."
Corin's gray hand had gone flat on the oilcloth.
"Silas said you told him," he said slowly. "Last night. If I run, they'll take the book."
"Yes."
"And if you stay—"
"If I stay, perhaps they'll only take me." The old man nodded. "And now there is no book for them to take. Only an old man by a fire, writing in a new one. A blank one. I bought it this morning on the way here, at Pinner's on Quill Row. He was just opening. He gave me a good price, because it was Midwinter." Something moved in the folded face. "I shall fill it with the weather, and what I had for my supper. And when they come, they can have that."
Nobody said anything.
"Don't open it," said Linus Hale. "Not yet. Not until after Monday. Whatever it is you're going to do on Monday. I don't want to know what it is, and I don't want you thinking about what's in that book while you're doing it." He looked at Corin over the gold spectacles. "Promise me."
"I promise."
"Write it."
Corin opened the Ledger and wrote it. Lyra watched him. Master Hale's book. Not to be opened till after Monday. I promised.
"Good." Linus Hale sat back on the crate. "And the key. Have you got it?"
Corin's hand went to his chest. To the string under his shirt.
"Yes."
"Don't show it to me. I know what it looks like." The old man looked down at his stick. "It opens a door. Under the Ministry. On a level that the Ministry will tell you does not exist. I have stood in front of that door four times in my life, boy, with that key in my hand. And four times I put it back in my pocket and went upstairs again." He was quiet a moment. "I was never brave enough. I want you to know that too. It's in the book. In the back. The last page."
"What's behind it?"
"I don't know," said Linus Hale. "That's the truth. I've never known. I know what I was told was behind it, sixty years ago, by a man who's dead now. And I know that I didn't want to find out if he was right." He lifted his eyes. "After Monday. If there is an after. Read the last page. And then decide."
Corin looked at him for a long time.
Then he said, very low, "Why me?"
The old man did not answer at once. He looked at Corin's gray hands on the oilcloth. At the stiff fingers. At the dull pewter going up under the cuffs.
"Because you were brought to me," he said at last, "when you were very young, with nothing in you. Nothing at all. Like a jar somebody had washed out and put back on the shelf. And I was told to make you useful." He paused. "And I made you kind instead. I didn't mean to. It just happened. You were such a kind boy, Corin. You used to put the jars back on the shelf with both hands, as if they could feel it."
He stopped.
"That's all," he said. "That's all I'm going to say about that. The rest is in the book. Or it isn't. I can't remember now which bits I wrote and which bits I only meant to."
Corin did not say anything. He could not. Lyra saw his throat move.
Linus Hale took up his stick. He put both blue hands on top of it, and leaned on it, and began to get up. In stages. Oren came and took his arm without being asked.
"One more thing," said the old man, when he was standing. "About Monday. About the case." He looked at Lyra. "It will open for blood. His blood. Not yours, Corin. Not mine. Not the Lady's, not the Auditor's, not anybody's. His. So it will open for her." He nodded at Lyra. "Or for her brother. Nobody else in this city." He looked back at Corin. "And when you take it out of the case, boy, take it gently. The way I took it in. Slowly. It's a whole evening. It's a man's whole last evening as himself. And there's something folded in it."
"Folded?"
"Small," said Linus Hale. "While I was taking it, at the end, near the second bell, your father did something. Inside. I felt it. It was like a fish going under a boat, in dark water. Something he folded up very small and put away from me, very fast, in a place I wasn't looking." His thin face was very still. "I let it go. I didn't follow it. I could have. I was good enough, then. I felt it go, and I let it." He looked at Lyra. "I think that was the only brave thing I did that night. I think it was the only brave thing I've done in twenty years."
Then he turned, on Oren's arm, and went out of the vault, tapping.
Lyra walked him up.
She did not mean to. She just got up off the floor and followed, and nobody stopped her. Up the crypt steps, slowly, one at a time, with Oren on his other side. Through the little door behind the altar. Into the cold green light of the empty church. The three old women were still sitting in the back pews in their shawls with their heads bowed, and did not look up.
At the great west door, the old man stopped.
He took his arm out of Oren's. He stood by himself, leaning on his stick, and looked at Lyra.
"I met your mother once," he said.
Lyra went still.
"Eleven years ago," said Linus Hale. "In the autumn. A week before she went away. She came to the Lamplighter's Rest, in the afternoon, by herself, and sat down across the fire from me, and asked the landlord for a pot of tea. I didn't know who she was. She had a plain cloak on, a traveling cloak, dark blue, with a hood. But I knew the voice. Everybody on the Hill knew that voice. The accent." He paused. "She asked me a question. Only one. She said, Master Hale, they tell me you are the oldest harvester in the city. Can a memory be hidden in a song?"
Lyra could not breathe.
"I said I didn't know," said Linus Hale. "I said I'd never heard of it being done. I said I didn't think it could be. A song isn't a jar. A song goes out of you into the air and it's gone." He looked at her. "And she smiled at me, and finished her tea, and put a coin on the table, and stood up. And she said, Then I'll find out."
He put his round black hat on.
"I don't know if she did," he said. "I never saw her again. But I thought you should have that. It's yours, not mine. I've been carrying it eleven years."
Then he put his blue hand on the great iron ring of the west door, and pulled, and the door groaned open, and let in the blade of white winter light, and the snow, and the cold.
And he went out into it, tapping, a small bent old man with a stick, going down the church steps one at a time in the snow, past the young man on the wall by the lych gate who was eating a pie and writing in a book.
Lyra watched him go until the door swung shut.
She stood in the cold empty church for a long time after that. By the font. In the light like the bottom of a pond. With her gloved hands pressed together.
The song that goes up and hangs.
She had the end of it now. Corin had given it to her last night. Going down and round and home. She had it all. The whole song. For the first time since she was nine.
Can a memory be hidden in a song?
She hummed it. Very softly. Under her breath. Alone in the church of Saint Bride's on the Sunday before Midwinter, with three old women in the back pews who did not look up.
And nothing happened. Nothing at all. It was just a song. Up, and up, and hanging. And down, and round, and home.
She did not know what she had expected. She went back down to the crypt.
Vespera's Masks
Vespera came at the noon bell, through Amos's door, with a bundle on her back like a peddler and soot on her face.
Lyra had not seen her since Tuesday. Nobody had. When Gant came to the arches on Wednesday at first light with his six men and their hooks, Vespera had been alone in her arch, with her needles on their ropes and her door-table of inks. And when Gant's men came through the curtain, she had not been there. Mother Grue's people said she went into the dye vats. Into the great stone tanks at the back of the Dye-Works, the old indigo tanks, where the blue water was waist-deep and so dark that a lamp held over it showed nothing. She had gone in, they said, with her bundle held over her head, and waded out the other side into the culvert, and nobody had seen her since.
She had been blue to the waist when she climbed out. She was still blue now. Lyra saw it as soon as she came into the light of the braziers. Her small gray hands were blue, and her wrists, and the hem of her skirt was stiff and dark with it. Even her face had a faint blue shadow under the soot, along the jaw, like a bruise.
She did not seem to notice.
She put the bundle down on the floor of the side vault and looked at Corin on his bench. At his leg in its splints. At his gray hands. At his face.
"Fenton," she said.
Corin looked at her.
"Marta sent word at the tenth bell," said Vespera. "By Bridie. Bridie came to the culvert and shouted down it until I came up. Half the Saltmarsh heard her." She knelt down beside the bundle and began to untie it. "His Grace's cellarman, Fenton. Tall. Thin. Sour. Gray beard. Stiff left leg. I know Fenton. Everybody who ever bought a bottle of wine on the Hill knows Fenton. He sold me a bottle once, twenty years ago, from the Marrow cellars, when I was young and stupid and wanted to impress somebody. He looked at me as if I were something the cat had brought in." She pulled the knot loose. "I remember his face. I'd remember that face anywhere. It looks like a lemon that's been told bad news."
Juno, on her pallet, snorted.
"Can you do it?" said Lyra.
Vespera did not answer at once. She opened the bundle.
It was her whole shop. Or as much of it as could be carried on a back through a dye tank. A roll of soft leather, which she unrolled across the floor of the vault, and inside it, in rows of little pockets, her needles. Hundreds of them. Long and short, thick and fine, straight and curved, some as thin as a hair. A row of small glass bottles, stoppered with wax, each with a color inside. Flesh-pink. Brown. Gray. A dull red like old brick. A white so white it seemed to glow. A little pot of something that smelled like burnt honey. A bundle of soft brushes. A square of fine gauze. A tiny pair of scissors shaped like a bird, with the blades for a beak.
And last of all, wrapped in a scrap of black velvet, the pot. Lyra remembered it from the arch. A small round pot, deep blue, with a thin gold rim, and a lid that fitted so tight you could not see the join.
Vespera set the pot down by itself, apart from the rest. She did not open it.
"I can do it," she said. "Not the paint. The paint was for Tuesday. The paint runs. Sweat, rain, a hot room, a glass of wine. You'll be in a hot room all night on Monday under a glass dome with five hundred people breathing, and the paint would be off your face by the tenth bell." She looked at Corin. "The binding."
"The one that hurts," said Corin.
"The one that hurts." Vespera did not smile. "I told you about it in the autumn. I told you again a week ago. It goes under the skin. Not on. Under. I put it in with the needles, a little at a time, and then I seal it with that." She nodded at the blue pot. "And it binds. To you. It sets. It takes a night and a day to set, and while it's setting it burns like you've put your face in a fire. And when it's set it's your face. For a week. You can wash it. You can sweat. You can cry, if you want. Nothing moves it. Not for a week." She paused. "And then it comes off. All at once. In the night. And you wake up with your own face again and a pillow full of gray dust."
"How long to put it in?"
"For a whole face? Fenton's face?" Vespera looked at Corin's face. Studied it. Long and narrow, with the heavy dark brows and the mouth that went down at the left corner. "Five hours. Six. If you keep still. If you don't, longer." She looked at his hands. "Not those. I can't do those. The gray won't take the ink. It just sits on top and flakes. You'll need gloves."
"Fenton wears gloves," said Lyra. "When he pours. White cotton. The Duke's men all do."
"Then that's lucky," said Vespera. "Because I'd have had nothing else."
She began to lay out the bottles.
"Not yet," said Corin.
She stopped.
"The price," said Corin. "You said a week ago. The jar. On the ledge, in the arch. With the V on it." He looked at the blue pot, sitting by itself on the floor. "You said I'd open it for you after Midwinter."
Vespera did not look at him. She looked at the pot.
"I lost the jar," she said.
The vault went quiet.
"In the dye tank," said Vespera. "I had it in my arms. With the bundle on my head. The water was up to my chest by the middle, and the bottom's all slime, and I slipped. And I had to choose. The bundle or the jar." Her small blue hands were quite still on the leather roll. "I chose the bundle. The jar went down in the indigo. It's at the bottom of the third tank at the Dye-Works now, with forty years of blue on top of it. Nobody will ever find it."
Nobody said anything.
Lyra did not know what had been in the jar. She had never asked. Corin had never asked either, she thought. It had sat on its brick ledge in the arch with a V on the seal, and Vespera had looked at it sometimes when she thought nobody saw, and that was all.
"So there's no price," said Vespera. Her voice was quite flat. "There's nothing for you to open. I'm doing this for nothing." She picked up a bottle. Flesh-pink. She held it up to the lamp and looked through it. "I've never done anything for nothing in my life. I don't know how. I'll probably do it badly."
"You won't," said Corin.
"No," said Vespera. "No, I won't." She put the bottle down. "Lie back. Take the pillow away. Flat. Somebody hold his hands, he'll want to grab at his face and he mustn't. Not once. If he touches it before it's set, it slides, and he'll have Fenton's nose on his cheek for a week."
Lyra held his hands.
She knelt on the cold stone floor beside the bench and took both his gray hands in both of hers, in Madame Hesper's thin gloves, and held them against his chest. And Vespera knelt on the other side with her needles and her bottles and her blue pot and the little bird-shaped scissors, and bent over his face, very close, with her soot-and-blue face a hand's width from his, and began.
It took six hours.
Lyra held his hands for five of them, and did not let go once.
She would remember it afterward as one of the longest things she had ever done. Longer than the walk across the Saltmarsh. Longer than the night on the barge when her father stopped tapping for ten breaths. Corin did not make a sound. Not at first. He lay flat on the stone bench under the skulls with his eyes shut and his jaw set and his gray hands in hers, and Vespera's small blue fingers moved over his face, and the needles went in, and out, and in. Fine as hairs. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A little at a time. Along the jaw, first. Then the cheeks. Then the nose. Each time a needle went in, Lyra felt his hands jump in hers. Just a little. Just once. And then go still again.
The smell of the inks filled the vault. Burnt honey. Iron. Something sharp, like vinegar and cloves.
At the third bell after noon, he started to shake.
Not the shaking from the garden. A different kind. The body's kind. A deep hard shudder that started in his shoulders and went down through him to his good foot, and came back, and went down again. Vespera did not stop. She said, "That's the binding taking. It's started to set. It'll burn now." And it did. Lyra could feel it. His face had gone red, a deep angry red under the new color, and hot. She could feel the heat of it from where she knelt, like the heat off a stove. And his hands in hers went tight, so tight that her fingers hurt through the gloves, and stayed tight.
She did not let go.
She sang to him. She did not decide to. She just found that she was doing it. Very low. Not her mother's song. Hessa's song. The one Hessa sang on the barge, the Saltmarsh song, the one about the tide coming in and going out and coming in again, that Lyra had sung to him in the arches the night Silas opened his leg. She sang it over and over. She did not know all the words. She hummed where she did not.
And near the fifth bell, between two needles, with his eyes still shut and his jaw still set, Corin said, very low, through his teeth:
"Rule one."
"Yes?"
"This is worse than the knee."
"I know."
"I'm telling you. Because it's true."
"I know," said Lyra. "Thank you."
And Vespera, bending over his face with a needle as fine as a hair between her blue fingers, said, without looking up, "Don't talk. You'll move your mouth. If you move your mouth I'll give you Fenton's sneer for a week and you'll have to wear it to the Duke's."
And Corin, under the needle, with his face burning, made a sound that Lyra had never heard him make before.
It took her a moment to understand that he was laughing.
