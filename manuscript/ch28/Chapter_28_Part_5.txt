Chapter 28: The Night Before, Part 5 of 5: Two Thousand One Hundred and Seven
Two Thousand One Hundred and Seven
After that, for a while, neither of them said anything.
The tinny clock in the dark house below struck three. Thin and small, like a spoon on a cup. Three times.
Wren got up and went to the skylight. She stood under it with her face turned up, looking at the black square of glass in the slope of the roof. There was no frost on it tonight. There were clouds over the city, low and thick, the color of wet ash, with a dull orange glow underneath them where the lamps of Veridia lit them from below. No stars. No moon. Only the clouds, moving very slowly from east to west, like a herd of great gray animals going somewhere in the night.
"I used to think," she said, without turning round, "when I first came here, that I'd fill all the shelves. That one day there'd be so many books there wouldn't be room to stand." A pause. "And then I'd stop. Because I'd be too old to climb the stairs to the doors anymore."
She looked along the shelves. At the long dark rows of small black spines, going away down both sides of the attic, each with its little white label.
"And then I started thinking I'd never stop," she said. "That I'd just keep going. Knocking. Writing. Knocking. Writing. Until I was an old woman with a stick, standing behind some young Inquisitor on some step, and watching some door open. And then going home and climbing up here very slowly and writing one more name." She was quiet. "That's what frightened me most. Not the knocking. Not the white faces. That I'd get used to it. That one day I'd write a name and not feel anything at all."
"Did you?"
"Not yet." She turned round. Her face was in shadow, with the candle behind her on the table, so that he could not see it. "I always feel something. Every one. Even the ones I didn't like. Even the ones who'd done terrible things, and deserved worse than they got. I always feel something, when I write them down."
She came back to the table. She did not sit down. She stood beside it, looking down at the candle, with her hands at her sides.
"I want to ask you something," she said.
"Ask."
"Tam Hollin."
Corin looked up at her.
"You said his name," said Wren. "When you told me about the glass room. You said he died on Monday night, under the Dye-Works. You said he asked you to take his Ash, so it wouldn't go to nothing." She looked at him. "I didn't knock for him. He wasn't one of mine. He was never on an order. He was put on the ice by Gant and the Lady and the man with the gentle voice, and nobody from the Inquisition ever went near him." A pause. "But I'd like to write him down. If you don't mind. In one of the books. So he's here. With the others."
Corin did not say anything for a moment. He could not.
Then he nodded.
Wren took down a book from the shelf. Not the new one, number two thousand one hundred and seven, with its fresh white label. The one before. Two thousand one hundred and six. She opened it near the back, where there were still blank pages, and laid it on the table in the candlelight, and sat down, and took up her pen.
"Tell me," she said. "What I should write. The way you'd want it written."
Corin took out his Ledger. He did not need to. He knew it by heart. He had written it on Monday night, with his hand shaking so badly that the letters went crooked across the page, by the light of a lantern on a nail under an arch. But he opened the Ledger anyway, to the page, and laid it beside her book, and read it out to her. Slowly. One line at a time. And she wrote it down, in her small neat upright hand, one line at a time, as he read.
Tam Hollin. Born on Carrow Street, by the tanneries.
Mother: Annie. Cleaned the Third Basement. A laugh like a kettle.
Father: a sailor. Went to sea. Never came back.
Boot boy at nine. Footman at sixteen. Could carry six plates on one arm without spilling the soup.
Liked oranges, and the sound of rain on a slate roof.
Never learned to swim. Was going to. One day. In summer.
When she had written the last line, she stopped. Her pen hovered over the page.
"Anything else?" she said.
Corin thought about it.
He thought of the white face on the sack pillow, looking like a boot boy asleep under a slate roof. Of the frost in the hair that would not melt. Of the three hours. Of Gideon, standing at the mouth of the arch with tears running down his empty face, saying I don't care. But he was good.
"He said 'run, kid'," said Corin. "To a boy on a stair, who had never been told to run before."
Wren wrote it.
"He held still for three hours," said Corin, "so it wouldn't go to nothing."
Wren wrote it.
"He asked."
Wren wrote that too. And then she sat for a moment looking at the page. And then, under it all, in a slightly different hand, smaller, a little less upright, as if it were something she was saying for herself and not for the book, she wrote one more line.
Not one of mine. Kept anyway.
She blotted it. She closed the book. She got up and put it back on the shelf, in its place, between two thousand one hundred and five and two thousand one hundred and seven.
Then she stood there, with her hand on its spine.
"You know what's odd," she said quietly. "I've done this two thousand one hundred and six times. Two thousand one hundred and seven, now. And I've never once had anybody sit with me while I did it." She did not turn round. "I've never once read the names out loud."
Corin looked at her back. At the gray cloak. At the brown hair pulled back so tight it looked painted on.
"Would you like to?" he said.
She turned her head and looked at him.
"Some of them," said Corin. "Not all. There isn't time for all. But some." He looked along the dark shelves. "Somebody should hear them. Not just the books."
Wren did not say anything for a long time.
Then she took down another book. Number one. The very first. It was older than the others, the black cover faded to a dull gray, the ribbon frayed. She brought it to the table and sat down and opened it at the first page, by the candle.
And she began to read.
She read slowly. In her clear, cool, careful voice. Not the minutes-of-a-meeting voice. Something underneath that. A name, and a street, and a few lines. What they had done for a living. What they had liked. What they had said at the door, if they had said anything. And then the next.
Ada Finch. Gull Lane. Mended nets. Kept a blackbird in a cage and talked to it. At the door she said, "Who'll feed Bobbin?" I fed Bobbin. For a month. Then he died too.
Thomas Gale, called Tommy. Carrow Street. Twelve. Stole bread. At the door he said nothing. He was holding his little brother's hand. They let the brother go.
Mother Ruth. The almshouse on Ash Street. Eighty-one. Remembered every hymn ever written. At the door she was singing. She went on singing for a moment, after. Then she stopped.
Corin listened.
He sat in the hard wooden chair with his elbows on his knees and his hands clasped between them and he listened, and the candle burned down, and Wren read. One book. Then the next. Not every name. She turned the pages, and chose, and read the ones she chose. Sometimes her voice went very quiet. Sometimes it stopped altogether, for a moment, and then went on. Once she laughed, very softly, at something an old man had said at his door, something rude about the Ministry, and then put her hand over her mouth.
At four o'clock she stopped.
She closed the book. Number nine. She sat with her hands on it.
"That's enough," she said. Her voice was hoarse. "For now."
"Thank you," said Corin.
She looked at him across the candle.
"Nobody's ever thanked me for them," she said. "Not once."
He thought of Elara, at the top of the Bank, looking surprised, just for a moment, when he thanked her for showing him the little black book of her lost days. Nobody has ever thanked me for that.
Two women with black books, he thought. Keeping count. At the top of two buildings, on opposite sides of the city, writing down what was lost so that somebody would know.
He wondered if they had ever met.
He thought perhaps they had. Once. A long time ago. In the dark, by a garden door. Or a schoolhouse step.
He did not say it.
The Letter That Wasn't Burned
"There's something else," said Wren. "About Rowan."
She had put the ninth black book back on its shelf. She stood beside the shelf with her hand on its spine, the way she had stood with her hand on number two thousand one hundred and six, and she did not turn round.
Corin waited.
"I told you he burned her letters," she said. "In the stove in the cadets' hall. In front of everybody. Still sealed."
"Yes."
"He did. Every month. For two years. I watched him do it." She was quiet. "Except once."
She turned round.
"Last winter," she said. "Just after Midwinter. A letter came for him. Like all the others. Cream paper, cheap, the kind you buy by the sheet at a stationer's. Her writing on the front. Small and fierce, slanting hard to the right. I knew it by then. I'd seen it so often." A pause. "He took it to the stove, the way he always did. All the cadets watching. Waiting for it to go in. And he stood there with it in his hand over the open door of the stove, with the fire roaring, for a long time."
"And?"
"And he didn't put it in." Wren's face did not change. "He just stood there. And the other cadets started to laugh, and somebody said something, I don't remember what, something cruel, and he turned round and hit him. Hard. In the face. And they fought. On the floor of the cadets' hall, by the stove, with everybody shouting. And I had to pull them apart, because I was the senior." She looked at her own hands. "And when I got them apart, Rowan was holding the letter against his chest. Crumpled. With blood on it. From the other boy's nose."
The candle burned between them.
"He never opened it," said Wren. "Not that I saw. But he never burned it either. He put it inside his tunic, against his skin, and that night, after lights out, I went round the dormitory with my lamp the way the seniors have to, and he was lying in his bed with his eyes open, looking at the ceiling. And his hand was on his chest. On the letter. Through his shirt."
She was quiet for a long moment.
"I think he still has it," she said. "I think he carries it. Under his coat. Every day." She looked at Corin. "I think he'll be carrying it tomorrow. On your step."
Corin did not say anything for a while.
He thought of Lord Thorne, tapping on the wall, four, two, nine, three, for weeks. Trying to tell somebody something. And nobody hearing.
He thought of Rowan Thorne, sixteen, with a sealed letter from his sister against his skin, that he would not open and could not burn.
The Thornes, he thought, were a family that kept things folded very small.
"Why are you telling me?" he said.
Wren looked at him.
"Because when you're under the potatoes," she said, "and you hear them on the stairs. Two white faces, and Kross, and me. And a boy of sixteen in a cadet's gray coat, with a barley sugar in his cheek that Kross gave him, trying to look as if he isn't frightened." Her voice did not change. "I want you to know that he's not only what Kross made. That's all. That there's a letter under his coat."
"Does Lyra know? That he kept one?"
"No."
Corin thought about it.
"Then one day," he said slowly, "somebody should tell her."
"Yes."
"When she's ready."
"Yes." Wren almost smiled. "Everybody in this city seems to be waiting for somebody else to be ready."
She came back to the table and sat down. She looked very tired now. In the candlelight her sharp pale face had gone gray under the eyes, the way Elara's had at the glass desk, and there was a smudge of ink on her cheek where she had pushed back a strand of hair that had come loose from its tight knot. Just one strand. It was the first time Corin had ever seen any of her hair out of place.
"Can I ask you something?" he said.
"You can ask."
"Why are you doing this? Any of it? The note. The order. The key. The drawing." He looked at her. "Kross will find out. Sooner or later. You said yourself he finds out everything."
Wren did not answer for a long time.
She looked along the shelves. At the long rows of small black books going away into the dark on both sides, each with its white label and its number.
"Because I've written two thousand one hundred and six names," she said at last. "And I've read them all, over and over, up here, at night. And I've learned something, reading them. Something I don't think anybody else in the Inquisition knows."
"What?"
"That every single one of them," said Wren, "opened the door." She looked at him. "Every one. Not because they were stupid. Not because they didn't know. Because somebody was on the other side of it who they thought might need them. A neighbor. A child. A friend. They heard the knock, and they thought what if it's somebody who needs me, and they opened it." She was quiet. "Every single door I ever stood in front of was opened by somebody being kind."
She put her hands flat on the table.
"I'd like," she said, "just once, for one door not to be opened. For one person to get away. Not because they were unkind. Because somebody on the other side was kind back."
Corin looked at her across the candle.
"Two thousand one hundred and seven," he said quietly.
"If you're lucky," said Wren. "If I'm quick. If Kross goes up the stairs first." She pushed the strand of hair back from her face, and it fell down again at once. "Get some sleep, Vane. You've got ninety-one steps and a trap door in the morning."
Before Dawn
He slept for two hours on the floor of the attic, under the skylight, with his gray coat over him and his head on a folded blanket that smelled of tallow and old paper.
He had not meant to. Wren had told him to. Lie down. You've got five flights of stairs and two white faces in the morning, and you can't do either of them asleep on your feet. And he had said he couldn't sleep, he was too tired to sleep, which made no sense but was true. And she had looked at him the way Mrs. Marta looked at him, and put the blanket on the floor, and turned her back and sat down at the table with a book.
And he had lain down, just for a moment, to show her he could not sleep.
And he dreamed.
He dreamed of rain.
Not ordinary rain. Not the gray, sour, sooty rain of the Lower Docks that ran down the gutters of Tallow Street and stank of the river. Clean rain. Soft and steady and loud, very loud, very close, drumming on a slate roof right over his head. So close that he could have reached up from where he lay and touched the slates, if it had not been dark.
It was dark. Completely dark. But he was not afraid.
That was the strange thing. That was the thing he noticed, in the dream, the way you notice things in dreams without being surprised by them. It was dark, and he was lying in a narrow bed, under a gray blanket with a hole in one corner where you could put your toe through, and the dark was all round him, pressing down, the way it always did. And he was not afraid.
Because he could hear the rain.
And under the rain, from somewhere below him, through the floor, a voice. A woman's voice. Singing something with no words, while she scrubbed the pans. And then laughing at herself, suddenly, for no reason. A laugh like a kettle coming to the boil.
He knew it was not his. He knew it was Tam's. Tam's bed. Tam's rain. Tam's mother, Annie, who had cleaned the Third Basement for nine years and sold everything she had to the Bank, a bit at a time, until there was nothing left.
But it was his now too. Tam had given it to him.
And in the dream, lying in the dark in Tam's bed, with Tam's rain on the roof, Corin heard another voice. Not Annie's. A boy's. Very young. Very close. Right beside him in the dark, as if there were somebody else in the narrow bed, or kneeling on the floor next to it, with his chin on the edge of the mattress.
Don't be frightened, said the boy's voice. It's only the dark. The dark is the day with its eyes shut. That's all. When it opens its eyes again, it'll be morning.
Corin turned his head, in the dream, to see who it was.
And woke up.
The skylight above him was gray. Not black anymore. A dull, dirty, pale gray, with the clouds still moving slowly across it, lower now, heavier, the color of wet slate. The candle on the table had burned out. There was a small puddle of tallow in the saucer, gone hard and white, with the black stub of the wick lying in it.
Wren was gone.
He sat up. The attic was cold. Very cold. His breath went up white in the gray light. The black books stood in their rows on both sides, going away into the shadows under the slope of the roof, silent, waiting. Two thousand one hundred and seven of them.
On the table, where the candle had been, there was a piece of paper. Folded once. With a stone on it, to hold it down. A small round smooth gray stone, the kind you find on a beach.
He got up and went to the table and unfolded the paper.
Gone to put the white coat on. Back stair is clear. I looked.
Kross will come to Tallow Street from the top, by the chandler's, with the constructs walking behind. I'll be at the back, with Rowan. Ten minutes to eleven, by Saint Bride's bell.
When they knock, count. One, two, three. Then run. I'll give you as long as I can.
Fourth cellar. The brass key. Then down, toward the river. Don't stop.
— W.
And under that, smaller, in the less upright hand. The one she had used to write Not one of mine. Kept anyway.
The stone is from the beach below the old ferry. I've had it since I was very small. I don't know who gave it to me. Bring it back.
Corin picked up the stone.
It was cold, and very smooth, and it fitted exactly into the hollow of his palm, as if it had been made for a hand. There was a faint line of white quartz running round it, all the way, like a ring.
He put it in his pocket. The left one. With his knife, and his stub of pencil, and his bit of string, and the brass key with its paper tag that said 4.
Then he put his hand on his chest, over the Ledger, and felt it there. Thinner than it had been. Lighter. With the names gone out of it. But not empty. READ THIS FIRST was still there. And Oakhaven. And Elara. And the key with the oak-leaf bow, and the little red reader, and the curl of orange peel, and the last page, glued to the back cover, with its shaking line.
The dark is the day with its eyes shut.
He went to the shelf at the end, by the floor, and bent down, and looked at the last book in the row.
2,107.
He did not touch it. He only looked at it, for a long moment. Every name he knew, in there. Every door. Kept.
Then he went down the long low room between the black books, bending his head under the beams. He lifted the wooden bar off the door. He opened it.
The morning came in. Cold and gray and wet. It was not raining yet. But it was going to. He could smell it, coming up off the river, on the wind. Real rain. Gray, sour, sooty, Lower Docks rain.
He went down the back stair, slowly, holding the rope, stepping over the gap where the missing step was. Through the little yard with its empty flowerpots and its coal shed. Through the gate into the narrow black gap beside the house. And out into Mercer Street.
It was empty. There was nobody on it at all. The shop fronts were still shuttered. The one lamp at the far end had gone out. Above him, the little window under the roof of J. Ambrose, Bookbinder, was dark.
Somewhere across the city, a church bell began to ring. Six o'clock. Slowly.
Corin turned up his collar, and felt the small stiff square of the pocket there, with its list. THOSE I OWE. And he began to walk.
South. Downhill. Through the Ink Quarter and over the embankment and along the river and up through the streets of the Lower Docks.
Home.
To Tallow Street. To No. 14. To the five flights of stairs that had been built by someone who hated people, and the narrow room at the top under the slope of the roof, with its one window and its narrow bed. To the passage that smelled of onions, and the purple door at the end of it, and Mrs. Marta behind it, who had never once in thirty-four years let anybody make her hide.
He had five hours.
He walked them slowly, at first, through the waking Ink Quarter. The shutters were coming down from the shop fronts one by one, with a bang and a rattle, and the smell of hot glue and wet paper was starting to come out of the back doors, and somewhere under his feet, in a cellar, the first press of the day began to thump. A boy went past him with a stack of newspapers on his shoulder, still damp from the printing, and the ink came off on his fingers and on his cheek where he had wiped it. A woman was washing a doorstep with a brush and a bucket of gray water. A man in a leather apron was standing in the door of a bookbinder's, yawning, scratching his chest, looking at the sky.
"Going to rain," the man said to Corin as he passed. The way strangers say things to each other early in the morning, when the street is nearly empty and it feels rude not to.
"Yes," said Corin.
"Wednesday," said the man. "Always rains on a Wednesday." And he went back inside.
Corin walked on.
He thought about the drawing. Wren's drawing, folded in four in his pocket, next to the brass key and the beach stone. He took it out as he walked, and unfolded it, and looked at it, and made himself see every line. The tall narrow house. 91. The passage, the purple door, the kitchen, the scullery. The square in the floor, with its ring. The twelve rungs. The honeycomb of cellars. The square of crosshatching. The long line going down the hill. Left. Not right.
He looked at it until he could see it with his eyes shut. Then he looked at it again.
Then, at the corner where the Ink Quarter met the embankment, by a brazier where a night watchman was warming his hands before going home, he held the paper out over the coals. The watchman looked at him, and looked at the paper, and said nothing. The edge caught. It curled. It went brown, and then black, and then gold, and the lines of the house and the stairs and the drain went into the fire one after another, the tall narrow box and the zigzag and the honeycomb and the long slanting line, until there was nothing left but a flake of gray ash that lifted on the heat and went up into the morning and was gone.
The paper, Wren had said. Not the memory.
He still had the memory. He shut his eyes, there by the brazier, and saw it all. Every line.
"Something you didn't want anybody to read?" said the watchman.
"Something I'd already read," said Corin. And he walked on.
The first drops of rain began to fall as he came along the embankment past the Ink Quarter end of the Lantern Bridge. Big cold drops, far apart, darkening the stones one by one. He did not hurry. He stopped by the first lamppost, with its plaque and its smooth place where a name had been, and he looked up at the lamp in its black iron cage.
It was still burning.
All the others had been put out at dawn by the lamplighter, the way they were every morning. Every lamp on the bridge was dark, two long lines of cold black iron going away across the gray river.
Except that one.
It burned on, small and gold and steady, behind its glass, in the gray wet morning. As if somebody had forgotten it. Or as if somebody had decided, on this one morning of all mornings, to leave it lit.
Corin looked at it for a long moment, with the rain beginning to fall on his upturned face.
Then he turned his back on the bridge and walked on, south, along the river, toward the Lower Docks, and Tallow Street, and whatever was going to knock on the door.
