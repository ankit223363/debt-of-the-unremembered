Chapter 28: The Night Before, Part 1 of 5: The Attic on Mercer Street
The Attic on Mercer Street
The back stair went up the outside of the house like a ladder somebody had nailed to the wall and then forgotten about.
It was made of wood, old and gray and soft with rot in places, and it had no proper rail, only a rope strung from iron rings along the brick, slick with the wet of the night. The steps were narrow. Some of them were cracked. One, near the top, was missing altogether, and Corin had to stretch his leg over the gap with the rope burning his palm and the dark yard spinning a long way below him. He went slowly. He did not look down after the first time.
At the top there was a small landing, no bigger than a doormat, under the overhang of the roof. A door. Low and narrow and made of plain boards, unpainted, gray with age, with a latch instead of a handle. No light showed under it.
He knocked twice.
Not slowly. Not quickly either. Just twice. Like a neighbor.
For a long moment nothing happened. The wind came round the corner of the roof and pulled at his coat. Somewhere below, in the yard, a cat was moving among the empty flowerpots. He heard one of them fall over, and roll, and stop.
Then the latch lifted.
The door opened a crack. Not much. A hand's width. And in the crack there was darkness, and the smell of tallow and old paper, and a face.
A sharp, pale, careful face. A long nose and a narrow chin and dark eyebrows drawn very straight across, like two strokes of a pen. Brown hair, pulled back from the forehead so tight that it looked painted on. Eyes that went over Corin quickly, once, from his hair to his boots, the way they had gone over him in Gutter Lane on the first day, when she had come round the corner in her long white coat with her black notebook in her hand. Missing nothing.
She was not wearing the white coat now. She was wearing the gray cloak, with the hood down on her shoulders. Under it a plain dark dress, like a clerk's wife might wear, or a governess. No silver open eye on her breast. Nothing to say what she was.
"You came," said Wren Halloway.
"You said I promised."
"You did." She looked past him, down into the dark yard, and up, at the line of the roofs against the sky. Then back at him. "Were you followed?"
"From the bridge. I lost him in the Ink Quarter. Behind the paper warehouse."
"Gray coat? Hat pulled down? Stands like he's waiting for a friend who's late?"
It was exactly how the man had stood. Corin nodded.
"Gray coat," he said. "Yes."
"That's Fisk." She said it flatly. "Nab Fisk. He works for Kross. He's the best follower in the Inquisition and he knows it." A pause. "If you lost him, it's because he let you. He'll have gone to Kross and said you went into the Ink Quarter and vanished, and Kross will smile and eat a barley sugar and say good."
"Why good?"
"Because it means you're frightened enough to try." She stepped back and opened the door wider. "Come in. Quickly. Mind your head."
He came in. She shut the door behind him and dropped a wooden bar across it, into two iron hooks, and then stood for a moment with her hand flat on the boards, listening.
The attic was dark.
Not quite dark. There was one candle, on the small square table at the far end, under the skylight. It was a short stub, burned nearly to the saucer, and it gave a little ring of yellow light on the table and on the floor around it, and nothing much else. But Corin knew the room. He had been here once before, on Sunday, in the cold winter afternoon light with the frost on the skylight in feathers and ferns. He knew what was in the dark.
The shelves. Rough pine planks on iron brackets, going up both sides of the long low room under the slope of the roof, each one a little shorter than the one below it, like the steps of a stair going up into nothing. And on every shelf, spine to spine, packed tight, small black books. All the same. The kind you could buy for a copper at any stationer's. The kind the Ministry gave its clerks. Each one with a little white label on its spine, and a number, and a date, in her small upright hand.
On Sunday there had been thirty candles burning among them. Tonight there was one. And in the dark, the books were only a shape. A long, slow, sloping shape on both sides, like the ribs of some great animal, and the room was its belly, and he was standing inside it.
"Why only one candle?" he said.
"Because Fisk can count lit windows," said Wren, "and there's a skylight." She took her hand off the door. "Sit down. There's a chair by the table. The other one."
He went down the long low room, between the dark shelves, bending his head under the beams, to the table at the end. There were two chairs. Plain wooden ones, hard, with straight backs. He sat in the one that faced the door. He did not think about it. It was only afterwards, a long time afterwards, that he realized he had chosen it without thinking, because a man who has been followed all night does not sit with his back to a door.
Wren noticed. He saw her notice. She did not say anything. She came and sat in the other one, with her back to the skylight, so that the candle was between them and her face was half in the light and half in shadow.
She put her hands flat on the table. The way she had done on Sunday. Very straight. Very still.
"Are you hurt?" she said.
"No."
"You look hurt."
"I'm tired."
"That too." She looked at him across the candle for a long moment. "I heard about Fuller's Yard. The rope-walk. Everybody's heard. And Linden Walk, yesterday morning. Moll's report says dockers fighting about money." A very small movement at the corner of her mouth. "Moll is a terrible liar on paper. Everybody in the Watch house knows it. Nobody says."
"Do they know it was me?"
"Kross knows. Kross knows everything you did, Vane. That's his whole point. He's had a man on you since Sunday noon, and another one since Monday dawn, and they've written down every door you went in at." She did not look away. "I've read the list. I'm not supposed to have. I did."
Corin felt something go cold in his stomach.
"Read it to me," he said.
Wren did not move for a moment. Then she reached into the pocket of her dark dress and took out a small black notebook. Not one from the shelves. A new one, the cover still stiff, the ribbon still flat. She opened it near the front. She did not look at it. She knew it by heart. Corin could see that she knew it by heart.
"Sunday," she said. "Seven in the evening. The kitchen door of the Memory Bourse. Ten o'clock, the Bell, Cinder Lane. Two in the morning, Monday, Fuller's Yard, the rope-walk. Dawn, Linden Walk, Pruett and Son, livery. Seven, Saint Bride's, the fish market, Wharf Street, the coal wharf. A barge at the foot of the steps. Black. Red sail." She stopped. "The barge has gone. They looked for it at noon. It wasn't there. They don't know where it went."
Corin let out a breath he had not known he was holding.
"Two o'clock, Monday," said Wren. "The Oblivion Bank, Lantern Quay. Side door. Four hours." She glanced up at him. "Four hours, Vane. Kross wants very badly to know what you did in there for four hours."
He did not answer.
"Dusk, the quay. Then the arches under the Dye-Works. All night." She turned a page. "Tuesday. Late morning. No. 14 Tallow Street, by the back alley. Two in the afternoon, out the same way. Three o'clock, the Lamplighter's Rest, Ferrule Street. Six hours." She looked up. "Then the Ink Quarter, and nothing." She closed the notebook. "That's the list."
The candle burned between them. Very small. Very steady.
"Every door," said Corin quietly.
"Every door."
"Except this one."
Wren looked at him across the little flame.
"Except this one," she said. "Yes. For now."
Me First
"You said you'd tell me first," said Wren. "When you knew."
"I know."
"Do you know?"
Corin looked at the candle.
He thought about what he knew. It was not much. It was a great deal. It was a man in a glass room with a face that slid away. A gold ring with a dome on it. A silver rod with a spiral that turned the wrong way. A gentle voice saying I emptied him myself. A Colonel in an iron-colored coat who wanted six old books. An empty column in a ledger. A fever that was not a fever. A key in the shape of an oak leaf.
He did not know what any of it meant. Not really. Not all of it. But he knew more than he had known on Sunday, when he sat in this chair with the frost on the skylight and made her a promise.
And he had promised.
"Some of it," he said.
Wren folded her hands on the table. "Then tell me some of it."
So he told her.
He told her about Tam. He had not meant to start there, but that was where it started, so that was where he started. A young man on a mattress against a warm wall, with frost in his hair that would not melt. Three hours of holding still. Take it. All of it. The rod. The gray light winding round and round onto the silver. The sea.
And then the glass room.
He told her about Vivienne in her black velvet chair, with her fan and her necklace of glowing vials, and her white knuckles. About Kross, unwrapping his barley sugar, folding the paper smaller and smaller. About the two big men behind Tam, too close. About the Colonel in the shadow, in his long coat the color of an iron gate, with the dull red at his cuffs, and his ice-pale eyes, and his deep heavy voice. The books. The six. You have them?
He told her about the lift.
Wren had not moved while he talked. She sat with her hands folded and her face half in the light and half in shadow, and she listened the way she did everything, very still and very carefully, missing nothing. When he got to the Colonel, he saw her eyes go narrow, very slightly. When he got to Kross standing up, and folding his gloved hands, and saying Sir, he saw her lips press together.
When he got to the lift, she stopped breathing.
Corin saw it. He saw her chest stop going up and down under the gray cloak. He went on. He told her about the man coming out of the lamplit cage. The plain dark coat. The long clean hands. The ring. The rod. The face that you could not look at, that your eyes slid off the way your foot slides off a wet stone.
And the voice.
"Gentle," he said. "That's the only way I can say it. It was the gentlest voice I've ever heard. Like a doctor at a bedside. It went into you. Into your chest. Like a warm stone. And then you didn't want to move. You only wanted him to keep talking."
Wren's hands had come apart on the table. They lay flat now, side by side. Very white.
"Go on," she said. Her voice was very quiet.
He went on. He told her about the rod touching Tam's forehead. About the hand going through Tam's rooms, quickly, lifting lids, looking for Corin. About He's grown, hasn't he. About I emptied him myself. I wrote on every door. All but one. About the locked door, locked from the inside by somebody who loved him very much. When it opens, I want to be the first thing he sees.
About Especially that one.
And the mouth, for less than a breath. Thin. Pale. Curving up.
When he had finished, nobody said anything for a long time.
The candle burned between them. It had burned down another quarter of an inch while he talked. Its flame leaned a little toward the door, the way candle flames do when there is a draught somewhere you cannot feel.
Wren was looking at the table. At her own white hands, flat on the wood.
"I've heard it," she said.
Corin did not move.
"The voice." She did not look up. "Once. Six years ago. Seven, nearly. On the day I swore my oath."
She was quiet for a moment. When she went on, her voice was flat and careful and level, the voice she used for reading out the minutes of a meeting. But Corin could hear what was under it. The same thing he had heard under Vivienne's calm, through Tam's ears. A wire, pulled very tight.
"When you finish at the Academy," she said, "they take you to the Hill. To the old part of the Ministry. The part with the towers. They take you at night, alone, with a hood over your head, so you don't see the way. And they put you in a room. A round room, all stone, with no windows, and one door, and a great many candles. And at one end of the room there's a screen. A tall screen, carved wood, very old, with holes cut in it in a pattern like leaves. And you kneel in front of the screen. And somebody on the other side of it asks you the questions."
She looked up at him.
"Will you serve the Ministry," she said, "in all things, and forget what it asks you to forget? And you say yes. Will you knock on any door it sends you to, and not ask who is behind it? And you say yes. Will you remember nothing that you are told not to? And you say yes." She was quiet for a moment. "There are twelve of them. You learn the answers by heart, before. Everybody does. It's only words."
"And the voice on the other side of the screen—"
"Was gentle," said Wren. "Yes. The gentlest voice I have ever heard. And it went into my chest like a warm stone. And by the end of the twelve questions I would have done anything it told me to. Anything at all. I would have walked into the river." Her face did not change. "And when I came out of that room, with the hood back over my head, and they took me down the stairs, I was crying. And I didn't know why. And I couldn't remember anything about it except the questions, and my own answers, and the screen with the leaves cut in it."
"Not his face."
"There was a screen."
"No. I mean—" Corin stopped. "I mean, even if there hadn't been. You wouldn't have been able to see it."
Wren looked at him for a long moment.
"No," she said. "I don't suppose I would."
She looked down at her hands again.
"Everybody in the Inquisition swears the oath," she said. "Everybody. Kross swore it. Brack swore it. Every Inquisitor who has ever knocked on a door in Veridia knelt in front of that screen and said yes twelve times to that voice." A pause. "Nobody ever talks about it. Nobody ever says, Did you hear him too? Did you feel it? You just don't. It's like—" She stopped.
"Like a word on the tip of your tongue," said Corin quietly. "That you can never say."
She looked at him sharply.
"The lamplighter," said Corin. "On the bridge. Tonight. He said his grandfather used to stand by the first post, by the plaque with the name chiseled out, and open his mouth, and nothing would come."
Wren did not say anything.
"And Malakor," said Corin. "The voice in my rod. He tried to tell me something tonight, about Oakhaven, about a night in the spring. And he couldn't. He said it was like trying to pick up water with a fork."
He looked at her across the candle.
"There's a lot of that," he said. "In this city. Things nobody can say. Have you noticed?"
Wren was very still.
"I've noticed," she said. "I've been noticing for six years." She reached out, slowly, and touched the spine of the nearest black book on the shelf beside her. Lightly. With one fingertip. "That's why I keep these."
The candle flame leaned toward the door.
"You kept your promise," said Wren. Her voice was quieter. "You told me first."
"Yes."
"Before Lyra."
"Lyra doesn't know any of it yet. She's on a barge somewhere past the north locks with her father. Juno knows some. Not all."
Wren nodded slowly.
"Then I'll keep mine," she said. "I said, on Sunday, that if you told me first, I'd tell you what I knew. Whatever it cost." She took a breath. "So here it is. And you'd better listen carefully, Vane. Because I'm only going to say it once, and after I've said it, I'm not an Inquisitor anymore. Not really. Not in any way that counts."
She put both hands flat on the table again.
"They're coming for you tomorrow," she said.
The Order
She took a folded paper out of her cloak and laid it on the table between them, beside the candle.
It was not a scrap, this time. It was a whole sheet. Thick white Ministry paper, the good kind, with the corners cut square and a deckled edge along one side. Folded in three. And on the outside of it, at the top, pressed into the paper so hard that it had left a dent, there was a stamp in gray ink. An eye. Wide open. With the open book under it.
The mark of the Inquisition.
"I copied it," said Wren. "This afternoon. In the records room, when the clerk went out for his dinner. I'm not supposed to be able to get into the records room. I've been getting in since my second year." A small movement at the corner of her mouth. "The original's in Kross's desk. This is exact. Every word."
She did not unfold it. She pushed it across the table toward him.
Corin unfolded it.
The writing was Wren's. He knew it now. Small and neat and quick and very upright. But it was laid out the way Ministry papers were always laid out, with a heading, and a date, and a number, and lines ruled across under the heading, and he could see exactly how the original must have looked.
OFFICE OF THE INQUISITION. ORDER OF COLLECTION.
No. 2,107.
Wednesday. The eleventh hour of the morning.
To be collected: C. VANE. Harvester. Senior Investigator, Harvesting Section. Last known lodging: No. 14 Tallow Street, Lower Docks. Top floor.
Collecting officer: Inquisitor S. Kross.
Attending: Inquisitor W. Halloway. Cadet R. Thorne (first collection).
Constructs: two.
Manner: Gentle. The subject is not to be damaged. Under no circumstances is the subject to be emptied at the door. He is to be brought whole to the Hill, to the east tower, and taken down.
If the subject is not at the lodging: the household to be collected in his place, and held, until he comes for them.
Household known: Mrs. M. (landlady). A girl, J., about fourteen (granddaughter).
By order.
And at the bottom, where a signature should have been, there was nothing. Only a note, in Wren's hand, in brackets.
(The original is not signed. It is sealed. A small red seal, in wax. A dome, with rays.)
Corin read it twice.
Then he laid it down on the table, very carefully, beside the candle, and sat looking at it.
No. 2,107.
He looked up at the shelves. At the long dark rows of small black books going away down both sides of the attic into the shadow. Each one with a little white label. A number, and a date.
"Two thousand one hundred and six," he said quietly.
"Yes."
"And I'm the next one."
Wren did not answer. She did not need to.
"Taken down," said Corin. "What does that mean? Brought whole to the Hill, to the east tower, and taken down."
Wren was quiet for a long moment.
"There's a door," she said at last. "In the east tower. At the bottom of the stairs. Below the cellars. Below the records room and the old vaults and the place where they keep the Erasers when they aren't walking. An iron door. Round. Like the door of a bank's strong room, only older. Much older." Her voice was very level. "I've never been through it. Nobody I know has ever been through it. Only Kross. And a few others. When they come back up, they don't talk about it."
"But you've been near it."
"Once. Two years ago. Kross sent me down to the last cellar with a message, and the iron door was standing open a crack, and I heard—" She stopped.
"What?"
"Music," said Wren. "An organ. One note. Very low. So low I felt it in my teeth more than heard it. Going on and on, and never stopping, the way the sea never stops. And over it, voices. A great many voices. Singing." She looked at him. "No words. Just voices. Hundreds of them, maybe. All singing the same thing, very softly, over and over, with no words in it. Like people humming in their sleep."
Corin felt the skin on the back of his neck go cold.
"They call it downstairs," said Wren. "The ones who know. Just that. He's gone downstairs. Take her downstairs. As if it were a kitchen."
She looked down at the copied order on the table.
"Every collection I've ever done," she said, "every one of the two thousand one hundred and six, the order said empty at the door. That's what the Erasers are for. They knock. Three times. Slowly. And whoever opens the door—" She stopped. "Rook made them so that there's a moment. A breath. Between the knock and the door. To give people time. To hide something. To say goodbye. To hold somebody's hand." Her voice did not change. "And then the door opens. It always opens. I don't know why. I've stood on two thousand steps and watched two thousand doors, and not one of them has ever stayed shut. People open them. Even when they know. Even when they've heard the knock up and down their street for weeks. They come to the door, and they open it, and they look up at the white faces. And the constructs put their hands on them. And afterwards they're—" She stopped again.
"Empty," said Corin. "Like Wick."
"Like Wick. Yes. Slates. Clean. Ready to be written on again." She was quiet. "And I go home, and I come up here, and I write their names in a black book, so somebody remembers that they were there."
She put her finger on the order. On one line.
Under no circumstances is the subject to be emptied at the door.
"I've never seen that before," she said. "Not once. In two thousand one hundred and six orders. Gentle. Not to be damaged. Brought whole." She looked at him. "He wants you whole, Vane. He wants every room in you just as it is. Every door. Especially the locked one."
When it opens, I want to be the first thing he sees.
"And this," said Wren. Her finger moved down the page. If the subject is not at the lodging: the household to be collected in his place, and held, until he comes for them. "This is why I sent you that note. Not home. Because if you were home, they'd take you. And I thought—" She stopped. For the first time since he had known her, she seemed not to know how to finish a sentence. "I thought if you weren't, you'd have one more day. To run. To do whatever you have to do."
She looked at the line again. The household to be collected in his place.
"And then I copied this out," she said quietly, "and I read it properly. And I understood what I'd done."
Corin looked at the paper for a long time.
Household known: Mrs. M. (landlady). A girl, J., about fourteen (granddaughter).
He thought of Mrs. Marta, at her kitchen table, with her account book and her pencil and her purple door bolted top and bottom. Nobody's ever made me hide behind it. And no gray man on a step is going to start now.
He thought of Juno, standing at the stove with her back to him, saying Three hours. If you're not back in three hours, I'm coming.
He had been gone a great deal longer than three hours.
"What time is it?" he said.
"Nearly eleven." Wren did not look at a clock. There was no clock. She simply knew, the way she knew everything. "Of the night. Tuesday."
"Twelve hours," said Corin.
"Twelve hours."
He folded the copy of the order up again, in three, along its creases. He held it in his hands. It was very light. It weighed nothing at all.
"Who's R. Thorne?" he said. "Cadet R. Thorne. First collection."
And Wren Halloway, who had stood on two thousand one hundred and six steps and watched two thousand one hundred and six doors open, and not wept at any of them, put her face in her hands.
