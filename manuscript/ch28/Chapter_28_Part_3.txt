Chapter 28: The Night Before, Part 3 of 5: The Way Down
The Way Down
"Show me," said Corin. "The way. From my room to the drain."
Wren looked at him. Then she nodded, once, and pulled a sheet of paper toward her across the table. The back of an old Ministry form, gone yellow, with printed lines on the other side that showed through faintly like the bones of a hand held up to a lamp. She uncorked the ink. She dipped the pen.
"You know your own stairs better than I do," she said. "So you draw the house. I'll draw what's under it."
He took the pen.
He had never drawn No. 14 Tallow Street before. He had never needed to. He had climbed its stairs twice a day for six years, in the dark and in the light, drunk with tiredness and sick with Ash and once, early on, with a broken wrist that he did not remember breaking. His feet knew every step. But his hand did not know how to put them on paper, and for a long moment he sat with the pen over the yellow sheet and could not begin.
Then he did.
A tall narrow box, for the house. Five floors. He drew lines across it for the floors, unevenly, because the floors of No. 14 were uneven. He drew the staircase, a long zigzag going up the middle, from the front door to the top. He drew the broken banister on the third floor, a little gap in the zigzag, without thinking, because his hand always reached for it there and found nothing.
He drew his room at the top. A small triangle, under the slope of the roof. With a square for the window, and a line for the bed.
"How many steps?" said Wren.
"From my door to the front door? Ninety-one."
She looked at him.
"I counted," said Corin. "Once. On a bad night. I couldn't sleep and I didn't have a candle, so I went down the stairs in the dark and counted them, and then back up and counted them again, to see if it came out the same." He shrugged. "It did. Ninety-one."
Wren wrote 91 beside the zigzag, in her small upright hand.
"And the cellars?"
He drew the ground floor. The front door. The dark passage going back, past the foot of the stairs, that smelled of onions. The purple door at the end of it, which he did not color, because he had nothing to color it with, but he drew a small circle on it, for the brass knob. Mrs. Marta's kitchen behind it. And the scullery behind the kitchen, with its back door into the yard, and the horseshoe over the back door.
And in the floor of the scullery, by the copper, a square.
"A trap," he said. "Wood. With a ring. Mrs. Marta keeps her potatoes down there. And coal. There's a ladder."
"How deep?"
"Ten rungs. Maybe twelve."
"And then?"
"I don't know," said Corin. "I've never been past the potatoes."
Wren took the pen back.
She drew, under his house, a long low shape. A row of shapes, going away to the left and right. The cellars. Not just under No. 14. Under all the houses on that side of Tallow Street, one after another, like the cells of a honeycomb.
"They're joined," she said. "Most people don't know. Most people in the Lower Docks think their cellar is their cellar, and that's the end of it. But the houses on Tallow Street were built on top of an older street. Two hundred years older, maybe more. And the old street had cellars too. Deep ones. And when they built the new houses, they just bricked over the top of the old cellars and dug new ones on top." She drew a line, under the honeycomb. A second layer. "There are doors between the old cellars. Bricked up, most of them. But not all."
She tapped the pen on one square in the lower row. The fourth from the end.
"The coal merchant's cellar," she said. "Under No. 14 and No. 16. His coal comes in through a chute from the street and goes down into the old cellar underneath, because it's bigger and drier. And at the back of it, there's a grating. Iron. In the floor." She drew a small square of crosshatched lines. "That's the brass key. And under the grating, there's the drain."
She drew it. A long line, going down. Not straight down. Slanting. Away from the houses, under the street, down the hill.
"It's old," she said. "Older than the street. Brick, with a curved roof. About this high." She held her hand up, flat, about level with her own shoulder. "You'll have to bend. There's water in the bottom. Not deep. Ankle deep, mostly. Deeper after rain." She looked at the skylight. "It's going to rain tomorrow."
"How do you know?"
"I can smell it." She did not smile. "The drain goes down the hill for about a quarter of a mile. Under Tallow Street, under Chandler's Row, under the bottom of Saint Bride's. And then it meets the big sewer. The one that runs along under Wharf Street to the Customs House." She drew a thick line, crossing the thin one. "When you get to the big sewer, go left. Toward the river. Not right. Right goes up under the Hill. And you don't want to go anywhere near the Hill."
"Left," said Corin.
"Left. And keep going. Don't stop. Don't go back for anything."
He looked at the drawing for a long time. The tall narrow box of the house. The zigzag of the stairs. 91. The square in the scullery floor. The honeycomb of the cellars. The square of crosshatching. The long slanting line going down the hill in the dark.
"Ninety-one steps," he said slowly. "And then the passage. And the purple door. And through the kitchen. And the scullery. And the trap. And twelve rungs. And then through the old cellars to the coal merchant's. And then the grating." He looked up at her. "That's not one breath."
"No."
"It's not ten breaths."
"No."
"Then how—"
"You won't be at the top of the house when they knock," said Wren.
He stared at her.
"Kross thinks you will be," she said. "He thinks you'll go up to your room, and shut your door, and sit on your bed, and wait. Because that's what you'd do. That's what he's watched you do, every night, for days. Fisk has written it down: Subject returns to lodging. Goes to top floor. Candle lit. Candle out." She tapped the little triangle at the top of the drawing, the room under the roof. "So that's where the constructs will go. Up the ninety-one steps. To your door. That's where they'll knock."
"And where will I be?"
Wren put the point of the pen on the square in the scullery floor. The trap. With the ring.
"Here," she said. "Under the potatoes. With the trap shut over your head. Listening."
Corin looked at the square.
"And when they knock on my door at the top of the house, and I don't open it—"
"Then they'll wait a breath. The way Rook made them. And then they'll break it." Her voice was very level. "And find the room empty. And the bed made. And the candle cold. And they'll stand there, and turn their white faces, slowly, the way they do. And Kross will come up the stairs behind them, eating his barley sugar, and look at the empty room, and smile, and say Well." A pause. "And then he'll start looking. And that will take time. Ninety-one steps of time. Down, and along the passage, and through every room."
"And Mrs. Marta? And Juno?"
"Gone." Wren looked at him. "Gone before ten. You send them out the back, through the alley, and away. To Moll's. To the Watch house back gate. Knock fast." She tapped the order, folded on the table. "The order says if the subject is not at the lodging. But you'll be at the lodging. Under the floor of it. So they won't be collected in your place. Not today. Not if you're there."
Corin sat back in his chair.
He looked at the drawing. At the square in the scullery floor, with the ring. At the line going down the hill.
"That's not a plan either," he said. "That's hiding under the potatoes and hoping."
"Yes."
"And if they don't go to the top first? If Kross guesses?"
"Then you'll need something else." She looked at him across the candle. "Something you've got that he hasn't thought of. Something you can burn."
Corin did not say anything.
He thought of all the things he had burned. The taste of his mother's soup. His first friend's name. The way the light came through the window of the schoolroom. Room after room after room, swept clean, with Oakhaven chalked on the doors.
And he thought of what was left. Not much. Not much at all. Some of it new. Tam's bed, and Tam's rain. The book in his Ledger. THIS BOOK IS CORIN VANE'S.
And some of it very, very old.
Things he did not know he had. Things that had come through the empty rooms with him, the way some things did. The way he knew how to tie his boots and which side of the road to walk on. The way he knew how to read.
The way he knew how to speak.
He sat very still in the hard wooden chair.
Wren was watching him.
"What?" she said.
"Nothing," said Corin. "Not yet."
She looked at him for a long moment. Then she folded the drawing of No. 14 in half, and in half again, and held it out to him across the candle.
"Learn it," she said. "Tonight. Then burn the paper. Not the memory. The paper."
He took it.
Rewriting the Ledger
"If they take you," said Wren, "they take that."
She was looking at the Ledger, lying on the table between them, closed, with its leather strap tied round it.
"Everything in it," she said. "Every name. Every door. Every place you've been and everybody you've talked to. Kross won't need Fisk anymore. He won't need anybody. He'll just open your book." A pause. "And whatever isn't in his list already will be by noon."
Corin looked at the Ledger.
He had known this. Of course he had known it. Oren had said it, in the stable at Pruett's, on Monday, with the Bank runner going away across the yard. If she puts her hand on that book, she'll know everything you know. And Linus Hale had said it, this afternoon, closing the book under his blue hand. The less you know, the fewer doors he can open.
But he had not thought about it properly. Not until now. Not until he was sitting in an attic at half past eleven at night with a copy of his own collection order folded in his pocket.
"Elara couldn't read it," he said slowly. "The Auditor. At the Bank. She put her finger on it and the glass went black, and she said there was something in it that didn't want to be read."
"Elara isn't Kross," said Wren. "And Kross isn't going to put his finger on it. He's going to open it and turn the pages. The ordinary way. With his eyes." She looked at him. "Your handwriting's very clear, Vane. I've seen it. Anybody could read it."
Corin did not say anything.
He untied the strap. He opened the Ledger at the front.
READ THIS FIRST.
He turned the page. And the next. And the next. Six years of pages, in his own small careful hand. He had not looked at them all together like this for a long time. He only ever read the first page, every morning. And then the last few, at night, to see what he had written that day. The rest he left alone. It was too heavy to read all at once. Like trying to carry a whole house on your back.
He turned the pages now.
Names. Everywhere. On every page. Toby, who calls me Inspector. Vespera. Hook Lane. The chair by the window. Mrs. Marta says rent is due Friday. Juno. 14. A key on a string. Terrence's girl. Oren. Captain. A scar from the temple to the mouth. Lyra Thorne. Lyra. Lyra. Moll at the Watch house. Knock fast. Briggs. Intake Hall. Fenn. Mother Grue. The arches under the Dye-Works. Hessa Doon. The Patient Grace. Wat. Robin. Bram. Quill Row. Under the loose board.
Every door.
He sat and looked at them.
"I can't burn it," he said. His voice came out strange. "If I burn it, I lose them. I won't remember any of them. Not properly. I'll wake up tomorrow and read the first page and I won't know who Juno is."
"I know."
"I can't carry it. If I carry it, he takes it."
"I know."
Corin looked at her across the candle.
"Then what?"
Wren did not answer at once. She got up from her chair. She went to the shelf behind her, the lowest one, near the floor, where the shelves were longest. She bent down and took something out from behind the last row of black books, where it had been pushed back against the slope of the roof, out of sight.
It was another black book. Small. Plain. The kind you could buy for a copper at any stationer's. Exactly like all the others on the shelves. But new. The cover was stiff. The black ribbon was still flat and folded. And there was no label on its spine.
She laid it on the table beside the Ledger.
"I bought it on Sunday," she said. "After you left. I didn't know why. I just went into a stationer's on Mercer Street and bought it and brought it home and put it at the back of the shelf." She looked at it. "I think I knew. Even then. I think I knew I'd need another one."
She sat down again.
"Two thousand one hundred and seven," she said. "It doesn't have a label yet. It doesn't have a name in it yet. It's just a black book, like all the others." She put one finger on its cover. "Nobody has ever opened one of the books on these shelves except me. Nobody even knows they're here. Not Kross. Not anybody. If somebody came up those stairs tomorrow and looked round this room, they'd see two thousand black books with numbers on them, and they'd think I was mad, and they'd go away again."
Corin looked at the new book.
Then he looked at the Ledger.
And he understood.
"Write them in here," said Wren. "The names. The doors. Everything Kross mustn't have. Copy them out of your Ledger into this one. Then cut them out of your Ledger. Carefully. With a knife, close to the spine, so nobody can tell there were ever pages there." She did not look away. "And leave this one here. On the shelf. With the others. Between two thousand one hundred and six and the wall."
"And when I need them?"
"Then you'll come back for them," said Wren. "When it's safe. Or somebody will bring them to you." A pause. "And until then, they'll be here. Kept. Remembered. Like everybody else."
Corin sat very still.
He thought of Hollis Marsh, in his loft full of jars, with his hand over the hole in his chest. I know they're not lost. I know they're somewhere. He thought of Elara at her glass desk, writing (The orange.) in her little black book in pencil. He thought of Hessa Doon, keeping the names of the river dead in the back of her tide book, so the river knows we know.
Everybody in this city, it seemed, was keeping something for somebody, in a book or a jar or a box, against the day it would be needed.
"All right," he said.
Wren gave him a pen. It was a good one, steel-nibbed, with a wooden handle worn smooth by her fingers. And a pot of ink, black, with the cork already out. She moved the candle so that it stood between the two books, the old brown Ledger and the new black one, and its light fell on both.
And Corin began to write.
He did it slowly. Very slowly. Much more slowly than he usually wrote. He went through the Ledger page by page, from the front, and every time he came to a name, or a place, or a door, he stopped. And read it. And read it again. And then copied it, exactly, word for word, into the new black book. In his smallest, clearest hand. And underneath it, if there was room, anything else he wanted to keep. A face. A voice. A thing they had said.
Juno. Fourteen. Terrence's girl. Marta's grand. Runs the roofs. A brass key on a string: NIGHT DOOR III. Says "Course I will." Holds your hand like it's nothing.
Mrs. Marta. No. 14 Tallow Street. The purple door at the end of the passage that smells of onions. Rent due Friday. You never forget the rent. Mutton and onion pie. "Nobody's ever made me hide behind it."
Oren Vance. Captain. Scar from the left temple across the cheek to the mouth. Short gray beard. Gray eyes, cold and steady. Burns words. Has paid: chair. Spoon. Held the gate gold. His brother sends his regards.
Lyra Thorne.
He stopped at that one for a long time.
Then he wrote it all. Every page of her. The step at Pruett's, with the knife. The coal dust on her face by the barge. The letter, small and fierce, slanting hard to the right. Eat something. Her father, tapping. Her brother, throwing her letters into the stove still sealed, so that everybody would see.
He did not know how long it took. Hours. The candle burned down and Wren lit another from it, without a word, and then another. Below in the dark house the tinny clock struck midnight, and then one, and then the half hour. His hand cramped. He stopped and shook it and went on. The new black book filled up, page after page, in his small clear hand, and the old brown Ledger, as he went, got lighter.
Because after every name, he took his knife.
It was a small knife, with a bone handle and a short blade, very sharp. He had had it as long as he could remember, which was six years. He opened the Ledger flat on the table, and laid the blade against the page, close to the spine, as close as he could get, the way Wren had said. And he cut. Slowly. Carefully. From the top to the bottom. And the page came away in his hand.
The first time, he could hardly do it. His hand would not move. He sat with the knife against the paper and the page with Juno on it under his palm, and he could not make himself cut. It felt like cutting off a finger.
Do it, boy, said Malakor quietly, behind his ear. It's not gone. It's in the other book. You've written it there. It's kept.
He did it.
And the next one was easier. And the one after that was easier still. And by the time the tinny clock struck two, there was a small pile of pages on the table beside the candle, in his own hand, cut clean from the spine. And the Ledger lay open in front of him, much thinner than it had been, with the names gone out of it like teeth from a jaw.
Wren took the cut pages. All of them. She did not read them. She folded them once, very neatly, and put them inside the back cover of the new black book, and closed it. Then she took a little white label from a box on the shelf, and her pen, and wrote on it, small and upright:
2,107.
And under it, a date. Tomorrow's.
She licked the label and pressed it onto the spine of the new black book. And she got up, and bent down, and put the book on the lowest shelf. At the end. Between book number two thousand one hundred and six and the wall.
It looked exactly like all the others.
Corin looked at it for a long time.
Then he looked down at what was left of his Ledger. The first page, still there. READ THIS FIRST. The pages about himself. The burned things. The lists. Oakhaven. The empty rooms. Elara.
He had not cut Elara out.
He had sat with the knife against that page for a long time. Elara. Auditor, Fourth Desk. Short dark hair, cut straight. A gold front tooth, over a gap. And he had not been able to. Because she was not a door he had knocked on. She was a door that had been there before all the others. On the first page. In the first line under Oakhaven.
If Kross took the Ledger, Kross would read that. He knew it. He would read it and know.
But Kross already knew. The man in the glass room already knew. Especially that one.
And Corin was not going to cut his sister out of his own book.
