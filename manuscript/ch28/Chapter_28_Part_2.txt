Chapter 28: The Night Before, Part 2 of 5: Cadet R. Thorne
Cadet R. Thorne
She did not cry for long.
Corin had not known she could cry at all. He sat across the candle from her and did not move and did not say anything, and after a while she took her hands away from her face, and her face was dry. Quite dry. As if she had only been holding it, not weeping into it. Only her eyes were red at the rims. And her mouth was set very hard.
"Rowan," she said. "Rowan Thorne."
Corin had known. He had known the moment he read the line. But hearing it was different.
"Lyra's brother," he said.
"Lyra's brother. Lord Aurelian's son. Sixteen years old." Wren's voice had gone flat again. Very careful. "When the house fell, when Lord Thorne burned and the Council took the seat and Cressida took the papers, they put Lyra out on the street with nothing. You know that. And they put Rowan in the Academy."
"The Inquisitor Academy."
"On the Hill. Behind the east tower. A great gray building with no windows on the ground floor." A pause. "I was there four years. I know every stone of it. It's where they make us."
She looked at the candle.
"He came in the autumn," she said. "Two years ago. Fourteen. With a trunk and a coat that was too good for the place and a face like a locked door. The other cadets hated him. A lord's son, from a house that had fallen. They called him names. They put things in his bed." Her hands were flat on the table again. "I was a senior cadet then. In my last year. I was set over the new ones, to teach them drill and letters and the rules. So I saw him every day."
"What was he like?"
Wren thought about it. She seemed to be choosing the words very carefully.
"Angry," she said. "All the time. Like a dog that's been kicked and has decided that it's going to bite first, from now on, before anybody can kick it again. He wouldn't talk to anybody. He wouldn't eat with the others. He did everything he was told, perfectly, faster and better than anybody, and he never once smiled." A pause. "He blamed her."
"Lyra."
"For all of it. For the house falling. For their father. For him being there. He'd decided she'd done something. Or not done something. That if she'd been a better daughter, or a cleverer one, or a different one, it wouldn't have happened." Wren shook her head slightly. "He was fourteen. You need somebody to blame, at fourteen. He picked her."
Corin thought of Lyra on the step of Pruett's house, with the kitchen knife in her hand and the bandage on her arm. Of Lyra on the steps by the Patient Grace, with coal dust on her face, saying He's been trying to tell us. All this time. And none of us could hear him. Of Lyra's letter, small and fierce, slanting hard to the right. I thought I would go mad.
She had never mentioned a brother. Not once. Not to him.
"And then Kross found him," said Wren.
The candle flame leaned toward the door.
"Kross comes to the Academy, sometimes," she said. "To look at the cadets. To pick out the ones he wants. He's very good at it. He walks along the line on the drill ground, eating a barley sugar, and he looks at each of you, and he smiles, and you can feel it, like a cold hand on the back of your neck. And then he goes away. And a week later, one of you gets sent for."
She was quiet for a moment.
"He stopped in front of Rowan," she said. "That first autumn. On the drill ground. In the rain. And he looked at him for a long time. And then he did something I've never seen him do with anybody else. He took a barley sugar out of his pocket. And he held it out." Her voice did not change. "And Rowan took it."
"And?"
"And after that, Kross came every week. Every week, for two years. He'd walk with Rowan round the drill ground. Talk to him. Listen to him. Nobody had listened to Rowan since his father burned. Not the masters. Not the other cadets. Not me, Saints help—" She stopped. Her mouth went tight. "Not me. I was too busy being good at being a cadet. And Kross listened. For hours. And he told Rowan that he was clever, and strong, and that the Ministry needed young men like him, and that one day, if he worked very hard, he could be anything he wanted. Even a Councillor. Even—" She stopped again.
"Even what?"
"Even the one who put things right," said Wren quietly. "For his family. That's what Kross told him. You can put it right, Rowan. Everything she broke. You can mend it."
Corin sat very still.
"He worships him," said Wren. "Kross. Rowan thinks Kross is the only person in Veridia who has ever been kind to him. And maybe he's right. Maybe he is." She looked at the copied order, folded on the table. "Kross bought him skills, too. Last year. In little stones, from the Bank. Sword-work. Riding. Tracking. Things that take years to learn, poured into him in an afternoon, so that he's better at them than masters who've spent their lives." A pause. "He doesn't know how to feel them, though. That's the thing about bought skills. Your hands know what to do. But you never learned it, so it doesn't feel like yours. Like wearing somebody else's boots that happen to fit."
"And now his first collection," said Corin.
"And now his first collection. A great honor. Every cadet dreams of it. Your first knock. Your first door." She looked at him across the candle. "And Kross chose you."
"Why?"
Wren did not answer at once.
"Because you're Lyra's," she said at last. "Because everybody in the Ministry who matters knows that you've been helping her. The Bourse. The townhouse. Pruett's. Fuller's Yard. Kross knows all of it. He's had a man on you since Sunday." Her voice was very flat. "And so he's going to take Rowan to your door. To the door of the man who's been helping the sister Rowan hates. And he's going to stand beside him on the step while the constructs knock. And Rowan is going to look up at that door and think, This is it. This is me putting it right. This is me taking something back from her."
She was quiet.
"And when it's done," she said, "he'll belong to Kross. All the way. Forever. There won't be anything left of Aurelian Thorne's son that Kross doesn't own."
The attic was very quiet. Somewhere below them, in the dark house, a board creaked. The candle had burned down so far that its flame was nearly in the saucer.
Corin looked at the copied order on the table.
Cadet R. Thorne (first collection).
He thought of a height chart on a nursery wall, at Crown Walk. Three sets of pencil marks going up the white paint. Lyra's. Rowan's. And the third set, gouged out.
He thought of Lord Thorne, propped up on three white pillows, tapping on the wall. Four, two, nine, three. And at sundown yesterday, on a barge somewhere past the north locks, looking at his daughter and saying one clear word.
Linus.
Not Rowan. Not Lyra. Linus.
"Does Lyra know?" he said. "Where he is? What Kross is doing?"
"She knows he's at the Academy. She's written to him. Every month, for two years. I know because I saw the letters come in, when I was a senior cadet." Wren's face did not change. "He never opened them. He used to throw them in the stove in the cadets' hall, still sealed, in front of everybody. So they'd see."
Corin closed his eyes.
"Don't tell her," said Wren. "Not yet. Not tonight. She's on that barge with her father, and her father is starting to come back, and she needs to be there. If you tell her Rowan is going to be standing on your doorstep tomorrow at eleven o'clock with Silas Kross and two Erasers, she'll come. You know she will. She'll come running across the city with a kitchen knife, and Kross will be waiting for her." A pause. "That's what he wants, Vane. Don't you see? That's the whole point of putting Rowan on the step. Not you. Her."
Especially that one.
Corin opened his eyes.
He thought of the mouth in the glass room, curving up. He thought of the gentle voice. People always come out of a house when you knock on their friends' doors.
"I won't tell her," he said.
Wren looked at him for a long time across the dying candle.
Then she reached out, and took a fresh candle from a box on the shelf beside her, and lit it from the stub, and pressed it down into the soft wax in the saucer before the old one could go out. The new flame caught and steadied. The shadows of the black books leaped up the slope of the roof and settled again.
"Good," she said. "Then the next question is what you're going to do."
The Choice
Corin did not answer at once.
He sat in the hard wooden chair with his back to the slope of the roof and the black books going up into the dark behind him, and he looked at the new candle. It burned very straight. Very clean. The flame was a small pale leaf of light, with a blue heart at the bottom of it, and it did not lean toward the door anymore. Whatever draught there had been had stopped.
"If I'm not there," he said slowly, "they take Mrs. Marta. And Juno."
"Yes."
"And hold them. Until he comes for them."
"Yes."
"Downstairs."
Wren did not say yes to that. She did not need to.
Corin thought about it. He made himself think about it properly. Not the way you think about a thing when you are frightened, in quick bright jumps, here and there, like a bird in a room. The way you think about a page in a file. Slowly. Line by line. From the top.
He could run.
He could get up from this chair, now, and go down the back stair and out through the yard and into the dark, and keep going. Over the roofs, the way Juno had taught him. Down to the river. Onto a barge, any barge, going anywhere. Out past the Customs House and the forts and the last of the lights, the way Tam had gone. He had a little money. He had the rod. He had the Ledger. He could be a long way from Veridia by morning.
And at eleven o'clock, two tall gray figures with white egg faces would walk down Tallow Street, and stop outside No. 14, and duck their heads under the lintel at exactly the same moment, like two trees bending in the same wind. And they would go down the dark passage that smelled of onions. And they would knock on the purple door.
Three times. Slowly.
And Mrs. Marta would open it. Because people always did. Because she had told him herself, at her kitchen table, with her red arms folded on her account book: I've opened it to every one of them, and looked them in the eye, and told them what I thought of them.
And Juno would be standing behind her.
He could warn them. He could send word tonight. Juno could take her grandmother over the roofs, to the arches, to Mother Grue. They could hide.
But the order said the household. And the man in the glass room had said his friends are the doors he's knocked on. And Kross had the list. Mother Grue's arches were on it. So was Pruett's. So was the coal wharf. So, by now, was the Lamplighter's Rest. There was nowhere they could go that was not already written down in a neat little list in Silas Kross's pocket.
And wherever they went, Kross would follow. Patiently. Eating barley sugar. Folding the papers small. Until Corin came.
Because Corin would come. Kross knew that. That was the whole point. That was what Especially that one meant, maybe. Or one of the things. That a man like Corin Vane would always, in the end, come back for the people who had opened their doors to him.
He looked at the candle.
"I'm going home," he said.
Wren did not move.
"Tomorrow," said Corin. "In the morning. Early. Before eleven." He looked up at her. "I'll be in my room at the top of the house. With the door shut. When they come."
"And then?"
"And then I'll think of something."
Wren looked at him for a long time across the clean pale flame.
"That's not a plan," she said.
"No."
"That's walking up five flights of stairs and sitting on your bed and waiting for the white faces."
"Yes."
"They'll take you. Brought whole to the Hill. Taken down." Her voice was very level. "And whatever is behind the locked door in you, he'll be the first thing it sees."
"Maybe."
"Maybe?"
Corin did not answer that. He did not know how. He only knew, somewhere very deep, in the place where Tam's bed under the slate roof was, and Tam's rain, and the laugh like a kettle, that he was not going to run and leave Mrs. Marta to open her purple door alone. He did not know what he was going to do instead. But it was not that.
Wren watched his face.
Then, slowly, she reached into her cloak again. Not for a paper this time. For something small. She laid it on the table between them, beside the candle.
It was a key.
Not like the one in his Ledger. Not old. Not iron. Not shaped like a leaf. A plain brass key, new and bright and cheap, with a round bow and a short shank and a little paper tag tied to it with string. On the tag, in her small upright hand, a number. 4.
"The fourth cellar," said Wren. "Under No. 14. Under all the cellars on Tallow Street, there's a drain. An old one. Older than the street. Brick. It runs down the hill under the houses, all the way to the river, and joins the big sewer under the Customs House." She looked at him. "The old drains are the only part of the city the Erasers were never taught. Rook built them for streets and doors and stairs. Not tunnels. They don't fit, for one thing. Seven feet tall. And they don't like the water."
"How do you know?"
"Because I watched one walk into the river once," said Wren. "Down by the old ferry. Chasing a man who'd jumped. It walked in up to its waist, and stopped, and stood there, and didn't move again until the tide went out. They had to send carts for it." A pause. "The Inquisition doesn't talk about that either."
Corin looked at the brass key.
"The fourth cellar under No. 14 belongs to the coal merchant," said Wren. "He keeps the grating at the back locked. That's the key to the grating. I took it off his ring on Saturday, when I went to look at your house."
"You went to look at my house. On Saturday."
"I've been looking at your house since Friday." She did not smile. "Since the night you came to the Third Basement and looked at me as if I might be somebody worth looking at. I wanted to know where you'd go. When it came."
Corin picked up the key.
It was light and warm from her pocket. The paper tag fluttered a little in the heat of the candle.
"If you go home in the morning," said Wren, "and they come at eleven, and they knock—then you'll have a breath. One breath, between the knock and the door. Rook made sure of that. The length of a long breath." She looked at the key in his hand. "It's five flights from your room to the cellar. You won't make it in a breath."
"No."
"So you'll need longer."
"How?"
Wren was quiet for a moment.
"I'll be on the step," she said. "Behind Kross. Behind Rowan. In my white coat, with my black notebook, the way I always am." Her voice did not change. "And I've stood on two thousand one hundred and six steps, Vane. I know exactly how long a breath is. And I know exactly how long I can make it last."
Corin looked at her.
"What will they do to you?" he said. "If they find out."
"They won't find out."
"If they do."
Wren Halloway looked back at him across the candle. Her sharp pale face was very calm. Her straight dark brows were very straight.
"Then you'll have to put me in a black book," she said. "Number two thousand one hundred and seven. Since you'll have escaped."
She said it quite seriously. And Corin, for the first time since he had walked into the attic, saw something move at the corner of her mouth that might, in a different face, on a different night, have been a smile.
The Other Key
Corin put the brass key in his pocket. The left one, with his knife and his pencil and his bit of string.
And his fingers touched the Ledger, through the cloth of his coat, over his heart. And he thought of the other key. The one inside it. Between the pages.
He had not meant to show her. He had told himself, on the long walk through the Ink Quarter with Fisk somewhere behind him, that he would not. That the less anybody knew, the fewer doors. That Linus Hale had slipped it into his pocket in secret, with his thin arms round him, and that a thing given in secret should be kept in secret.
But she had given him the brass one. Freely. On the table. With her own name as the price.
He took out the Ledger.
Wren watched him do it. She did not say anything. She watched him untie the leather strap, and open the book, carefully, near the back, where it was fat with the things he had put between its pages. Lyra's letter on its torn page with the leaf border. The pale green card from the Fourth Desk. The curl of hard brown orange peel. The little faded red reader, with its corners worn through. Her own scrap of paper. Not home.
And the key.
He took it out and laid it on the table, beside the candle, where the brass one had been.
It lay there in the pale clean light. Dark iron, almost black, smooth and shining in places where fingers had rubbed it. Three plain blunt teeth. A round thin shank. And the bow. The flat broad leaf, with its long stem and its rounded edges going in and out, in and out, in deep soft curves. And the vein down the middle, cut fine, with the smaller veins branching off it like the bones of a tiny wing.
Wren looked at it.
And Corin, watching her, saw nothing at all happen to her face.
Nothing. That was what he noticed. Not white, not gray, not anything. Not the narrowing of the eyes that he had seen when he told her about the Colonel. Not the tightening of the mouth. Her face stayed exactly as it was. Sharp and pale and careful. Calm. Perfectly calm.
Too calm.
It was the calm of a woman who has been taught, for four years in a gray building with no windows on the ground floor, how to keep her face still while somebody reads her. And who is using every single thing she was taught. All at once. Very hard.
Her hands lay flat on the table. They did not move.
"Where did you get that?" she said.
Her voice was the minutes-of-a-meeting voice. Clear and cool and polite.
"Somebody put it in my pocket," said Corin. "This afternoon. I didn't feel them do it."
"Who?"
He did not answer.
Wren looked at the key for a long moment more. Then she said, "May I?" Exactly the way Elara had said it, at the glass desk, with her bare hand over the Ledger. That is not the same as yes.
"Yes," said Corin.
She picked it up.
She did it slowly. With two fingers. By the end of the shank, the way you would pick up something that might be hot. She lifted it off the table and held it up between them, at the height of her eyes, with the candle behind it, so that the light came round the edges of the iron leaf and made a thin gold line all round it, like the gold line round the edge of Oren's barrier.
She turned it. Slowly. One way. Then the other. The candlelight moved on the little cut veins.
And her thumb moved.
Corin saw it. Just once. Very small. Her thumb came round from the side of the shank, where she was holding it, and touched the leaf. The flat of it. In the middle. And moved along the vein. From the stem to the tip. Slowly. The way you trace a line on a map with your finger, to find your way. The way you trace the letters of a name on a stone, in the dark, to read it.
As if her thumb knew the way.
Then it stopped. And went back to the side of the shank. And she put the key down on the table again, very carefully, exactly where it had been.
"I've never seen it before," she said.
Corin did not say anything.
He thought of Malakor's voice, that afternoon, in the Lamplighter's Rest. Look at his left hand. Look at the thumb.
He thought of Linus Hale, rubbing and rubbing his thumb against the side of his finger, every time he told a kind lie.
He looked at Wren's hands, flat and still on the table. And her face, calm and still in the candlelight. And he thought that he had never in his life seen anyone work so hard to keep so still.
"No," he said quietly. "I don't suppose you have."
They sat in silence for a while. The new candle burned straight and pale between them. Below, in the dark house, a clock that Corin had not known was there struck the half hour. Half past eleven. A thin, tinny sound, like a spoon on a cup.
Wren was the first to speak.
"Whoever gave it to you," she said, "was he old?"
Corin looked at her.
"Old," said Wren. Her eyes were on the key, not on him. "Thin. Gray. With round spectacles. With ink on his—" She stopped.
The word hung in the air between them, over the candle, unfinished.
Corin did not move. He did not breathe. He watched her face, and he saw, for the first time since he had walked into the attic, the calm break. Not much. A crack. A hairline crack, like the crack across the blue stone in Lord Thorne's shell box. Her lips pressed together, hard. Her eyes shut, for less than a moment, and opened again.
"Fingers," said Corin softly. "Ink on his fingers. Blue. Right into the skin."
Wren said nothing.
"How do you know that?" said Corin.
She did not answer for a long time.
Then she said, in a voice that was not the minutes-of-a-meeting voice at all, a voice that was much younger, and much quieter, and that seemed to come from somewhere a very long way off, "I don't know."
She looked at the key.
"I don't know how I know," she said. "I've never met him. I'm sure I've never met him. I'd remember." Her hands, on the table, had begun, very slightly, to shake. "But when I saw that leaf, I saw a hand. An old man's hand. With blue on the fingers. Holding it out. To me. In the dark. By a door." She swallowed. "And a voice. Saying—"
She stopped again.
"Saying what?" said Corin.
Wren looked at him. Her eyes were very wide. Very dark. And wet.
"Keep it safe, little one," she said. "Keep it safe until somebody comes who needs it."
The candle flame did not move.
Corin thought of a page in a small black book with a single letter on its cover. Brought to me at Crown Walk by H.L. himself, at night, by the garden door. Female. Aged about two years.
He thought of a height chart on a nursery wall. A third set of marks, a little higher than Lyra's. From a child about two years older. Measured every birthday from two to six. And then the name gouged out. And under the gouge, a W. And a tiny oak leaf.
He thought of Wren Halloway, twenty-two years old, with brown hair pulled back so tight it looked painted on, who had been taken into the Inquisition Academy as a girl and had no family that anybody knew of.
He did not say any of it.
Not because he did not want to. Because he looked at her face, cracked open in the candlelight, and he saw that she was standing at the edge of something very deep and very dark, the way he had stood at the edge of the empty rooms in himself, and that if he said one more word, one name, one date, she would fall in. And she was going to stand on his doorstep at eleven o'clock tomorrow morning in a white coat, beside Silas Kross, and keep her face perfectly still.
She could not do that if she fell in tonight.
Not yet, Linus Hale had said. Please. Not yet. She's not ready.
No. He had said he. He's not ready. He had meant Corin.
But it fitted her too.
"It's a key," said Corin gently. "That's all. An old key. I don't know what it opens. Maybe nothing anymore."
Wren looked at him for a long time.
Then, slowly, the crack closed. He watched it close. Her mouth relaxed. Her eyes dried. Her hands went still on the table. And her face was calm again, sharp and pale and careful, the way it had been when she opened the door to him.
But not quite the same. He thought it would never be quite the same.
"Keep it safe," she said quietly.
"I will."
He put the oak-leaf key back between the pages of the Ledger, next to the little red reader, and closed the book over it, and tied the strap.
And Wren watched him do it, every inch of the way, the way Linus Hale had watched the silver rod go back into his pocket. As if she were watching something precious being put away into a drawer, and was not sure she would ever see it again.
