Chapter 28: The Night Before, Part 4 of 5: For the Dark
For the Dark
There was one more part of the Ledger he had not gone through.
The very back. The last pages. Past the place where his writing stopped, most nights. Past the pages he had filled in the last two weeks, with Tam and Hollis and the glass room and the Fourth Desk. Right at the end, inside the back cover, where the leather was softest and most worn.
He kept a list there.
He had started it a long time ago. In the first month, perhaps. In the first week. He did not remember starting it. He only knew it had always been there, at the back, on the last three pages, in a column down the left side, and that he had added to it now and then, over the years, when he found something that belonged on it.
At the top of the first page, in big black letters, pressed deep:
FOR THE DARK.
And under it, the list.
He did not read it often. He read it only on the bad nights. The nights when the candle had burned down and there was no money for another one, and he lay on his narrow bed at the top of No. 14 Tallow Street with the black pressing down on him from the slope of the roof, and his heart going like a drum, and his hands wet, and a terror in him so old and so deep that he did not know where it came from. Only that it had been there before Oakhaven. Before the empty rooms. You used to fear the dark. He had read that line on the first page every morning for six years. He had never needed to read it. He had always known.
On those nights he would light a match, if he had one, and open the Ledger at the back, and read the list. By the light of the match, before it burned his fingers. And sometimes it helped.
He turned to it now, by the light of Wren's candle, to see if there was anything on it that should go into the new black book.
FOR THE DARK.
The dark is only the room with the light gone out. Everything that was in it is still there.
Count the things you can hear. The river. A cart. Your own breath. That's three. You're still here.
Mrs. Marta snores. Through four floors. If you can hear her, it's not that late.
Vespera says ink looks black in the dark but it's every color in the morning.
Juno sleeps on the roof in summer. She says the dark is just the sky without its coat on.
Morning always comes. It has come every single time. Two thousand times. More.
Corin read it slowly, down the column.
Some of the lines he remembered writing. Juno sleeps on the roof in summer. That was only last year. He remembered her saying it, sitting on the ridge of the roof outside his window with her knees up, eating an apple. Mrs. Marta snores. He remembered writing that one, too, at two in the morning, laughing, on a bad night, and how the laughing had helped more than the line.
He turned to the second page.
More lines. Older ones, in a hand that was more careful, more upright, the hand of the first years. Light a match. Then you can see for a moment. A moment is enough. Say your name out loud. Corin. Corin Vane. Say it till you believe it. Put your hand on your chest. Feel the word there. It's still there.
He turned to the third page.
And stopped.
The third page was the last page of the Ledger. The very last. The one glued down to the inside of the back cover. It was the softest and the most worn of all the pages, and the corner of it had curled up with years of his thumb lifting it.
There were only two lines on it.
The first was in his own ordinary hand. The hand of every night. Small and clear and careful.
Morning always comes.
He had written that one twice, then. Once on the first page, once here. He did not remember doing it.
And under it, there was a second line.
It was in his hand too. He knew it at once. The same big black letters. The same hard slope to the right. The same way of pressing so deep that the pen had cut the paper, in two places, right through to the leather underneath.
But it shook.
Every letter of it shook. Not a little. A great deal. The lines wavered up and down like the line of a hill seen through heat. The loops went wide and wild and came back crooked. It was his hand. But it was his hand as if it had been written in the dark, by feel, with no light at all. Or in great pain. Or with somebody holding his wrist, very hard, and steering it. Or a very, very long time ago.
The dark is the day with its eyes shut.
And after it, at the end of the line, a little apart, smaller, a single letter, and a dash.
— K.
Corin sat very still.
He had never seen it before.
That was not quite true. He must have seen it. It was on the last page of his own Ledger. He must have turned to it a hundred times, on a hundred bad nights, with a match burning down in his fingers. His thumb had worn the corner of that very page soft. He must have read it.
But he did not remember it. Not the line. Not the K. Not writing it. Not reading it. Nothing. It was as if he were looking at it for the first time in his life, here, now, in Wren's attic, at two in the morning, by the light of her candle.
The dark is the day with its eyes shut.
He read it again. And again.
And something happened in his chest.
It was very small. It was like the thing that had happened in the glass room, through Tam, when the locked door at the end of his house had shuddered. Only not like that at all. Not a shudder. Not a blow. Softer. Like a knock. One knock. Very gentle. From the other side of a door. The way you might knock on a door where a child is sleeping, to see if it is awake, without wanting to wake it if it isn't.
And for one moment, less than a moment, he was not afraid of the dark.
He had been afraid of the dark every night of his life that he could remember. Every single night. Even with Juno on the roof and Mrs. Marta snoring and the list at the back of the Ledger. It was the oldest thing in him. Older than his own name.
And for one moment it was not there.
And then it was again.
"Vane?"
Wren's voice. Quiet. He looked up. She was watching him across the candle.
"What is it?" she said.
He turned the Ledger round, without speaking, and pushed it across the table toward her. Open at the last page.
She looked down at it.
He saw her read the first line. Morning always comes. Then the second. He saw her eyes stop on it. On the shaking letters. On the places where the pen had cut through.
"That's your hand," she said slowly.
"Yes."
"But it isn't."
"No."
She looked at it for a long time.
"Something written in a hand that is not yours," she said quietly, "or yours, but not now. That's what the Auditor said. You told me."
"Yes."
"Is this it?"
"I don't know," said Corin. "Maybe."
Wren put her finger, very lightly, on the K. The single letter, a little apart, at the end of the line.
"Who's K?" she said.
Corin opened his mouth.
And behind his left ear, Malakor made a sound.
It was not a word. It was the sound a man makes when he is trying to lift something and it slips out of his hands. A sharp, startled, broken little sound. And then, very fast, very strained:
K— Ka— Kae—
And nothing.
Corin waited. The candle burned. Wren was looking at him.
Malakor?
Gone, said the dry old voice at last. Very quietly. Very tired. It's gone, boy. I had it. I had it right on the tip of my tongue, the way the lamplighter's grandfather had the name on the bridge. A boy's name. A boy who waved. A long pause. And then it slid. Like all the others.
A boy who waved?
But Malakor did not say anything else.
Corin looked down at the line. At the shaking letters. The dark is the day with its eyes shut.
"I don't know who K is," he said to Wren. "I don't know anyone called K."
It was true. He did not.
But he looked at the line, and something in him that was older than his name, older than the dark, older than the empty rooms with Oakhaven written on their doors, knew it. Knew the voice that had said it. Not his own voice. A boy's voice. Very young. Very kind. Saying it to him once, in the dark, a long time ago, to help.
And he had written it down.
Somehow. Somewhere. In a hand that shook so badly he could hardly form the letters. And then forgotten he had ever done it.
He did not cut that page out.
He did not copy it into the new black book. He could not have said why. It belonged where it was. At the very back. On the last page. Glued to the cover. Where his thumb had worn the corner soft on a hundred bad nights, reaching for it in the dark without knowing what he was reaching for.
He took the Ledger back from Wren and closed it, gently, and tied the strap.
"Keep it," said Wren softly. It was not a question.
"Yes."
She nodded. She did not ask anything else. She looked at him across the candle for a long moment with her sharp pale careful face, and then she looked away, at the skylight, at the black square of the night above them.
"It's a good line," she said. "Whoever K was."
The Voice in the Rod
Wren went down to the yard for water.
She took a tin jug from the shelf by the door and lifted the bar and went out onto the little landing, and Corin heard her feet on the back stair, going down, quick and light and sure, stepping over the missing step without stopping, the way you step over a thing you have stepped over a thousand times. Then the creak of the yard gate. Then the squeal of a pump handle, far below, and the splash of water into tin.
He was alone in the attic.
The candle burned on the table. The black books stood in their rows. The skylight was a square of dull orange-black above him, where the clouds hung low over the city with the lamplight under them.
He took the silver rod out of his pocket and laid it on the table, beside the Ledger.
It lay there in the candlelight. Thin and bright. The spiral at its tip turning to the right. So that it's always going forward.
"Malakor," he said.
Out loud. He had never done that before. In six years, he had never once spoken to the voice out loud. He had always thought at it, the way you think at somebody in a crowded room, behind your face, so that nobody else can hear. But there was nobody else here. Only the books.
For a long moment there was no answer.
Then, behind his left ear, very quietly:
I'm here, boy.
"You were weeping. In the inn. When he went."
A long silence.
Yes.
"I didn't know you could."
Neither did I. The dry voice was very tired. I haven't for a long time. Eighteen years, nearly. Not since they put me in here. A pause. It's not the same as with a face. There's nothing to run down. It's more like—a cold, going out of you. And then a warm, coming in after. And then the cold again. Another pause. He held me. Linus. In his hand. Do you know how long it's been since anybody held me who knew it was me?
Corin did not answer. He did not need to.
You hold me every day, said Malakor. I know. I'm grateful, boy. Don't think I'm not. But you hold me the way you hold a tool. A good tool, that you're fond of, that you'd be sorry to lose. The voice was very quiet. He held me the way you hold a friend's hand at a graveside.
The candle flame was very still.
"They said you drowned," said Corin. "Drunk. Below the old ferry."
I heard.
"Did you?"
No. A long breath, if a voice in a rod could breathe. No. I never touched a drop. Not after Oakhaven.
"What happened?"
Malakor did not answer for a long time. Corin sat and waited. Below, in the yard, the pump squealed again, and stopped.
I don't know, said Malakor at last. That's the truth, boy. I don't know. I remember the road up the valley, in the dark. I remember the oak at the top of it, black against the stars, and the schoolhouse bell tower beside it, and the smell of the stream. I remember thinking, I'm too late. And then— A pause. Then nothing. A smooth place. Like the stone on the bridge. And then I was in here. In the cold. In the dark. In a drawer in a desk in the Harvesting Section, with the new rods all round me in their boxes, and nobody to talk to. For twelve years.
"Twelve years."
Until a young man with no name came in one morning, six years ago, with his shoulders up round his ears, and sat down at that desk, and opened the drawer. And picked me up. And I said, very quietly, the way you'd say it to a frightened horse, Easy, boy. And you nearly dropped me.
Corin found that he was almost smiling.
"I remember that," he said. "I thought I was going mad."
You were, a little. You'd only been out of the white room a week. A pause. But you kept me. You didn't send me to be melted. You put me in your pocket. And you talked to me. In your head. Every day. The dry voice was very low. Six years, boy. You're the only one who ever talked to me. In eighteen years. Except him.
Corin looked at the rod on the table.
"The man in the glass room," he said. "With the left spiral. You knew him."
Silence.
"Linus said you argued with him. In the schoolroom. After the children went home. He said he'd never seen you so angry."
Or so frightened, said Malakor. Yes. I remember that. I remember the argument. I remember the room. I remember the slates on the children's desks, and the smell of chalk, and the stove going out. A pause. I can't remember his face.
"Nobody can."
No. I suppose they can't. The voice was strained, the way it had been on the bridge, trying to lift the name. I remember what he said. That a rod should turn so that everything goes in and nothing comes back out. I remember what I said back. I said a harvester takes what the dead want to give, and no more, and that a man who builds a rod to take everything isn't a harvester, he's a— It stopped.
"A what?"
I don't know. The word's gone. Slid off. Bitterly. That's how good he is, boy. Eighteen years dead, near enough, and I still can't finish a sentence about him.
Corin was quiet.
"Malakor," he said. "Tomorrow."
I know.
"If they take me. If I don't get to the drain. If they take me up the Hill, to the east tower, and down. Downstairs." He looked at the rod. "He'll take you too. He'll take you out of my pocket. And he'll know you. Won't he. He'll know whose rod you are."
The voice did not answer at once.
Yes, it said. He'll know.
"What will he do?"
I don't know. Melt me, maybe. At last. A pause. Or keep me. In a drawer. In the dark. The way he keeps everything. Another pause, longer. I think I'd rather be melted, boy. If you're asking.
"I'm asking."
Then listen. The voice changed. Became very clear. Very steady. The voice of a man saying something he has thought about for a long time. If they take you, and you can't get away, and they're putting their hands on you—break me. Snap me. Across your knee. Like a stick. It's only silver. It's old. It'll go.
Corin stared at the rod.
"And you?"
I'll go too. Wherever it is the dead go when they're let out. Into the wind. Into the river. Into some child's dream on Cinder Lane. A dry, small sound that might have been a laugh. Like Hollis's jars. Vespera was right about that. It's better than a drawer.
"I won't."
You will if you have to.
"I won't, Malakor."
Boy. Very gently. Gentler than Corin had ever heard it. Linus said it. Keep it close. Never let anybody take it from you. He meant it. If the only way to keep it from him is to break it, then that's keeping it. A pause. Promise me.
Corin did not answer.
He sat in the hard wooden chair and looked at the thin silver rod on the table, with its fine spiral turning the right way, that an old man had cut by hand, a hair at a time, all one winter, by a schoolhouse stove, for a friend.
Below, the yard gate creaked. Feet on the back stair. Quick and light and sure.
"I'll try," said Corin.
That's not a promise.
"Juno said that too."
Juno's a clever girl. A pause. It'll do. For now.
The latch lifted. Wren came in with the tin jug, water slopping over its lip, and her cheeks pink from the cold, and stopped in the doorway, and looked at him. At the rod on the table. At his face.
"Were you talking to someone?" she said.
"Yes," said Corin.
She looked at the rod for a long moment. She did not ask who. She came in and shut the door and dropped the bar across it and put the jug down on the table.
"Tell him I said good evening," she said.
And behind Corin's ear, for the first time in all the years he had known it, the dry old voice said nothing at all, because it was too surprised.
